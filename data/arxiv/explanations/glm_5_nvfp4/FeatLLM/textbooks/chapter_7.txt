\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 7: # Empirical Evaluation: Datasets, Baselines, and Results

The empirical validation of any novel machine learning framework requires rigorous evaluation across diverse benchmark datasets, careful selection of competitive baselines, and thorough analysis of performance under various experimental conditions. This chapter presents the comprehensive experimental evaluation of the FeatLLM framework, demonstrating its efficacy for few-shot tabular learning across multiple domains and comparing it against both traditional machine learning approaches and contemporary large language model-based methods. Through systematic experimentation, we examine not only raw predictive performance but also crucial practical considerations such as data efficiency, robustness to spurious correlations, and computational cost.

# Benchmark Datasets: Standard and Novel Domains

A robust empirical evaluation requires datasets that span diverse domains, vary in complexity, and present different challenges for machine learning algorithms. The evaluation of FeatLLM employs thirteen distinct tabular datasets, carefully selected to include both well-established benchmarks and newer datasets that address potential concerns about data memorization by large language models.

The benchmark collection includes nine standard datasets that have been widely used in the machine learning literature, many of which originate from the UCI Machine Learning Repository. The Adult dataset addresses income prediction, determining whether an individual earns more than \$50,000 annually based on demographic and employment features. The Bank dataset focuses on marketing applications, predicting whether a customer will subscribe to a term deposit. The Blood dataset presents a healthcare-related prediction task, determining whether blood donors will return for subsequent donations. The Car dataset involves predicting vehicle quality ratings based on various attributes. The Communities dataset addresses a social science application, predicting crime rates in specified regions. The Credit-g dataset is a financial risk assessment task, predicting whether an individual poses a good or bad credit risk. The Diabetes dataset involves medical diagnosis, predicting the presence of diabetes in patients. The Heart dataset addresses cardiovascular health, predicting the occurrence of coronary artery disease. Finally, the Myocardial dataset involves predicting chronic heart failure complications from myocardial infarction data.

These standard datasets vary substantially in their characteristics. Some, like Communities and Myocardial, include over one hundred attributes, presenting significant challenges due to the limited context window sizes of large language models. This variability in feature dimensionality is essential for evaluating whether methods can handle realistic, complex tabular data rather than only simplified low-dimensional problems.

However, evaluating solely on established benchmark datasets introduces a potential confound: large language models are trained on vast text corpora that may include these datasets or related discussions. If an LLM has effectively memorized the training examples from a dataset, its performance may appear artificially high without genuinely demonstrating few-shot learning capability. To address this critical concern, the evaluation incorporates four additional datasets that are either too recent to have been included in LLM pre-training or are entirely synthetic.

The Cultivars dataset addresses an agricultural prediction task, determining the grain yield level of soybean cultivars. This dataset was donated to the UCI Machine Learning Repository after September 2023, ensuring it was not part of the pre-training data for the GPT-3.5 model used in experiments. The NHANES dataset involves predicting a person's age group from health record data, and similarly, it was recently donated to UCI after the relevant LLM's pre-training cutoff. The Sequence-type dataset is a synthetic classification task where the goal is to categorize a given numerical sequence as one of four types: arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, or Collatz. The Solution-mix dataset is another synthetic task, predicting whether the concentration percentage of a mixture from four solutions exceeds a threshold of 0.5. These synthetic datasets are particularly valuable because they are mathematically constructed rather than sampled from real-world data, guaranteeing they could not have been memorized by the LLM during pre-training.

The inclusion of both standard benchmarks and novel datasets provides a more complete picture of model capabilities. Strong performance on standard datasets demonstrates practical applicability to common machine learning problems, while strong performance on novel datasets provides evidence that the model is genuinely learning from the few-shot examples rather than merely retrieving memorized information.

# Experimental Setup: K-Shot Learning and Evaluation Protocols

The experimental methodology follows established protocols for few-shot learning, where the goal is to achieve strong predictive performance given only a small number of labeled training examples. This setting reflects many real-world scenarios where labeled data is expensive or time-consuming to obtain, making it essential to learn efficiently from limited supervision.

In the $k$-shot learning framework, only $k$ labeled samples are randomly sampled from the full dataset to form the training set. The value of $k$ represents a crucial experimental parameter, with smaller values presenting more challenging learning scenarios. The experiments evaluate performance across multiple values of $k$, including 4, 8, 16, and 32 shots. These values span the range from extremely data-scarce regimes, where most traditional machine learning methods struggle, to settings where more conventional approaches become viable.

The evaluation uses the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) as the primary metric. AUC is particularly appropriate for few-shot learning evaluation because it measures the model's ability to rank positive instances higher than negative instances, regardless of the specific probability threshold chosen. This threshold-independent quality is important because few-shot learning settings often lack sufficient data to reliably calibrate probability thresholds. Furthermore, AUC ranges from 0 to 1, with 0.5 indicating random performance and 1.0 indicating perfect discrimination, making it easily interpretable across different datasets.

To ensure reliable performance estimates despite the inherent randomness in few-shot sample selection, each experiment is repeated three times with different random seeds governing the choice of training and test sets. The reported results include both the mean and standard deviation of AUC values across these repetitions, providing insight into both typical performance and variability.

For model selection and hyperparameter tuning, $k$-fold cross-validation is employed on the few-shot training set. The value of $k$ is set to either 2 or 4, ensuring that the training folds include at least one example of each class. This careful design prevents situations where cross-validation fails due to missing class labels in a fold, which could otherwise occur in extremely low-shot regimes.

The implementation uses GPT-3.5 (specifically the gpt-3.5-turbo-0613 variant) as the large language model backbone for FeatLLM. The LLM inference uses a temperature parameter of 0.5 to introduce controlled randomness in rule generation, facilitating diversity across ensemble members. The top-p value is set to the default value of 1 in the API. The number of ensemble trials is set to 20, meaning the entire rule generation and training process is repeated 20 times, with predictions averaged across all trials. The number of rules extracted per class is set to 10, providing a balance between having sufficient expressive power and maintaining reasonable prompt length.

For the downstream linear model that learns rule importance weights, training uses the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01 for 200 epochs. The optimal epoch is selected using $k$-fold cross-validation on the training data. The weights are constrained to be non-negative, implemented by taking the maximum of learned weights and zero before computing class logits.

# Baseline Methods: A Comprehensive Comparison Framework

To properly contextualize the performance of FeatLLM, the experimental evaluation includes nine baseline methods spanning three categories: conventional machine learning approaches, pre-trained and semi-supervised methods, and large language model-based approaches.

The first category includes three conventional tabular learning methods that represent standard practice in applied machine learning. Logistic Regression (LogReg) is a fundamental linear classification method that models the log-odds of class membership as a linear combination of features. Despite its simplicity, logistic regression often provides competitive performance on tabular data, particularly when features are informative and the relationship with the target is approximately linear. XGBoost is a gradient boosting framework that has become one of the most successful methods for tabular prediction, combining decision tree ensembles with regularization techniques to prevent overfitting. XGBoost typically requires more training data than simple linear models but can capture complex nonlinear relationships. Random Forest is another tree-based ensemble method that constructs multiple decision trees on bootstrapped samples and averages their predictions. These conventional methods provide an important reference point: if FeatLLM cannot outperform these standard approaches in few-shot regimes, it would indicate that the LLM-based feature engineering approach is not providing genuine value.

The second category includes methods that leverage unlabeled data or pre-training to improve few-shot performance. SCARF is a contrastive learning approach for tabular data that learns representations by contrasting augmented views of data points. By leveraging large amounts of unlabeled data, such methods aim to learn useful feature representations that transfer to the labeled few-shot task. STUNT is a more recent semi-supervised approach that generates synthetic training data through self-training techniques. However, these methods require access to substantial unlabeled datasets, which may not be feasible in many real-world applications. TabPFN represents a different approach to incorporating prior knowledge: it pre-trains a Bayesian neural network on synthetic data generated from diverse distribution types. After pre-training, TabPFN can perform inference on new tabular datasets without further training by conditioning on both training and test samples. The prior knowledge encoded in the pre-trained weights helps overcome the data scarcity problem.

The third category includes methods that directly leverage large language models for tabular prediction. In-context learning represents the most straightforward approach: few-shot training examples are serialized into text and included in the LLM prompt along with feature and task descriptions, and the LLM generates predictions directly without any training or fine-tuning. This method tests the raw in-context learning capability of LLMs on tabular data. TABLET enhances basic in-context learning by incorporating additional hints derived from external classifiers, providing rule-sets and prototypes to improve the quality of inference. TabLLM employs parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques such as IA3 to adapt the LLM to the specific tabular prediction task. By tuning a small number of parameters while keeping most of the model frozen, TabLLM aims to leverage both the LLM's prior knowledge and the information in the few-shot examples.

It is worth noting that the baseline methods differ in their requirements and constraints. Traditional methods like logistic regression and XGBoost can be applied to any tabular dataset but may overfit with very few examples. Methods like SCARF and STUNT require additional unlabeled data, which may not always be available. TabLLM requires access to the model weights for fine-tuning, which is not possible with API-only access to proprietary models. Methods that require passing each sample through the LLM at inference time face significant computational costs when scaling to large datasets. FeatLLM is designed to overcome these limitations: it requires only API-level access, does not need unlabeled data, and performs inference using a simple linear model without requiring LLM queries for each sample.

For hyperparameter tuning of traditional machine learning methods, Grid-search combined with $k$-fold cross-validation is used to determine optimal parameters. In-context learning and TABLET use GPT-3.5 as the backbone, while TabLLM uses T0, following the original implementation. The restriction to T0 for TabLLM arises because TabLLM requires access to model weights for fine-tuning, and GPT-3.5 is only accessible via API without weight access.

# Performance Analysis: FeatLLM Versus Baselines

The central question of the empirical evaluation is whether FeatLLM achieves strong predictive performance compared to baselines, particularly in the challenging low-data regime. The results across all thirteen datasets demonstrate that FeatLLM consistently ranks as either the top performer or achieves second place among all compared methods.

The performance advantage of FeatLLM over alternative LLM-based approaches is substantial, averaging approximately 10% improvement in AUC over methods like TabLLM and STUNT. This improvement holds across diverse domains including healthcare (Diabetes, Heart, Myocardial), finance (Credit-g, Bank), social sciences (Communities), and synthetic tasks (Sequence-type, Solution-mix). The consistency of these improvements across such varied domains suggests that the rule-based feature engineering approach is capturing a general principle rather than being tailored to specific data characteristics.

A particularly notable finding is that some baseline methods failed to produce predictions on datasets with large numbers of features. The Communities and Myocardial datasets contain over 100 attributes each, which exceeds the prompt length limitations of LLM-based methods that attempt to serialize all features for each sample. In contrast, FeatLLM's bagging and ensemble approach successfully handles these high-dimensional datasets by selecting subsets of features for each trial. This practical capability is crucial for real-world applications, where tabular datasets often contain numerous features that practitioners have collected over time.

The ablation studies provide insight into which components contribute most to performance. Removing weight tuning from the linear model causes performance degradation, with the impact increasing as the number of shots grows. This finding indicates that when more labeled data is available, the model can more accurately estimate the importance of different rules, making the tuning process more valuable. Conversely, removing feature descriptions or reasoning instructions has a larger impact when the number of shots is small, suggesting that efficient utilization of LLM prior knowledge becomes more critical in the most data-scarce regimes. The ensemble approach consistently improves performance across all settings, confirming that aggregating predictions from multiple rule sets enhances robustness.

Beyond raw predictive performance, the evaluation examines computational efficiency through training and inference time comparisons. On the Adult dataset with 16 training samples, FeatLLM achieves inference time comparable to conventional machine learning methods, while LLM-based approaches like in-context learning and TabLLM require substantially more inference time due to the need to query the LLM for each test sample. The training time of FeatLLM is comparable to other LLM-based methods, requiring approximately 30 API queries that can be parallelized. This efficiency analysis demonstrates that FeatLLM successfully achieves its design goal of leveraging LLM capabilities while avoiding the computational overhead of per-sample LLM queries at inference time.

# Data Efficiency: Achieving Competitive Performance with Fewer Shots

One of the most compelling findings from the empirical evaluation is the remarkable data efficiency of FeatLLM compared to conventional machine learning methods. The framework achieves competitive performance using dramatically fewer labeled examples, demonstrating the value of incorporating LLM prior knowledge into the feature engineering process.

Specifically, FeatLLM using only 4 labeled samples achieves performance comparable to conventional machine learning baselines that require 32 labeled samples. This eight-fold reduction in labeling requirements has profound practical implications. In domains where labeling requires expert knowledge—such as medical diagnosis, legal document classification, or specialized industrial quality control—each labeled example carries significant cost. An eight-fold reduction in labeling requirements could translate to substantial cost savings or enable applications where labeling budgets are severely constrained.

This data efficiency stems from FeatLLM's ability to leverage the prior knowledge encoded in large language models. When the LLM generates rules for predicting a disease, for example, it draws on medical knowledge accumulated during pre-training—understanding that older age, certain symptoms, and specific risk factors are associated with disease outcomes. This knowledge allows the framework to generate informative features even when the few-shot training data is insufficient to learn these relationships from scratch.

The evaluation also examines robustness to spurious correlations, a common failure mode in few-shot learning. In low-data regimes, models are susceptible to learning accidental correlations that appear in the small training set but do not generalize. To test this, experiments add columns from an unrelated dataset (Adult) to the Heart dataset, creating irrelevant features that might appear correlated with the target by chance. The results show that FeatLLM exhibits the least performance degradation among compared methods when such spurious features are introduced. This robustness arises because FeatLLM's rule generation process explicitly considers the relationship between features and the task based on prior knowledge, preventing the generation of rules based on irrelevant columns. Analysis confirms that rules based on noisy, unrelated columns account for only 1-2% of total generated rules.

The quality of features generated by FeatLLM can be directly assessed by measuring the AUROC between individual generated features and target labels. Before tuning the linear model weights, the majority of generated rules show AUROC above 0.5, indicating they are informative for the prediction task. After tuning, rules with learned weights below a threshold of 0.1 are effectively filtered out, as their contributions to predictions become negligible. This automatic filtering mechanism provides an additional layer of quality control, ensuring that only genuinely useful features contribute to final predictions.

The hyperparameter analysis reveals insights into the framework's behavior. Performance improves as the number of ensemble members increases, but the gains begin to plateau around 20 ensemble members. The number of rules per class shows a less pronounced effect: while 10 rules per class was selected as a reasonable default, the exact number does not strongly impact performance. This finding suggests that having a moderate number of diverse rules is more important than precisely optimizing this parameter. However, generating too many rules can increase input dimensionality and potentially lead to overfitting in the low-shot regime, creating a trade-off between expressiveness and generalization.

These empirical findings collectively demonstrate that FeatLLM successfully addresses the key challenges of few-shot tabular learning. By treating LLMs as feature engineers rather than end-to-end predictors, the framework achieves strong predictive performance with minimal labeled data, maintains computational efficiency at inference time, and remains robust to spurious correlations that often plague few-shot learning approaches.
