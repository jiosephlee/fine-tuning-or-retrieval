Chapter 1: # Introduction to LLM-Enhanced Tabular Learning

# The Rise of Large Language Models and Their Reasoning Capabilities

In recent years, the field of artificial intelligence has witnessed a remarkable transformation with the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs). These models, trained on extensive and diverse text corpora, have demonstrated an impressive ability to generate suitable responses for previously unseen tasks without requiring task-specific fine-tuning. This capability, often referred to as in-context learning, represents a paradigm shift in how we approach machine learning problems. By incorporating a task description and a few examples into prompts, LLMs can understand the context of a task and "attend" to important features and examples, showcasing their remarkable reasoning capabilities.

The power of LLMs stems from their rich prior knowledge accumulated during training on massive text datasets. This knowledge can substitute for the need for expert domain knowledge in many areas, playing a crucial role in the automation of diverse applications. For instance, LLMs have been successfully applied to dialogue analysis and program repair, demonstrating their versatility across different domains. The ability to tackle challenging and unseen reasoning problems positions LLMs as a transformative technology with immense potential for various machine learning applications, including the domain of tabular learning that forms the focus of this chapter.

A key aspect of LLM reasoning is their capacity for what researchers call "chain-of-thought" processing. When presented with a problem, LLMs can be guided through structured reasoning paths that mimic how an expert human might approach a task. This structured approach allows the models to filter out uninformative features and focus on more significant aspects of the problem. The emergent abilities of LLMs—their capacity to perform tasks that were not explicitly trained—have opened new possibilities for applying these models to domains that traditionally relied heavily on domain expertise and feature engineering.

# The Importance of Tabular Data in Real-World Machine Learning Applications

Tabular data—data organized in rows and columns—remains the dominant format in real-world machine learning applications. From financial risk assessment to healthcare diagnostics, from customer churn prediction to scientific research, tabular datasets form the backbone of decision-making systems across virtually every industry. The structured nature of tabular data, with its clearly defined features and target variables, makes it particularly suited for supervised learning tasks where the goal is to predict outcomes based on input characteristics.

The significance of tabular learning extends beyond its ubiquity. In many critical applications, the relationships between features and targets are governed by domain-specific knowledge that practitioners have accumulated over years of study. For example, in healthcare, the knowledge that older individuals face increased risk of certain diseases is invaluable for disease prediction tasks. Similarly, in finance, understanding the factors that influence credit risk or market behavior requires expertise that has traditionally been the domain of specialists. This is precisely where the prior knowledge embedded in LLMs becomes relevant—the extensive text corpora used to train these models contain vast amounts of information about relationships, causes, and effects that could potentially benefit tabular learning tasks.

However, despite its importance, tabular data presents unique challenges that distinguish it from other data modalities like images or text. Unlike the homogeneous structure of image pixels or word tokens, tabular features are heterogeneous—they may be numerical, categorical, or ordinal, each requiring different treatment. The features also have semantic meaning encoded in their names and definitions, which can provide valuable context for learning algorithms. This heterogeneity and semantic richness make tabular data both challenging to work with and uniquely positioned to benefit from the knowledge captured in language models.

# Challenges in Few-Shot Learning: Data Scarcity and Spurious Correlations

Few-shot learning—the ability to learn from a small number of labeled samples—has emerged as a critical capability in modern machine learning. The advancement of few-shot learning has enabled the extraction of generalizable representations from data, even with minimal labeling costs. While initially focusing on image data, few-shot learning has proven its strong generalizability across various data modalities including text, audio, and, more recently, tabular data. However, learning from limited samples introduces significant challenges that are particularly pronounced in the tabular domain.

The primary challenge in few-shot learning is the risk of learning spurious correlations. When the number of labeled samples is small relative to the number of features, there is an increased likelihood that the model will identify patterns that exist in the training data by chance rather than reflecting true underlying relationships. These spurious correlations lead to models that perform well on training data but fail to generalize to unseen samples. In the tabular setting, where datasets may contain dozens or hundreds of features, this problem is exacerbated—the ratio of samples to features becomes unfavorable, and the model has insufficient information to distinguish meaningful patterns from noise.

To address these challenges, recent research efforts have employed additional sources of information or introduced domain-specific prior knowledge to impart appropriate inductive biases for model training. Approaches have included utilizing large quantities of more readily-available unlabeled datasets with judicious augmentation for semi-supervised learning, or seeking good model initialization through task-agnostic unsupervised pre-training. Unsupervised objectives can involve masking or corrupting part of the data and then using a reconstruction objective, or employing data augmentation to apply contrastive learning objectives. However, the heterogeneous nature of tabular data makes it challenging to find universally applicable augmentations, and these strategies have not been notably effective in few-shot settings. This limitation motivates the exploration of alternative approaches that can leverage external knowledge sources—such as that embedded in LLMs—to guide learning when data is scarce.

# Current Limitations of LLM-Based Tabular Learning: Inference Costs, Prompt Size Limits, and Training Constraints

The application of Large Language Models to tabular learning has generated significant interest, with researchers exploring methods to harness the rich prior knowledge encapsulated by these models. Current approaches typically propose serializing tabular data into text and feeding them into the input prompts of LLMs. By including tabular data, feature descriptions, and task descriptions in the input prompts, these methods can infer the relationships between tasks and features to perform predictions. Research has evolved to either specifically train models using parameter-efficient tuning techniques like LoRA or IA3, or employ in-context learning by adding few-shot example demonstrations to the prompts without training.

Despite the effectiveness of these methods in improving few-shot tabular learning, they suffer from fundamental limitations that pose challenges for real-world application. The first major constraint is computational cost: for end-to-end predictions, at least one LLM inference per sample is required, making it computationally expensive. This per-sample inference requirement becomes particularly problematic in industrial settings where low latency and high throughput are essential. Unlike traditional machine learning models that can make predictions efficiently once trained, LLM-based approaches require passing each new sample through the entire model, incurring significant time and computational resources.

A second critical limitation concerns accessibility and training constraints. Most current methods require fine-tuning the LLM for high accuracy, making them infeasible to apply to LLMs that do not come with accessible training codebases or tuning APIs. This is a prominent concern given that recently-proposed top-performance LLMs, such as GPT-4 and PaLM, only permit limited access via inference APIs. Practitioners cannot fine-tune these models even if they wanted to, limiting the applicability of approaches that depend on parameter updates. Furthermore, most approaches are not suitable with lengthy prompts—when the number of features in tabular data grows to the point where the serialized text length becomes long, the approach often turns infeasible or experiences a decline in performance, a phenomenon sometimes referred to as being "lost in the middle." These constraints pose fundamental challenges to the real-world application of LLM-based tabular learning models and motivate the search for alternative approaches.

# The Paradigm Shift: Treating LLMs as Feature Engineers Rather Than End-to-End Predictors

Given the limitations of current LLM-based tabular learning approaches, a fundamental rethinking of how we utilize these models is necessary. Rather than treating LLMs as end-to-end predictors—where each sample passes through the model to generate a prediction—we can assign them the role of feature engineers. This paradigm shift represents a more efficient way to utilize the prior knowledge embedded in LLMs while avoiding the computational and accessibility constraints of existing methods.

The key insight behind this approach is that LLMs can directly infer and generate rules that determine which feature conditions result in identifying each class. For instance, for the task of predicting a particular disease, the LLM can infer criteria such as "if the patient is over 65 years old AND has high blood pressure, then the risk of the disease is elevated." These criteria, or rules, can then be used to create new features that replace or augment existing ones in solving tasks. This approach shifts the capability of the LLM from prediction to feature generation, yielding a simpler downstream model—once the features are discovered, a complex machine learning model is no longer needed for inference, significantly improving inference latency.

This feature engineering perspective offers several conceptual advantages. First, it separates the knowledge extraction phase—which requires the LLM's sophisticated reasoning capabilities—from the prediction phase—which can be accomplished with a simple, efficient model. By leveraging the in-context learning ability of LLMs, it becomes possible to extract features without needing model training, simply by adding example demonstrations to the prompts. Second, by employing techniques from ensemble methods, like feature bagging, the challenge of excessive prompt size can be mitigated. When tabular data exceed the allowable input prompt size, bagging helps reduce it, ensuring robust performance. The paradigm thus addresses the key limitations of existing approaches while preserving the benefits of LLM-generated knowledge.

# Overview of FeatLLM Objectives: Efficiency, Interpretability, and Performance

The FeatLLM framework embodies this paradigm shift, introducing a novel in-context learning approach that employs LLMs as feature engineers to produce an input dataset that is optimally suited for tabular predictions. The framework is designed with three primary objectives in mind: computational efficiency, practical accessibility, and strong predictive performance.

The first objective, efficiency, is achieved by eliminating the need to send queries to the LLM for each sample at inference time. Unlike existing LLM-based approaches that require at least one LLM inference per prediction, FeatLLM uses the LLM only during the feature generation phase. The generated features are then used to infer class likelihood with a simple downstream machine learning model, such as linear regression, yielding high performance few-shot learning. At inference time, only this simple predictive model with the discovered features is used, resulting in computational costs comparable to conventional machine learning methods.

The second objective addresses accessibility constraints. FeatLLM merely requires API-level access to LLMs, making it applicable to the many top-performance models that only provide inference APIs without training access. By structuring the input prompt to guide the LLM through a two-step reasoning process—first understanding the problem and inferring relationships between features and targets, then deriving decisive rules—the framework extracts high-quality features without requiring any model fine-tuning. The framework also overcomes prompt size limitations through its ensemble approach with bagging, selecting subsets of features or instances for each trial to ensure robust performance even on datasets with numerous features.

The third objective is performance. Across numerous tabular datasets from a wide range of domains, FeatLLM generates high-quality rules that significantly—10% on average—outperform alternatives such as TabLLM and STUNT. The framework demonstrates that LLMs proficiently utilize both prior knowledge and the knowledge from the data, extracting features that are informative for the target task. Using just 4 shots, FeatLLM achieves performance comparable to conventional tabular baselines that require 32 shots, demonstrating remarkable data efficiency. By combining the prior knowledge embedded in LLMs with principled feature engineering and ensemble techniques, FeatLLM represents a significant advancement in few-shot tabular learning.
