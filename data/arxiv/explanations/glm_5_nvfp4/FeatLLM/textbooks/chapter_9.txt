\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 9: # Conclusion and Future Horizons

The intersection of large language models and tabular learning represents a fascinating frontier in machine learning research, and the FeatLLM framework marks a significant step forward in this domain. Throughout this chapter, we have explored how FeatLLM reimagines the role of LLMs in tabular learning—not as end-to-end predictors, but as sophisticated feature engineers capable of extracting meaningful rules from limited data. This concluding chapter synthesizes the key contributions of this work, examines its broader implications for real-world applications, addresses important limitations and potential risks, and charts promising directions for future research.

## Summary of Contributions: Bridging LLMs and Tabular Learning via Feature Engineering

The central contribution of FeatLLM lies in its novel conceptualization of how LLMs can be leveraged for tabular learning tasks. Rather than treating LLMs as black-box predictors that must process each sample individually during inference, FeatLLM assigns them the role of feature engineers. This paradigm shift is both elegant and practical. The LLM is tasked with understanding the underlying criteria that determine class membership—for instance, in a medical diagnosis task, the LLM infers rules about which feature conditions indicate the presence of a particular disease. These rules are then parsed into executable code that transforms raw input features into binary indicators, which are subsequently used by a simple linear model for prediction.

This approach yields several important advantages over existing methods. First, by decoupling feature generation from prediction, FeatLLM eliminates the computational burden of querying the LLM for each new sample at inference time. Once the rules have been extracted and the linear model has been trained, predictions can be made rapidly without any further interaction with the LLM. This represents a substantial improvement in inference latency compared to methods like TabLLM, in-context learning, and TABLET, all of which require at least one LLM inference per sample. Second, FeatLLM requires only API-level access to LLMs, making it compatible with state-of-the-art models like GPT-4 that do not provide access to training codebases or fine-tuning APIs. This is a crucial practical consideration, as many of the most capable LLMs available today are only accessible through inference APIs. Third, through the use of feature bagging and ensemble methods borrowed from traditional machine learning, FeatLLM effectively addresses the challenge of prompt size limitations. When tabular datasets contain numerous features, the serialized text length can exceed the LLM's context window or lead to degraded performance; bagging allows the framework to work with subsets of features and samples, with the ensemble aggregating results across multiple trials.

The empirical results presented in the paper demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach across 13 diverse tabular datasets spanning multiple domains. FeatLLM consistently outperformed alternative methods, achieving an average improvement of 10% over baselines like TabLLM and STUNT. Remarkably, with only 4 training examples, FeatLLM achieved performance comparable to conventional machine learning methods that required 32 examples. This dramatic improvement in data efficiency underscores the value of leveraging LLM prior knowledge in low-shot regimes.

## Broader Impact: Applications in High-Cost Labeling Domains

The practical implications of FeatLLM extend far beyond academic benchmarks. The framework is particularly well-suited for real-world scenarios where labeled data is scarce and expensive to obtain. Finance and healthcare represent two domains where these constraints are especially acute. In healthcare, for example, labeling medical records often requires expert annotation by physicians, whose time is both valuable and limited. A disease prediction task might require careful review of patient histories, test results, and clinical notes to determine whether a particular condition is present. The cost of such labeling can be prohibitive, limiting the amount of training data available for conventional machine learning approaches.

FeatLLM addresses this challenge by supplementing limited labeled data with the rich prior knowledge embedded in LLMs. Because LLMs are trained on extensive text corpora that include medical literature, clinical guidelines, and health-related discussions, they possess substantial domain knowledge that can be extracted through appropriate prompting. When presented with a medical prediction task, the LLM can draw upon its knowledge about risk factors, symptom patterns, and disease progression to generate meaningful features. For instance, in predicting myocardial infarction complications, the LLM might generate rules related to patient age, blood pressure thresholds, or specific biomarker levels—reflecting medical knowledge that would otherwise require explicit encoding by domain experts.

Similarly, in finance, applications such as credit risk assessment, fraud detection, and investment prediction often face labeling challenges. Historical data may be limited, or labels may require retrospective analysis by financial experts. The ability to achieve strong predictive performance with minimal labeled samples can significantly reduce the cost and time required to deploy machine learning systems in these contexts.

The broader impact statement in the paper emphasizes that FeatLLM's contribution to data efficiency helps mitigate the problem of overfitting in low-sample regimes. By incorporating prior knowledge from LLMs, the framework provides an inductive bias that guides learning toward meaningful patterns rather than spurious correlations in the limited training data. This has important implications for practitioners across industries who face data scarcity but need reliable predictive models.

## Potential Risks: Societal Biases and Misinformation

While the incorporation of LLM prior knowledge offers substantial benefits, it also introduces potential risks that must be carefully considered. Large language models, trained on vast corpora of internet text, inevitably absorb the biases present in their training data. These biases can manifest in various ways—from demographic biases related to race, gender, and socioeconomic status to cultural biases reflecting the dominant perspectives in the training corpus. When LLMs are used to generate features for predictive tasks, these biases can be encoded directly into the learned representations.

The paper explicitly acknowledges this concern in its broader impact statement. The prior knowledge extracted from LLMs "directly affects the predictions," potentially "dominating over the observed or collected labeled data samples." This creates a situation where predictions may reflect not only the patterns in the training data but also the preconceptions and biases embedded in the LLM's prior knowledge. In sensitive applications like healthcare or criminal justice, such biases could lead to disparate outcomes across different demographic groups or perpetuate existing societal inequities.

Consider, for example, a healthcare prediction task where the LLM generates rules based on medical knowledge that historically underrepresented certain populations in clinical research. The resulting features might be less informative or even misleading for patients from these populations, leading to inferior predictive performance. Similarly, in financial applications, historical biases in lending practices might be reflected in the LLM's prior knowledge, potentially generating features that perpetuate discriminatory patterns.

The issue of misinformation presents another significant risk. LLMs can generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect information—a phenomenon often referred to as "hallucination." In the context of feature engineering, this could manifest as rules that appear semantically meaningful but are not actually predictive of the target variable, or worse, rules based on incorrect causal reasoning. The paper's ablation studies reveal that FeatLLM can indeed "learn spurious correlations and misunderstand causal relationships between task and features if columns from closely related data are randomly attached." This highlights the importance of careful validation of generated features, particularly in high-stakes applications.

Addressing these risks requires ongoing research into bias detection and mitigation in LLM-generated features, as well as transparency about the sources of prior knowledge that influence predictions. Practitioners must be vigilant about evaluating whether generated features reflect genuine predictive signals or encoded biases.

## Limitations: Focus on Low-Shot Regimes and Specific Feature Types

Despite its impressive performance, FeatLLM has several limitations that constrain its applicability. The framework is explicitly designed for and evaluated on low-shot learning regimes—scenarios where only a handful of labeled examples are available. The paper's experiments use $k$-shot settings with $k$ ranging from 2 to 32, reflecting the intended use case of data-scarce environments. While this focus is appropriate given the framework's strengths in leveraging prior knowledge, it raises questions about performance in data-rich settings. The paper explicitly states that "FeatLLM is designed to focus on low-shot learning regime only," acknowledging this scope limitation.

The linear model trained on generated features may not capture complex nonlinear relationships that become exploitable with larger datasets. As the number of training samples increases, more sophisticated models like gradient boosting or neural networks trained directly on raw features might eventually match or exceed FeatLLM's performance. The value of LLM prior knowledge is greatest when labeled data is minimal; as more data becomes available, the information extracted from the data itself may supersede prior knowledge in importance.

Another limitation concerns the types of features generated. FeatLLM focuses on rule-based features—binary indicators of whether certain conditions are satisfied. While this approach yields interpretable features and works well for many tabular tasks, it may not capture all relevant feature transformations. For example, features based on arithmetic combinations of input columns, polynomial transformations, or more complex logical structures might be useful in some applications but are not explicitly explored in the current framework. The generated rules are constrained to specific formats based on feature types, which may limit expressiveness in some scenarios.

Additionally, the reliance on a fixed number of rules per class (set to 10 in the experiments) represents a design choice that balances prompt length against feature expressiveness. The hyperparameter analysis shows that while 10 rules performed well, there was no statistically significant difference across various numbers of rules. However, this one-size-fits-all approach may not be optimal for all tasks, particularly those with complex decision boundaries or those involving many relevant features.

## Future Directions: Expanding Scope and Enhancing Capabilities

The conclusion of the paper outlines an ambitious agenda for future research that addresses many of the current limitations while expanding the framework's capabilities. Three primary directions emerge: extending to larger datasets, exploring diverse feature types, and enhancing interpretability.

The first direction involves expanding FeatLLM's capabilities "for datasets with a larger number of samples." This would require rethinking the balance between prior knowledge and data-derived information. In low-shot regimes, prior knowledge from the LLM dominates the feature generation process. As the number of training samples increases, the framework could be adapted to give greater weight to patterns discovered directly in the data. One potential approach might involve using the LLM to generate a broader space of candidate features, then using data-driven selection methods to identify the most informative features for larger datasets. Alternatively, hybrid approaches could combine LLM-generated features with features learned through traditional feature engineering or representation learning methods.

The second direction focuses on "exploring various feature types beyond rules." While rule-based features offer interpretability and computational efficiency, other feature transformations might capture complementary information. For example, the LLM could be prompted to generate mathematical transformations (such as ratios, products, or polynomial terms), threshold-based features with learned cutoffs, or even text-based features derived from combinations of categorical variables. The framework could also be extended to generate features for regression tasks, time-series prediction, or multi-label classification, broadening its applicability across machine learning tasks.

The third direction emphasizes "bringing interpretability" to the framework. While FeatLLM already offers a degree of interpretability through its use of explicit rules and a simple linear model, there is room for deeper analysis. Understanding which rules contribute most to predictions, how rules interact across different ensemble members, and why certain rules are generated by the LLM could provide valuable insights. Such interpretability would enable practitioners to validate that generated features align with domain knowledge, identify potential biases or spurious correlations, and explain predictions to stakeholders. This is particularly important in regulated domains like healthcare and finance, where model interpretability is often a legal or ethical requirement.

Beyond these three directions, the paper's broader impact statement suggests another important area for future work: systematic analysis of "the impact of the societal biases and misinformation embedded in the prior knowledge of LLMs." Developing methods to detect, quantify, and mitigate biases in LLM-generated features would enhance the responsible deployment of FeatLLM in real-world applications. This might involve fairness constraints on the linear model, diversity requirements in generated rules, or post-hoc analysis of feature impacts across demographic groups.

The journey from traditional tabular learning to LLM-enhanced feature engineering represents a significant evolution in how we approach data-scarce prediction tasks. FeatLLM demonstrates that the rich knowledge encoded in large language models can be effectively extracted and operationalized for tabular learning, achieving impressive performance while maintaining computational efficiency. As research continues along the directions outlined above, the integration of LLMs with traditional machine learning is poised to yield even more powerful and responsible tools for practitioners across domains.
