\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

# Introduction to Long Context Windows in LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable success across a wide variety of tasks, demonstrating impressive capabilities in natural language understanding, generation, and reasoning. However, despite their widespread adoption and effectiveness, these models face a fundamental constraint that significantly limits their applicability in many real-world scenarios: the context window size. The context window represents the maximum number of tokens that a model can process during inference, and this seemingly simple parameter has profound implications for what an LLM can accomplish. When a model's context window is exceeded, its performance degrades substantially because it must process positions that it never encountered during training. This limitation creates bottlenecks in numerous important applications, including in-context learning scenarios where the model needs to process numerous examples simultaneously, and LLM agents that must maintain coherent understanding over extended conversations or complex task sequences. Understanding the significance of context windows, the challenges in extending them, and the innovative solutions being developed to overcome these limitations is essential for anyone seeking to work with or improve upon modern language models.

## The Significance of Large Context Windows in LLMs

The context window of a Large Language Model serves as the working memory through which the model processes and relates information. A larger context window directly translates to an enhanced capacity for the model to maintain coherence, reference earlier information, and perform complex reasoning over extended sequences. Consider a scenario where a model is asked to analyze a lengthy legal document, write a comprehensive summary of a book, or engage in a multi-turn conversation that spans hundreds of exchanges. In each of these cases, the model's ability to access and reason about information from earlier portions of the input is crucial for producing high-quality outputs.

The practical importance of large context windows becomes particularly evident in several key application domains. In-context learning, one of the most powerful capabilities of modern LLMs, relies heavily on the model's ability to process multiple demonstration examples before generating a response. When the context window is limited, the number of examples that can be provided is severely constrained, potentially limiting the model's ability to learn the intended task pattern. Similarly, LLM agents—systems that use language models as reasoning engines to plan and execute multi-step actions—require maintaining awareness of the entire task history to make coherent decisions. As these agents tackle increasingly complex problems, the demands on context length grow proportionally.

Beyond these specific applications, large context windows enable fundamentally new use cases that are impractical or impossible with limited context. Whole-document processing becomes feasible, allowing models to understand complete articles, research papers, or even small books without fragmentation. Long-form generation tasks, such as writing extended narratives or detailed technical documentation, benefit from the model's ability to maintain consistency throughout. Furthermore, retrieval-augmented generation systems can incorporate larger amounts of retrieved context, potentially improving the relevance and accuracy of generated responses. The desire to unlock these capabilities has driven significant research effort toward extending context windows beyond their original pre-trained limits, making this one of the most active areas of LLM development.

## Current Limitations of Pre-trained Models

Despite the clear advantages of large context windows, most pre-trained LLMs operate under surprisingly constrained context limits. For instance, LLaMA2, one of the most widely used open-source language models, was originally trained with a context window of only $4096$ tokens. This $4$k limit, while sufficient for many common tasks, represents a significant bottleneck for applications requiring longer sequences. When input sequences exceed this boundary, the model encounters position indices that were never seen during training, leading to rapid performance degradation. The model essentially enters uncharted territory where its learned positional representations no longer apply, resulting in outputs that become increasingly incoherent or irrelevant as the sequence length grows beyond the training limit.

The severity of this limitation becomes apparent when considering the mismatch between typical document lengths and model context windows. Many real-world texts—academic papers, technical documentation, legal contracts, and novels—often contain tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens. Processing such documents with a $4$k context window requires either aggressive truncation, which discards potentially important information, or fragmentation into multiple chunks, which breaks the logical coherence of the document. Neither approach is satisfactory, as both compromise the model's ability to understand the full context and produce appropriately informed responses.

Recent research efforts have made progress in extending context windows beyond their original training limits. Through fine-tuning on longer texts, researchers have successfully extended context windows to approximately $128$k tokens. These extensions represent meaningful improvements, enabling models to handle longer documents than previously possible. However, the extension factor is still limited—reaching $128$k from an original $4$k represents a $32\times$ increase, which, while substantial, still falls short of what many applications require. The gap between current extended context capabilities and the ideal of truly unlimited context processing motivates continued research into more effective extension methods. Understanding why extending beyond approximately $128$k has proven so challenging requires examining the fundamental obstacles that arise when pushing context windows to extreme lengths.

## Three Major Obstacles to Extension

Extending the context window of a pre-trained LLM involves significantly more complexity than simply increasing a configuration parameter. Three interconnected obstacles conspire to make extreme context extension a formidable technical challenge: the introduction of catastrophic values from new position indices, the scarcity of sufficiently long training data, and the prohibitive computational costs associated with training on extremely long sequences.

The first obstacle concerns the fundamental nature of how transformers process positional information. When a model's context window is extended beyond its training limit, the model must now process position indices that it never encountered during pre-training. For example, extending from $4$k to beyond $1000$k introduces more than $90\%$ new position indices—positions that have no learned representations in the model. These untrained positions introduce what researchers term "catastrophic values," leading to out-of-distribution issues that make fine-tuning difficult to converge. The model's existing positional embeddings, carefully learned during pre-training to encode relative and absolute position information, suddenly must extrapolate far beyond their designed range. This extrapolation creates instability in the attention mechanism, as the positional information becomes unreliable and the model struggles to distinguish between tokens at different positions.

The second major obstacle is the availability of suitable training data. Fine-tuning to extend a context window typically requires training texts of corresponding lengths—extending to $128$k tokens ideally requires documents of $128$k tokens or longer. However, texts exceeding $1000$k tokens are remarkably scarce in existing datasets. Most curated text corpora consist of documents far shorter than what would be needed for million-token context training. Book datasets, while containing lengthy documents, still represent a limited resource, and the quality and diversity of extremely long texts pose additional challenges. This data scarcity creates a chicken-and-egg problem: we need long texts to train long-context models, but we need long-context models to effectively process the long texts we do have.

The third obstacle is computational cost. Training on extremely long sequences demands extraordinary computational resources, both in terms of memory and processing time. The attention mechanism in transformers scales quadratically with sequence length, meaning that doubling the sequence length roughly quadruples the computational requirements. Training on sequences of $1000$k or more tokens would require prohibitively extensive training hours and GPU resources, making such training impractical for most research teams and organizations. Even when efficient attention implementations are available, the sheer volume of data that must be processed for each training step makes extreme-length training a significant engineering challenge. These three obstacles—catastrophic position values, data scarcity, and computational costs—have collectively limited context window extensions to approximately $128$k tokens in most prior work.

## Overview of the LongRoPE Approach and Its Achievements

LongRoPE represents a breakthrough in context window extension, achieving for the first time an extension to an impressive $2048$k tokens—over two million tokens—while maintaining performance at the original short context window. This achievement represents an order of magnitude improvement over previous state-of-the-art methods, pushing the boundary of what was thought possible for pre-trained LLMs. Remarkably, LongRoPE accomplishes this feat with only $1000$ fine-tuning steps at within $256$k training lengths, demonstrating remarkable efficiency compared to what direct training on million-token sequences would require. The method can be applied to any LLM that uses RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding) positional encoding, making it broadly applicable across the landscape of modern language models.

The core insight behind LongRoPE lies in recognizing that positional interpolation—the process of adjusting position encodings to accommodate longer sequences—is not a uniform process. Previous methods treated all positions and all dimensions of the positional encoding similarly, applying the same interpolation strategy across the board. LongRoPE instead identifies and exploits two forms of non-uniformity in positional interpolation, using an efficient evolutionary search algorithm to discover optimal rescale factors for different RoPE dimensions and token positions. This non-uniform approach preserves more of the original positional information, particularly in key dimensions and positions that are most critical for model performance.

The practical achievements of LongRoPE have been demonstrated through extensive experiments on models including LLaMA2 and Mistral. The extended models maintain low perplexity across the full range from $4$k to $2048$k evaluation lengths, demonstrating coherent language modeling even at extreme context sizes. In passkey retrieval tasks—where a model must find a specific piece of information hidden within a sea of irrelevant text—LongRoPE achieves over $90\%$ accuracy even at the $2048$k context length. Crucially, models extended via LongRoPE retain comparable accuracy on standard benchmarks designed within the original $4096$ context window, showing that the extension does not come at the cost of performance on shorter contexts. The models retain their original architecture with only minor modifications to positional embeddings, allowing them to reuse most pre-existing optimizations and infrastructure.

## Key Innovations: Non-uniform Interpolation, Progressive Extension, and Short Context Recovery

LongRoPE's success rests on three key technical innovations that work in concert to achieve extreme context extension while preserving model quality. These innovations address different aspects of the challenge and together provide a comprehensive solution to the context window extension problem.

The first innovation is the identification and exploitation of non-uniformities in positional interpolation. Through careful empirical analysis, the LongRoPE team discovered two critical forms of non-uniformity that existing methods failed to leverage effectively. First, different RoPE dimensions exhibit varying information densities and should be interpolated differently—lower dimensions, which encode higher-frequency information, benefit from less interpolation, while higher dimensions can tolerate more aggressive scaling. Second, the initial token positions in an input sequence should be handled differently from later positions, as these early positions receive large attention scores and are crucial to the attention layers' functioning. By searching for optimal rescale factors that respect these non-uniformities, LongRoPE provides better initialization for fine-tuning and enables impressive extensions even in non-fine-tuning scenarios—achieving up to $8\times$ extension without any additional training.

The second innovation is a progressive extension strategy that avoids the need to directly train on million-token sequences. Rather than attempting to extend directly from $4$k to $2048$k, LongRoPE first extends and fine-tunes the model to $256$k, then applies a second positional interpolation on this fine-tuned model to reach $2048$k. This two-stage approach leverages the fact that the non-uniform interpolation method can achieve $8\times$ extension without fine-tuning—so extending a $256$k fine-tuned model by $8\times$ reaches the target $2048$k. This progressive strategy dramatically reduces training costs while still achieving the desired extreme context length. The fine-tuning phase requires only $1000$ steps total: $400$ steps at $128$k followed by $600$ steps at $256$k.

The third innovation addresses a subtle but important problem: after extending to an extremely long context window, performance on shorter contexts can degrade because the positional embeddings become "crowded" in their original range. To mitigate this, LongRoPE readjusts the positional embedding factors specifically for shorter context lengths like $4$k and $8$k. During inference, when the actual sequence length is shorter than the maximum extended length, the model dynamically uses these readjusted factors. This recovery mechanism ensures that the model maintains strong performance on the original short-context tasks even after being extended to handle millions of tokens. Together, these three innovations—non-uniform interpolation, progressive extension, and short context recovery—enable LongRoPE to push the boundaries of what LLMs can process, opening new possibilities for long-context applications while preserving the models' existing capabilities.

# Background: Rotary Positional Embeddings and Interpolation

# Mechanics of Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE)

Transformer models, despite their remarkable success across various tasks, have an inherent limitation: their architecture does not naturally encode the order of input tokens. Since transformers process all tokens in parallel through self-attention mechanisms, they require explicit positional information to understand the sequential nature of language. This positional information is typically incorporated through position embeddings, which encode where each token appears in a sequence. Among the various approaches to position embedding, Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE) has emerged as the dominant choice in recent large language models, including LLaMA2 and Mistral, which serve as the foundation models discussed throughout this paper.

The fundamental insight behind RoPE is that it encodes positional information through rotation operations in the embedding space. Rather than adding a positional vector to the token embedding, RoPE applies a rotation to the query and key vectors in the attention mechanism. This rotation is determined by the position of each token, meaning that tokens at different positions are rotated by different angles. The beauty of this approach lies in how it naturally captures relative positional information through the geometric properties of rotation: when two vectors are rotated by different amounts, their dot product depends on the difference between their rotation angles, not their absolute positions. This property makes RoPE particularly well-suited for modeling relative distances between tokens, which is crucial for understanding language structure.

The practical implementation of RoPE involves applying rotation matrices to pairs of dimensions in the embedding space. For a model with embedding dimension $d$, RoPE divides the dimensions into $d/2$ pairs, with each pair corresponding to a different rotation frequency. This multi-frequency design allows the model to capture positional information at multiple scales: lower dimensions correspond to higher frequencies and can distinguish fine-grained positional differences, while higher dimensions correspond to lower frequencies and capture broader positional patterns. This multi-scale representation is analogous to how Fourier transforms decompose signals into components of varying frequencies, and it plays a crucial role in understanding why different interpolation strategies affect different dimensions differently.

# Mathematical Formulation of RoPE Angles and Frequencies

To understand how RoPE can be modified to extend context windows, we must first examine its precise mathematical formulation. For a token at position index $n$, its corresponding RoPE encoding can be expressed as a sequence of cosine and sine values:

$$\left[ \cos(n\theta_0), \sin(n\theta_0), \cos(n\theta_1), \cdots, \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}), \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1}) \right]$$

In this formulation, $d$ represents the embedding dimension of the model. Each pair of consecutive elements in this sequence corresponds to one dimension pair in the embedding space. The term $n\theta_i$ represents the rotary angle for the token at position $n$ in the $i$-th dimension pair. Importantly, these angles are not uniform across all dimensions; instead, they vary according to a carefully designed frequency schedule.

The rotation frequencies $\theta_i$ follow a geometric progression defined by:

$$\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$$

Here, $\theta$ is the base value, with a default of 10000 in standard RoPE implementations. This exponential decay in frequencies ensures that lower dimensions (smaller values of $i$) have higher frequencies, while higher dimensions have lower frequencies. Concretely, when $i=0$, the frequency is $\theta^0 = 1$, meaning each position increment causes a full cycle rotation. As $i$ increases, the frequency decreases exponentially, meaning higher dimensions change more slowly with position.

To see why this matters, consider what happens when the position index $n$ increases. For low dimensions (small $i$), the angle $n\theta_i$ changes rapidly, allowing the model to distinguish between adjacent positions. However, for high dimensions (large $i$), the angle changes very slowly, meaning these dimensions cannot reliably distinguish between nearby positions. This is not a bug but a feature: high-frequency dimensions capture fine-grained local structure, while low-frequency dimensions capture global patterns that persist across long sequences. This multi-frequency design becomes critical when we consider how to extend context windows, as different interpolation strategies affect different frequency bands differently.

# Linear Position Interpolation: Scaling Position Indices

When extending the context window of a pre-trained LLM, a naive approach would simply use the model beyond its trained context length. However, this leads to catastrophic failures because the model encounters position indices it has never seen during training. These out-of-distribution positions introduce unstable values that cause the attention mechanism to behave unpredictably. Position Interpolation (PI) addresses this challenge through a clever insight: instead of using position indices beyond the trained range, we can mathematically map new positions back into the range the model has seen.

The core idea of Position Interpolation is to linearly scale down the position indices. If a model was trained with positions $[0, L-1]$ and we want to extend it to length $L'$, we simply divide all position indices by the extension ratio $s = L'/L$. Formally, for a target extension ratio $s$, the rotation angles of all positions are linearly reduced by using $\lambda = s$ as the rescale factor across all RoPE dimensions. This means that position $n$ in the extended context is treated as if it were at position $n/s$ in the original context.

To understand this intuitively, imagine the original context window as a ruler of length $L$. When we want to measure a longer sequence of length $L'$, we can either extend the ruler (extrapolation) or compress the object being measured (interpolation). PI chooses the latter: it compresses the longer sequence to fit within the original ruler's length. The position information becomes more "crowded" in this compressed space, which is both a strength and a limitation. On one hand, it keeps all positions within the trained distribution, avoiding out-of-distribution issues. On the other hand, this crowding makes it harder for the model to distinguish between closely positioned tokens, especially at high extension ratios.

The practical consequence of this crowding effect is that PI tends to underperform when the extension ratio becomes large. The paper notes that PI necessitates fine-tuning to work effectively, as the model needs to learn to operate in this more crowded positional space. Without fine-tuning, the model struggles because the relative distances between adjacent tokens have been compressed, degrading the attention mechanism's ability to focus on the most relevant tokens.

# NTK-aware Interpolation: Handling Frequency Dimensions

The limitations of linear Position Interpolation motivated researchers to consider whether all RoPE dimensions should be treated equally. The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory provides a theoretical framework for understanding this problem from an information encoding perspective. The key insight is that RoPE's multi-frequency structure means different dimensions serve different purposes, and a uniform scaling strategy like PI fails to account for these differences.

NTK-aware interpolation recognizes that high-frequency dimensions (lower RoPE dimensions) and low-frequency dimensions (higher RoPE dimensions) should be handled differently. High-frequency dimensions are responsible for distinguishing nearby positions, and applying aggressive interpolation to these dimensions would severely impair the model's local resolution. Conversely, low-frequency dimensions operate at a coarser scale and can tolerate more aggressive interpolation without catastrophic information loss.

The NTK approach therefore distributes the interpolation pressure non-uniformly across RoPE dimensions. Specifically, it scales lower (high frequency) dimensions less and higher (low frequency) dimensions more. Mathematically, this corresponds to using $\lambda = s^{i}$ as the rescale factor for dimension $i$, where $s$ is the extension ratio and $i$ indexes the RoPE dimensions. Notice that for $i=0$ (the highest frequency dimension), $\lambda = s^0 = 1$, meaning this dimension undergoes no scaling at all—this is pure extrapolation. As $i$ increases, $\lambda$ increases, meaning higher dimensions undergo more aggressive interpolation.

This strategy results in a combination of interpolation and extrapolation: high-frequency dimensions extrapolate beyond their trained range, while low-frequency dimensions interpolate within it. The practical benefit is that NTK-aware methods can extend context windows without fine-tuning, typically achieving up to a $4\times$ extension. An improvement called Dynamic NTK further refines this approach by adjusting the extension ratio at each position based on the current sequence length, making the method more adaptive to varying input lengths. However, even these methods have limits: beyond roughly a $4\times$ extension, performance degrades, suggesting that the non-uniformity captured by NTK-aware approaches is still insufficient for extreme extensions.

# YaRN: Combining Interpolation Strategies Across Dimension Groups

Building on the insights from NTK-aware interpolation, YaRN (Yet another RoPE extension) introduces a more sophisticated approach by categorizing RoPE dimensions into distinct groups based on their frequencies and applying different interpolation strategies to each group. This approach acknowledges that the relationship between frequency and optimal interpolation strategy is more nuanced than a simple monotonic function.

YaRN divides the RoPE dimensions into three frequency-based groups. The first group consists of high-frequency dimensions that undergo extrapolation with $\lambda = 1$, meaning their rotation angles are not modified at all. This preserves the model's ability to distinguish nearby positions with high precision. The second group comprises middle-frequency dimensions that employ the NTK-aware scaling, balancing between preserving local information and adapting to longer contexts. The third group contains low-frequency dimensions that use linear interpolation (PI), as these dimensions already have limited resolution for nearby positions and benefit most from aggressive scaling.

The effectiveness of YaRN stems from its recognition that different frequency bands require different treatments. High-frequency dimensions are essential for local precision and should not be compressed; extrapolating them works well because even at extended positions, their high rotation rate means they continue to provide useful relative positional information. Low-frequency dimensions, by contrast, rotate so slowly that they barely distinguish between positions even in the original context; scaling these dimensions aggressively is acceptable because the information loss is minimal. The middle frequencies require a balanced approach, which NTK-aware scaling provides.

However, YaRN has a notable limitation: the grouping of RoPE dimensions currently depends on human-designed rules derived from empirical experiments. The paper points out that this may result in sub-optimal performance for new LLMs, as the optimal grouping may depend on factors specific to each model architecture and training regimen. Furthermore, the paper's experiments reveal cases where YaRN underperforms even compared to simpler methods like PI and NTK. For instance, on the PG19 dataset, YaRN's perplexity spikes after 7k tokens in an 8k context size, suggesting it fails to reach the target context window length for non-fine-tuned LLMs. This indicates that rule-based approaches, while more sophisticated than uniform methods, still miss important aspects of how positional information should be transformed.

# Limitations of Uniform and Rule-based Interpolation Methods

The progression from Position Interpolation to NTK-aware methods to YaRN represents increasing sophistication in handling RoPE's multi-frequency structure. Yet all these approaches share a common limitation: they rely on predetermined rules that do not account for the full complexity of positional information in transformer models. The paper identifies a crucial insight that motivates LongRoPE: positional embedding exhibits complex non-uniform information entropy in the transformer architecture, and this subtle non-uniformity is not effectively leveraged by existing approaches.

The first limitation concerns the assumption that interpolation should be uniform across tokens at different positions. Existing methods apply the same transformation regardless of where a token appears in the sequence. However, the paper's empirical analysis reveals that initial tokens in input sequences receive disproportionately large attention scores, making them particularly important for the attention mechanism. Treating these tokens the same as later tokens may introduce unnecessary information loss. The paper demonstrates that retaining the starting tokens without position interpolation improves performance, and the optimal number of starting tokens to preserve depends on the target extension length.

The second limitation concerns the assumption that dimension-wise interpolation can be captured by simple rules. While YaRN's three-group strategy improves upon uniform approaches, the boundaries between groups and the interpolation strategies within each group are determined by human intuition and empirical testing. The paper's evolutionary search reveals that the optimal rescale factors $\lambda$ for each dimension are highly non-uniform and do not conform to the fixed formulas used in PI, NTK, or YaRN's group-wise calculations. This suggests that the relationship between RoPE dimensions and optimal interpolation is more complex than existing methods assume.

A third limitation emerges from the paper's finding that current methods are sub-optimal for both fine-tuning and non-fine-tuning scenarios. Table 1 in the paper compares perplexity under different methods without fine-tuning, showing that searched solutions significantly outperform existing linear and non-uniform interpolations. Even YaRN, despite its sophisticated grouping strategy, underperforms compared to PI and NTK on certain datasets because it does not reliably reach the target context window length. These findings collectively suggest that the path toward longer context windows requires moving beyond rule-based interpolation toward methods that can discover optimal transformations specific to each model and target length.

# Discovery of Non-Uniformities in Positional Interpolation

Understanding why existing context window extension methods fall short requires a deep examination of the positional embedding landscape. This chapter presents a systematic empirical analysis that reveals two fundamental non-uniformities in positional interpolation—insights that form the theoretical foundation of LongRoPE. Through careful experimentation, we uncover that RoPE dimensions and token positions exhibit complex patterns that existing uniform or rule-based interpolation methods fail to capture, leading to suboptimal performance and information loss.

# Analyzing the Sub-optimality of Current Interpolation Methods

To appreciate the significance of non-uniform positional interpolation, we must first understand the limitations of current approaches. Position Interpolation (PI), NTK-based methods, and YaRN represent the state-of-the-art in extending LLM context windows, yet each exhibits fundamental shortcomings that limit their effectiveness, particularly at extreme extension ratios.

Position Interpolation takes the most straightforward approach: it linearly interpolates position indices within the pre-trained length limit by applying a uniform rescale factor $\lambda = s$ across all RoPE dimensions, where $s$ represents the extension ratio. While simple and intuitive, this approach introduces a critical problem. By uniformly compressing all positional information, PI makes position embeddings very "crowded," fundamentally hindering the model's ability to distinguish between closely positioned tokens. When tokens that were originally far apart are suddenly mapped to nearly identical positional representations, the attention mechanism struggles to maintain meaningful distinctions, leading to degraded performance.

NTK-based methods emerged from a more sophisticated theoretical foundation, drawing on Neural Tangent Kernel theory to recognize that different RoPE dimensions serve different functions. These methods propose that interpolation pressure should be distributed non-uniformly across dimensions: lower dimensions with higher frequencies require less interpolation, while higher dimensions with lower frequencies can accommodate more interpolation. This is formalized as $\lambda = s^i$, where $i$ indexes the RoPE dimension. While this represents progress over uniform interpolation, NTK-based approaches rely on a fixed mathematical formula that may not capture the full complexity of how positional information is processed in actual Transformer architectures.

YaRN introduced further sophistication by categorizing RoPE dimensions into three frequency-based groups, each receiving different treatment: high-frequency dimensions undergo extrapolation with $\lambda = 1$, low-frequency dimensions receive linear interpolation following PI, and middle dimensions employ the NTK approach. However, YaRN's grouping strategy depends heavily on human-designed rules and empirical observations. The boundaries between groups and the specific interpolation strategies applied to each group are determined through manual experimentation, which may not generalize well across different LLM architectures or extension ratios.

The fundamental issue underlying all these approaches is that positional embedding exhibits what we term complex non-uniform information entropy in the Transformer architecture. This subtle non-uniformity in how positional information is distributed and processed is not effectively leveraged by existing methods. The uniform scaling of PI ignores dimension-specific characteristics entirely. NTK's formula-based approach, while acknowledging dimensional differences, imposes a rigid mathematical structure that may not align with the actual information distribution in trained models. YaRN's rule-based grouping, though more flexible, still relies on human intuition rather than data-driven optimization.

This naturally raises two critical questions: First, is the current positional interpolation optimal? Second, are there unexplored non-linearities in how positional information should be scaled for context extension? These questions motivated the empirical investigation that led to the discovery of the two key non-uniformities discussed in the following sections.

# Finding 1: Non-uniformity Across RoPE Dimensions

The first major finding from the empirical analysis concerns the non-uniform behavior across RoPE dimensions. Through systematic experimentation using evolutionary search guided by perplexity metrics, researchers discovered that RoPE dimensions exhibit substantial non-uniformities that current methods do not effectively handle.

The experimental methodology employed evolution search to discover better non-uniform positional interpolations for LLaMA2-7B. The search was guided by perplexity measurements using five random samples from the PG19 validation set. This approach allowed for data-driven exploration of the rescale factor space without being constrained by human-designed rules or fixed mathematical formulas.

Table comparisons reveal striking differences between searched solutions and existing methods. On both PG19 and Proof-pile test sets, the searched non-uniform solutions demonstrate significant perplexity improvements without any fine-tuning. This indicates that current linear methods (PI) and non-uniform methods (Dynamic-NTK and YaRN) are indeed sub-optimal. A particularly notable observation is that YaRN actually underperforms compared to PI and NTK on PG19. This counterintuitive result occurs because YaRN fails to reach the target context window length for non-fine-tuned LLMs—specifically, YaRN's perplexity spikes dramatically after 7k tokens when attempting to achieve an 8k context size.

The key insight emerges from examining the rescaled factors $\lambda$ discovered through search. In the unified RoPE formulation:

$$\left[cos\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\right), sin \left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\right), cos\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^1}\right), \cdot\cdot\cdot, sin \left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{d/2-1}}\right) \right]$$

The searched $\lambda$ values become truly non-uniform, differing substantially from the fixed scale $s$ used in PI, NTK's formulaic calculation $\lambda = s^i$, or YaRN's group-wise calculations. Each RoPE dimension receives its own optimized rescale factor, allowing for fine-grained control over how positional information is compressed or preserved.

These non-uniform factors yield significant improvements in language modeling performance, as measured by perplexity, for both 8k and 16k context windows without any fine-tuning. The mechanism behind this improvement relates to how positional information is preserved during extension. The resulting positional embeddings effectively retain information from the original RoPE, particularly in key dimensions that prove critical for model performance. By minimizing unnecessary distortion in these important dimensions while allowing more aggressive compression in less critical ones, the searched interpolations reduce the difficulty the LLM faces in distinguishing closely positioned tokens.

This finding has profound implications for how we should think about positional interpolation. Rather than applying uniform rules or fixed formulas, optimal interpolation requires dimension-specific treatment that accounts for the actual role each dimension plays in the model's positional understanding. Lower RoPE dimensions, which correspond to higher frequencies, may require less interpolation to preserve fine-grained positional distinctions. Higher dimensions, corresponding to lower frequencies, may tolerate more aggressive interpolation without significant information loss. However, the optimal allocation is not predetermined by theory but must be discovered through search, as it depends on the specific characteristics of the pre-trained model and the target extension ratio.

# Finding 2: Non-uniformity Across Token Positions

The second major finding reveals that token positions themselves exhibit non-uniformity in how they should be treated during positional interpolation. Specifically, the initial tokens in an input sequence should receive special treatment—undergoing less interpolation or even full extrapolation—compared to tokens appearing later in the sequence.

This finding emerged from a hypothesis motivated by observations in prior work, particularly Streaming LLM and LM-Infinite. Researchers noted that initial tokens receive disproportionately large attention scores in Transformer attention layers, making them crucially important to the overall attention computation. If these initial positions were subjected to the same interpolation pressure as later positions, the model might lose critical information encoded in these high-attention positions.

To validate this hypothesis, experiments extended LLaMA2's context window to 8k and 16k using PI and NTK, but with a crucial modification: the first $\hat{n}$ tokens (where $\hat{n} \in \{0, 2, ..., 256\}$) were kept without any position interpolation. When $\hat{n} = 0$, the method reverts to the original PI or NTK behavior, providing a baseline for comparison. The results revealed two key observations.

First, retaining the starting tokens without position interpolation demonstrably improves performance. Across different extension ratios and interpolation methods, models that preserve the original positional embeddings for initial tokens consistently achieve better perplexity scores. This confirms the hypothesis that initial positions carry disproportionate importance and should not be subjected to the same compression as later positions.

Second, the optimal number of starting tokens $\hat{n}$ depends on the target extension length. This dependency makes intuitive sense: larger extension ratios require more aggressive interpolation overall, which may necessitate protecting more initial positions from compression. Conversely, smaller extension ratios may achieve optimal performance with fewer protected positions, as the overall interpolation pressure is lower to begin with.

The theoretical justification for this finding relates to how attention mechanisms process sequences. In Transformer architectures, the attention mechanism computes weighted sums over all positions, with weights determined by compatibility scores between positions. Initial tokens often serve as "anchor" positions that subsequent tokens attend to repeatedly, establishing important contextual baselines. By preserving their original positional embeddings, the model retains accurate positional relationships for these high-importance positions, enabling more effective attention computation throughout the extended context.

This finding introduces a new dimension of non-uniformity that must be considered alongside dimensional non-uniformity. Optimal positional interpolation must account for both which RoPE dimensions are being scaled and which token positions are receiving treatment. The interplay between these two forms of non-uniformity creates a rich optimization landscape that simple uniform methods cannot effectively navigate.

# Impact of Non-uniformities on Information Entropy and Attention

The discovery of these two non-uniformities has profound implications for understanding how positional embedding operates within Transformer architectures. At a fundamental level, positional embedding exhibits complex non-uniform information entropy—a characteristic that existing approaches have largely overlooked.

Information entropy, in this context, refers to the distribution and density of positional information across the embedding space. When we apply positional interpolation, we are essentially redistributing this information. Uniform interpolation methods treat all positional information as equally important, compressing everything by the same factor. This approach ignores the reality that different dimensions and different positions contribute unequally to the model's understanding of sequence structure.

The non-uniformity across RoPE dimensions relates to the different roles played by high-frequency and low-frequency components. High-frequency dimensions (lower indices in RoPE) encode fine-grained positional distinctions, helping the model distinguish between adjacent or nearby tokens. Low-frequency dimensions (higher indices) encode broader positional context, helping the model understand long-range dependencies and overall sequence structure. When we apply uniform interpolation, we risk destroying fine-grained positional information through over-compression of high-frequency dimensions, while potentially not compressing low-frequency dimensions enough to accommodate the extended context.

The non-uniformity across token positions relates to the attention mechanism's inherent structure. Attention in Transformers is not uniform—some positions receive more attention than others, and initial positions often serve as critical reference points. By preserving the original embeddings for these important positions, we maintain the positional relationships that the attention mechanism depends on most heavily. This prevents the "attention drift" that can occur when all positions are interpolated uniformly, causing the model to lose track of where important information is located within the extended sequence.

Together, these two forms of non-uniformity explain why existing methods plateau in their extension capabilities. PI's uniform compression ignores both forms of non-uniformity, leading to information loss across the board. NTK addresses dimensional non-uniformity through a fixed formula but ignores positional non-uniformity entirely. YaRN attempts more nuanced dimensional treatment through grouping but still applies uniform treatment to token positions. None of these methods fully exploit the complex interplay between dimensional and positional information entropy.

The practical consequence of ignoring these non-uniformities is information loss. When positional information is compressed uniformly, the model loses its ability to make fine-grained positional distinctions. This manifests as increased perplexity on long sequences and degraded performance on tasks requiring precise positional understanding, such as retrieval from specific locations within a long document. By identifying and exploiting these non-uniformities, we can preserve critical information while still achieving the necessary compression to extend the context window.

# Benefits of Non-uniform Interpolation for Fine-tuning Initialization

While the non-uniform positional interpolation discoveries yield impressive results in non-fine-tuning scenarios, their benefits extend equally to fine-tuning settings. Proper initialization is crucial for successful fine-tuning, and the searched non-uniform interpolations provide significantly better starting points than existing methods.

The experimental evidence for this benefit comes from fine-tuning experiments where LLaMA2-7B was fine-tuned with searched RoPE rescale factors for a 64k context window. The results demonstrate that the searched method significantly outperforms both PI and YaRN, both before and after fine-tuning. This dual advantage—improving both the pre-fine-tuning baseline and the post-fine-tuning performance—highlights the fundamental importance of proper initialization.

To understand why better initialization matters, consider what happens during fine-tuning. When extending to longer context windows, the model encounters new position indices that were never seen during pre-training. These new positions introduce catastrophic values that can cause out-of-distribution issues, making fine-tuning difficult to converge. This is particularly problematic when extension ratios exceed 25x, where more than 90% of positions are new to the model.

If the initialization provided by positional interpolation is poor, the model starts fine-tuning from a state where positional understanding is already degraded. The model must then simultaneously learn to handle new positions while recovering from the damage caused by poor initialization. This dual burden makes convergence slower and less stable, requiring more fine-tuning steps and potentially yielding inferior final performance.

In contrast, non-uniform interpolation that preserves key dimensions and token positions provides a strong foundation for fine-tuning. By minimizing information loss before fine-tuning begins, the model starts from a state where its positional understanding remains largely intact. The model's existing knowledge about positional relationships is preserved, particularly for dimensions and positions that prove most critical for performance. Fine-tuning then focuses primarily on adapting to the new positions rather than recovering from initialization-induced damage.

This benefit has practical implications for training efficiency. With better initialization, fine-tuning converges faster and requires fewer training steps. The experiments show that LongRoPE achieves impressive results with only 1,000 fine-tuning steps—a remarkably small number given the magnitude of the extension from 4k to 2048k tokens. Without effective non-uniform interpolation, achieving similar results would likely require substantially more training steps and computational resources.

The benefits extend beyond efficiency to final model quality. Models initialized with searched non-uniform interpolations not only converge faster but also achieve better final perplexity scores. This suggests that the optimization landscape for positional interpolation is complex enough that poor initialization can lead to convergence to suboptimal local minima, whereas better initialization enables finding superior solutions.

# Extension Capabilities Without Fine-tuning

One of the most practically significant findings is that non-uniform positional interpolation enables substantial context window extension without any fine-tuning whatsoever. Specifically, the searched non-uniform interpolations achieve 8x extension—a dramatic improvement over existing methods.

To appreciate the magnitude of this achievement, consider the capabilities of current methods without fine-tuning. PI typically requires fine-tuning for any meaningful extension, as uniform interpolation rapidly degrades performance. NTK-based methods can extend context windows without fine-tuning but are usually limited to maximum extension ratios of approximately 4x. YaRN's performance in non-fine-tuning scenarios can actually underperform simpler methods, as evidenced by perplexity spikes after relatively modest sequence lengths.

The evolutionary search method, by fully exploiting both forms of non-uniformity, achieves 8x extension without fine-tuning—extending LLaMA2's context window from 4k to 32k while maintaining reasonable perplexity throughout. This is visualized in experimental figures showing perplexity curves across different evaluation lengths. While existing methods like PI, NTK, and YaRN cause perplexity to spike dramatically after approximately 2x extension, the searched non-uniform method maintains relatively stable perplexity across the full 8x range.

The mechanism behind this capability relates to information preservation. By identifying and preserving key dimensions (through dimension-specific rescale factors) and key positions (through position-specific treatment), the non-uniform interpolation minimizes the total information loss during extension. The model retains enough of its original positional understanding to generalize to longer sequences without additional training.

This capability has important practical implications. Fine-tuning for long context windows is expensive—it requires long training sequences, significant GPU resources, and careful hyperparameter tuning. The ability to achieve meaningful extension without fine-tuning democratizes access to longer context windows, enabling practitioners to extend their models without substantial computational investment.

Furthermore, this capability enables a progressive extension strategy. Rather than attempting to jump directly from 4k to extremely long contexts like 2048k, models can be progressively extended: first using non-uniform interpolation for moderate extension without fine-tuning, then fine-tuning at an intermediate length, and finally applying another round of non-uniform interpolation to reach the target length. This progressive approach avoids the challenges of attempting to fine-tune under very large extension ratios, where the prevalence of new positions (over 90% for extensions beyond 100x) makes convergence difficult.

The 8x extension capability thus serves as both a practical tool for moderate context extension and a building block for achieving much larger extensions through the progressive strategy. It represents a fundamental advancement in our understanding of how positional information can be manipulated to extend context windows efficiently and effectively.

# The LongRoPE Methodology: Search and Optimization

# Problem formulation: Minimizing prediction loss via rescale factors

The core challenge in extending the context window of Large Language Models lies in bridging the gap between the positional embeddings the model was trained on and the new positional indices required for longer sequences. LongRoPE addresses this challenge by reformulating the positional interpolation problem as a search optimization problem. Rather than relying on hand-crafted rules or heuristics, the method systematically searches for the optimal rescale factors that minimize the model's prediction loss on long sequences.

The problem formulation begins with a clear objective: given a target context window size $L'$ and a collection of lengthy input documents $\mathbf{X}$, where each document $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}$ exceeds $L'$ in token length, we seek to find the rescale factors that enable the model to process these sequences with minimal prediction error. The fundamental insight is that the rotary angle at each position and dimension can be rescaled independently, allowing for a highly flexible positional interpolation scheme.

Formally, let us denote the original rotary angle of the $i^{th}$ dimension in the RoPE embedding at token position $n$ as $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$. The optimization objective is expressed as minimizing the next token prediction loss:

$$\underset {\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}; \, |\mathbf{x}|\ge L'}{ \operatorname {arg\,min} } \,  \mathcal{L}\, \left( \text{LLM} (\text{RoPE}, \mathbf{X})\right)$$

The key innovation lies in how the RoPE embeddings are modified. LongRoPE introduces a set of rescale factors $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i}, \hat{n})$ that encode two forms of non-uniformity. For the $i^{th}$ RoPE dimension at position $n$, the rescaled RoPE embedding becomes:

$$\text{RoPE}_{(n)}= \left[\cdot\cdot,cos\left(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i}, \hat{n})\times\frac{n}{\beta^i}\right), sin\left(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i}, \hat{n})\times\frac{n}{\beta^i}\right),\cdot\cdot\right]$$

The rescale factor $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i},\hat{n})$ is defined conditionally based on the token position:

$$\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i},\hat{n})=\begin{cases}
1 & \mbox{if } n<\hat{n}\\
{\frac{1}{\lambda}_{i}} & \mbox{if } n\geq\hat{n}
\end{cases}$$

This formulation elegantly captures both forms of non-uniformity discovered through empirical analysis. The parameter $\hat{\lambda}_{i}$ represents the dimension-specific rescale factor, allowing each RoPE dimension to be treated differently. The parameter $\hat{n}$ represents a token position threshold, below which the original RoPE embedding is preserved without any interpolation. This design reflects the finding that initial tokens in a sequence receive high attention scores and benefit from less aggressive interpolation.

The optimization goal is therefore to find the optimal set of rescale factors $(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{0},\hat{n}), \mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{1},\hat{n}), ..., \mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i},\hat{n}), ...)$ across all $d$ RoPE dimensions such that the target LLM achieves minimum perplexity on input samples of length $L'$. The loss function $\mathcal{L}$ represents the perplexity, which measures how well the model predicts the next token in a sequence. Lower perplexity indicates better language modeling performance and suggests that the positional embeddings are well-suited for the extended context window.

# Designing the search space: Dimensions (λ) and token positions (n)

Having established the optimization objective, the next critical step involves defining a search space that comprehensively captures the two forms of non-uniformity while remaining computationally tractable. The search space design directly influences the quality of solutions that can be discovered and the efficiency of the search process.

For the dimension-specific rescale factors, LongRoPE allows each RoPE dimension to have its own unique rescale factor $\lambda_i$. To simplify the search space design, the method searches for $\lambda_i$ and $\hat{n}$ rather than directly searching for $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i}, \hat{n})$, where the relationship $\hat{\lambda}_{i}=1/\lambda_i$ holds. The rescale factor $\lambda_i$ is permitted to range from a minimum value of $1.0$, corresponding to direct extrapolation with no interpolation, to a maximum value of $s\times1.25$, which represents more aggressive interpolation than standard Position Interpolation. Here, $s$ denotes the target context window extension ratio. The step size for searching is set at $0.01$, providing sufficient granularity to explore the solution space.

The design choice of allowing $\lambda_i=1.0$ for extrapolation is significant. When $\lambda_i=1.0$, the original rotary angle is preserved without modification, which can be beneficial for certain dimensions that encode high-frequency information. At the other extreme, allowing values up to $s\times1.25$ provides flexibility beyond what standard Position Interpolation offers, potentially enabling better compression of positional information for dimensions that can tolerate more aggressive interpolation.

For the token position non-uniformity, the parameter $\hat{n}$ controls the number of initial token positions that are preserved without any positional interpolation. When $\hat{n}>0$, the first $\hat{n}$ tokens use the original RoPE embedding, while tokens at positions $n\geq\hat{n}$ have the rescale factor applied. Empirically, the search space for $\hat{n}$ is defined as the discrete set $\{0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 64, 128, 256\}$. This set captures the intuition that the optimal number of preserved initial tokens likely increases with sequence length but follows a logarithmic rather than linear scale. When $\hat{n}=0$, all token positions use the searched rescale factors, reverting to a purely dimension-based non-uniform interpolation.

The resulting search space is enormous. To illustrate the scale, consider a modest extension ratio of $s=4\times$. With $\lambda_i$ ranging from $1.0$ to $5.0$ with step size $0.01$, there are $400$ possible values per dimension. For a model with $d/2=64$ RoPE dimensions (typical for a 7B parameter model), combined with $14$ possible values for $\hat{n}$, the total number of choices is approximately $400^{64}\times14=4\times10^{167}$. This astronomical number highlights why naive exhaustive search is infeasible and motivates the need for intelligent search strategies.

# Introduction to Evolutionary Search for high-dimensional spaces

Given the exponentially expanding search space, LongRoPE employs an evolutionary search algorithm to efficiently navigate the vast landscape of possible positional interpolation solutions. Evolutionary algorithms are particularly well-suited for this task because they can explore diverse regions of the solution space simultaneously while progressively refining promising candidates.

The evolutionary search procedure follows a standard template with population-based optimization. The algorithm maintains a population of $P$ individuals, where each individual represents a candidate solution consisting of rescale factors for all RoPE dimensions and a token position threshold. At each iteration, the algorithm evaluates the fitness of each individual by computing the perplexity of the target LLM when using the corresponding rescale factors. Individuals with lower perplexity are considered more fit and have a higher probability of being selected for reproduction.

The reproduction process involves two genetic operators: mutation and crossover. Mutation introduces random perturbations to an individual's rescale factors, allowing exploration of nearby solutions. Crossover combines rescale factors from two parent individuals to create offspring, potentially inheriting beneficial traits from both parents. The top-$k$ individuals from each generation are preserved as elites and carried forward to the next generation, ensuring that good solutions are not lost during the search process.

The fitness evaluation for each individual requires computing the perplexity of the LLM on a set of long documents. Specifically, the algorithm applies the candidate rescale factors to the target LLM and computes the perplexity on input samples $\mathbf{X}$ that exceed the target context length $L'$. This evaluation is computationally expensive, particularly for large values of $L'$, because it requires running inference on very long sequences. Therefore, minimizing the number of fitness evaluations while still finding high-quality solutions is a key optimization objective.

The search is guided by perplexity computed on a small number of samples. For target window sizes within $256k$, the algorithm uses five random samples from the PG19 validation set, with each sample having a minimum length requirement equal to the target context length. For extremely long windows exceeding $512k$, the population size, mutation size, and crossover size are halved to manage computational costs, and perplexity is measured on three random samples from the Pile-Books3 validation set. The algorithm runs for a maximum of $\mathcal{T}=40$ iterations, providing sufficient opportunity for convergence while limiting computational overhead.

# Optimization 1: Monotonically non-decreasing constraint

To dramatically reduce the search space and improve efficiency, LongRoPE introduces a monotonically non-decreasing constraint on the rescale factors. This constraint requires that the rescale factors increase monotonically with the RoPE dimension index:

$$\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$$

for all dimensions $i=0, 1, ..., 63$. This constraint is grounded in the theoretical insights from Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory, which suggests that lower RoPE dimensions encode higher-frequency information and therefore require less interpolation. Conversely, higher dimensions encode lower-frequency information and can tolerate more aggressive interpolation.

The intuition behind this constraint can be understood by considering what the different RoPE dimensions represent. Lower dimensions correspond to higher rotation frequencies, meaning they change more rapidly as position increases. These dimensions are responsible for distinguishing between nearby token positions. If these dimensions are interpolated too aggressively, the positional information becomes too compressed, making it difficult for the model to distinguish adjacent tokens. Higher dimensions, with their lower frequencies, change more slowly and encode more global positional information. These can be compressed more without losing critical positional distinctions.

By enforcing this monotonicity constraint, the search algorithm only considers RoPE configurations where lower dimensions receive less interpolation (smaller $\lambda_i$) and higher dimensions receive more interpolation (larger $\lambda_i$). This constraint eliminates vast regions of the search space that would likely yield poor solutions, significantly reducing the number of unnecessary perplexity computations.

The practical impact of this constraint is substantial. Without the constraint, naive mutation and crossover operators would frequently generate candidate solutions that violate the theoretical relationship between dimension and interpolation requirements. These solutions would then undergo expensive perplexity evaluation only to be found inadequate. By filtering out such solutions before evaluation, the search algorithm can focus its computational budget on exploring more promising regions of the solution space. The constraint effectively reduces the search space from $400^{64}$ to the number of non-decreasing sequences of length $64$ drawn from $400$ possible values, which is a combinatorial number approximately $\binom{400+64-1}{64}$—still enormous but vastly smaller than the unconstrained space.

# Optimization 2: Optimized initial population generation

The second optimization technique addresses the initialization of the evolutionary search. Rather than randomly generating the initial population, which risks starting from poor solutions and requiring many iterations to reach quality candidates, LongRoPE incorporates domain knowledge by seeding the initial population with known interpolation methods.

The initial population of $P$ individuals is constructed by including the three RoPE rescale factor configurations corresponding to Position Interpolation (PI), NTK, and YaRN as guaranteed members. For PI, all dimensions use the same rescale factor $\lambda_i=s$. For NTK, the rescale factor follows $\lambda_i=s^{i}$, creating a progressive interpolation across dimensions. For YaRN, the rescale factors follow a piecewise pattern based on dimension groups. By including these three configurations, the search begins with known baselines that have been validated in prior work.

For the remaining $P-3$ individuals in the initial population, the algorithm generates random variants by mutating the three baseline configurations with probability $p$. This mutation-based generation ensures diversity in the initial population while still starting from reasonably good solutions rather than completely random ones. The mutation probability $p=0.3$ provides a balance between exploration and preservation of the baseline characteristics.

This optimized initialization strategy serves multiple purposes. First, it ensures that the search does not waste iterations finding solutions that are already known to exist. Second, it provides a competitive baseline from the very beginning—if none of the evolved solutions outperform the baselines, the search can still return the best of PI, NTK, or YaRN. Third, by mutating around these baselines, the algorithm can discover incremental improvements that build upon existing knowledge rather than starting from scratch.

The combination of optimized initialization and monotonicity constraint creates a powerful synergy. The initial population contains solutions that already satisfy the monotonicity constraint, and subsequent mutation and crossover operations are designed to maintain this constraint. This means that throughout the search process, all candidate solutions are theoretically motivated and efficiently evaluable.

# Efficiency gains and convergence of the search algorithm

The two optimization techniques—monotonically non-decreasing constraint and optimized initial population generation—work together to make the search algorithm practical for real-world applications. The efficiency gains manifest in two ways: reduced per-iteration computational cost and faster convergence to high-quality solutions.

The computational savings from the monotonicity constraint are particularly significant for large target context lengths. When $L'$ is large, each perplexity computation requires processing very long sequences, which is time-consuming even on modern GPUs. By filtering out solutions that violate the constraint before evaluation, the algorithm avoids wasting resources on unpromising candidates. The paper demonstrates that this constraint eliminates the need to evaluate solutions that are theoretically inconsistent with the frequency-based interpretation of RoPE dimensions.

The optimized initialization accelerates convergence by starting the evolutionary process in promising regions of the search space. Traditional evolutionary algorithms with random initialization often require numerous iterations to escape poor local optima. By contrast, LongRoPE's initialization ensures that the population immediately contains competitive solutions, and the evolutionary process can focus on refining and recombining these solutions to discover even better ones.

The effectiveness of this search algorithm is demonstrated by its ability to extend LLaMA2's context window from $4k$ to $32k$ without any fine-tuning—an $8\times$ extension. This represents a significant improvement over existing methods. Standard Position Interpolation causes perplexity to spike after just a $2\times$ extension in non-fine-tuning scenarios. NTK-based methods and YaRN similarly struggle beyond $4\times$ extension without fine-tuning. LongRoPE's searched non-uniform positional interpolation preserves key dimensions and token positions, minimizing the information loss typically associated with positional interpolation.

The searched rescale factors are highly non-uniform, differing substantially from the fixed scale $s$ used in PI, the formulaic calculation in NTK, and the group-wise rules in YaRN. This non-uniformity reflects the complex information entropy distribution in positional embeddings within the Transformer architecture. By discovering and exploiting these subtle non-uniformities through evolutionary search, LongRoPE achieves substantial performance improvements, demonstrating that human-designed rules for positional interpolation are sub-optimal compared to data-driven search approaches.

# Strategies for Extreme Context Extension

Extending the context window of a large language model to extreme lengths—beyond two million tokens—presents formidable challenges that cannot be addressed through naive approaches. The journey from a standard 4k context window to a 2048k window requires careful orchestration of multiple techniques, each building upon the previous. This chapter explores the practical implementation strategy used to achieve such unprecedented context lengths, detailing the progressive training pipeline and the mechanisms employed to preserve performance on shorter sequences.

## Challenges of Direct 2048k Extension

The straightforward approach to extending an LLM's context window would be to directly search for appropriate RoPE rescale factors targeting a 2048k context window and then fine-tune the model on texts of that length. However, this seemingly simple approach encounters several insurmountable obstacles that make direct extension impractical.

The first and most fundamental challenge concerns the introduction of new position indices. When extending from a 4k context window to over 1000k tokens, more than 90% of the position indices become new—positions the model has never encountered during its original pre-training. These untrained new position indices introduce what the authors term "catastrophic values," leading to severe out-of-distribution issues. The model's positional embeddings, which were carefully learned during pre-training to represent token positions meaningfully, suddenly find themselves in uncharted territory. This makes the fine-tuning process extremely difficult to converge, as the optimization landscape becomes treacherous with these unfamiliar positional representations.

The second major obstacle lies in the scarcity of suitable training data. Fine-tuning typically requires texts of lengths corresponding to the target context window. While datasets containing moderately long texts exist, texts exceeding one million tokens are exceedingly rare. Even finding documents of 100k tokens proves challenging, let alone texts spanning multiple millions of tokens. This data scarcity creates a fundamental bottleneck: without appropriate training examples, the model cannot learn to effectively utilize the extended context window.

Beyond data availability, computational considerations present a third formidable barrier. Training on extra-long texts demands substantial computational resources. The memory requirements scale with the sequence length, and the training time increases dramatically as well. Processing sequences of 2048k tokens would require prohibitively extensive training hours and GPU resources, making the approach economically and practically infeasible for most research teams and organizations.

A fourth challenge, perhaps more subtle but equally important, concerns the attention mechanism's behavior when spread across extremely long sequences. As the context window expands to accommodate millions of tokens, the attention becomes dispersed—spread thinly across numerous token positions. This dispersion degrades the model's performance on the original short context tasks, as the attention patterns that worked well for short sequences no longer function optimally when the model has learned to attend to much longer contexts.

The authors' empirical experiences confirmed these challenges. Directly fine-tuning LLMs under such a large extension ratio proved difficult, with convergence issues and performance degradation being persistent problems. These observations motivated the development of an alternative, more sophisticated approach: a progressive extension strategy that circumvents these obstacles through careful staging.

## The Progressive Extension Pipeline: 4k to 256k to 2048k

The key insight enabling extreme context extension is that the LongRoPE search algorithm works effectively on both the original pre-trained LLM and on fine-tuned extended LLMs. This observation unlocks a progressive strategy that achieves the target 2048k context window without requiring direct fine-tuning on texts of extreme lengths. The approach requires only 1000 fine-tuning steps at training lengths within 256k tokens—a dramatic reduction in computational requirements compared to direct training on 2048k sequences.

The progressive extension pipeline proceeds through three carefully designed stages. The first stage extends the pre-trained LLM to 256k tokens using the LongRoPE search algorithm. Taking LLaMA2 as an example, the authors conduct searches for target context window sizes of both 128k and 256k tokens. At this initial stage, the extension ratio reaches 32× for the 128k target and 64× for the 256k target. These ratios, while substantial, remain within the realm of feasibility for the search algorithm to handle effectively. The non-uniform positional interpolation discovered through evolutionary search provides a robust foundation for the subsequent fine-tuning stage.

The second stage involves fine-tuning the model to achieve the 256k context window. The fine-tuning process itself is staged: the model first undergoes 400 fine-tuning steps using RoPE rescale factors optimized for the 128k context window. Upon completion of these initial steps, the RoPE rescale factors are updated to those optimized for 256k, and training continues for an additional 600 steps. This two-stage fine-tuning approach proves more efficient and stable than attempting to fine-tune directly to 256k in one go. The progressive approach allows the model to adapt gradually to increasingly long contexts, with each stage building upon the adaptations learned in the previous stage.

The third and final stage leverages the remarkable finding that non-uniform positional interpolation enables an 8× extension even without fine-tuning. Since the model has been fine-tuned to handle 256k tokens, a secondary search for new RoPE rescale factors can extend it to 2048k—a further 8× extension—without requiring additional fine-tuning. This secondary search is conducted on the fine-tuned 256k-length LLM, and the resulting rescale factors ultimately achieve the 2048k context window. The final extension ratio from the original 4k to 2048k reaches 512×, yet this is achieved through careful composition of the intermediate stages rather than through direct optimization.

This progressive strategy elegantly sidesteps the challenges that make direct extension impractical. By working with intermediate context lengths of 256k and below, the training remains computationally tractable. The scarcity of extremely long texts becomes less problematic since the fine-tuning uses documents of 256k tokens or less—lengths that are more readily available in existing datasets. The gradual extension allows the model to adapt its attention patterns progressively, avoiding the catastrophic introduction of new positions that would occur with a single massive extension.

## Fine-Tuning on Intermediate Lengths (128k and 256k)

The fine-tuning stage of the progressive pipeline requires careful configuration of hyperparameters and data preparation to ensure stable and effective training. The authors' experimental setup provides detailed guidance on the practical considerations for fine-tuning on intermediate lengths.

For LLaMA2, the fine-tuning employs a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ with linear decay and a global batch size of 32. The training data comes from the Redpajama dataset, which is chunked into 128k segments. Each segment is bookended with beginning-of-sequence (BOS) and end-of-sequence (EOS) tokens to properly demarcate the training examples. The staged fine-tuning begins with 400 steps using RoPE rescale factors optimized for 128k tokens. This initial training is conducted on 8 A100 GPUs using a distributed training system. After completing these initial steps, the RoPE rescale factors are swapped for those optimized for 256k tokens, and training continues for an additional 600 steps. The 256k training requires 16 A100 GPUs, reflecting the increased memory demands of longer sequences.

The two-stage fine-tuning approach—first to 128k, then to 256k—offers several advantages over direct training to 256k. First, it provides better initialization for the longer context window, as the model has already adapted to handling 128k tokens before tackling the more challenging 256k extension. Second, it allows for more stable training dynamics, as each stage operates within a more manageable extension ratio. Third, it enables more efficient use of computational resources, as the 128k stage requires fewer GPUs and can potentially catch convergence issues before committing to the more expensive 256k training.

For Mistral models, the fine-tuning follows a slightly different configuration, aligning with prior work on YaRN. A constant learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-6}$ and a global batch size of 64 are employed. Both the 128k and 256k models use 400 training steps on the Together Computer's Long-Data Collections dataset, with a training sequence length of 16k. This configuration requires 4 A100 GPUs. The use of a shorter training length (16k versus 128k for LLaMA2) reflects different design choices about how to balance training efficiency with context extension capability, though the authors note that this choice affects Mistral's ability to further extend the context window after fine-tuning.

The comparison between LLaMA2 and Mistral fine-tuning highlights an important practical consideration: the choice of training sequence length affects the model's ultimate extension capability. Models fine-tuned on longer sequences within their target context window tend to achieve better performance when further extended through the secondary search. This observation suggests that while shorter training sequences reduce computational requirements, they may limit the model's ability to generalize to even longer contexts.

## Secondary Positional Interpolation on Fine-tuned Models

A crucial innovation in the LongRoPE approach is the recognition that the evolutionary search for non-uniform positional interpolation works effectively not only on pre-trained models but also on models that have already undergone fine-tuning for extended contexts. This insight enables the secondary positional interpolation that bridges the gap from 256k to 2048k.

After fine-tuning a model to handle 256k tokens, the secondary search operates on this fine-tuned extended LLM. The search algorithm identifies new RoPE rescale factors optimized for the target 2048k context window. Since the model has already learned to handle positions up to 256k, the secondary extension of 8× operates on a model that already possesses substantial long-context capability. This is fundamentally different from attempting to extend the original 4k model directly to 2048k, as the intermediate fine-tuning has already adapted the model's attention patterns and positional representations to handle long-range dependencies.

The effectiveness of the secondary search is validated through systematic experiments. The authors take their fine-tuned LLaMA2-256k model and extend it to 512k, 1024k, and 2048k using both standard positional interpolation methods (PI and YaRN) and the LongRoPE search algorithm. The results demonstrate that the non-uniform positional interpolation discovered through evolutionary search sustains a consistent level of perplexity across these extreme lengths. In contrast, the perplexity under PI and YaRN increases rapidly with the extension ratio, indicating that these standard methods struggle with the secondary extension.

The success of the secondary positional interpolation can be attributed to several factors. First, the fine-tuned model has already learned to distribute attention across long sequences, so the additional extension does not introduce as severe out-of-distribution issues as extending from the original 4k window. Second, the evolutionary search can exploit the model's existing adaptations, finding rescale factors that build upon the learned representations rather than working against them. Third, the non-uniform interpolation can preserve the most important positional information from the fine-tuned 256k window while enabling further extension.

The comparison between different fine-tuning lengths for the secondary extension reveals an interesting trade-off. For LLaMA2, LongRoPE-2048k performs better when fine-tuned at 256k compared to 128k. This improvement stems from the smaller secondary extension ratio: extending from 256k to 2048k requires an 8× ratio, while extending from 128k to 2048k requires a 16× ratio. The smaller ratio allows the search algorithm to find more effective rescale factors and results in better performance at the target length. This finding underscores the importance of the intermediate fine-tuning length in determining the success of the final extension.

## Short Context Window Recovery: Readjusting Rescale Factors

A known issue with positional interpolation is that extending to extremely long context windows degrades performance on shorter sequences. This degradation occurs because positional interpolation forces position embeddings in higher dimensions to reside in much narrower regions than they originally occupied. With a 512× extension ratio, the positions within the original 4k context window become particularly crowded—the model loses the ability to distinguish between positions that were originally well-separated.

The authors observe this phenomenon clearly after extending to 2048k: performance drops within the original context window compared to the unmodified pre-trained model. This degradation is unacceptable for practical applications, as users expect the model to maintain its capabilities on standard-length inputs even after gaining the ability to process extremely long sequences.

To address this issue, LongRoPE implements a recovery mechanism through additional evolutionary search. The key idea is to search for separate RoPE rescale factors specifically optimized for short context lengths—typically 4k and 8k tokens. These shorter-length rescale factors are applied conditionally based on the actual input length during inference, allowing the model to use the most appropriate positional representations for each sequence.

The search for short-context rescale factors operates on the extended LLM, but with modified constraints. Specifically, the maximum allowable searched $\lambda$ values are reduced because shorter lengths require less positional interpolation. By constraining the search to smaller rescale factors, the algorithm encourages solutions that preserve more of the original positional information appropriate for shorter sequences.

The effectiveness of this recovery mechanism is demonstrated through empirical evaluation. The authors compare the perplexity of LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k on the Proof-pile dataset at 4k and 8k lengths, with and without the short-context rescale factors. The results show significant performance improvement at these short context lengths when using the specialized factors. Furthermore, evaluation on standard LLM benchmarks designed for the original 4096-token context window shows that LongRoPE-2048k models achieve comparable results to the original models, with some cases even showing slight improvements.

The recovery mechanism represents an important principle: extreme context extension should not come at the cost of performance on common, shorter inputs. By maintaining separate sets of rescale factors for different context lengths, the model effectively gains the ability to operate across a vast range of sequence lengths—from 4k to 2048k—while maintaining strong performance throughout.

## Dynamic Inference with Length-Specific Rescale Factors

The implementation of short context window recovery necessitates a dynamic inference mechanism that can select the appropriate RoPE rescale factors based on the input sequence length. This dynamic adjustment is a crucial component of the LongRoPE approach, ensuring that the model always operates with optimal positional representations.

During inference, the LLM continuously monitors the sequence length and adjusts the RoPE rescale factors accordingly. When the input sequence length falls below 8k tokens, the model updates its RoPE embeddings to use the rescale factors optimized for short contexts. For longer sequences, it switches to the factors optimized for the extended context window. This dynamic switching happens seamlessly during the forward pass, without requiring any changes to the model architecture or significant computational overhead.

The dynamic inference mechanism can be implemented efficiently because RoPE embeddings are computed on-the-fly during inference rather than being pre-computed and stored. The positional encoding for a token at position index $n$ is given by:

$$\left[ \cos\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\right), \sin\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\right), \cos\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^1}\right), \ldots, \sin\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{d/2-1}}\right) \right]$$

where the rescale factors $\lambda(\beta)^i$ determine how each dimension is interpolated. By maintaining multiple sets of these factors—optimized for different context lengths—the model can switch between them by simply loading the appropriate set during the computation of positional encodings.

The practical benefits of dynamic inference extend beyond performance maintenance. Users of the model need not be aware of the underlying complexity; they simply pass inputs of varying lengths, and the model automatically selects the optimal configuration. This transparency at the user level contrasts with approaches that require explicit configuration or that exhibit degraded performance on certain input lengths.

Furthermore, the dynamic mechanism allows for future extensibility. Additional sets of rescale factors could be optimized for intermediate lengths—such as 32k or 64k tokens—enabling even finer-grained adaptation to input characteristics. The search algorithm's flexibility means that new configurations can be discovered as needed without retraining the model.

The combination of progressive extension, secondary positional interpolation, short-context recovery, and dynamic inference represents a comprehensive solution to the challenge of extreme context extension. Each component addresses a specific aspect of the problem, and together they enable LLMs to operate effectively across a previously unattainable range of sequence lengths—from the standard 4k tokens to an unprecedented 2048k tokens, all while maintaining strong performance throughout.

# Experimental Evaluation and Results

Understanding how to evaluate methods for extending language model context windows requires a comprehensive examination of both the experimental methodology and the empirical findings. # Experimental Setup: Models and Baselines

The experimental framework centers on two widely-used large language model architectures: LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B. These models serve as representative examples of modern LLMs that employ Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), making them suitable testbeds for evaluating positional interpolation methods. LLaMA2-7B has an original context window of 4096 tokens, while Mistral-7B also operates within a similar range. The choice of these models is particularly meaningful because they represent different design philosophies in modern LLM development, and demonstrating effectiveness across both architectures provides strong evidence for the generalizability of the method.

The fine-tuning process differs between the two model families, reflecting their distinct training dynamics and architectural considerations. For LLaMA2, the fine-tuning employs a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ with linear decay and a global batch size of 32. The training proceeds in two phases: an initial 400 steps on the Redpajama dataset, which is chunked into 128k segments and properly bounded with beginning-of-sequence and end-of-sequence tokens, followed by an additional 600 steps to reach the target 256k context window. The computational requirements scale with the target context length, where training for 128k context size requires 8 A100 GPUs, while the 256k extension demands 16 A100 GPUs. This staged approach to training proves more efficient than directly fine-tuning to 256k, as it allows the model to gradually adapt to longer sequences.

For Mistral, the fine-tuning configuration takes a different approach suited to that model's characteristics. A constant learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-6}$ is employed alongside a larger global batch size of 64. Following the methodology established in prior work on YaRN, both the 128k and 256k models undergo 400 training steps on Together Computer's Long-Data Collections, using a sequence length of 16k. This configuration requires 4 A100 GPUs for training. The use of a 16k training length, while shorter than the target extension, reflects practical considerations in training efficiency, though as we shall see, this choice has implications for the ultimate extension capability.

The search algorithm for identifying optimal RoPE rescale factors operates with carefully chosen hyperparameters. For target context window sizes within 256k, the evolutionary search uses a population size $P=64$, with mutation and crossover sizes $N_1 = N_2 = 16$. The mutation probability $p$ is set to 0.3, and the algorithm runs for a maximum of $\mathcal{T}=40$ iterations, selecting the top-32 individuals for mutation and crossover in each iteration. The perplexity metric guiding the search is calculated using 5 random samples from the PG19 validation set, with each sample meeting a minimum length requirement equal to the target context length. For context windows exceeding 512k tokens, the population, mutation, and crossover sizes are halved to manage computational costs, and perplexity evaluation uses 3 random samples from the Pile-Books3 validation set.

The baselines against which LongRoPE is compared represent the state-of-the-art in context window extension methods. These include Together-32k, which represents a production-quality extended context model; Code LLaMA, which achieves context extension through large-scale fine-tuning; LongLoRA-full-FT-100k, demonstrating efficient fine-tuning approaches; and various implementations of positional interpolation methods including PI, NTK, Dynamic-NTK, and YaRN applied to both LLaMA and Mistral. The LongRoPE models are specifically designated as LongRoPE-2048k (ft=128k) and LongRoPE-2048k (ft=256k) for LLaMA2 and Mistral respectively, indicating the fine-tuning length used before the final extension to 2048k.

The evaluation itself encompasses three distinct categories of tasks, each designed to probe different aspects of extended context capability. First, perplexity evaluation on long documents directly measures the language modeling capability of the extended models. Second, passkey retrieval provides a synthetic but precise measure of the effective context window—the maximum length at which the model can reliably extract information. Third, standard benchmarks evaluated within the original context window size ensure that extension does not compromise performance on typical tasks.

# Perplexity Analysis on Long Documents

Perplexity serves as a fundamental metric for evaluating language model quality, measuring how well a probability model predicts a sample. In the context of extended context windows, perplexity analysis reveals whether the model can effectively leverage longer context to improve its predictions. A well-functioning extended model should show decreasing perplexity as context length increases, indicating that additional context provides useful information for prediction.

The evaluation employs three distinct datasets, each chosen to provide different perspectives on model performance. The PG19 dataset provides a comprehensive test of long-document language modeling, using the entire test split of 100 documents. The Proof-pile dataset, which contains mathematical and scientific texts, follows the established evaluation protocol of randomly selecting 10 samples, each with at least 128k tokens in length. For the extremely long context evaluation beyond 2000k tokens, the Books3 dataset is employed, with 20 books selected that each exceed 2048k tokens. The use of a sliding window approach—with window size 256 for shorter evaluations and 256k for the longest contexts—ensures consistent measurement across different context lengths.

The results within the 256k context window range demonstrate clear patterns of effectiveness. The extended models show an overall decreasing perplexity trend from 4k to 256k evaluation lengths, providing strong evidence that these models can successfully leverage longer context. This pattern of decreasing perplexity is particularly significant because it indicates that the model is not merely tolerating longer sequences but actually benefiting from the additional context information. A model that simply accepts longer inputs without degrading would maintain constant perplexity; the observed decrease suggests genuine utilization of extended context.

Even with a context window 16 times longer than typical—a condition that normally challenges the preservation of shorter-length performance—the LongRoPE-2048k models outperform state-of-the-art baselines within the 256k context length. This result is remarkable because it demonstrates that achieving extreme extension does not require sacrificing performance at intermediate lengths. The non-uniform positional interpolation preserves critical positional information even when scaling to very long contexts.

The evaluation beyond 2000k tokens reveals both the successes and limitations of the extension approach. LongRoPE successfully extends both LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B's context windows to 2048k tokens while achieving perplexity comparable to or superior than baselines operating at much shorter lengths of 8k-128k. This result validates the central claim of achieving context windows beyond 2 million tokens while maintaining competitive performance.

However, the two model families exhibit notably different behavior patterns at extreme lengths. LLaMA2's performance aligns with theoretical expectations: perplexity decreases gracefully with longer contexts, showing only marginal increases at 1024k and 2048k. This pattern suggests that LLaMA2 successfully leverages the extended context throughout the full range of evaluated lengths. The slight perplexity increases at the extreme end are consistent with the model operating near the boundary of its effective context window.

Mistral presents a different profile. While it outperforms baselines at shorter lengths, its perplexity exceeds 7 beyond 256k tokens. This behavioral difference can be traced back to the different fine-tuning configurations employed. For Mistral, the fine-tuning used a 16k sequence length rather than the full target length, which affects the model's ability to extend its context window after fine-tuning. This finding highlights an important principle: the training sequence length during fine-tuning influences the ultimate extension capability, and shorter training sequences may limit how far the context window can be pushed.

The comparison between LongRoPE-2048k models fine-tuned at different lengths further illuminates these dynamics. For LLaMA2, the model fine-tuned at 256k performs better than the one fine-tuned at 128k. This advantage stems from the smaller secondary extension ratio—extending from 256k to 2048k represents an 8$\times$ extension, while extending from 128k to 2048k requires a 16$\times$ extension. The smaller extension ratio proves easier for the non-uniform positional interpolation to handle, resulting in better preservation of positional information.

# Passkey Retrieval: Validating the Effective Context Window

While perplexity provides a continuous measure of language modeling quality, the passkey retrieval task offers a more direct assessment of what researchers call the "effective context window"—the practical limit within which a model can reliably retrieve specific information from a long context. This task has become a standard benchmark in context window extension research precisely because it provides unambiguous, binary outcomes: either the model successfully retrieves the hidden passkey or it does not.

The passkey retrieval task operates through a carefully designed synthetic evaluation protocol. A random passkey, defined as a five-digit number, is hidden somewhere within a long document composed primarily of irrelevant text. The model must identify and extract this passkey when prompted. The task is performed across 10 iterations, with the passkey placed at a random location uniformly distributed across the evaluation context length. This randomization ensures that the evaluation tests retrieval capability across the entire context window rather than focusing on specific positions.

The results reveal a stark contrast between LongRoPE and existing methods. Existing models' retrieval accuracy rapidly drops to 0 beyond 128k tokens, indicating that their effective context window ends around that threshold. In contrast, LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k (ft=256k) maintains retrieval accuracy at or above 90% from 4k all the way to 2048k tokens. This sustained high accuracy across such an extreme length range demonstrates that the extended context window is genuinely functional—the model can actually access and retrieve information from any position within the 2-million-token context.

The Mistral variant shows similarly impressive, though slightly different, behavior. LongRoPE-Mistral-2048k (ft=128k) maintains perfect 100% accuracy up to 1800k tokens, with accuracy dropping to 60% at the full 2048k length. This performance profile aligns with the perplexity observations discussed earlier, where Mistral showed slight increases in perplexity at the extreme 2048k length. The drop to 60% at 2048k, while not perfect, still represents a dramatic improvement over baseline methods that cannot function at all beyond 128k.

The passkey retrieval results carry significant implications for practical applications. A model that can retrieve specific information from anywhere in a 2-million-token context opens possibilities for tasks that were previously infeasible. For example, processing entire codebases, analyzing complete books or document collections, or maintaining coherent conversations over extended interactions all become tractable with such extended context windows.

# Performance on Standard Benchmarks Within the Original Context Window

A critical concern in context window extension is whether achieving longer contexts compromises performance on shorter, standard tasks. This concern arises because positional interpolation fundamentally modifies how position information is encoded, and these modifications might not be neutral with respect to performance on sequences within the original context window. The evaluation therefore includes standard benchmarks designed for the original 4096-token context window to assess this potential degradation.

The evaluation uses the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard, a widely-recognized benchmark suite, in both zero-shot and few-shot settings. The specific tasks include 25-shot ARC-Challenge, which tests reasoning about scientific concepts; 10-shot HellaSwag, evaluating common sense reasoning about physical situations; 5-shot MMLU, covering diverse academic and professional knowledge; and 0-shot TruthfulQA, measuring the model's tendency to generate truthful versus hallucinated responses.

The results demonstrate that LongRoPE-2048k models achieve comparable results on these original benchmarks. This finding is significant because it addresses one of the primary concerns about context window extension: that modifying positional embeddings might introduce systematic biases or artifacts that degrade general performance. The preservation of benchmark performance indicates that the non-uniform positional interpolation successfully extends the context window while leaving the underlying language model capabilities intact.

An especially notable result is that LongRoPE-Mistral-2048k actually outperforms the original Mistral on TruthfulQA by 0.5 percentage points. This improvement, while modest, suggests that the extension process does not merely preserve capabilities but may in some cases enhance them. The mechanism behind this improvement remains an interesting question for future research, though it may relate to better calibration of position information across the model's computations.

The LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k model fine-tuned at 256k shows slightly more performance degradation on some benchmarks compared to its 128k fine-tuned counterpart, but the differences remain within reasonable ranges for most tasks. This slight degradation represents a trade-off: better performance at extreme lengths comes with marginal cost in short-context performance. The practical significance of this trade-off depends on the specific use case—applications requiring the full 2048k context may accept small short-context performance variations, while applications primarily working within standard context lengths might prefer configurations optimized for that regime.

# Ablation Studies: Impact of the Second Interpolation and Non-uniformities

Ablation studies serve to isolate the contribution of individual components to overall performance, providing insight into which design choices matter and why. The LongRoPE ablation experiments examine three key aspects: the effectiveness of the second positional interpolation, the recovery mechanism for shorter context lengths, and the contribution of each form of non-uniformity.

The progressive extension strategy employs a second non-uniform positional interpolation on the already fine-tuned extended model. To validate this approach, experiments extend the fine-tuned LLaMA2-256k model to 512k, 1024k, and 2048k using both the proposed method and comparison methods (PI and YaRN). The results demonstrate that non-uniform positional interpolation sustains a consistent level of perplexity across these extension ratios, while the comparison methods show perplexity that quickly increases as the extension ratio grows. This finding validates the central insight that non-uniform interpolation, discovered through evolutionary search, handles large extension ratios more gracefully than methods based on fixed formulas or human-designed rules.

The recovery mechanism for shorter context lengths addresses the observation that extending to extremely long contexts can degrade performance on shorter sequences. The mechanism performs an additional evolutionary search specifically for short context lengths (4k and 8k), decreasing the maximum allowable scale factors to encourage less interpolation. The perplexity comparison of LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k on Proof-pile at 4k and 8k lengths, combined with average benchmark accuracy measurements, demonstrates significant performance improvements at short context lengths. This result confirms that the recovery mechanism successfully mitigates the crowding effect that positional interpolation can introduce at shorter lengths.

The analysis of the two forms of non-uniformities examines their individual and combined contributions. Experiments extend LLaMA2-7B to short lengths (16k and 32k) using three approaches: standard PI, searching only for RoPE dimension non-uniformity, and searching for both non-uniformities together. Additional experiments extend the fine-tuned 256k LLaMA2 to 2048k using the same procedure.

The results reveal that non-uniformity in RoPE dimension significantly reduces perplexity compared to PI's linear interpolation. This finding confirms that different RoPE dimensions require different treatment—a uniform approach loses information that can be preserved through dimension-specific rescaling. Non-uniformity in token position—the differential treatment of initial tokens—clearly improves performance at 16k and 32k lengths. However, at the extreme 2048k length, the token position non-uniformity does not show the same impact. This difference may occur because preserving only initial tokens without interpolation becomes less useful when the sequence spans such extreme lengths. The practical importance of initial tokens may be diluted when the context contains millions of tokens. This observation points to a limitation of the current approach and suggests directions for future research: understanding how different non-uniformities scale with extension ratio could lead to even more effective interpolation strategies.

# Comparative Analysis with State-of-the-Art Extension Methods

Placing the experimental results in context requires a systematic comparison with existing approaches to context window extension. The comparative analysis reveals both the relative strengths of different methods and the specific advantages that non-uniform positional interpolation provides.

Position Interpolation (PI), which serves as a foundational baseline, applies linear scaling across all RoPE dimensions with rescale factor $\lambda = s$ equal to the extension ratio. This uniform approach introduces the "crowded positions" problem, where position information becomes compressed as all positions are scaled down uniformly. The experimental results consistently show PI underperforming at high extension ratios, with perplexity degrading and retrieval accuracy falling as extension increases. PI requires fine-tuning to function at any extension ratio beyond very small increases.

NTK-based methods, including dynamic NTK, introduce the principle of differential treatment across dimensions. By scaling lower (high frequency) dimensions less and higher (low frequency) dimensions more, these methods avoid some crowding effects. Dynamic NTK adjusts the extension ratio at each position based on current sequence length. While these methods can extend context windows without fine-tuning up to approximately 4$\times$, they struggle beyond that threshold. The experimental comparisons show that while NTK approaches outperform PI at moderate extension ratios, both are eventually surpassed by methods that more carefully optimize the interpolation.

YaRN represents the state-of-the-art prior to LongRoPE, dividing RoPE dimensions into three frequency-based groups with different interpolation strategies for each. The grouping depends on human-designed rules based on empirical observations. The experiments reveal that YaRN sometimes underperforms even PI and NTK at certain evaluation points, particularly when it does not reach the target context window length for non-fine-tuned models. For instance, YaRN's perplexity spikes after 7k tokens in an 8k context size evaluation. This finding suggests that human-designed groupings may not be optimal for all models and extension ratios.

LongRoPE addresses these limitations through automated search rather than human-designed rules. The evolutionary search discovers optimal rescale factors for each dimension and identifies the optimal token position threshold $\hat{n}$ for initial token preservation. The comparative results demonstrate that this automated, data-driven approach consistently outperforms methods based on theoretical principles alone. The 8$\times$ extension achieved without fine-tuning represents a substantial improvement over the approximately 4$\times$ limit of previous methods.

The comparison also reveals practical advantages beyond raw performance metrics. The LongRoPE approach maintains model architecture with only minor modifications to positional embeddings, allowing reuse of existing optimizations and infrastructure. Models extended via LongRoPE can leverage most pre-existing implementations, making deployment more straightforward than approaches requiring architectural changes.

The progressive extension strategy, while adding complexity to the training pipeline, proves essential for achieving extreme extension ratios. Direct fine-tuning to 2048k tokens would face prohibitive computational costs and convergence challenges. The progressive approach—extending to 256k with fine-tuning, then applying a second interpolation to reach 2048k—balances computational efficiency with effective extension. The experimental validation shows that this approach achieves results comparable to what one might expect from direct training, had such training been feasible.

# Conclusion and Broader Implications

# Summary of LongRoPE Contributions

The LongRoPE framework represents a significant advancement in the field of large language model context extension, achieving what was previously thought impractical: extending the context window of pre-trained LLMs to an unprecedented 2048k tokens. This achievement addresses fundamental limitations that have constrained context window extensions to approximately 128k tokens in prior work. The methodology introduced in this paper rests on three interconnected innovations that work synergistically to overcome the challenges of fine-tuning costs, scarcity of long training texts, and the catastrophic values introduced by new token positions.

The first contribution lies in the identification and exploitation of two forms of non-uniformities in positional interpolation. Previous approaches, including Position Interpolation (PI), NTK-aware methods, and YaRN, applied relatively uniform scaling strategies across RoPE dimensions and token positions. LongRoPE reveals that effective positional interpolation must account for varying RoPE dimensions and varying token positions. Lower RoPE dimensions and initial starting token positions benefit from less interpolation, meaning they should retain more of their original positional characteristics. The paper demonstrates that these non-uniformities, when properly leveraged, minimize the information loss caused by positional interpolation. This insight leads to a remarkable capability: an $8\times$ context window extension without any fine-tuning, a feat that previous methods could not achieve.

The second innovation introduces a progressive extension strategy that enables reaching the 2048k context window without requiring direct fine-tuning on extremely long texts. Rather than attempting to extend directly from 4k to 2048k—which would require prohibitively expensive computational resources and faces convergence difficulties due to the large extension ratio—LongRoPE adopts a staged approach. The strategy begins by searching for and fine-tuning to a 256k context window, using approximately 1000 fine-tuning steps. This fine-tuned extended LLM then undergoes a second positional interpolation search to reach the target 2048k context window. This progressive approach leverages the $8\times$ extension capability of the non-uniform interpolation, making the leap from 256k to 2048k achievable without additional fine-tuning.

The third contribution addresses a critical concern in context window extension: the degradation of performance on the original short context window. When extending to extremely long context windows through positional interpolation, positions within the original context window become crowded, as the position embeddings are compressed into a narrower region. LongRoPE mitigates this by conducting an additional evolutionary search to adjust RoPE rescale factors specifically for short context lengths such as 4k and 8k. During inference, the model dynamically applies these adjusted rescale factors when the sequence length falls below the threshold, effectively recovering the original performance on shorter contexts.

# Comparison with Retrieval-Based and Attention-Based Approaches

The landscape of context window extension methodologies encompasses several distinct paradigms, each with its own philosophical approach and practical implications. Understanding how LongRoPE positions itself relative to these alternatives illuminates both its design philosophy and its practical advantages.

Retrieval-based approaches represent one major category of solutions for handling long contexts. These methods employ external memory modules to store long past context and utilize retrieval mechanisms to fetch relevant documents during inference. Notable examples include approaches that augment LLMs with retrieval capabilities. While these methods can effectively extend the effective context available to a model, they require explicit and often substantial modifications to the LLM architecture. The introduction of external memory and retrieval components adds complexity to both the model design and the inference pipeline. Furthermore, retrieval-based approaches inherently treat context extension as an information retrieval problem, which may not capture all the nuanced relationships present in truly long-context scenarios. In contrast, LongRoPE operates as a more lightweight solution, requiring only minor modifications to the positional embedding mechanism. This design choice preserves the original model architecture while extending its native context processing capabilities. The paper explicitly notes that LongRoPE can handle a broader range of long-context tasks beyond retrieval, including long document summarization and few-shot learning scenarios where understanding the full context is essential.

Attention-based context window extensions offer another alternative paradigm. These methods attempt to extend the effective input context using the original LLM context window length by manipulating attention mechanisms. Approaches such as LM-Infinite, Streaming LLM, and parallel window attention introduce novel attention masks to mitigate the attention explosion issue caused by new positions. The core insight is that standard attention mechanisms struggle when positions exceed the training range, leading to unstable or meaningless attention scores. By carefully designing attention masks, these methods can process longer sequences within the original positional embedding constraints. The paper acknowledges that these attention-based efforts and positional interpolation methods are complementary rather than competing. While attention masking addresses the computational and stability aspects of processing long sequences, positional interpolation addresses the fundamental representation problem of how to encode positions beyond the training range.

Fine-tuning based approaches constitute a third category, focusing on how to effectively fine-tune pre-trained LLMs with modified position embeddings for longer context. Methods like Code LLaMA, LLaMA2 Long, and ScaledRoPE adopt a straightforward strategy: choose a very large base value for RoPE and fine-tune on the target length. This approach, while conceptually simple, requires substantial computational investment for each target length. More recent innovations like LongLoRA and PoSE aim to reduce the computational overhead associated with fine-tuning for long context lengths exceeding 128k tokens. The paper notes that LongRoPE is orthogonal to these efficient fine-tuning works, meaning the techniques could potentially be combined for even greater efficiency. LongRoPE's contribution here is its flexibility for various target lengths and its unique capability to achieve extensions beyond 2 million tokens, a range that purely fine-tuning based approaches have not demonstrated.

# Potential Applications of 2048k Context Models

The achievement of extending context windows to 2048k tokens opens numerous application possibilities that were previously impractical or impossible with standard LLMs constrained to 4k or even 128k context windows. The paper identifies several key application domains that stand to benefit from this capability.

In-context learning with numerous examples represents one of the most immediate applications. Standard in-context learning is limited by the context window, restricting the number of examples that can be provided to guide the model's behavior. With a 2048k context window, models can incorporate hundreds or thousands of examples, enabling more comprehensive demonstration of desired behaviors and potentially improving performance on specialized tasks. This is particularly valuable in scenarios where task specifications are complex or where the model needs to understand subtle patterns across many examples.

LLM agents represent another transformative application domain. Agents that interact with environments, execute multi-step reasoning, and maintain state over extended interactions generate substantial contextual history. A 2048k context window allows agents to maintain coherent understanding over much longer interaction histories, enabling more sophisticated planning, better context awareness, and more consistent behavior across extended task executions. The ability to process million-token contexts means agents can work with extensive documentation, maintain long-term memory of interactions, and synthesize information from diverse sources without the fragmentation that limited context windows impose.

Long document summarization emerges as a natural application given the context length capabilities. Traditional summarization with limited context windows requires chunking strategies that may lose coherence across document sections or miss global themes. With 2048k context, entire books, extensive research corpora, or lengthy legal documents can be processed holistically, enabling summaries that capture the full scope and interconnected themes of the source material. # Limitations and Areas for Future Research

While LongRoPE achieves remarkable results, the paper acknowledges several limitations and identifies directions for future investigation. Understanding these limitations provides important context for interpreting the results and guides future research efforts.

The ablation study reveals an interesting finding regarding the token position non-uniformity. While non-uniformity in token position—specifically, preserving the initial tokens without interpolation—clearly improves performance at 16k and 32k lengths, this effect diminishes at the 2048k scale. The paper hypothesizes that at extremely long lengths, preserving only the initial tokens without interpolation becomes non-useful. This observation suggests that the benefits of token position non-uniformity may be scale-dependent, and the optimal strategy for 2048k contexts may differ from that for shorter extensions. The paper explicitly notes this as an area for future work, leaving open the question of whether alternative token position strategies might prove beneficial at million-token scales.

The performance differences observed between LLaMA2 and Mistral at the 2048k scale warrant further investigation. Mistral demonstrates strong performance at shorter lengths but shows perplexity exceeding 7 beyond 256k tokens. The paper attributes this to the fine-tuning procedure: for Mistral's 128k and 256k fine-tuning, the experiments followed YaRN's settings using a 16k training length, which affected Mistral's ability to further extend the context window after fine-tuning. This observation suggests that the training procedure and the relationship between fine-tuning length and target extension length deserve careful consideration, and optimal strategies may differ across model architectures.

The paper's experimental design, while comprehensive, leaves certain questions unanswered. The search algorithm, though effective, requires computational investment proportional to the target context length due to the time-consuming nature of perplexity calculations. Future work might explore more efficient search strategies or analytical approaches to determining optimal rescale factors. Additionally, the current approach determines rescale factors based on perplexity minimization, and alternative optimization objectives might yield different trade-offs worth exploring.

The broader question of whether the 2048k context window represents genuine long-context understanding versus clever manipulation of positional embeddings remains an open research question. While the perplexity and passkey retrieval results are encouraging, more sophisticated evaluation benchmarks specifically designed for million-token contexts would provide deeper insight into the models' capabilities and limitations.
