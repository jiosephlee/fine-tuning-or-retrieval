\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 1: # Introduction: The Challenge of Controlling Diffusion Models

# The Rise of Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

The field of generative artificial intelligence has witnessed a remarkable transformation in recent years, largely catalyzed by the emergence and rapid advancement of text-to-image diffusion models. These models have demonstrated an unprecedented ability to synthesize photorealistic images from textual descriptions, bridging the gap between human language and visual creativity in ways previously thought impossible. The journey toward this achievement has been enabled by two parallel streams of innovation: the development of diffusion-based generative models and the advancement of vision-language representation learning.

Diffusion models operate on a fundamentally different principle than earlier generative approaches. Rather than attempting to generate images in a single step, these models learn to gradually denoise a noisy image over multiple time steps, eventually arriving at a coherent and detailed output. This denoising process has proven to be remarkably effective for high-fidelity image synthesis. The theoretical foundations were laid by research in denoising diffusion probabilistic models and score-based generative models, which established the mathematical framework for training models that can reverse a noise injection process.

Several landmark models have demonstrated the power of this approach. GLIDE and Imagen represent early efforts that train diffusion models directly in pixel space. GLIDE jointly trains the text encoder alongside a diffusion prior using paired text-image data, creating an integrated system for text-guided generation. Imagen, in contrast, leverages a frozen pretrained text encoder, demonstrating that powerful pretrained language models can provide sufficient semantic understanding without additional training. Stable Diffusion and DALL-E2 shifted the paradigm by training diffusion models in latent space rather than pixel space. Stable Diffusion employs VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook as its latent space, dramatically reducing computational requirements while maintaining generation quality. DALL-E2 adopts CLIP to construct a joint latent embedding space that can represent both images and text in a shared semantic framework.

The capabilities of these models extend far beyond simple image generation. They can render complex scenes, understand abstract concepts, and produce images that faithfully interpret nuanced textual prompts. However, despite these impressive achievements, text guidance alone can be ambiguous and insufficient for providing fine-grained and accurate control over the generated images. A user might want to generate an image of a specific subject—say, their pet dog—in various novel contexts, or they might want to guide the generation process with additional structural constraints like edge maps or segmentation masks. These requirements expose a fundamental limitation: while pretrained diffusion models are remarkably versatile, guiding them toward specific downstream tasks without degrading their core generative capabilities remains a significant challenge.

# Downstream Tasks: Subject-Driven Generation vs. Controllable Generation

The paper under consideration addresses two specific downstream tasks that exemplify the challenge of controlling diffusion models: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. These tasks represent distinct but related problems in the broader landscape of image generation, and understanding their nuances is essential for appreciating the motivation behind the proposed approach.

Subject-driven generation addresses a scenario that is intuitive yet technically challenging: given just a few images of a specific subject, the task is to generate new images of that same subject in different contexts using text prompts as guidance. Consider a user who wants to generate images of their personal belongings, pets, or even themselves in various imaginative scenarios. The model must somehow learn to recognize and preserve the defining visual characteristics of the subject—its identity, texture, color patterns, and distinctive features—while being flexible enough to place that subject in novel contexts dictated by the text prompt. This task is sometimes referred to as personalization, as it effectively personalizes a general-purpose model to recognize and generate specific subjects.

The challenge here is multifold. First, the model must extract meaningful representations of the subject from very few images—often as few as three to five examples. Second, it must integrate these representations into its existing knowledge base without overwriting or degrading its ability to generate diverse and high-quality images. Third, it must maintain enough flexibility to combine the subject identity with arbitrary text prompts that may specify novel poses, backgrounds, styles, or interactions. The DreamBooth approach addresses this by finetuning the diffusion model with a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior preservation loss, using a customized token to represent the subject. However, naive finetuning approaches can easily lead to model degradation or insufficient subject preservation.

Controllable generation, the second task, addresses a complementary challenge: enabling the model to take in additional control signals beyond text prompts. These control signals could include Canny edge maps, segmentation maps, depth maps, human pose skeletons, or any other structural guidance that constrains the spatial organization of the generated image. The goal is to generate images that simultaneously satisfy the textual description and align with the provided control signal. For instance, a user might want to generate an image of "a person sitting on a bench" while also providing a specific pose skeleton that dictates exactly how the person should be positioned.

This task is fundamentally about expanding the input modality of the model. While the pretrained model expects text as its primary conditioning signal, controllable generation requires the model to understand and respect additional structural constraints. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter represent recent approaches to this problem, finetuning pretrained diffusion models to adapt to additional control signals. These methods have achieved impressive results, but they often require substantial finetuning resources and may introduce computational overhead during inference.

Both tasks essentially boil down to a common question: how can we effectively finetune text-to-image diffusion models for downstream tasks without sacrificing the remarkable generative capabilities acquired during pretraining? The answer to this question requires a principled understanding of what makes diffusion models work and what aspects of their structure must be preserved during finetuning.

# Desiderata for Finetuning: Efficiency and Generalizability Preservation

When designing finetuning strategies for large pretrained models, researchers must navigate a delicate balance between adaptation and preservation. The paper articulates two primary desiderata that guide the development of effective finetuning methods: training efficiency and generalizability preservation. Understanding these criteria provides a framework for evaluating existing methods and appreciating the innovations proposed in the paper.

Training efficiency encompasses both the number of trainable parameters required for adaptation and the number of training epochs needed to achieve satisfactory performance. In the context of large diffusion models, which may contain billions of parameters, the number of trainable parameters directly impacts memory requirements and computational costs. Methods that require finetuning the entire model demand substantial hardware resources, potentially placing them out of reach for many researchers and practitioners. Moreover, the number of training epochs affects both the time required for adaptation and the practical usability of the method. Methods that converge slowly require more iterations, increasing both computational costs and the time needed to adapt to new tasks.

Beyond computational considerations, training efficiency also has implications for data efficiency. Methods that can adapt with fewer training images are more practical for real-world applications where collecting extensive datasets for every new subject or control task would be prohibitive. The paper emphasizes that an ideal finetuning method should achieve strong performance with minimal data requirements.

Generalizability preservation, the second criterion, is arguably the more subtle and challenging requirement. A pretrained diffusion model has acquired extensive knowledge about the visual world—the appearance of objects, the physics of light, the composition of scenes, and the semantics of visual concepts. This knowledge enables the model to generate diverse, high-fidelity images across an enormous range of prompts. The challenge of finetuning is to adapt the model for a specific downstream task while preserving this broad generative capability.

Poor generalizability preservation manifests in several ways. The finetuned model might exhibit mode collapse, losing the ability to generate diverse outputs and instead producing repetitive or limited variations. It might suffer from quality degradation, generating images that are noticeably less photorealistic or coherent than those produced by the original model. It might lose semantic understanding, failing to properly interpret text prompts or generate appropriate compositions. Any of these failures would undermine the practical utility of the finetuned model, as it would no longer serve as a reliable tool for image generation.

The tension between adaptation and preservation is fundamental. Adapting too aggressively risks destroying the pretrained knowledge, while being too conservative may fail to achieve the desired level of control for the downstream task. Existing finetuning methods attempt to navigate this tension, but the paper argues that they lack a principled foundation for understanding what should be preserved and how to ensure preservation.

# Limitations of Existing Methods: The Euclidean Distance Fallacy

Existing finetuning methods for diffusion models have largely operated under an implicit assumption: a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned model weights and the pretrained model weights indicates better preservation of the pretrained model's capabilities. This assumption, while intuitively appealing, has significant limitations that the paper critiques and seeks to address.

The standard finetuning approach updates neuron weights through gradient descent with a small learning rate. The small learning rate serves an implicit regularizing function, encouraging the finetuned weights to remain close to their pretrained values in Euclidean space. Similarly, methods like LoRA introduce low-rank constraints on the weight updates, restricting the space of possible finetuned models in a way that inherently limits deviation from the pretrained model. These approaches are grounded in the intuition that large changes to model weights are likely to disrupt the carefully learned representations from pretraining.

However, the paper argues that the Euclidean difference to the pretrained model alone is unable to fully capture the degree of semantic preservation. This is a profound observation that shifts the focus from weight-level metrics to structural properties of the network. Consider an analogy: two paintings might use nearly identical colors and brushstrokes while conveying completely different subjects, while two other paintings might differ significantly in their low-level details while depicting the same scene. The Euclidean distance between pixel values would not capture these semantic relationships.

The paper proposes that a more structural measure to characterize the difference between the finetuned model and the pretrained model can greatly benefit the preservation of pretraining performance as well as finetuning stability. The key insight is that the semantic information encoded in neural networks is not simply about the individual weight values, but rather about the relational structure among neurons—specifically, the angles between neurons.

This perspective draws inspiration from prior research showing that hyperspherical similarity encodes semantic information effectively. The angles between neurons in a layer—their relative orientations in the weight space—may be crucial for representing the knowledge stored in the network. When finetuning disrupts these angular relationships, even while keeping Euclidean distance small, the semantic structure of the model can be damaged. This explains why existing methods that focus solely on Euclidean constraints may still fail to preserve the pretrained model's generative capabilities.

The paper introduces the concept of hyperspherical energy as a formal measure for quantifying this angular structure. Hyperspherical energy is defined as the sum of hyperspherical similarity between all pairwise neurons in the same layer, capturing the level of neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, the hyperspherical energy is formally defined as:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the $i$-th normalized neuron. This measure characterizes the pairwise relational structure among neurons, providing a structural quantification of the network's organization that goes beyond simple Euclidean distance.

# Introduction to Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): A Principled Approach

Motivated by the limitations of existing methods and the insight that angular structure encodes semantic information, the paper introduces Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) as a principled approach to finetuning diffusion models. The central idea behind OFT is elegantly simple yet theoretically grounded: learn a layer-shared orthogonal transformation for neurons such that their pairwise angles are preserved during finetuning.

The theoretical foundation for OFT rests on a key invariance property of hyperspherical energy: the pairwise hyperspherical similarity is provably preserved if the same orthogonal transformation is applied to all neurons. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. When such a transformation is applied to the neurons in a layer, it rotates or reflects all neurons by the same amount, preserving the angles between any pair of neurons.

This property leads to a natural formulation for finetuning. Rather than freely adjusting the weights, OFT constrains the finetuned weights to be an orthogonal transformation of the pretrained weights:

$$\bm{W}=\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0$$

where $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weights and $\bm{R}$ is a learnable orthogonal matrix. This constraint guarantees that:

$$\left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\| = 0$$

In other words, the hyperspherical energy of the finetuned model exactly matches that of the pretrained model. This is the key theoretical guarantee that distinguishes OFT from existing methods: it provably preserves the angular structure that encodes semantic information.

The intuition for why orthogonal transformation works for finetuning neurons draws an illuminating parallel to signal processing. In the 2D Fourier transform, an image can be decomposed into magnitude and phase spectra. Remarkably, the phase spectrum—which encodes angular information between the input and basis—preserves the major part of semantics. A phase spectrum combined with a random magnitude spectrum can still reconstruct the original image without losing its semantic content. This phenomenon suggests that angular relationships are fundamental to representing visual semantics.

By analogy, changing neuron directions is key to semantically modifying generated images, which is precisely the goal of both subject-driven and controllable generation. However, changing neuron directions with unconstrained freedom risks destroying the pretrained generative performance. OFT strikes a balance by preserving the angles between any pair of neurons, allowing semantic modification while maintaining the structural integrity of the pretrained knowledge.

OFT can also be interpreted as adjusting the canonical coordinate system for neurons in the same layer. Rather than modifying neurons individually and potentially disrupting their relationships, OFT learns a global transformation that applies uniformly across all neurons. This global approach provides sufficient flexibility for adaptation while enforcing a structural constraint that preserves semantic organization.

To further improve stability, the paper proposes Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which adds an additional constraint limiting the deviation of the orthogonal matrix from the identity matrix. Formally, COFT imposes:

$$\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$$

This constraint ensures that the finetuned model remains within a small neighborhood of the pretrained model, combining the hyperspherical energy preservation of OFT with an explicit bound on overall deviation. COFT can be viewed as combining two constraints: minimal hyperspherical energy difference and constrained deviation from the pretrained model, making explicit what existing methods leave implicit.

A practical advantage of OFT and COFT is that they introduce no additional inference overhead. Once training is complete, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied directly into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This means the finetuned model operates exactly like the original model during inference, with identical computational requirements and latency.
