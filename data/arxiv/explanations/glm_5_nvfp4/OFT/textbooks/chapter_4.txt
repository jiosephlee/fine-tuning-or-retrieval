\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 4: # Enhancing Stability: Constrained and Re-scaled OFT

# The Need for Stability: Balancing Flexibility and Regularity

The challenge of finetuning large pretrained models lies in navigating a fundamental tension between adaptability and preservation. When we finetune a text-to-image diffusion model for a downstream task, we want the model to learn new capabilities—such as generating images of a specific subject or responding to new control signals—while simultaneously retaining the rich semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining. This tension manifests as a trade-off between flexibility and regularity, and understanding this trade-off is essential for designing effective finetuning strategies.

Standard finetuning approaches, which directly update model weights using gradient descent with a small learning rate, represent the most flexible end of the spectrum. These methods place minimal constraints on how the model can change, allowing the weights to move freely in parameter space. However, this freedom comes at a significant cost. Without proper constraints, standard finetuning often exhibits poor stability and can easily cause model collapse, where the model loses its generative capabilities entirely. The phenomenon of model collapse is particularly pronounced in text-to-image diffusion models, where excessive finetuning can destroy the semantic structures that enable coherent image generation.

The paper identifies a key conceptual gap in existing approaches: the assumption that a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned model and the pretrained model indicates better preservation of pretrained abilities. While this assumption may occasionally hold, the Euclidean difference alone is unable to fully capture the degree of semantic preservation. A more structural measure is needed to characterize the difference between the finetuned and pretrained models. This observation motivates the development of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, and subsequently, the constrained and re-scaled variants that further refine this balance.

The block-diagonal parameterization used in OFT can itself be viewed as a form of regularization. By structuring the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal matrix with $r$ blocks, the method constrains the space of allowable transformations. Each block operates on a subset of neuron dimensions, and this structural constraint limits the complexity of the transformation while still preserving the essential property of maintaining hyperspherical energy. The choice of block size represents a knob that practitioners can adjust: fewer blocks (closer to $r=1$) provide more flexibility, while more blocks impose stronger regularization.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): The Radius Constraint

While OFT successfully preserves the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model through orthogonal transformations, there remains room for improvement in terms of stability. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) builds upon OFT by introducing an additional constraint that limits how far the finetuned model can deviate from the pretrained model. This constraint takes the form of a radius constraint on the hypersphere, ensuring that the finetuned weights remain within a controlled neighborhood of the pretrained weights.

The mathematical formulation of COFT modifies the forward pass of a fully connected layer as follows. Given a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ and input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the output $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is computed as:

$$\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$$

subject to two constraints. The first constraint is the orthogonality condition from standard OFT: $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$. The second, novel constraint is the deviation constraint: $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This $\epsilon$-deviation constraint ensures that the orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ cannot stray too far from the identity matrix, which in turn ensures that the finetuned weights $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ remain close to the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$.

COFT can be understood as combining two explicit constraints. The first constraint, inherited from OFT, ensures minimal hyperspherical energy difference by preserving the pairwise angles between neurons. The second constraint explicitly limits the deviation from the pretrained model. While existing finetuning methods often implicitly encourage small deviations through techniques like small learning rates, COFT makes this constraint explicit and enforceable. This explicit formulation provides practitioners with direct control over the stability-flexibility trade-off through the hyperparameter $\epsilon$.

The radius constraint has an intuitive geometric interpretation. When $\epsilon$ is large, the constraint becomes loose, and COFT behaves similarly to standard OFT, allowing the orthogonal transformation to make substantial changes to the neuron directions. When $\epsilon$ is small, the constraint becomes tight, forcing $\bm{R}$ to remain close to the identity matrix, and thus the finetuned model behaves increasingly similarly to the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model. The paper demonstrates that COFT yields even better finetuning stability than OFT due to this explicit deviation constraint.

# Implementing the Constraint: Projected Gradient Descent via Neumann Series

The practical implementation of COFT requires addressing a technical challenge: how to incorporate the $\epsilon$-deviation constraint into the Cayley-parameterized orthogonal matrix. Recall that the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is constructed using Cayley parameterization as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. Directly enforcing the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ on this parameterization is nontrivial due to the matrix inverse involved in the Cayley transform.

The solution comes from analyzing the behavior of the Cayley transform through the lens of the Neumann series. Under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm, we can approximate the orthogonal matrix as:

$$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$$

This approximation reveals that when $\bm{Q}$ is small, the deviation $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is approximately proportional to $\bm{Q}$. Therefore, the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ can be translated into an equivalent constraint on $\bm{Q}$. Specifically, we can move the constraint inside the Cayley transform and enforce $\|\bm{Q} - \bm{0}\| \leq \epsilon'$, where $\bm{0}$ denotes an all-zero matrix and $\epsilon'$ is a hyperparameter related to $\epsilon$.

The constraint on $\bm{Q}$ is straightforward to enforce using projected gradient descent. At each optimization step, after computing the gradient update for $\bm{Q}$, we project $\bm{Q}$ back onto the feasible set defined by $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$. This projection ensures that $\bm{Q}$ never exceeds the specified bound, which in turn ensures that $\bm{R}$ never deviates too far from the identity matrix.

The initialization of $\bm{Q}$ plays an important role in ensuring that COFT starts from the pretrained model. To achieve identity initialization for the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, we initialize $\bm{Q}$ as an all-zero matrix. When $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$, the Cayley transform yields $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, which corresponds to no transformation of the pretrained weights. This initialization strategy mirrors the zero initialization used in methods like LoRA, ensuring that the finetuning process begins exactly at the pretrained model and gradually adapts from there.

# Re-scaled OFT: Learning Magnitude Scaling Coefficients

While OFT and COFT focus exclusively on modifying the directions of neurons through orthogonal transformations, there is an opportunity to enhance flexibility by also adjusting the magnitudes. This motivation leads to the development of Re-scaled OFT, a simple yet effective extension that learns magnitude scaling coefficients alongside the orthogonal transformation.

The key insight behind Re-scaled OFT is that re-scaling neurons—multiplying them by positive scalar values—does not change the hyperspherical energy. This is because hyperspherical energy is computed using normalized neurons: $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. When we multiply a neuron by any positive scalar, the normalization cancels out this scaling, leaving the normalized neuron unchanged. Therefore, we can modify neuron magnitudes without affecting the pairwise angles that OFT was designed to preserve.

The mathematical formulation of Re-scaled OFT introduces a learnable diagonal matrix $\bm{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ defined as $\bm{D} = \text{diag}(s_1, \cdots, s_n)$, where $s_1, \cdots, s_n$ are positive learnable parameters. The forward pass becomes:

$$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$$

where $\bm{R}$ is the orthogonal matrix and $\bm{W}^0$ is the pretrained weight matrix. In contrast to the original OFT forward pass where only $\bm{R}$ is learnable, Re-scaled OFT has both $\bm{D}$ and $\bm{R}$ as learnable parameters.

The diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ effectively learns a separate scaling coefficient for each neuron. Each diagonal element $s_i$ controls the magnitude of the $i$-th neuron in the output. Since the diagonal matrix adds only $n$ additional parameters (one per neuron), the increase in parameter count is negligible compared to the parameters needed for the orthogonal matrix. This makes Re-scaled OFT a parameter-efficient enhancement that provides additional flexibility without significant computational overhead.

The paper notes that while the experiments primarily use the original OFT to demonstrate the effectiveness of orthogonal transformation alone, Re-scaled OFT is generally better in practice. The added flexibility of learning magnitude scaling allows the model to make finer adjustments to the neuron activations, potentially capturing nuances that directional transformations alone might miss.

# Analyzing the Trade-off between Flexibility and Regularity

The development of OFT, COFT, and Re-scaled OFT reflects a systematic exploration of the trade-off between flexibility and regularity in finetuning. Understanding this trade-off provides valuable insights for practitioners who must choose the appropriate method for their specific application.

At one extreme, standard finetuning offers maximum flexibility by allowing unconstrained updates to all model weights. This flexibility, however, comes at the cost of poor stability. Without constraints, the optimization process can wander into regions of parameter space where the model loses its generative capabilities. The phenomenon of model collapse—where the finetuned model produces nonsensical or degraded outputs—is a direct consequence of excessive flexibility without adequate regularity.

OFT occupies a balanced position in this trade-off. By constraining weight updates to be orthogonal transformations, OFT ensures that the pairwise angles between neurons remain unchanged. This constraint preserves the hyperspherical energy, which the paper identifies as crucial for maintaining the semantic generation ability of the pretrained model. The orthogonality constraint provides sufficient regularity to prevent model collapse while remaining flexible enough to adapt to downstream tasks. The paper demonstrates that OFT converges faster than alternatives, which can be attributed to both the effectiveness of directional updates for modifying semantics and the smooth optimization landscape induced by finetuning on a hypersphere manifold.

COFT shifts the balance further toward regularity by introducing the $\epsilon$-deviation constraint. The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ provides direct control over the trade-off: larger values approach the flexibility of standard OFT, while smaller values enforce stronger adherence to the pretrained model. This explicit control simplifies hyperparameter tuning, as practitioners can set a relatively large iteration number and appropriate $\epsilon$ without worrying about model collapse. The paper shows that COFT achieves even better stability than OFT, making it particularly suitable for applications where robustness is paramount.

Re-scaled OFT represents a move toward greater flexibility while maintaining the benefits of orthogonal transformation. By allowing magnitude scaling through the diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$, Re-scaled OFT expands the expressiveness of the transformation without sacrificing the preservation of hyperspherical energy. The magnitude of each neuron becomes a learnable parameter, providing additional degrees of freedom that can capture task-specific variations. This extension demonstrates that the framework can be enhanced in complementary ways that respect the underlying principles.

The block-diagonal structure of the orthogonal matrix introduces another dimension to this trade-off. With fewer blocks (smaller $r$), each block has larger dimensions and can represent more complex transformations, increasing flexibility. With more blocks (larger $r$), each block becomes smaller and more constrained, increasing regularity. The extreme case of sharing all blocks imposes the strongest regularization, transforming all channels with the same orthogonal matrix. Practitioners can adjust $r$ based on the complexity of the downstream task and the desired balance between adaptability and stability.
