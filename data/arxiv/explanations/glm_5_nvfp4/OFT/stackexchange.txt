\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is hyperspherical energy and why does it matter for finetuning?
Question:
I'm reading the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) paper, and I'm struggling to understand what hyperspherical energy actually represents. The paper defines it as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ are normalized neurons. 

I get that it involves pairwise distances between neurons on a unit hypersphere, but what does this quantity actually tell us about the neural network? Why should I care about preserving it during finetuning? The paper mentions it 'characterizes the pairwise relational structure among neurons' but I'm not clear on what that means intuitively.
Answer:
**What Hyperspherical Energy Measures**

Hyperspherical energy quantifies how "uniformly spread out" neurons are on a unit hypersphere. After normalizing each neuron to unit length, all neurons lie on the surface of a hypersphere in weight space. The energy term $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ is large when two neurons are close together (small distance $\rightarrow$ large inverse), and small when they're far apart. Summing over all pairs gives you a measure of neuron "clustering" — high energy means neurons are bunched together (potentially redundant), while low energy means they are more uniformly distributed.

Think of it like measuring electron repulsion in physics: particles naturally want to spread out to minimize potential energy. Similarly, neurons with low hyperspherical energy are well-distributed across the semantic space.

**Why This Matters for Finetuning**

The key insight from the paper is that *angular relationships between neurons encode semantic information*. The authors demonstrate this with a compelling autoencoder experiment: when they discard magnitude information and keep only angular information (using cosine similarity instead of inner product), the autoencoder can still reconstruct images almost perfectly. Conversely, keeping magnitude while discarding angular information produces meaningless outputs. This shows that the *directions* of neurons — not their magnitudes — primarily store semantic knowledge.

When you finetune a pretrained model, you want to adapt it to new tasks *without destroying* the rich semantic knowledge captured during pretraining. Standard finetuning methods (like DreamBooth) implicitly assume that keeping the Euclidean distance small between finetuned and pretrained weights preserves this knowledge. But the paper argues this is insufficient — the *structural relationships* between neurons matter more.

Hyperspherical energy captures these structural relationships. If finetuning drastically changes this energy, it means the relative positions of neurons have shifted significantly, potentially destroying the pretrained semantic structure. By preserving hyperspherical energy, you constrain finetuning to maintain the essential "knowledge geometry" of the pretrained model.

**Why Orthogonal Transformation Preserves It**

Here's the elegant mathematical property the paper exploits: applying the *same* orthogonal transformation (rotation/reflection) to all neurons preserves all pairwise angles. Since hyperspherical energy only depends on angles between normalized neurons, it remains invariant under orthogonal transformation. This is why OFT parameterizes the weight update as $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal — it gives you flexibility to adapt the model while *provably* preserving hyperspherical energy.

Notably, the paper emphasizes *preserving* (not minimizing) hyperspherical energy. In classification tasks, minimizing this energy to achieve uniform neuron distribution is beneficial. But for finetuning diffusion models, the pretrained energy level encodes valuable semantic structure that shouldn't be destroyed — it should be maintained.

### Why does an orthogonal transformation preserve hyperspherical energy?
Question:
In the OFT paper, the authors claim that applying the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons preserves hyperspherical energy. Specifically, they say that if $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix, then the hyperspherical energy difference is minimized to zero.

I understand that orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and angles, but I'm confused about why this guarantees that $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$. Can someone explain this mathematically? Is there a proof or intuition for why the sum of inverse pairwise distances remains unchanged?
Answer:
The key insight is that orthogonal transformations are **isometries**—they preserve all distances and angles in the vector space. Let me walk through the mathematics step by step.

Given the hyperspherical energy definition from the paper:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ is the normalized neuron on the unit hypersphere.

**Proof of preservation:**

If $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}$ orthogonal, then for each neuron:
$$\bm{w}_i = \bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i$$

For the normalized version:
$$\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\|}$$

Since orthogonal matrices preserve norms ($\|\bm{R}\bm{v}\| = \|\bm{v}\|$ for any vector $\bm{v}$), we have:
$$\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\|} = \bm{R}\frac{\bm{w}^0_i}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\|} = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i$$

Now examine the pairwise distances in the energy computation:
$$\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\| = \|\bm{R}(\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j)\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|$$

The last equality holds because orthogonal transformations preserve distances. Therefore:
$$\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1} = \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|^{-1}$$

Summing over all pairs gives $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$.

**Intuition:** Think of the neurons as points on a unit sphere. Hyperspherical energy depends only on the angular relationships (pairwise distances on the sphere surface). An orthogonal transformation rotates or reflects the entire sphere as a rigid body—all points move together, so their relative positions and angles remain unchanged. It's like rotating a globe: the distances between cities on its surface stay the same regardless of how you spin it.

### How does Cayley parameterization enforce orthogonality?
Question:
The OFT paper mentions using Cayley parameterization to generate orthogonal matrices efficiently, with the formula $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix.

I have two questions about this:

1. Why does this formula produce an orthogonal matrix? Is there a derivation or proof?
2. The paper mentions this only produces matrices with determinant 1 (special orthogonal group). Why does this limitation not affect performance in practice? Wouldn't we sometimes need reflections (determinant -1)?
Answer:
The Cayley parameterization is a classic tool from linear algebra that provides an elegant mapping between skew-symmetric matrices and orthogonal matrices. Let me address both of your questions.

## Why does the Cayley transform produce an orthogonal matrix?

The key insight is that skew-symmetric matrices ($\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$) and orthogonal matrices are intimately connected through this transform. Here's a direct proof:

Starting with $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, we need to verify that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$.

First, compute the transpose using $(\bm{AB})^\top = \bm{B}^\top\bm{A}^\top$ and $(\bm{A}^{-1})^\top = (\bm{A}^\top)^{-1}$:

$$\bm{R}^\top = [(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}]^\top(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^\top = (\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^\top)^{-1}(\bm{I} + \bm{Q}^\top)$$

Since $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$), this simplifies to:

$$\bm{R}^\top = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$$

Now multiplying by $\bm{R}$:

$$\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$$

The crucial observation is that $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}) = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2$ (they commute). Therefore:

$$\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2)(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$$

Substituting $\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2 = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$:

$$\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I}$$

## Why only determinant +1, and why doesn't it matter?

The Cayley transform can only reach the **special orthogonal group** $SO(n)$ — orthogonal matrices with $\det(\bm{R}) = +1$. This is because:

1. The Cayley transform is continuous and $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ maps to $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$
2. $SO(n)$ is the connected component containing the identity
3. Matrices with $\det = -1$ (reflections) form a disconnected component that cannot be reached through continuous paths from $\bm{I}$

This limitation doesn't affect performance for two key reasons. First, OFT initializes $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ (equivalently $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$), and gradient-based optimization naturally stays within the connected component containing the starting point — having access to reflections would be irrelevant since they're unreachable from the identity via gradient descent anyway.

Second, the goal is to make small perturbations to pretrained weights while preserving hyperspherical energy. Rotations near the identity provide sufficient flexibility for this, and as the paper demonstrates empirically, the special orthogonal group is expressive enough to achieve strong finetuning performance without needing reflections.

### Why does the autoencoder experiment show that angular information preserves semantics?
Question:
In Section 3.1 of the OFT paper, the authors describe a toy experiment with a convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images. They compare three ways to generate feature maps: (a) inner product, (b) magnitude only, and (c) angular information only.

The paper claims this shows 'angular information of neurons can almost perfectly recover the input images, while the magnitude of neurons contains no useful information.' But I'm confused - how can angular information alone reconstruct images if the network was trained with standard inner products? Isn't the magnitude part of what the network learned during training?
Answer:
The key to understanding this experiment is recognizing that it reveals where the learned semantic information is stored, not how it was learned during training.

During training, the autoencoder uses standard inner products: $z = \bm{w}^\top \bm{x} = \|\bm{w}\| \|\bm{x}\| \cos(\theta)$. The network learns weights $\bm{w}$ through backpropagation, and these weights naturally have both magnitude $\|\bm{w}\|$ and direction $\hat{\bm{w}} = \bm{w}/\|\bm{w}\|$. At test time, the experiment decomposes this inner product into two components: the magnitude component $\|\bm{w}\| \|\bm{x}\|$ and the angular component $\cos(\theta) = \frac{\bm{w}^\top \bm{x}}{\|\bm{w}\| \|\bm{x}\|}$.

The surprising finding is that the angular component alone can almost perfectly reconstruct the images, while the magnitude component contains essentially no useful information. This demonstrates that during training, the network encodes semantic information primarily in the *directions* of the neurons (their angular relationships with inputs), not in their magnitudes. The magnitudes essentially act as global scaling factors that don't carry semantic content.

This phenomenon parallels the 2D Fourier transform analogy mentioned in the paper: when you decompose an image into magnitude and phase spectra, the phase spectrum (which is angular information) preserves most of the semantic structure. You can reconstruct a recognizable image from the phase spectrum combined with a *random* magnitude spectrum, but not vice versa.

So the experiment doesn't contradict that the network learned with full inner products during training—it simply reveals that the learned weights store their semantic knowledge primarily in their angular structure rather than their magnitudes. This insight motivates OFT's core principle: preserving the pairwise angles between neurons (via orthogonal transformations) is sufficient to maintain semantic generation capability while allowing flexible finetuning.

### What is the relationship between OFT and LoRA?
Question:
The OFT paper compares itself to LoRA throughout, and mentions that 'OFT and LoRA represent two distinct ways to be parameter-efficient.' I understand that LoRA uses a low-rank additive update $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{B}\bm{A}$ while OFT uses a multiplicative orthogonal transformation $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$.

However, the paper also says 'Since $\bm{W}^0$ is typically full-rank, OFT also performs low-rank weight update when $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is low-rank.' I find this confusing. If OFT applies a full-rank orthogonal transformation, how can the update be low-rank? What exactly is the connection between these two approaches?
Answer:
The relationship between OFT and LoRA centers on how both methods achieve parameter-efficient updates, but through fundamentally different structural constraints. Let me clarify the apparent contradiction you've identified.

## The Key Distinction: $\bm{R}$ vs. $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$

The confusion arises from mixing up two different matrices. In OFT, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ itself is **always full-rank**—this is guaranteed by orthogonality, since $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ implies $\bm{R}$ is invertible.

However, the paper's statement refers to $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$, which is entirely different. To see the connection to LoRA, we can rewrite OFT's forward pass:

$$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$$

This reveals that OFT's effective weight update is $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$. When $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is low-rank and $\bm{W}^0$ is full-rank, the entire update becomes low-rank because $\text{rank}(\bm{A}\bm{B}) \leq \min(\text{rank}(\bm{A}), \text{rank}(\bm{B}))$.

## Why Would $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ Be Low-Rank?

This occurs due to OFT's block-diagonal parameterization. When $\bm{R}$ is structured as:

$$\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots, \bm{R}_r)$$

the deviation $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ inherits this block structure. With many blocks (large $r$), each block $\bm{R}_i - \bm{I}_{d/r}$ is constrained to a smaller subspace. Since OFT initializes $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ and typically learns small deviations (especially with COFT's $\epsilon$-constraint), $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ often has small entries concentrated along the block-diagonal, leading to a low "effective rank."

## Two Paths to Parameter Efficiency

The paper highlights that OFT and LoRA represent philosophically different approaches:

- **LoRA** uses additive updates with explicit low-rank factorization: $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{B}\bm{A}$, reducing parameters through rank constraints
- **OFT** uses multiplicative updates with block-diagonal orthogonal matrices, reducing parameters through sparsity structure

Both can achieve low-rank effective updates, but OFT additionally guarantees preservation of hyperspherical energy (pairwise neuron angles), which LoRA cannot. This structural preservation is what gives OFT its stability advantages during finetuning of diffusion models.

### How does the block-diagonal structure reduce parameters while maintaining orthogonality?
Question:
The OFT paper proposes using a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)$ to reduce the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$.

I understand that a block-diagonal matrix composed of orthogonal blocks is still orthogonal overall. But what does this mean for the transformation being applied? If I have 8 blocks instead of 1, does each block only transform a subset of the neuron dimensions? Does this mean neurons in different blocks cannot interact with each other through the transformation? What are the practical implications of this restriction?
Answer:
You've correctly identified the key trade-off of the block-diagonal structure. Let me clarify what this means both mathematically and practically.

**Yes, each block transforms an independent subset of dimensions.** With $r$ blocks, your $d$-dimensional space is partitioned into $r$ subspaces of dimension $d/r$. The transformation $\bm{R}$ applies a separate orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}_i$ to each subspace independently. Dimensions belonging to different blocks have no interaction—rotation in block 1 cannot affect dimensions in block 2.

**This is indeed a restriction on expressiveness.** A full orthogonal matrix can rotate any direction to any other direction, mixing all dimensions freely. With block-diagonal structure, you can only rotate within each $d/r$-dimensional subspace. The paper acknowledges this limitation in Section 6: "the parameter efficiency of OFT is largely dependent on the block diagonal structure which inevitably introduces additional biases and limits the flexibility."

**Why does this trade-off work well in practice?**

The key insight is that the paper applies OFT to weight matrices $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, transforming them as $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Each row of $\bm{W}^0$ corresponds to one input dimension. When you apply the block-diagonal $\bm{R}$, you're effectively saying: groups of input dimensions can be rotated together, but different groups rotate independently.

The paper notes an interesting interpretation for convolution layers in Section 6: "When we use the same number of blocks as the number of input channels, each block only transforms a unique neuron channel, similar to learning depth-wise convolution kernels."

**The practical significance is that orthogonality is preserved globally despite local structure.** Since each $\bm{R}_i$ satisfies $\bm{R}_i^\top\bm{R}_i = \bm{I}$, the full matrix satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$. This means hyperspherical energy—the pairwise angular relationships between neurons—is still preserved exactly, which is OFT's core theoretical guarantee. What you sacrifice is the ability to rotate neurons from different blocks relative to each other, but the *relative angles within each block* remain invariant.

**The parameter reduction is substantial.** For $d=1024$ with $r=8$ blocks: a full orthogonal matrix needs $\frac{1024 \times 1023}{2} \approx 524,000$ parameters, while the block-diagonal version needs $\frac{1024 \times (128-1)}{2} \approx 65,000$ parameters—an $8\times$ reduction.

### What does the epsilon constraint in COFT actually constrain?
Question:
The paper introduces Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) with an additional constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. They then approximate this using Neumann series to get $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q}$, and constrain $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$.

I'm confused about what norm is being used here. Is it the Frobenius norm? Spectral norm? Also, how does constraining $\bm{Q}$ relate back to the original constraint on $\bm{R}$? The Neumann series approximation seems like it would only be valid for small $\bm{Q}$, so what happens when the constraint is tight?
Answer:
The paper specifies that the Neumann series approximation is valid "in the operator norm," which means **spectral norm** (denoted $\|\cdot\|_2$ or $\|\cdot\|_{op}$) is being used, not Frobenius norm.

The relationship between the two constraints works as follows. The Neumann series expansion gives:
$$(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \bm{Q}^k$$

which converges when $\|\bm{Q}\|_{op} < 1$. Using this, we can derive the exact relationship:

$$\bm{R} - \bm{I} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} - \bm{I} = 2\bm{Q}(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$$

By sub-multiplicativity of the spectral norm:
$$\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\|_{op} \leq 2\|\bm{Q}\|_{op} \cdot \|(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\|_{op} \leq \frac{2\epsilon'}{1-\epsilon'}$$

where we used $\|(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\|_{op} \leq \frac{1}{1-\|\bm{Q}\|_{op}} \leq \frac{1}{1-\epsilon'}$.

So constraining $\|\bm{Q}\|_{op} \leq \epsilon'$ implicitly bounds $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|_{op}$ by approximately $2\epsilon'$ (when $\epsilon'$ is small), since $\frac{2\epsilon'}{1-\epsilon'} \approx 2\epsilon'$.

Regarding your concern about tight constraints: when $\epsilon'$ approaches 1, the Neumann series approximation indeed becomes invalid. This is why in practice, $\epsilon'$ must be kept small (typically $\epsilon' \ll 1$) to ensure both the series converges and the approximation $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q}$ remains accurate. The paper's approach enforces this through projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$, keeping it within a small neighborhood of zero—precisely where the approximation is valid. When $\epsilon'$ is small, COFT essentially constrains $\bm{R}$ to stay near the identity matrix in the spectral norm sense, which ensures the finetuned weights remain close to the pretrained weights.

### Why does preserving hyperspherical energy prevent destroying pretrained knowledge?
Question:
The central claim of the OFT paper is that preserving hyperspherical energy during finetuning prevents the destruction of pretrained knowledge. The authors state 'we hypothesize that a good finetuned model should have a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to the pretrained model.'

But I'm not convinced. If I apply the same rotation to all neurons, I preserve their pairwise angles, but haven't I still dramatically changed what each individual neuron represents? The neurons now point in completely different directions from the original network. How does preserving pairwise relationships alone maintain the semantic knowledge learned during pretraining? Shouldn't the absolute directions matter too?
Answer:
Your concern is thoughtful, but it rests on an assumption that the absolute directions of individual neurons encode pretrained knowledge. The OFT paper makes a more subtle claim: the semantic knowledge is encoded in the **relational structure** between neurons—their pairwise angular relationships—rather than in their absolute orientations.

The key insight is that neural computation in a layer involves inner products: $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x}$. When you apply an orthogonal transformation to the weights, $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, the forward pass becomes:

$$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{R}^\top\bm{x}$$

This reveals that OFT is mathematically equivalent to applying $\bm{R}^\top$ to the **input** before using the original weights. In other words, OFT learns a rigid rotation of the input coordinate system rather than arbitrarily warping individual neurons. The pretrained computation $\bm{W}^0$ is preserved; only the "viewing angle" of the input changes.

The paper supports this with a toy autoencoder experiment (Figure 1) demonstrating that angular information alone can almost perfectly reconstruct images, while magnitude information is largely irrelevant. This suggests that what matters is the angular *relationships* among neurons—the geometry they form on the hypersphere—not their absolute positions.

Your analogy about rotating all neurons is correct in that individual neuron directions change. But consider: if all neurons rotate together, the *shape* they collectively form—the pattern of "which neurons point near each other" and "which point apart"—remains intact. This geometric configuration is what the paper argues encodes the pretrained knowledge.

Additionally, OFT initializes $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ (identity), and COFT further constrains $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$, ensuring the transformation stays close to the pretrained model. This prevents large, unconstrained changes that would destroy learned representations.

In short: preserving hyperspherical energy preserves the *relational structure* among neurons. The absolute coordinate system is somewhat arbitrary—what matters is how neurons relate to each other, and orthogonal transformations preserve exactly those relationships while still allowing meaningful adaptation for downstream tasks.

### Why does OFT converge faster than LoRA or standard finetuning?
Question:
The paper claims that OFT converges significantly faster than LoRA and other baselines, with Figure 3 showing that OFT reaches good performance in 8 epochs while LoRA needs 20. The paper briefly mentions two reasons: (1) changing neuron directions is the most effective way to modify semantics, and (2) 'finetuning neurons on a smooth hypersphere manifold' yields better optimization.

I don't fully understand either explanation. For (1), why would changing directions be faster than changing magnitudes? For (2), what makes the optimization landscape 'smoother' when constrained to orthogonal transformations? Is there a theoretical justification or is this just empirical observation?
Answer:
The faster convergence of OFT comes from two complementary aspects that the paper explains both theoretically and empirically.

**Why changing directions is more effective than changing magnitudes:**

The paper provides a compelling toy experiment (Figure 2) that directly addresses this. They train a convolutional autoencoder on flower images using standard inner products, then at test time compare three ways to generate features: (a) the original inner product, (b) magnitude-only information, and (c) angular-only information. The results show that angular information alone can almost perfectly reconstruct the input images, while magnitude contains essentially no useful semantic information.

This connects to a well-known phenomenon in signal processing: in 2D Fourier transforms, the phase spectrum (angular information) preserves most of the semantic content. An image's phase spectrum combined with a *random* magnitude spectrum can still reconstruct the original image semantically. Since neural network neurons store information through their angles relative to input activations, modifying these directions is the most direct path to changing semantics—which is exactly what OFT is designed to do.

**Why the optimization landscape is smoother:**

OFT constrains weight updates to orthogonal transformations, which can be interpreted as optimizing on the manifold of the orthogonal group $\mathrm{O}(d)$. This manifold has favorable geometric properties: it is a smooth, compact Riemannian manifold without the sharp local minima that plague unconstrained weight spaces. When neurons are normalized to the unit hypersphere, their relationships are governed by pairwise angles, and orthogonal transformations preserve these relationships exactly (the hyperspherical energy remains constant).

The paper cites empirical verification from prior work (Liu et al., 2021) showing that orthogonal parameterization yields better optimization dynamics. Crucially, Figure 3 in the paper demonstrates that removing the orthogonality constraint causes the method to completely fail—no realistic images are generated and control is lost. This confirms that the constraint itself, not just the parameterization, is essential for stable and fast convergence.

In essence, OFT converges faster because it operates directly on the most semantically meaningful parameter space (neuron directions) while the orthogonal constraint ensures a well-behaved optimization landscape that avoids the instabilities and collapse modes that plague unconstrained finetuning methods.

### In re-scaled OFT, why is the diagonal matrix multiplied on the right?
Question:
In Section 3.5, the paper introduces re-scaled OFT with the forward pass $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$ where $\bm{D}$ is a learnable diagonal matrix for scaling each neuron.

I notice there's an errata note saying the original paper had a typo with $\bm{D}$ on the left. But I'm confused about why the correct placement is $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$ rather than $\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Since $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ where each column is a neuron, shouldn't scaling each neuron involve multiplying from the left? What does multiplying $\bm{D}$ from the right actually do to the weight matrix?
Answer:
The placement of $\bm{D}$ on the right is indeed correct, and the confusion stems from how matrix multiplication interacts with column-wise operations.

Let's think through this carefully. In the paper's notation, $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is organized such that **each column represents a neuron**. That is, if we write $\bm{W}^0 = [\bm{w}_1^0, \bm{w}_2^0, \ldots, \bm{w}_n^0]$, then $\bm{w}_i^0 \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the $i$-th neuron.

Now, here's the key insight about diagonal matrix multiplication: when you multiply a matrix by a diagonal matrix **on the right**, each **column** gets scaled by the corresponding diagonal element. Specifically, with $\bm{D} = \text{diag}(s_1, \ldots, s_n)$:

$$\bm{W}^0\bm{D} = [s_1\bm{w}_1^0, s_2\bm{w}_2^0, \ldots, s_n\bm{w}_n^0]$$

Each neuron (column) $\bm{w}_i^0$ is independently scaled by its own coefficient $s_i$.

Conversely, if you multiplied by a diagonal matrix **on the left**, you would scale each **row** instead of each column. This would not correspond to scaling individual neurons—it would mix together components across all neurons.

The errata note corrects a typo where $\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ was written. That formula would be problematic for two reasons: (1) the dimensional inconsistency if $\bm{D}$ were meant to be $n \times n$, and (2) even if $\bm{D}$ were $d \times d$ to make dimensions work, it would scale rows rather than neurons, losing the intended semantics.

To summarize the complete re-scaled OFT forward pass $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$: first each neuron $\bm{w}_i^0$ is scaled by $s_i$ to give $s_i\bm{w}_i^0$, then all neurons are jointly rotated by the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, and finally the dot products with input $\bm{x}$ are computed. The diagonal scaling preserves the hyperspherical energy since normalization removes magnitude anyway—this is precisely why the paper notes that "re-scaling neurons does not change the hyperspherical energy."
