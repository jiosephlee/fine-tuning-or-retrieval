\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does Cayley parameterization enforce orthogonality?
Question:
The OFT paper mentions using Cayley parameterization to generate orthogonal matrices efficiently, with the formula $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix.

I have two questions about this:

1. Why does this formula produce an orthogonal matrix? Is there a derivation or proof?
2. The paper mentions this only produces matrices with determinant 1 (special orthogonal group). Why does this limitation not affect performance in practice? Wouldn't we sometimes need reflections (determinant -1)?
Answer:
The Cayley parameterization is a classic tool from linear algebra that provides an elegant mapping between skew-symmetric matrices and orthogonal matrices. Let me address both of your questions.

## Why does the Cayley transform produce an orthogonal matrix?

The key insight is that skew-symmetric matrices ($\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$) and orthogonal matrices are intimately connected through this transform. Here's a direct proof:

Starting with $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, we need to verify that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$.

First, compute the transpose using $(\bm{AB})^\top = \bm{B}^\top\bm{A}^\top$ and $(\bm{A}^{-1})^\top = (\bm{A}^\top)^{-1}$:

$$\bm{R}^\top = [(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}]^\top(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^\top = (\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^\top)^{-1}(\bm{I} + \bm{Q}^\top)$$

Since $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$), this simplifies to:

$$\bm{R}^\top = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$$

Now multiplying by $\bm{R}$:

$$\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$$

The crucial observation is that $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}) = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2$ (they commute). Therefore:

$$\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2)(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$$

Substituting $\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2 = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$:

$$\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I}$$

## Why only determinant +1, and why doesn't it matter?

The Cayley transform can only reach the **special orthogonal group** $SO(n)$ — orthogonal matrices with $\det(\bm{R}) = +1$. This is because:

1. The Cayley transform is continuous and $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ maps to $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$
2. $SO(n)$ is the connected component containing the identity
3. Matrices with $\det = -1$ (reflections) form a disconnected component that cannot be reached through continuous paths from $\bm{I}$

This limitation doesn't affect performance for two key reasons. First, OFT initializes $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ (equivalently $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$), and gradient-based optimization naturally stays within the connected component containing the starting point — having access to reflections would be irrelevant since they're unreachable from the identity via gradient descent anyway.

Second, the goal is to make small perturbations to pretrained weights while preserving hyperspherical energy. Rotations near the identity provide sufficient flexibility for this, and as the paper demonstrates empirically, the special orthogonal group is expressive enough to achieve strong finetuning performance without needing reflections.
