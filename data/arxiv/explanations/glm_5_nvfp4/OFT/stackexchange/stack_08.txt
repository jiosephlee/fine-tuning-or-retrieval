\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why does preserving hyperspherical energy prevent destroying pretrained knowledge?
Question:
The central claim of the OFT paper is that preserving hyperspherical energy during finetuning prevents the destruction of pretrained knowledge. The authors state 'we hypothesize that a good finetuned model should have a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to the pretrained model.'

But I'm not convinced. If I apply the same rotation to all neurons, I preserve their pairwise angles, but haven't I still dramatically changed what each individual neuron represents? The neurons now point in completely different directions from the original network. How does preserving pairwise relationships alone maintain the semantic knowledge learned during pretraining? Shouldn't the absolute directions matter too?
Answer:
Your concern is thoughtful, but it rests on an assumption that the absolute directions of individual neurons encode pretrained knowledge. The OFT paper makes a more subtle claim: the semantic knowledge is encoded in the **relational structure** between neurons—their pairwise angular relationships—rather than in their absolute orientations.

The key insight is that neural computation in a layer involves inner products: $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x}$. When you apply an orthogonal transformation to the weights, $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, the forward pass becomes:

$$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{R}^\top\bm{x}$$

This reveals that OFT is mathematically equivalent to applying $\bm{R}^\top$ to the **input** before using the original weights. In other words, OFT learns a rigid rotation of the input coordinate system rather than arbitrarily warping individual neurons. The pretrained computation $\bm{W}^0$ is preserved; only the "viewing angle" of the input changes.

The paper supports this with a toy autoencoder experiment (Figure 1) demonstrating that angular information alone can almost perfectly reconstruct images, while magnitude information is largely irrelevant. This suggests that what matters is the angular *relationships* among neurons—the geometry they form on the hypersphere—not their absolute positions.

Your analogy about rotating all neurons is correct in that individual neuron directions change. But consider: if all neurons rotate together, the *shape* they collectively form—the pattern of "which neurons point near each other" and "which point apart"—remains intact. This geometric configuration is what the paper argues encodes the pretrained knowledge.

Additionally, OFT initializes $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ (identity), and COFT further constrains $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$, ensuring the transformation stays close to the pretrained model. This prevents large, unconstrained changes that would destroy learned representations.

In short: preserving hyperspherical energy preserves the *relational structure* among neurons. The absolute coordinate system is somewhat arbitrary—what matters is how neurons relate to each other, and orthogonal transformations preserve exactly those relationships while still allowing meaningful adaptation for downstream tasks.
