\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why does the autoencoder experiment show that angular information preserves semantics?
Question:
In Section 3.1 of the OFT paper, the authors describe a toy experiment with a convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images. They compare three ways to generate feature maps: (a) inner product, (b) magnitude only, and (c) angular information only.

The paper claims this shows 'angular information of neurons can almost perfectly recover the input images, while the magnitude of neurons contains no useful information.' But I'm confused - how can angular information alone reconstruct images if the network was trained with standard inner products? Isn't the magnitude part of what the network learned during training?
Answer:
The key to understanding this experiment is recognizing that it reveals where the learned semantic information is stored, not how it was learned during training.

During training, the autoencoder uses standard inner products: $z = \bm{w}^\top \bm{x} = \|\bm{w}\| \|\bm{x}\| \cos(\theta)$. The network learns weights $\bm{w}$ through backpropagation, and these weights naturally have both magnitude $\|\bm{w}\|$ and direction $\hat{\bm{w}} = \bm{w}/\|\bm{w}\|$. At test time, the experiment decomposes this inner product into two components: the magnitude component $\|\bm{w}\| \|\bm{x}\|$ and the angular component $\cos(\theta) = \frac{\bm{w}^\top \bm{x}}{\|\bm{w}\| \|\bm{x}\|}$.

The surprising finding is that the angular component alone can almost perfectly reconstruct the images, while the magnitude component contains essentially no useful information. This demonstrates that during training, the network encodes semantic information primarily in the *directions* of the neurons (their angular relationships with inputs), not in their magnitudes. The magnitudes essentially act as global scaling factors that don't carry semantic content.

This phenomenon parallels the 2D Fourier transform analogy mentioned in the paper: when you decompose an image into magnitude and phase spectra, the phase spectrum (which is angular information) preserves most of the semantic structure. You can reconstruct a recognizable image from the phase spectrum combined with a *random* magnitude spectrum, but not vice versa.

So the experiment doesn't contradict that the network learned with full inner products during training—it simply reveals that the learned weights store their semantic knowledge primarily in their angular structure rather than their magnitudes. This insight motivates OFT's core principle: preserving the pairwise angles between neurons (via orthogonal transformations) is sufficient to maintain semantic generation capability while allowing flexible finetuning.
