\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Faster, Stable, and Precise: How OFT Outperforms LoRA and ControlNet

Text-to-image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion have revolutionized generative AI, but adapting them for specific downstream tasks remains a challenge. Whether you are trying to generate images of a specific subject (subject-driven generation) or control the generation with edge maps (controllable generation), the standard finetuning approaches often fall short.

Popular methods like LoRA and DreamBooth are widely used, but they come with significant drawbacks: they can be slow to converge, difficult to tune, and prone to "forgetting" the semantic knowledge learned during pretraining—a phenomenon often leading to model collapse. A new method proposed in the paper "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning" (OFT) offers a compelling alternative, demonstrating superior stability, faster convergence, and higher fidelity compared to existing baselines.

## The Challenge: Finetuning Without Breaking the Model

Finetuning a large pretrained model is a balancing act. You want the model to learn a new task, but you don't want it to lose the general generative abilities it learned during pretraining.

Most existing methods, like DreamBooth, use a small learning rate to nudge the weights slightly, hoping to minimize the Euclidean distance between the pretrained and finetuned weights. LoRA adds a low-rank component to update the weights. However, these approaches implicitly assume that a small distance in weight space equals good preservation of semantic ability.

The authors of OFT argue that this isn't enough. They observe that the semantic information in a neural network is largely encoded in the *angles* between neurons, rather than just their magnitudes. When finetuning destroys these angular relationships, the model loses its semantic grip, leading to poor results or model collapse.

## Enter Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

OFT takes a fundamentally different approach. Instead of tweaking weights directly, OFT applies a learnable orthogonal transformation to the neurons.

Mathematically, for a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ (where $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$) such that the new weights are $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$.

Think of this as rotating the coordinate system of the neurons. Because the transformation is orthogonal, it preserves the angles between every pair of neurons. This property provably preserves the "hyperspherical energy" of the network, maintaining the semantic structure of the pretrained model.

## Battling Model Collapse: OFT vs. DreamBooth

One of the most striking results in the paper is OFT's resistance to model collapse during subject-driven generation.

In experiments involving DreamBooth, the authors observed that standard finetuning becomes unstable quickly. After 2,000 training iterations, DreamBooth often starts generating collapsed, unrealistic images. Similarly, LoRA frequently fails to preserve the specific identity of a subject, sometimes blending features incorrectly (e.g., generating a "yellow fur" texture when trying to put a subject in a "yellow shirt").

In contrast, both OFT and its constrained variant, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), remain remarkably stable. Even after 2,000 iterations, the models continue to generate high-fidelity images that adhere to the text prompt without losing the subject's identity. This stability is crucial because it alleviates the need to carefully tune the number of training epochs for every new subject.

## Speed and Predictability: OFT vs. ControlNet and LoRA

When it comes to controllable generation (e.g., generating an image from a segmentation map or a pose skeleton), training speed and convergence behavior are critical.

The paper highlights a significant efficiency advantage for OFT. In a landmark-to-face generation task, the authors compared the convergence speed of OFT against ControlNet and LoRA.

*   **LoRA:** It took LoRA roughly 20 epochs to converge to a good performance level.
*   **OFT:** The OFT framework reached the same performance level in just 8 epochs—less than half the training time.

Furthermore, the training dynamics of OFT are much smoother. ControlNet is known for a "sudden convergence" phenomenon, where the model suddenly starts working after a specific number of epochs (often around the 8th epoch). This unpredictability makes hyperparameter tuning difficult. OFT, however, exhibits smooth and predictable convergence, making it much easier to use in practice.

## Inference Efficiency: A Hidden Bonus

A major advantage of OFT over adapter methods like ControlNet is the lack of inference overhead.

ControlNet works by adding extra trainable copies of network blocks, which increases the computational cost every time you generate an image. OFT, however, learns a transformation matrix $\bm{R}$. Once training is complete, you can simply multiply $\bm{R}$ into the original weights $\bm{W}^0$ to get a merged weight matrix $\bm{W}$. This means OFT generates images at the exact same speed as the original pretrained model.

## Final Thoughts

OFT presents a principled and highly effective way to finetune diffusion models. By focusing on preserving the angular relationships between neurons, it avoids the pitfalls of standard finetuning. The empirical results are clear: compared to LoRA and DreamBooth, OFT offers better stability and prevents model collapse. Compared to ControlNet, it converges faster and adds no inference overhead. For anyone looking to adapt large diffusion models, OFT appears to be a faster, more stable, and more precise path forward.
