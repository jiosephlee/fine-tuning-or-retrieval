\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Beyond Fine-Tuning: How Orthogonal Finetuning is Revolutionizing AI Art

Large text-to-image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion and DALL-E have transformed how we create visual content. These models can generate photorealistic images from simple text prompts, demonstrating an impressive understanding of visual concepts. However, when we try to adapt these powerful models to specific downstream tasks—like generating images of a particular person or following edge maps—we run into a fundamental challenge: how do we customize these models without destroying the valuable knowledge they learned during pretraining?

## The Catastrophic Forgetting Problem

When you fine-tune a pretrained diffusion model on a new task, there's always a risk of "catastrophic forgetting." The model may learn to perform the new task but lose its ability to generate high-quality, diverse images. This manifests in several ways: generated images might lose their realism, the model might become less responsive to text prompts, or it might collapse into generating repetitive outputs.

Existing fine-tuning approaches tackle this problem implicitly. Methods like DreamBooth use very small learning rates to keep the fine-tuned model close to the original in terms of Euclidean distance. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) adds small learnable components to the model, assuming the weight updates have a low-rank structure. While these approaches work reasonably well, they lack a principled understanding of what exactly should be preserved during fine-tuning.

## A Structural Perspective: Hyperspherical Energy

The key insight behind Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) comes from asking a deeper question: what structural property of a neural network encodes its semantic knowledge? The authors argue that the angles between neurons—their directional relationships on a unit hypersphere—are crucial.

To understand why, consider a simple experiment. If you train a convolutional autoencoder on images and then try to reconstruct those images using only the angular information (normalized neurons), you can recover the original images almost perfectly. The magnitude information alone, however, contains almost no useful semantic content. This suggests that the directions of neurons—the angles between them—store most of the semantic information learned during pretraining.

The authors formalize this intuition using **hyperspherical energy**, which measures the pairwise relationships between neurons. Specifically, for a layer with neurons $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}$, the hyperspherical energy is defined as:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the normalized neuron. This metric captures how uniformly distributed the neurons are on the unit hypersphere. Preserving this energy during fine-tuning means preserving the semantic structure learned during pretraining.

## Enter Orthogonal Finetuning

Here's the elegant mathematical insight that powers OFT: applying the same orthogonal transformation (rotation or reflection) to all neurons in a layer provably preserves their pairwise angles. This means if we constrain our fine-tuning to learn orthogonal transformations, we can adapt the model to new tasks without changing its hyperspherical energy.

Concretely, for a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and transforms the weights:

$$\bm{W} = \bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0, \quad \text{where} \quad \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$$

The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ rotates or reflects all neurons while preserving their relative angles. Think of it as rotating the entire coordinate system for the neurons in a layer—the relationships between neurons stay the same, but their orientation in space changes.

## Making OFT Practical

A full orthogonal matrix for a layer with $d$ neurons requires $d(d-1)/2$ parameters, which can be expensive. OFT addresses this through **block-diagonal parameterization**, splitting the orthogonal matrix into smaller independent blocks:

$$\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)$$

This reduces the parameter complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, where $r$ is the number of blocks. Despite this efficiency gain, the resulting matrix remains strictly orthogonal, so there's no sacrifice in preserving hyperspherical energy.

## Constrained OFT: Adding Stability

While OFT preserves the relative structure of neurons, the authors introduce an additional constraint for extra stability. **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)** limits how far the transformation can deviate from the identity:

$$\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$$

This constraint ensures the fine-tuned model stays within a small neighborhood of the pretrained model. The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ controls the trade-off between flexibility (ability to adapt to new tasks) and regularity (preservation of pretrained knowledge). With smaller $\epsilon$, the model behaves more like the original; with larger $\epsilon$, it gains more flexibility to adapt.

## Impressive Results Across Tasks

The authors evaluate OFT on two important fine-tuning tasks:

**Subject-driven generation**: Given a few images of a specific subject (like a dog), generate new images of that subject in different contexts. OFT significantly outperforms DreamBooth and LoRA in preserving subject identity while following text prompts accurately.

**Controllable generation**: Generate images following additional control signals like edge maps or segmentation maps. OFT converges much faster than ControlNet—achieving in 8 epochs what LoRA needs 20 epochs to accomplish—while using fewer parameters and introducing no additional inference overhead.

Perhaps most impressively, OFT demonstrates remarkable stability during training. While DreamBooth can collapse after too many iterations, OFT and COFT remain stable even with extended training, making them much easier to use in practice without careful hyperparameter tuning.

## Why This Matters

OFT represents a shift from heuristic fine-tuning approaches to principled methods grounded in the mathematical structure of neural networks. By identifying that neuron directions encode semantic information and designing a method that preserves this structure, OFT achieves better results with less computational cost.

The method is also architecture-agnostic—it works for fully connected layers, attention mechanisms, and convolutional layers. And crucially, once training is complete, the learned orthogonal matrix can be multiplied into the original weights, meaning there's **zero inference overhead** compared to the original model.

As text-to-image models continue to grow in size and capability, methods like OFT will become increasingly important. They offer a path to customization without sacrificing the remarkable generative abilities these models learn during pretraining—a principled approach to keeping the magic intact while steering it toward specific goals.
