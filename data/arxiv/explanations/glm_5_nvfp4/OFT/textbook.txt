\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Introduction: The Challenge of Controlling Diffusion Models

# The Rise of Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

The field of generative artificial intelligence has witnessed a remarkable transformation in recent years, largely catalyzed by the emergence and rapid advancement of text-to-image diffusion models. These models have demonstrated an unprecedented ability to synthesize photorealistic images from textual descriptions, bridging the gap between human language and visual creativity in ways previously thought impossible. The journey toward this achievement has been enabled by two parallel streams of innovation: the development of diffusion-based generative models and the advancement of vision-language representation learning.

Diffusion models operate on a fundamentally different principle than earlier generative approaches. Rather than attempting to generate images in a single step, these models learn to gradually denoise a noisy image over multiple time steps, eventually arriving at a coherent and detailed output. This denoising process has proven to be remarkably effective for high-fidelity image synthesis. The theoretical foundations were laid by research in denoising diffusion probabilistic models and score-based generative models, which established the mathematical framework for training models that can reverse a noise injection process.

Several landmark models have demonstrated the power of this approach. GLIDE and Imagen represent early efforts that train diffusion models directly in pixel space. GLIDE jointly trains the text encoder alongside a diffusion prior using paired text-image data, creating an integrated system for text-guided generation. Imagen, in contrast, leverages a frozen pretrained text encoder, demonstrating that powerful pretrained language models can provide sufficient semantic understanding without additional training. Stable Diffusion and DALL-E2 shifted the paradigm by training diffusion models in latent space rather than pixel space. Stable Diffusion employs VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook as its latent space, dramatically reducing computational requirements while maintaining generation quality. DALL-E2 adopts CLIP to construct a joint latent embedding space that can represent both images and text in a shared semantic framework.

The capabilities of these models extend far beyond simple image generation. They can render complex scenes, understand abstract concepts, and produce images that faithfully interpret nuanced textual prompts. However, despite these impressive achievements, text guidance alone can be ambiguous and insufficient for providing fine-grained and accurate control over the generated images. A user might want to generate an image of a specific subject—say, their pet dog—in various novel contexts, or they might want to guide the generation process with additional structural constraints like edge maps or segmentation masks. These requirements expose a fundamental limitation: while pretrained diffusion models are remarkably versatile, guiding them toward specific downstream tasks without degrading their core generative capabilities remains a significant challenge.

# Downstream Tasks: Subject-Driven Generation vs. Controllable Generation

The paper under consideration addresses two specific downstream tasks that exemplify the challenge of controlling diffusion models: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. These tasks represent distinct but related problems in the broader landscape of image generation, and understanding their nuances is essential for appreciating the motivation behind the proposed approach.

Subject-driven generation addresses a scenario that is intuitive yet technically challenging: given just a few images of a specific subject, the task is to generate new images of that same subject in different contexts using text prompts as guidance. Consider a user who wants to generate images of their personal belongings, pets, or even themselves in various imaginative scenarios. The model must somehow learn to recognize and preserve the defining visual characteristics of the subject—its identity, texture, color patterns, and distinctive features—while being flexible enough to place that subject in novel contexts dictated by the text prompt. This task is sometimes referred to as personalization, as it effectively personalizes a general-purpose model to recognize and generate specific subjects.

The challenge here is multifold. First, the model must extract meaningful representations of the subject from very few images—often as few as three to five examples. Second, it must integrate these representations into its existing knowledge base without overwriting or degrading its ability to generate diverse and high-quality images. Third, it must maintain enough flexibility to combine the subject identity with arbitrary text prompts that may specify novel poses, backgrounds, styles, or interactions. The DreamBooth approach addresses this by finetuning the diffusion model with a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior preservation loss, using a customized token to represent the subject. However, naive finetuning approaches can easily lead to model degradation or insufficient subject preservation.

Controllable generation, the second task, addresses a complementary challenge: enabling the model to take in additional control signals beyond text prompts. These control signals could include Canny edge maps, segmentation maps, depth maps, human pose skeletons, or any other structural guidance that constrains the spatial organization of the generated image. The goal is to generate images that simultaneously satisfy the textual description and align with the provided control signal. For instance, a user might want to generate an image of "a person sitting on a bench" while also providing a specific pose skeleton that dictates exactly how the person should be positioned.

This task is fundamentally about expanding the input modality of the model. While the pretrained model expects text as its primary conditioning signal, controllable generation requires the model to understand and respect additional structural constraints. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter represent recent approaches to this problem, finetuning pretrained diffusion models to adapt to additional control signals. These methods have achieved impressive results, but they often require substantial finetuning resources and may introduce computational overhead during inference.

Both tasks essentially boil down to a common question: how can we effectively finetune text-to-image diffusion models for downstream tasks without sacrificing the remarkable generative capabilities acquired during pretraining? The answer to this question requires a principled understanding of what makes diffusion models work and what aspects of their structure must be preserved during finetuning.

# Desiderata for Finetuning: Efficiency and Generalizability Preservation

When designing finetuning strategies for large pretrained models, researchers must navigate a delicate balance between adaptation and preservation. The paper articulates two primary desiderata that guide the development of effective finetuning methods: training efficiency and generalizability preservation. Understanding these criteria provides a framework for evaluating existing methods and appreciating the innovations proposed in the paper.

Training efficiency encompasses both the number of trainable parameters required for adaptation and the number of training epochs needed to achieve satisfactory performance. In the context of large diffusion models, which may contain billions of parameters, the number of trainable parameters directly impacts memory requirements and computational costs. Methods that require finetuning the entire model demand substantial hardware resources, potentially placing them out of reach for many researchers and practitioners. Moreover, the number of training epochs affects both the time required for adaptation and the practical usability of the method. Methods that converge slowly require more iterations, increasing both computational costs and the time needed to adapt to new tasks.

Beyond computational considerations, training efficiency also has implications for data efficiency. Methods that can adapt with fewer training images are more practical for real-world applications where collecting extensive datasets for every new subject or control task would be prohibitive. The paper emphasizes that an ideal finetuning method should achieve strong performance with minimal data requirements.

Generalizability preservation, the second criterion, is arguably the more subtle and challenging requirement. A pretrained diffusion model has acquired extensive knowledge about the visual world—the appearance of objects, the physics of light, the composition of scenes, and the semantics of visual concepts. This knowledge enables the model to generate diverse, high-fidelity images across an enormous range of prompts. The challenge of finetuning is to adapt the model for a specific downstream task while preserving this broad generative capability.

Poor generalizability preservation manifests in several ways. The finetuned model might exhibit mode collapse, losing the ability to generate diverse outputs and instead producing repetitive or limited variations. It might suffer from quality degradation, generating images that are noticeably less photorealistic or coherent than those produced by the original model. It might lose semantic understanding, failing to properly interpret text prompts or generate appropriate compositions. Any of these failures would undermine the practical utility of the finetuned model, as it would no longer serve as a reliable tool for image generation.

The tension between adaptation and preservation is fundamental. Adapting too aggressively risks destroying the pretrained knowledge, while being too conservative may fail to achieve the desired level of control for the downstream task. Existing finetuning methods attempt to navigate this tension, but the paper argues that they lack a principled foundation for understanding what should be preserved and how to ensure preservation.

# Limitations of Existing Methods: The Euclidean Distance Fallacy

Existing finetuning methods for diffusion models have largely operated under an implicit assumption: a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned model weights and the pretrained model weights indicates better preservation of the pretrained model's capabilities. This assumption, while intuitively appealing, has significant limitations that the paper critiques and seeks to address.

The standard finetuning approach updates neuron weights through gradient descent with a small learning rate. The small learning rate serves an implicit regularizing function, encouraging the finetuned weights to remain close to their pretrained values in Euclidean space. Similarly, methods like LoRA introduce low-rank constraints on the weight updates, restricting the space of possible finetuned models in a way that inherently limits deviation from the pretrained model. These approaches are grounded in the intuition that large changes to model weights are likely to disrupt the carefully learned representations from pretraining.

However, the paper argues that the Euclidean difference to the pretrained model alone is unable to fully capture the degree of semantic preservation. This is a profound observation that shifts the focus from weight-level metrics to structural properties of the network. Consider an analogy: two paintings might use nearly identical colors and brushstrokes while conveying completely different subjects, while two other paintings might differ significantly in their low-level details while depicting the same scene. The Euclidean distance between pixel values would not capture these semantic relationships.

The paper proposes that a more structural measure to characterize the difference between the finetuned model and the pretrained model can greatly benefit the preservation of pretraining performance as well as finetuning stability. The key insight is that the semantic information encoded in neural networks is not simply about the individual weight values, but rather about the relational structure among neurons—specifically, the angles between neurons.

This perspective draws inspiration from prior research showing that hyperspherical similarity encodes semantic information effectively. The angles between neurons in a layer—their relative orientations in the weight space—may be crucial for representing the knowledge stored in the network. When finetuning disrupts these angular relationships, even while keeping Euclidean distance small, the semantic structure of the model can be damaged. This explains why existing methods that focus solely on Euclidean constraints may still fail to preserve the pretrained model's generative capabilities.

The paper introduces the concept of hyperspherical energy as a formal measure for quantifying this angular structure. Hyperspherical energy is defined as the sum of hyperspherical similarity between all pairwise neurons in the same layer, capturing the level of neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, the hyperspherical energy is formally defined as:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the $i$-th normalized neuron. This measure characterizes the pairwise relational structure among neurons, providing a structural quantification of the network's organization that goes beyond simple Euclidean distance.

# Introduction to Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): A Principled Approach

Motivated by the limitations of existing methods and the insight that angular structure encodes semantic information, the paper introduces Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) as a principled approach to finetuning diffusion models. The central idea behind OFT is elegantly simple yet theoretically grounded: learn a layer-shared orthogonal transformation for neurons such that their pairwise angles are preserved during finetuning.

The theoretical foundation for OFT rests on a key invariance property of hyperspherical energy: the pairwise hyperspherical similarity is provably preserved if the same orthogonal transformation is applied to all neurons. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. When such a transformation is applied to the neurons in a layer, it rotates or reflects all neurons by the same amount, preserving the angles between any pair of neurons.

This property leads to a natural formulation for finetuning. Rather than freely adjusting the weights, OFT constrains the finetuned weights to be an orthogonal transformation of the pretrained weights:

$$\bm{W}=\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0$$

where $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weights and $\bm{R}$ is a learnable orthogonal matrix. This constraint guarantees that:

$$\left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\| = 0$$

In other words, the hyperspherical energy of the finetuned model exactly matches that of the pretrained model. This is the key theoretical guarantee that distinguishes OFT from existing methods: it provably preserves the angular structure that encodes semantic information.

The intuition for why orthogonal transformation works for finetuning neurons draws an illuminating parallel to signal processing. In the 2D Fourier transform, an image can be decomposed into magnitude and phase spectra. Remarkably, the phase spectrum—which encodes angular information between the input and basis—preserves the major part of semantics. A phase spectrum combined with a random magnitude spectrum can still reconstruct the original image without losing its semantic content. This phenomenon suggests that angular relationships are fundamental to representing visual semantics.

By analogy, changing neuron directions is key to semantically modifying generated images, which is precisely the goal of both subject-driven and controllable generation. However, changing neuron directions with unconstrained freedom risks destroying the pretrained generative performance. OFT strikes a balance by preserving the angles between any pair of neurons, allowing semantic modification while maintaining the structural integrity of the pretrained knowledge.

OFT can also be interpreted as adjusting the canonical coordinate system for neurons in the same layer. Rather than modifying neurons individually and potentially disrupting their relationships, OFT learns a global transformation that applies uniformly across all neurons. This global approach provides sufficient flexibility for adaptation while enforcing a structural constraint that preserves semantic organization.

To further improve stability, the paper proposes Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which adds an additional constraint limiting the deviation of the orthogonal matrix from the identity matrix. Formally, COFT imposes:

$$\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$$

This constraint ensures that the finetuned model remains within a small neighborhood of the pretrained model, combining the hyperspherical energy preservation of OFT with an explicit bound on overall deviation. COFT can be viewed as combining two constraints: minimal hyperspherical energy difference and constrained deviation from the pretrained model, making explicit what existing methods leave implicit.

A practical advantage of OFT and COFT is that they introduce no additional inference overhead. Once training is complete, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied directly into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This means the finetuned model operates exactly like the original model during inference, with identical computational requirements and latency.

# Theoretical Foundations: Neurons and Hyperspherical Energy

# Neurons on the Hypersphere: Directions vs. Magnitudes

To understand the theoretical foundations of Orthogonal Finetuning, we must first revisit how we conceptualize neurons in neural networks. A neuron in a fully connected layer can be represented as a weight vector $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$, where $d$ is the input dimension. When we consider a layer with multiple neurons, we can organize them as columns of a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \cdots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$. Traditionally, when we think about modifying neural networks during finetuning, we tend to focus on the Euclidean distance between weight vectors—how far the finetuned weights have moved from their pretrained values in terms of their overall magnitude and direction combined.

However, a more nuanced view emerges when we decompose each neuron into two distinct components: its direction and its magnitude. Mathematically, any neuron $\bm{w}_i$ can be written as $\bm{w}_i = \|\bm{w}_i\| \cdot \hat{\bm{w}}_i$, where $\|\bm{w}_i\|$ is the magnitude (or norm) of the neuron, and $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ is the unit vector pointing in the neuron's direction. This decomposition is not merely a mathematical curiosity—it reveals fundamentally different roles that these two components play in the neural network's computation.

The key insight from research on hyperspherical neural networks, particularly from SphereNet, demonstrates that training a neural network with all neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere yields comparable capacity and even better generalizability. This remarkable finding implies that the direction of neurons—their orientation in the high-dimensional space—can fully capture the most important information from the data. In other words, if we were to strip away all magnitude information and keep only the directional information of neurons, the network would still retain essentially all of its semantic processing capabilities.

This leads us to a powerful geometric interpretation: we can view neurons as points lying on the surface of a unit hypersphere in $d$-dimensional space. Each neuron is uniquely identified by its angular position on this hypersphere, and the relationships between neurons are characterized by the angles between them—their pairwise angular separations. When we adopt this viewpoint, the question of how to properly finetune a neural network transforms into a question of how to manipulate these points on the hypersphere while preserving their meaningful relationships.

# The Semantic Importance of Angular Information

The observation that neuron directions encode semantic information is not merely theoretical—it has deep empirical support. The paper draws a compelling analogy from signal processing, specifically from the 2D Fourier transform, which provides intuitive insight into why angular information is semantically crucial. When an image is decomposed using the 2D Fourier transform, it separates into two components: the magnitude spectrum and the phase spectrum. The phase spectrum, which captures angular information between the input and the basis functions, preserves the major portion of the semantic content of the image.

This phenomenon from signal processing carries a profound implication: if you take the phase spectrum of an image and pair it with a completely random magnitude spectrum, you can still reconstruct the original image without losing its essential semantic content. The recognizable structures, objects, and meaning of the image remain intact despite the magnitude information being thoroughly corrupted. This tells us that angular relationships—the phase—fundamentally encode what the image "means" or represents, while magnitude information relates more to variations in intensity and contrast.

The paper provides concrete evidence for this claim through a carefully designed toy experiment. The researchers trained a standard convolutional autoencoder on flower images, using the standard inner product operation to produce feature maps during training. In mathematical terms, for each element $z$ of the feature map, they computed $z = \bm{w}^\top \bm{x}$ where $\bm{w}$ is the convolution kernel and $\bm{x}$ is the input in the sliding window. Critically, the training was performed using only the standard inner product—no special angular activation functions were used.

During testing, the researchers compared three different ways to generate the feature map: the standard inner product that was used during training, using only magnitude information (computing $\|\bm{w}\| \cdot \|\bm{x}\|$), and using only angular information (computing the angle between $\bm{w}$ and $\bm{x}$). The results were striking: the angular information could almost perfectly recover the input flower images, preserving all their semantic content, while the magnitude information contained no useful information whatsoever—producing outputs that bore no resemblance to the original images.

This experiment powerfully demonstrates that the angles between neurons and inputs—essentially, their directional relationships—are the primary carriers of semantic information in neural networks. The magnitude information, while potentially useful for other aspects of computation, does not appear to encode the meaningful content that we associate with image semantics. This finding has profound implications for how we should think about finetuning: if we want to modify the semantic content of a network's outputs, we should focus our attention on modifying neuron directions. However, doing so carelessly risks destroying the carefully learned semantic structures from pretraining. This tension—between the need to modify directions to achieve new tasks and the need to preserve existing semantic relationships—lies at the heart of the Orthogonal Finetuning approach.

# Defining Hyperspherical Energy (HE)

With the understanding that neurons can be viewed as points on a unit hypersphere and that their angular relationships carry semantic meaning, we can now introduce a formal measure to quantify these relationships. Hyperspherical Energy (HE) provides a mathematical framework for characterizing the pairwise relational structure among neurons within the same layer.

To build intuition, imagine placing several points on the surface of a sphere. These points could be arranged in many different configurations: they might be clustered together in one region, spread uniformly across the entire surface, or arranged in some other pattern. Hyperspherical Energy gives us a way to quantify how these points are distributed relative to each other. When points are uniformly distributed across the hypersphere—with large angular distances between all pairs—the energy is low. When points cluster together—having small angular distances—the energy is high.

Formally, for a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \cdots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ representing a layer with $n$ neurons, we first normalize each neuron to obtain $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. These normalized neurons lie on the unit hypersphere. The Hyperspherical Energy is then defined as:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

This formula sums over all pairs of distinct neurons, computing the inverse of the Euclidean distance between each pair of normalized neurons on the hypersphere. The reason for using the inverse distance becomes clear when we consider what this metric captures: when two neurons are close together on the hypersphere (small angular separation), their distance $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ is small, making its inverse large. Conversely, when neurons are far apart (large angular separation), the inverse is small. Thus, Hyperspherical Energy increases when neurons cluster together and decreases when neurons spread apart.

An important property to note is that Hyperspherical Energy depends only on the directions of neurons, not their magnitudes. Since we normalize each neuron before computing the pairwise distances, scaling a neuron by any factor leaves its contribution to the energy unchanged. This aligns perfectly with our earlier discussion about the semantic importance of angular information—HE captures precisely the aspects of neuron configuration that matter most for semantic representation.

The minimum Hyperspherical Energy occurs when all neurons are uniformly distributed across the hypersphere, maximizing their pairwise distances. This uniform configuration represents neurons without redundancy—each neuron occupies its own distinct region of the semantic space. The maximum energy, in contrast, occurs when all neurons point in the same direction, representing complete redundancy.

# Hyperspherical Energy as a Measure of Semantic Preservation

The introduction of Hyperspherical Energy provides us with a principled way to think about what makes a good finetuned model. The central hypothesis of the OFT framework is that a well-finetuned model should have minimal difference in Hyperspherical Energy compared to its pretrained counterpart. But why should preserving HE correlate with preserving the semantic capabilities of a model?

The answer lies in what HE represents. Since HE captures the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, preserving HE means preserving the relative positions of neurons on the hypersphere. These relative positions encode how the layer processes information—neurons with similar directions respond to similar input patterns, while neurons with different directions respond to different patterns. The angular structure of neurons essentially encodes the "knowledge" embedded in that layer.

Consider what happens during pretraining of a large text-to-image diffusion model. Through exposure to millions of image-text pairs, the network develops neurons that respond to various visual concepts—edges, textures, objects, semantic categories, and their combinations. The angular relationships between neurons encode how these concepts relate to each other. Two neurons that point in similar directions might both respond to related visual features, while neurons pointing in orthogonal directions might process fundamentally different types of information.

When we finetune this model for a downstream task—whether subject-driven generation or controllable generation—we want to modify the model's behavior without destroying this carefully learned semantic structure. If we change the angular relationships between neurons too dramatically, we risk corrupting the knowledge that makes the model capable of generating high-quality, semantically coherent images. The pairwise angles between neurons become a fingerprint of the pretrained model's semantic capabilities.

This perspective also explains why standard finetuning approaches can fail. Standard finetuning updates weights using gradient descent with a small learning rate, implicitly minimizing $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$ where $\bm{M}$ is the finetuned model and $\bm{M}^0$ is the pretrained model. This constraint assumes that a small Euclidean distance between weight matrices correlates with preservation of pretrained capabilities. However, this assumption overlooks the structural relationships between neurons—two weight matrices might have small Euclidean distance while having dramatically different angular structures.

The paper argues that preserving Hyperspherical Energy—maintaining the pairwise angular relationships among neurons—provides a more structural and meaningful measure of semantic preservation. Rather than merely staying close to the pretrained weights in Euclidean space, HE preservation ensures that the relational structure of the neurons remains intact. This structural preservation is what enables OFT to adapt models to new tasks while maintaining their core semantic capabilities.

# How Orthogonal Transformations Preserve Hyperspherical Energy

Given that preserving Hyperspherical Energy is desirable for semantic preservation, the natural question becomes: how can we modify a network while keeping its HE unchanged? The paper reveals an elegant mathematical property: Hyperspherical Energy is provably preserved when we apply the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer.

An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ satisfies the constraint $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. Geometrically, orthogonal transformations consist of rotations and reflections—they preserve the lengths of vectors and the angles between them. When we multiply a neuron $\bm{w}_i$ by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to obtain $\bm{R}\bm{w}_i$, the magnitude stays the same: $\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i\| = \|\bm{w}_i\|$. More importantly, the angle between any two neurons is preserved.

To see this mathematically, consider two normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$ on the unit hypersphere. After applying the orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$, the distance between them becomes:

$$\|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \sqrt{(\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j)^\top(\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j)}$$

$$= \sqrt{(\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j)^\top\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}(\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j)}$$

$$= \sqrt{(\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j)^\top\bm{I}(\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j)}$$

$$= \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$$

Since every pairwise distance remains unchanged, the sum of inverse distances—the Hyperspherical Energy—remains exactly the same. This invariance property is the theoretical foundation of Orthogonal Finetuning.

The beauty of this approach is that it provides a principled way to modify a neural network's behavior while provably preserving its semantic structure. By learning an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ that transforms the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ into finetuned weights $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, we can adapt the network to new tasks without risking the destruction of learned semantic relationships. The orthogonal transformation can be interpreted as adjusting the coordinate system for neurons—rotating or reflecting them as a group—while keeping their relative angular positions fixed.

This approach strikes an optimal balance between flexibility and regularity. The orthogonal transformation is sufficiently flexible to enable meaningful adaptation—orthogonal matrices form a rich manifold with many degrees of freedom, capable of rotating neurons to new absolute positions. At the same time, the approach is sufficiently constrained—by preserving pairwise angles, it maintains the structural relationships that encode the pretrained model's knowledge.

The paper notes that this approach differs fundamentally from training methods that aim to minimize Hyperspherical Energy. In classification problems, minimizing HE pushes neurons toward a uniform distribution on the hypersphere, which can be beneficial for generalization. However, for finetuning, minimizing HE would destroy the pretrained angular structure—uniformly distributing neurons would erase the semantic relationships carefully learned during pretraining. Instead, OFT aims to preserve HE exactly, keeping the pretrained semantic structure intact while allowing the network to adapt through orthogonal transformations of the entire neuron ensemble.

# The Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) Methodology

# The OFT Framework: Multiplicative Orthogonal Transformation

The Orthogonal Finetuning framework introduces a fundamentally different approach to adapting pretrained neural networks compared to conventional finetuning strategies. To understand the core idea, we must first consider how standard finetuning operates and why it may fall short when adapting powerful text-to-image diffusion models.

In conventional finetuning, the model weights are updated using gradient descent with a small learning rate. This approach implicitly assumes that maintaining a small Euclidean distance between the finetuned model and the pretrained model will preserve the pretrained model's generative capabilities. However, this assumption does not fully capture the structural properties that are essential for preserving semantic information. The OFT framework takes a different approach by introducing a principled constraint on how the weights can be modified.

The central insight of OFT is to apply a layer-shared orthogonal transformation to the neurons in each layer of the neural network. Consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ from the pretrained model, where $\bm{W}^0 = \{\bm{w}_1, \cdots, \bm{w}_n\}$ and each $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents the $i$-th neuron. In the standard forward pass, given an input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the output vector $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is computed as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$.

OFT modifies this forward pass by learning an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that transforms the pretrained weights. The finetuned forward pass becomes:

$$\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$$

where the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies the orthogonality constraint:

$$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$$

Here, $\bm{I}$ denotes the identity matrix. This constraint ensures that $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix, meaning it preserves the inner product between any two vectors it transforms. The determinant of $\bm{R}$ can be either $1$ or $-1$, corresponding to rotation or reflection transformations, respectively.

The key distinction between OFT and standard finetuning is that OFT modifies the weights in a multiplicative manner through the orthogonal transformation, rather than adding to them directly. This multiplicative approach fundamentally constrains how the weights can change, ensuring that certain structural properties of the pretrained model are preserved. Specifically, the orthogonal transformation preserves the angles between all pairs of neurons in the same layer, which we will see is crucial for maintaining the semantic information encoded in the pretrained model.

It is important to note that OFT learns a different orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ for each layer of the neural network. However, within a single layer, the same orthogonal matrix is shared across all neurons, ensuring a consistent transformation that preserves the relational structure among neurons. This layer-shared approach provides a good balance between flexibility for adaptation and regularity for stability.

# Mathematical Formulation: Minimizing the Difference in Hyperspherical Energy

To understand why orthogonal transformation is the right approach for finetuning, we need to introduce the concept of hyperspherical energy and examine its relationship to the semantic information stored in neural networks.

Hyperspherical energy provides a measure of the pairwise relational structure among neurons on a unit hypersphere. For a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \cdots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, we first define the normalized neurons as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$, which projects each neuron onto the unit hypersphere. The hyperspherical energy of the layer is then defined as:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

This formulation captures the level of neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere. The hyperspherical energy sums the inverse Euclidean distances between all pairs of normalized neurons, meaning that neurons that are closer together on the hypersphere contribute more to the energy. A higher hyperspherical energy indicates that neurons are more clustered, while a lower energy indicates that neurons are more uniformly distributed.

The OFT framework is motivated by the hypothesis that preserving the hyperspherical energy during finetuning is crucial for maintaining the pretrained model's semantic generation ability. Empirical observations suggest that hyperspherical similarity encodes semantic information well, and that angular differences between neurons characterize the semantic gap between different features. This is further supported by the observation that neural networks trained with neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere can achieve comparable capacity and even better generalizability, implying that the directions of neurons capture the most important information from data.

A toy experiment demonstrating this phenomenon involves training a convolutional autoencoder on flower images. During training, the standard inner product is used to produce feature maps. During testing, three different methods are compared for generating feature maps: the inner product used in training, magnitude information alone, and angular information alone. The results show that the angular information can almost perfectly recover the input images, while the magnitude contains no useful information. This experiment implies that the angles (directions) of neurons play the major role in storing semantic information.

The mathematical foundation of OFT stems from an important invariance property: the hyperspherical energy is provably preserved under orthogonal transformation. To see why this is the case, consider that an orthogonal transformation preserves the inner product between any two vectors. Since the cosine similarity between two normalized vectors depends only on their inner product, and the hyperspherical energy depends only on the distances between normalized neurons (which in turn depend on the angles between them), applying the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons preserves the pairwise hyperspherical similarity.

Formally, OFT can be interpreted as minimizing the hyperspherical energy difference between the finetuned model and the pretrained model:

$$\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\| \Leftrightarrow \min_{\bm{W}} \bigg{\|} \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}-\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i-\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|^{-1}\bigg{\|}$$

where $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weights and $\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i$ represents the normalized pretrained neurons.

The attainable minimum of this objective is zero, and this minimum can be achieved when $\bm{W}$ and $\bm{W}^0$ differ only by a rotation or reflection, that is, when $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ for some orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. This is precisely the strategy that OFT employs: instead of directly optimizing the weight matrix $\bm{W}$ to minimize the hyperspherical energy difference, OFT parameterizes the weight update as an orthogonal transformation of the pretrained weights, which guarantees zero hyperspherical energy difference by construction.

This formulation provides a principled foundation for finetuning. Unlike existing methods that implicitly assume smaller Euclidean distance from the pretrained model indicates better preservation of pretrained ability, OFT explicitly preserves the structural measure of hyperspherical energy. This structural preservation is particularly important for text-to-image diffusion models, where the semantic generation ability depends critically on the relational structure among neurons.

# Initialization Strategy: Starting from the Pretrained Weights

A critical aspect of any finetuning method is the initialization strategy, which determines where the optimization process begins. For OFT, proper initialization ensures that the finetuning process starts exactly from the pretrained model, allowing for gradual adaptation while maintaining the benefits of the pretrained weights.

The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ in OFT is initialized as an identity matrix $\bm{I}$. This initialization is crucial because when $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, the finetuned weights become:

$$\bm{W} = \bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0 = \bm{I} \cdot \bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0$$

This means that at the start of finetuning, the model is exactly equivalent to the pretrained model. The identity matrix initialization serves the same purpose as the zero initialization strategy used in LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), where the added low-rank matrices are initialized to zero so that the model starts from the pretrained weights.

Starting from the pretrained weights is essential for several reasons. First, it ensures that the finetuning process begins from a known good state—the pretrained model has already learned useful representations from large-scale data, and we want to preserve these representations while adapting to the downstream task. Second, it provides a stable starting point for optimization, avoiding the instability that could arise from random initialization. Third, it allows for controlled adaptation, where the model gradually moves away from the pretrained weights as the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is updated during training.

During training, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is updated to minimize the task-specific loss function while maintaining its orthogonality. As $\bm{R}$ deviates from the identity matrix, the finetuned weights $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0$ move away from the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$. However, because $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout training, the hyperspherical energy of the layer is preserved regardless of how much $\bm{R}$ changes from the identity.

The initialization strategy also has implications for the finetuning stability. Because the model starts exactly from the pretrained weights and the orthogonal transformation provides a regularized update, OFT tends to be more stable during finetuning compared to methods that use small learning rates with less structured updates. The stability is further enhanced by the Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) variant, which adds an additional constraint on how far $\bm{R}$ can deviate from the identity matrix. In COFT, the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ is added, where $\epsilon$ is a hyperparameter controlling the maximum allowed deviation.

For COFT, the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ used in the Cayley parameterization is initialized as an all-zero matrix. This initialization corresponds to $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ through the Cayley transform relationship, ensuring that COFT also starts from the pretrained weights. The $\epsilon$-deviation constraint in COFT provides explicit control over the flexibility-stability trade-off: larger values of $\epsilon$ allow for more flexible adaptation, while smaller values keep the model closer to the pretrained model.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization: The Cayley Transform

While the theoretical foundation of OFT relies on orthogonal matrices, a practical implementation must address how to efficiently parameterize and maintain orthogonality during training. Standard orthogonalization methods like the Gram-Schmidt process, although differentiable, are computationally expensive and not suitable for large-scale neural networks.

The OFT framework adopts Cayley parameterization as an efficient method to generate orthogonal matrices. The Cayley transform provides a mapping from skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices with determinant 1 (the special orthogonal group). Specifically, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is constructed as:

$$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$$

where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. A skew-symmetric matrix has zeros on its diagonal and negative-symmetric off-diagonal elements, meaning that $Q_{ij} = -Q_{ji}$ for all $i \neq j$.

The Cayley transform has several desirable properties for OFT. First, it guarantees that the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal by construction, eliminating the need for explicit orthogonalization during training. Second, it only produces orthogonal matrices with determinant 1, which correspond to rotation transformations. While this means that reflection transformations are excluded, empirical results show that this limitation does not affect performance in practice. Third, the mapping from $\bm{Q}$ to $\bm{R}$ is differentiable, allowing gradients to flow through the transformation during backpropagation.

The computational cost of the Cayley transform primarily comes from computing the matrix inverse $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. While this can be done using standard linear algebra operations, it introduces some computational overhead. However, this cost is typically acceptable given the benefits of maintaining orthogonality without explicit constraints.

For COFT, which adds a deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$, the Cayley transform provides a convenient way to incorporate this constraint. By applying the Neumann series expansion, the Cayley transform can be approximated as:

$$\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$$

under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm. This approximation reveals that the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ can be translated to a constraint on $\bm{Q}$: $\|\bm{Q} - \bm{0}\| \leq \epsilon'$, where $\epsilon'$ is a hyperparameter related to $\epsilon$. This new constraint on $\bm{Q}$ can be easily enforced using projected gradient descent, where after each gradient update, $\bm{Q}$ is projected onto the feasible set defined by the constraint.

The combination of Cayley parameterization and projected gradient descent provides an efficient implementation of COFT that maintains both orthogonality and deviation constraints without expensive constrained optimization procedures.

# Parameter Efficiency: Block-Diagonal Structure

Even with the Cayley parameterization, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can still be parameter-inefficient when the neuron dimension $d$ is large. For a weight matrix of size $d \times n$, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which can become prohibitive for modern neural networks with large hidden dimensions.

To address this challenge, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure for the orthogonal matrix. Instead of learning a single large orthogonal matrix, the matrix $\bm{R}$ is decomposed into $r$ smaller block matrices:

$$\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r) = \begin{bmatrix} \bm{R}_1 \in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r}) & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \bm{R}_r \in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r}) \end{bmatrix} \in \text{O}(d)$$

where $\text{O}(d)$ denotes the orthogonal group in dimension $d$, and each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d/r \times d/r}$ is an orthogonal matrix. The block-diagonal structure means that $\bm{R}$ is composed of $r$ independent orthogonal blocks along the diagonal, with zeros elsewhere.

This block-diagonal parameterization significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters. For an orthogonal matrix of size $d \times d$, the number of free parameters is $d(d-1)/2$, resulting in a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. With the $r$-block diagonal structure, the number of parameters becomes $d(d/r - 1)/2$, resulting in a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. The reduction factor $r$ directly controls the trade-off between parameter efficiency and representational capacity.

Further parameter reduction can be achieved by sharing the block matrices across all blocks, that is, $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$. This shared-block approach reduces the parameter complexity to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$, since only one block needs to be learned and then replicated $r$ times.

Importantly, the block-diagonal structure preserves the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$. Each block $\bm{R}_i$ is an orthogonal matrix, and the direct sum of orthogonal matrices is itself orthogonal. This means that the hyperspherical energy is still preserved under the block-diagonal orthogonal transformation, even though the flexibility of the transformation is reduced.

The block-diagonal structure also has an interesting interpretation when applied to convolution layers. When the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, similar to learning depth-wise convolution kernels. When all blocks are shared, OFT transforms neurons with an orthogonal matrix shared across channels, providing a form of parameter sharing that can improve generalization.

For transformers, where OFT is typically applied to attention weights, the block-diagonal structure can be applied per-head or shared across heads, providing flexibility in how the orthogonal transformation is structured.

# Complexity Analysis: OFT vs. LoRA

A comprehensive comparison between OFT and LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) reveals fundamental differences in their approach to parameter-efficient finetuning. Both methods aim to reduce the number of trainable parameters while maintaining the ability to adapt to downstream tasks, but they achieve this through different structural constraints.

LoRA introduces a low-rank constraint for the weight update. Given a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, LoRA adds a low-rank matrix $\Delta \bm{W} = \bm{B}\bm{A}$ where $\bm{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r'}$ and $\bm{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{r' \times n}$, with $r' \ll \min(d, n)$. The forward pass becomes $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0 + \bm{B}\bm{A})^\top \bm{x}$. The number of trainable parameters in LoRA is $r'(d + n)$, which scales linearly with the dimensions $d$ and $n$.

In contrast, OFT applies a multiplicative orthogonal transformation. The number of trainable parameters for OFT with $r$ blocks is $d(d/r - 1)/2$. When block sharing is applied, this becomes approximately $d^2/(2r^2)$.

To illustrate the difference concretely, consider a weight matrix of size $128 \times 128$. LoRA with rank $r' = 8$ has $8 \times (128 + 128) = 2,048$ trainable parameters. OFT with $r = 8$ blocks (without block sharing) has $128 \times (128/8 - 1)/2 = 128 \times 15/2 = 960$ trainable parameters. This example shows that OFT can achieve comparable or better parameter efficiency than LoRA.

The complexity comparison becomes more nuanced when considering the relationship between the reduction factors and the dimension $d$. If both $r$ and $r'$ are proportional to $d$ (e.g., $r = r' = \alpha d$ for some constant $0 < \alpha \leq 1$), then LoRA's parameter complexity becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$, while OFT's parameter complexity becomes $\mathcal{O}(d)$. Under this scaling, OFT is asymptotically more parameter-efficient.

Beyond parameter efficiency, OFT and LoRA differ fundamentally in their structural constraints. LoRA exploits a low-rank structure, assuming that the weight update can be well-approximated by a low-rank matrix. OFT exploits a sparsity structure through the block-diagonal orthogonality, assuming that the orthogonal transformation can be decomposed into independent blocks.

These different approaches have implications for finetuning behavior. LoRA's low-rank constraint controls the magnitude of the weight update by limiting the rank, while OFT's orthogonal constraint controls the angular relationship between neurons by preserving hyperspherical energy. The paper demonstrates that OFT achieves faster convergence and better stability compared to LoRA across both subject-driven generation and controllable generation tasks, suggesting that preserving the angular structure is more important than limiting the rank for these applications.

Furthermore, OFT introduces no additional inference overhead. After finetuning, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain the equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This means the inference speed remains identical to the pretrained model, whereas methods like ControlNet introduce additional inference overhead through added network branches.

# Enhancing Stability: Constrained and Re-scaled OFT

# The Need for Stability: Balancing Flexibility and Regularity

The challenge of finetuning large pretrained models lies in navigating a fundamental tension between adaptability and preservation. When we finetune a text-to-image diffusion model for a downstream task, we want the model to learn new capabilities—such as generating images of a specific subject or responding to new control signals—while simultaneously retaining the rich semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining. This tension manifests as a trade-off between flexibility and regularity, and understanding this trade-off is essential for designing effective finetuning strategies.

Standard finetuning approaches, which directly update model weights using gradient descent with a small learning rate, represent the most flexible end of the spectrum. These methods place minimal constraints on how the model can change, allowing the weights to move freely in parameter space. However, this freedom comes at a significant cost. Without proper constraints, standard finetuning often exhibits poor stability and can easily cause model collapse, where the model loses its generative capabilities entirely. The phenomenon of model collapse is particularly pronounced in text-to-image diffusion models, where excessive finetuning can destroy the semantic structures that enable coherent image generation.

The paper identifies a key conceptual gap in existing approaches: the assumption that a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned model and the pretrained model indicates better preservation of pretrained abilities. While this assumption may occasionally hold, the Euclidean difference alone is unable to fully capture the degree of semantic preservation. A more structural measure is needed to characterize the difference between the finetuned and pretrained models. This observation motivates the development of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, and subsequently, the constrained and re-scaled variants that further refine this balance.

The block-diagonal parameterization used in OFT can itself be viewed as a form of regularization. By structuring the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal matrix with $r$ blocks, the method constrains the space of allowable transformations. Each block operates on a subset of neuron dimensions, and this structural constraint limits the complexity of the transformation while still preserving the essential property of maintaining hyperspherical energy. The choice of block size represents a knob that practitioners can adjust: fewer blocks (closer to $r=1$) provide more flexibility, while more blocks impose stronger regularization.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): The Radius Constraint

While OFT successfully preserves the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model through orthogonal transformations, there remains room for improvement in terms of stability. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) builds upon OFT by introducing an additional constraint that limits how far the finetuned model can deviate from the pretrained model. This constraint takes the form of a radius constraint on the hypersphere, ensuring that the finetuned weights remain within a controlled neighborhood of the pretrained weights.

The mathematical formulation of COFT modifies the forward pass of a fully connected layer as follows. Given a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ and input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the output $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is computed as:

$$\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$$

subject to two constraints. The first constraint is the orthogonality condition from standard OFT: $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$. The second, novel constraint is the deviation constraint: $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This $\epsilon$-deviation constraint ensures that the orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ cannot stray too far from the identity matrix, which in turn ensures that the finetuned weights $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ remain close to the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$.

COFT can be understood as combining two explicit constraints. The first constraint, inherited from OFT, ensures minimal hyperspherical energy difference by preserving the pairwise angles between neurons. The second constraint explicitly limits the deviation from the pretrained model. While existing finetuning methods often implicitly encourage small deviations through techniques like small learning rates, COFT makes this constraint explicit and enforceable. This explicit formulation provides practitioners with direct control over the stability-flexibility trade-off through the hyperparameter $\epsilon$.

The radius constraint has an intuitive geometric interpretation. When $\epsilon$ is large, the constraint becomes loose, and COFT behaves similarly to standard OFT, allowing the orthogonal transformation to make substantial changes to the neuron directions. When $\epsilon$ is small, the constraint becomes tight, forcing $\bm{R}$ to remain close to the identity matrix, and thus the finetuned model behaves increasingly similarly to the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model. The paper demonstrates that COFT yields even better finetuning stability than OFT due to this explicit deviation constraint.

# Implementing the Constraint: Projected Gradient Descent via Neumann Series

The practical implementation of COFT requires addressing a technical challenge: how to incorporate the $\epsilon$-deviation constraint into the Cayley-parameterized orthogonal matrix. Recall that the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is constructed using Cayley parameterization as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. Directly enforcing the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ on this parameterization is nontrivial due to the matrix inverse involved in the Cayley transform.

The solution comes from analyzing the behavior of the Cayley transform through the lens of the Neumann series. Under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm, we can approximate the orthogonal matrix as:

$$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$$

This approximation reveals that when $\bm{Q}$ is small, the deviation $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is approximately proportional to $\bm{Q}$. Therefore, the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ can be translated into an equivalent constraint on $\bm{Q}$. Specifically, we can move the constraint inside the Cayley transform and enforce $\|\bm{Q} - \bm{0}\| \leq \epsilon'$, where $\bm{0}$ denotes an all-zero matrix and $\epsilon'$ is a hyperparameter related to $\epsilon$.

The constraint on $\bm{Q}$ is straightforward to enforce using projected gradient descent. At each optimization step, after computing the gradient update for $\bm{Q}$, we project $\bm{Q}$ back onto the feasible set defined by $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$. This projection ensures that $\bm{Q}$ never exceeds the specified bound, which in turn ensures that $\bm{R}$ never deviates too far from the identity matrix.

The initialization of $\bm{Q}$ plays an important role in ensuring that COFT starts from the pretrained model. To achieve identity initialization for the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, we initialize $\bm{Q}$ as an all-zero matrix. When $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$, the Cayley transform yields $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, which corresponds to no transformation of the pretrained weights. This initialization strategy mirrors the zero initialization used in methods like LoRA, ensuring that the finetuning process begins exactly at the pretrained model and gradually adapts from there.

# Re-scaled OFT: Learning Magnitude Scaling Coefficients

While OFT and COFT focus exclusively on modifying the directions of neurons through orthogonal transformations, there is an opportunity to enhance flexibility by also adjusting the magnitudes. This motivation leads to the development of Re-scaled OFT, a simple yet effective extension that learns magnitude scaling coefficients alongside the orthogonal transformation.

The key insight behind Re-scaled OFT is that re-scaling neurons—multiplying them by positive scalar values—does not change the hyperspherical energy. This is because hyperspherical energy is computed using normalized neurons: $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. When we multiply a neuron by any positive scalar, the normalization cancels out this scaling, leaving the normalized neuron unchanged. Therefore, we can modify neuron magnitudes without affecting the pairwise angles that OFT was designed to preserve.

The mathematical formulation of Re-scaled OFT introduces a learnable diagonal matrix $\bm{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ defined as $\bm{D} = \text{diag}(s_1, \cdots, s_n)$, where $s_1, \cdots, s_n$ are positive learnable parameters. The forward pass becomes:

$$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$$

where $\bm{R}$ is the orthogonal matrix and $\bm{W}^0$ is the pretrained weight matrix. In contrast to the original OFT forward pass where only $\bm{R}$ is learnable, Re-scaled OFT has both $\bm{D}$ and $\bm{R}$ as learnable parameters.

The diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ effectively learns a separate scaling coefficient for each neuron. Each diagonal element $s_i$ controls the magnitude of the $i$-th neuron in the output. Since the diagonal matrix adds only $n$ additional parameters (one per neuron), the increase in parameter count is negligible compared to the parameters needed for the orthogonal matrix. This makes Re-scaled OFT a parameter-efficient enhancement that provides additional flexibility without significant computational overhead.

The paper notes that while the experiments primarily use the original OFT to demonstrate the effectiveness of orthogonal transformation alone, Re-scaled OFT is generally better in practice. The added flexibility of learning magnitude scaling allows the model to make finer adjustments to the neuron activations, potentially capturing nuances that directional transformations alone might miss.

# Analyzing the Trade-off between Flexibility and Regularity

The development of OFT, COFT, and Re-scaled OFT reflects a systematic exploration of the trade-off between flexibility and regularity in finetuning. Understanding this trade-off provides valuable insights for practitioners who must choose the appropriate method for their specific application.

At one extreme, standard finetuning offers maximum flexibility by allowing unconstrained updates to all model weights. This flexibility, however, comes at the cost of poor stability. Without constraints, the optimization process can wander into regions of parameter space where the model loses its generative capabilities. The phenomenon of model collapse—where the finetuned model produces nonsensical or degraded outputs—is a direct consequence of excessive flexibility without adequate regularity.

OFT occupies a balanced position in this trade-off. By constraining weight updates to be orthogonal transformations, OFT ensures that the pairwise angles between neurons remain unchanged. This constraint preserves the hyperspherical energy, which the paper identifies as crucial for maintaining the semantic generation ability of the pretrained model. The orthogonality constraint provides sufficient regularity to prevent model collapse while remaining flexible enough to adapt to downstream tasks. The paper demonstrates that OFT converges faster than alternatives, which can be attributed to both the effectiveness of directional updates for modifying semantics and the smooth optimization landscape induced by finetuning on a hypersphere manifold.

COFT shifts the balance further toward regularity by introducing the $\epsilon$-deviation constraint. The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ provides direct control over the trade-off: larger values approach the flexibility of standard OFT, while smaller values enforce stronger adherence to the pretrained model. This explicit control simplifies hyperparameter tuning, as practitioners can set a relatively large iteration number and appropriate $\epsilon$ without worrying about model collapse. The paper shows that COFT achieves even better stability than OFT, making it particularly suitable for applications where robustness is paramount.

Re-scaled OFT represents a move toward greater flexibility while maintaining the benefits of orthogonal transformation. By allowing magnitude scaling through the diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$, Re-scaled OFT expands the expressiveness of the transformation without sacrificing the preservation of hyperspherical energy. The magnitude of each neuron becomes a learnable parameter, providing additional degrees of freedom that can capture task-specific variations. This extension demonstrates that the framework can be enhanced in complementary ways that respect the underlying principles.

The block-diagonal structure of the orthogonal matrix introduces another dimension to this trade-off. With fewer blocks (smaller $r$), each block has larger dimensions and can represent more complex transformations, increasing flexibility. With more blocks (larger $r$), each block becomes smaller and more constrained, increasing regularity. The extreme case of sharing all blocks imposes the strongest regularization, transforming all channels with the same orthogonal matrix. Practitioners can adjust $r$ based on the complexity of the downstream task and the desired balance between adaptability and stability.

# Experimental Evaluation and Applications

# Task 1: Subject-Driven Generation (DreamBooth Setting)

The first major application domain for Orthogonal Finetuning is subject-driven generation, a task that tests a model's ability to learn and preserve the visual identity of a specific subject from only a handful of reference images. This task follows the experimental framework established by DreamBooth, which has become a standard benchmark for evaluating personalization methods in text-to-image diffusion models. The fundamental challenge lies in adapting a large pretrained model to recognize and generate a novel subject—such as a specific pet, object, or person—while maintaining the model's原有的 generative capabilities and semantic understanding.

In the experimental setup, Stable Diffusion v1.5 serves as the foundational pretrained text-to-image model. This choice is deliberate, as Stable Diffusion represents one of the most capable and widely-studied open-source diffusion models, providing a strong baseline for comparison. The task requires the model to learn from just a few images of a subject, typically ranging from three to five photographs, and then generate novel images of that same subject in different contexts as specified by text prompts. For example, given several images of a specific dog, the model should be capable of generating images of that same dog wearing a yellow shirt, sitting in a bowl, or placed in entirely novel environments.

The baselines for comparison include DreamBooth and LoRA, both of which represent the current state-of-the-art approaches for subject-driven generation. DreamBooth finetunes the entire diffusion model using a specialized loss function that combines a reconstruction loss with a class-specific prior preservation loss. The prior preservation loss is crucial because it prevents the model from overfitting to the limited training images, which would otherwise cause language drift and mode collapse. LoRA, or Low-Rank Adaptation, takes a different approach by introducing trainable low-rank decomposition matrices into the existing weight matrices, thereby reducing the number of trainable parameters while still allowing for effective adaptation.

For fair comparison across all methods, the same loss function from DreamBooth is employed uniformly. This ensures that differences in performance can be attributed to the finetuning methodology itself rather than to variations in the training objective. The experimental design also controls for random initialization effects by repeating the finetuning process with 30 different random seeds for each method, minimizing the influence of stochastic factors on the reported results. This rigorous approach to experimental design allows for meaningful statistical comparisons between methods.

The training process involves finetuning the attention weights within the diffusion model, as this is where LoRA is typically applied. To maintain a fair comparison, OFT and its constrained variant COFT are applied to the same layers where LoRA operates. The hyperparameter settings for each baseline follow the recommendations from their respective original publications, using the best-known configurations to ensure that each method is performing at its optimal level. This attention to experimental fairness is essential for drawing valid conclusions about the relative merits of different finetuning approaches.

# Evaluation Metrics: Subject Fidelity (DINO, CLIP-I) and Text Alignment

Evaluating the quality of subject-driven generation requires a multi-faceted approach that captures different aspects of the generated images. The evaluation framework addresses four key dimensions: subject fidelity, text prompt fidelity, and sample diversity. Each of these dimensions is quantified using established metrics that have been validated in prior literature.

Subject fidelity measures how well the generated images preserve the visual identity of the original subject. Two complementary metrics are employed for this purpose: DINO and CLIP-I. The DINO metric leverages the ViT S/16 DINO embeddings, which are self-supervised vision transformer features trained using a distillation approach. To compute this metric, the average pairwise cosine similarity between the DINO embeddings of generated images and real reference images is calculated. The DINO embeddings are particularly valuable because they capture fine-grained visual features that are crucial for identity preservation, having been trained to produce consistent representations of the same object under different augmentations.

The CLIP-I metric provides a complementary measure of subject fidelity by computing the average pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between generated and real images. CLIP, or Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training, learns a joint embedding space for images and text, and its image encoder produces embeddings that capture semantic visual features. While DINO focuses on instance-level features, CLIP-I captures more semantic and high-level visual characteristics. Together, these two metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of how faithfully the generated images represent the original subject.

Text prompt fidelity, measured using CLIP-T, evaluates how well the generated images align with the text prompts provided during generation. This metric computes the average cosine similarity between the CLIP text embedding of the prompt and the CLIP image embedding of the generated images. High CLIP-T scores indicate that the generated images successfully incorporate the semantic elements specified in the text prompt, such as particular objects, actions, or settings. This metric is crucial because a successful subject-driven generation system must not only preserve the subject identity but also follow the text instructions for generating novel contexts.

Sample diversity is assessed using LPIPS, or Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity. Rather than measuring similarity between generated and reference images, LPIPS computes the average pairwise cosine similarity between generated images of the same subject using the same text prompt. Lower LPIPS scores indicate higher diversity, meaning the model is capable of generating varied outputs rather than producing nearly identical images each time. This metric is important for evaluating whether the finetuned model maintains the generative diversity that is characteristic of diffusion models, or whether it has collapsed into producing limited variations of the training images.

The combination of these four metrics enables a comprehensive evaluation that balances multiple objectives. A good finetuning method should achieve high DINO and CLIP-I scores (indicating strong subject preservation), high CLIP-T scores (indicating good text alignment), and low LPIPS scores (indicating maintained diversity). The relative performance across these metrics reveals trade-offs inherent in different finetuning approaches.

# Task 2: Controllable Generation (ControlNet Setting)

The second major application domain tests the framework's capability for controllable generation, where the goal is to enable a pretrained diffusion model to accept additional control signals beyond text prompts. This task follows the experimental paradigm established by ControlNet and T2I-Adapter, which have demonstrated impressive results in conditioning diffusion models on structural guidance such as edges, segmentation maps, and pose keypoints.

The experimental design encompasses three challenging controllable generation tasks, each testing different aspects of structural control. The first task, Canny edge to image (C2I), is conducted on the COCO dataset. Given a Canny edge map extracted from an image, the model must generate a photorealistic image that matches the structural outlines defined by the edge map while also satisfying a text prompt. This task tests the model's ability to hallucinate reasonable content within the constraints imposed by edge boundaries, requiring it to infer textures, colors, and semantic content from sparse structural information.

The second task, segmentation map to image (S2I), is performed on the ADE20K scene parsing dataset. Given a semantic segmentation map that assigns a class label to each pixel, the model must generate an image where the visual content matches the specified semantic regions. This task is particularly challenging because it requires the model to understand both the semantic meaning of different regions and the visual coherence required to create realistic transitions between adjacent semantic areas.

The third task, landmark to face (L2F), uses the CelebA-HQ dataset to test facial generation conditioned on landmark keypoints. Given a set of facial landmarks specifying the positions of eyes, nose, mouth, and face outline, the model must generate a face that precisely matches the specified pose and structure. This task demands high precision in spatial control because small deviations in landmark alignment can result in noticeable artifacts or unnatural facial appearances.

The baseline methods for comparison include ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. ControlNet achieves controllability by training a separate copy of the encoder blocks that learn to process the additional control signal and inject it into the pretrained diffusion model. T2I-Adapter takes a similar approach but uses a lightweight adapter network to align the control signal with the diffusion model's internal representations. These adapter-based approaches introduce additional parameters and computational overhead, whereas OFT and COFT modify the existing weights directly without adding architectural complexity.

All methods are trained for 20 epochs on each dataset, providing sufficient training time for convergence while maintaining consistency across experimental conditions. The evaluation employs the same Stable Diffusion v1.5 backbone for all methods, ensuring that performance differences arise from the finetuning methodology rather than differences in the underlying model architecture.

# A Novel Metric: Control Consistency for Evaluating Controllability

A significant contribution of the experimental framework is the introduction of a new evaluation metric called control consistency. Traditional metrics for evaluating image generation quality, such as FID or CLIP score, are insufficient for assessing controllable generation because they do not measure whether the generated images actually satisfy the structural constraints imposed by the control signals. Control consistency addresses this gap by quantifying how accurately the generated images match the input control conditions.

The fundamental idea behind control consistency is elegantly simple: extract the control signal from the generated image using an off-the-shelf detection or segmentation model, and then compare this extracted signal with the original input control signal. If the model has successfully learned to follow the control signal, the extracted signal should closely match the input. This approach transforms the evaluation problem into a standard computer vision task, leveraging existing high-performance models to provide reliable ground truth comparisons.

For the Canny edge to image task, the control consistency is measured using Intersection over Union (IoU) and F1 score. After generating an image from a given Canny edge map, Canny edge detection is applied to the generated image to extract the edges. The IoU between the extracted edges and the original input edges quantifies the spatial overlap, while the F1 score provides a balanced measure of precision and recall. These metrics directly assess whether the structural boundaries in the generated image correspond to the specified edge constraints.

For the segmentation map to image task, the control consistency metrics include mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy. A semantic segmentation model is applied to the generated image to obtain a predicted segmentation map, which is then compared pixel-by-pixel with the input segmentation map. Mean IoU averages the intersection over union across all semantic classes, providing equal weight to both common and rare classes. Mean accuracy computes the average per-class pixel accuracy, while overall accuracy measures the percentage of correctly classified pixels across the entire image.

For the landmark to face task, the control consistency is quantified using the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the control landmarks and the predicted landmarks. A facial landmark detection model extracts the keypoint locations from the generated face image, and the Euclidean distance between these detected landmarks and the input landmark coordinates is computed and averaged across all keypoints. Lower distances indicate more accurate adherence to the specified facial structure.

This control consistency evaluation framework provides a rigorous and objective assessment of controllability that goes beyond subjective visual inspection. By grounding the evaluation in established computer vision models, the metric ensures that comparisons between methods are meaningful and reproducible. The use of multiple task-specific metrics also allows for nuanced analysis of performance across different types of control signals.

# Quantitative Results: Performance over Baselines

The quantitative results demonstrate consistent and significant improvements achieved by the OFT framework across both task domains. For subject-driven generation, the evaluation metrics reveal that both OFT and COFT substantially outperform DreamBooth and LoRA in preserving subject identity while maintaining competitive performance in text alignment and diversity.

In terms of subject fidelity metrics, OFT and COFT achieve considerably higher DINO and CLIP-I scores compared to both DreamBooth and LoRA. This improvement indicates that the orthogonal transformation approach more effectively preserves the visual identity of subjects in generated images. The superior DINO scores suggest that fine-grained instance-level features are better maintained, while the higher CLIP-I scores demonstrate improved preservation of semantic visual characteristics. These improvements are consistent across 30 different random seeds, demonstrating the robustness of the results.

The text prompt fidelity, measured by CLIP-T, shows that OFT and COFT achieve slightly better or comparable performance to the baselines. This finding is significant because it demonstrates that the improvements in subject fidelity do not come at the cost of reduced text adherence. The finetuned models remain capable of following complex text prompts that specify novel contexts and attributes. The diversity metric, measured by LPIPS, similarly shows competitive performance, indicating that the OFT framework maintains the generative diversity characteristic of diffusion models.

For controllable generation, the control consistency metrics reveal even more dramatic improvements. OFT and COFT yield substantially stronger and more accurate control than the baselines across all three tasks. The adapter-based approaches, T2I-Adapter and ControlNet, converge more slowly and achieve worse final results despite introducing additional parameters. This finding challenges the assumption that adding architectural complexity necessarily improves performance.

The comparison with LoRA is particularly instructive. While LoRA performs better than ControlNet on the segmentation to image task, it performs worse on the Canny edge to image and landmark to face tasks. More importantly, the performance ceiling of LoRA appears relatively low—even with careful hyperparameter tuning, LoRA cannot match the performance achieved by the OFT framework. In contrast, OFT's performance has not yet saturated; empirical observations indicate that performance continues to improve as the number of trainable parameters increases, suggesting potential for even stronger results with additional capacity.

The convergence speed analysis reveals another key advantage of the OFT framework. For the landmark to face task, plotting the face landmark error across training epochs shows that OFT and COFT achieve significantly faster convergence than the baselines. Specifically, it takes LoRA 20 epochs to reach the performance that OFT achieves by the 8th epoch. This 2.5x improvement in convergence speed has substantial practical implications, reducing computational costs and enabling faster iteration cycles during model development.

# Qualitative Analysis: Visualizing Stability and Convergence

The qualitative analysis provides intuitive insights into the behavior of different finetuning methods, revealing fundamental differences in training dynamics and stability. These visual comparisons complement the quantitative metrics by illustrating phenomena that numbers alone cannot capture.

The analysis of finetuning stability and convergence speed reveals striking differences between methods. After 400 training iterations, both OFT and COFT achieve good control over the generated images, producing recognizable subject identities in the specified contexts. In contrast, LoRA fails to preserve subject identity even at this early stage, generating images that bear little resemblance to the training subject. DreamBooth achieves reasonable control at 400 iterations, though with less precision than the OFT framework.

The divergence between methods becomes more pronounced with extended training. After 2000 iterations, DreamBooth begins generating collapsed images—a phenomenon where the model loses diversity and produces unrealistic or degraded outputs. This mode collapse represents a fundamental failure mode of naive finetuning, where the model overfits to the limited training data and loses its generative capabilities. LoRA exhibits different failure modes, such as generating incorrect attributes (for example, generating yellow fur instead of a yellow shirt). In stark contrast, both OFT and COFT continue to achieve stable and consistent control, demonstrating remarkable robustness to overfitting.

This insensitivity to the number of finetuning iterations has important practical implications. For DreamBooth and LoRA, achieving good results requires careful tuning of the iteration count to stop training at an optimal point before degradation occurs. Different subjects may require different iteration counts, adding complexity to the finetuning process. The OFT framework eliminates this concern—users can set a relatively large iteration count without risk of model collapse, greatly simplifying the hyperparameter tuning process. For COFT with an appropriate $\epsilon$ constraint, both the learning rate and iteration count become nearly effortless to configure.

The convergence behavior in controllable generation tasks provides additional evidence for the superior stability of the OFT framework. Visual comparison of generated faces across training epochs shows that OFT and COFT converge smoothly and stably, with the generated face pose gradually aligning with the control landmarks. This gradual, predictable progression allows practitioners to anticipate model behavior and make informed decisions about training duration. In contrast, ControlNet exhibits a phenomenon of sudden convergence, where controllability emerges abruptly after the 8th epoch. This discontinuous training dynamic makes it difficult to predict when the model will achieve acceptable performance and complicates decisions about when to stop training.

The data efficiency of OFT provides further evidence for its effectiveness. In one striking result, OFT achieves good convergence using only 5% of the ADE20K dataset, substantially outperforming both ControlNet and LoRA which require significantly more training data. This sample efficiency is particularly valuable in scenarios where labeled training data is scarce or expensive to obtain.

# Insights, Analysis, and Future Directions

Having explored the technical foundations and empirical performance of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), we now turn our attention to understanding why this method works so effectively and what its broader implications are for the field of text-to-image diffusion models. This chapter synthesizes the theoretical insights that underpin OFT's success, examines its practical advantages for real-world deployment, and outlines the open problems that present exciting opportunities for future research. By understanding these deeper principles, practitioners can better appreciate when and how to apply OFT, while researchers can identify promising directions for extending this work.

# Why OFT Converges Faster: Optimization Landscapes and Semantic Modifications

One of the most striking empirical findings in the OFT framework is its remarkable convergence speed compared to existing methods. Experiments demonstrate that OFT achieves convergence in approximately eight epochs, while methods like LoRA require up to twenty epochs to reach comparable performance levels. This substantial acceleration is not merely a happy coincidence but stems from fundamental properties of how OFT operates on neural network parameters. Understanding this phenomenon requires examining both the semantic implications of OFT's parameterization and the geometric properties of the optimization landscape it induces.

The first key insight comes from empirical observations about which aspects of neuron parameters carry semantic information. The authors conduct a compelling toy experiment using a standard convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images. During training, the network uses the standard inner product between convolution kernel weights $\bm{w}$ and input patches $\bm{x}$ to produce feature maps. At test time, the authors compare three methods for generating feature maps: using the standard inner product (as in training), using only magnitude information, and using only angular information. The results are striking: the angular information of neurons can almost perfectly recover the input images, while the magnitude of neurons contains no useful information. Importantly, this result holds even though cosine activation was not applied during training—the network was trained entirely using standard inner products.

This experiment carries a profound implication: the directions of neurons, rather than their magnitudes, play the major role in storing semantic information. When we finetune a pretrained model, our goal is typically to modify the semantic content of generated images while preserving the underlying generative capabilities. Given that angular information encodes semantics, it follows that modifying neuron directions should be the most effective strategy for semantic adaptation. This is precisely what OFT is designed to do—by applying orthogonal transformations, OFT systematically adjusts neuron directions while preserving their pairwise angular relationships.

The second factor contributing to OFT's rapid convergence relates to the geometric properties of the optimization landscape. OFT can be viewed as finetuning neurons on a smooth hypersphere manifold. When neurons are constrained to lie on a unit hypersphere and transformations are restricted to orthogonal operations, the resulting optimization landscape exhibits favorable properties that facilitate faster convergence. The manifold structure eliminates certain pathological behaviors that can arise in unconstrained optimization, such as the model drifting into regions of parameter space that produce degraded outputs. Previous work on orthogonal over-parameterized training has empirically verified these optimization benefits, and OFT inherits similar advantages.

Furthermore, the data efficiency of OFT is noteworthy. Experimental results show that OFT can converge effectively using only five percent of the training dataset—a remarkable feat that further underscores the efficiency of orthogonal parameterization. This combination of rapid convergence and data efficiency makes OFT particularly attractive for practical applications where computational resources and training data may be limited.

# Connection to Fourier Transforms: Phase Spectrum and Semantics

The intuition behind why orthogonal transformation works for finetuning neurons draws a fascinating parallel to signal processing, specifically the two-dimensional Fourier transform. In classical signal processing, an image can be decomposed into two components: the magnitude spectrum and the phase spectrum. The magnitude spectrum encodes information about the strength of different frequency components, while the phase spectrum encodes information about the alignment and positioning of these components relative to one another.

A remarkable property of the Fourier transform is that the phase spectrum preserves the major portion of an image's semantic content. This can be demonstrated through a simple but powerful experiment: if one takes the phase spectrum of an original image and combines it with a random magnitude spectrum, the resulting reconstruction—obtained through inverse Fourier transform—will still resemble the original image without significant loss of semantic content. The recognizable structure, objects, and overall composition remain intact despite the magnitude information being completely replaced with random values. Conversely, preserving the magnitude spectrum while randomizing the phase spectrum results in images that bear no resemblance to the original.

This phenomenon from signal processing provides deep insight into why orthogonal transformation is effective for finetuning. The phase spectrum, which represents angular information between the input and the basis functions in Fourier analysis, parallels the angular relationships between neurons in a neural network. Just as the phase spectrum encodes the essential semantic structure of an image in Fourier analysis, the angles between neurons encode the essential semantic knowledge captured by a neural network. This analogy suggests that modifying neuron directions—while preserving their pairwise angular relationships—is the key to semantically modifying generated images without destroying the underlying knowledge.

The connection becomes clearer when we consider what OFT actually does. By applying an orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer, OFT effectively rotates or reflects the entire set of neurons as a rigid structure. This operation preserves all pairwise angles between neurons, meaning that the relative angular relationships—which encode the semantic knowledge—are maintained. At the same time, the absolute directions of neurons are modified, allowing the model to adapt to new downstream tasks. This balance between modification and preservation is precisely what makes OFT effective: it provides sufficient flexibility to adapt the model's behavior while maintaining the structural relationships that encode pretrained knowledge.

The authors formalize this intuition through the concept of hyperspherical energy, which characterizes the pairwise neuron relationships on a unit hypersphere. The hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}$ is defined as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$, where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the normalized neuron. This measure captures the level of neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere and encodes the semantic structure of the network. By preserving hyperspherical energy through orthogonal transformation, OFT ensures that the semantic generation ability of the pretrained model is not destroyed during finetuning.

# Architecture Agnosticism: Applying OFT to Transformers and CNNs

One of the most practically valuable properties of OFT is its architecture-agnostic nature. The method can, in principle, be applied to any type of neural network, making it a versatile tool for practitioners working with different model architectures. This flexibility stems from the fact that orthogonal transformation is a general mathematical operation that can be applied to any weight matrix, regardless of the specific architectural context in which it is used.

For Transformer architectures, which form the backbone of modern text-to-image diffusion models, OFT integrates naturally with the standard model components. In Transformers, LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is typically applied to the attention weights, and to ensure fair comparison, the authors apply OFT to the same attention weight layers. The attention mechanism, which computes relationships between different positions in a sequence, relies on weight matrices that transform query, key, and value representations. OFT can be applied to any of these weight matrices by learning an orthogonal transformation matrix $\bm{R}$ that modifies the pretrained weights.

The application to convolutional neural networks (CNNs) reveals particularly interesting properties of OFT's block-diagonal structure. When finetuning convolutional layers, the block-diagonal parameterization of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ takes on meaningful interpretations. The block-diagonal form can be written as:

$$\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)=\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{R}_1\in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r}) & & \\
& \ddots & \\
& & \bm{R}_r\in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r})
\end{bmatrix}\in \text{O}(d)$$

where $\text{O}(d)$ denotes the orthogonal group in dimension $d$. When the number of blocks $r$ is set equal to the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, which is analogous to learning depth-wise convolution kernels—a technique widely used in efficient neural network architectures. Alternatively, when all blocks in $\bm{R}$ are shared, OFT transforms neurons using an orthogonal matrix that is applied uniformly across all channels, providing a different but equally valid parameterization strategy.

The versatility of OFT across architectures is further demonstrated by its application to various tasks and model types in the experimental section. The method is applied to Stable Diffusion v1.5, which uses a U-Net architecture with attention layers operating in a latent space. Despite the complexity of this architecture, OFT successfully adapts the model for both subject-driven generation and controllable generation tasks. The ability to work seamlessly across different architectural choices makes OFT a practical choice for practitioners who may need to finetune models with varying structural configurations.

# Deployment Efficiency: Zero Additional Inference Overhead

In the realm of practical machine learning deployment, computational efficiency during inference is often just as important—if not more important—than training efficiency. Production systems must serve predictions with minimal latency, and any additional computational overhead can translate into increased costs, slower response times, and poorer user experiences. A significant advantage of OFT is that it introduces no additional inference overhead to the finetuned model, making it immediately practical for real-world deployment.

To understand why OFT achieves zero inference overhead, consider how the method operates during training versus inference. During finetuning, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ that transforms the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to produce finetuned weights. The forward pass during training is computed as $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, which requires multiplying the orthogonal matrix with the pretrained weights at each forward pass. However, once training is complete and the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ has been learned, a simple optimization becomes possible.

In the inference stage, we can merge the learned orthogonal matrix into the pretrained weight matrix through a single matrix multiplication to obtain an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This merged weight matrix directly replaces the original pretrained weights in the model, and inference proceeds exactly as it would with the original model—no orthogonal transformation needs to be applied at inference time. The forward pass simply becomes $\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}$, which is computationally identical to the original pretrained model's forward pass.

This property stands in stark contrast to adapter-based methods like ControlNet, which introduce additional network branches that must be computed alongside the main model during inference. These additional branches effectively double (or more) the computational cost of generating each image, creating a significant burden for production deployments. OFT avoids this pitfall entirely by virtue of its multiplicative parameterization: once the orthogonal transformation is merged into the weights, it disappears as a separate computational entity.

The practical implications of this efficiency are substantial. For large-scale deployment of text-to-image diffusion models, where generating a single image already requires substantial computation, eliminating inference overhead translates directly into cost savings and improved throughput. Users deploying OFT-finetuned models need not worry about increased latency or computational requirements compared to the base pretrained model. This property also simplifies deployment logistics, as OFT-finetuned models can directly replace pretrained models in existing inference pipelines without modification.

# Limitations: Scalability of Matrix Inversion

While OFT demonstrates impressive performance across multiple metrics, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations that present opportunities for improvement. The primary technical limitation concerns the computational approach used to guarantee orthogonality of the transformation matrix.

OFT guarantees orthogonality through Cayley parametrization, which constructs the orthogonal matrix using the formula $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. This elegant mathematical formulation ensures that $\bm{R}$ is always an orthogonal matrix with determinant one (belonging to the special orthogonal group). However, the Cayley transform involves a matrix inverse operation, which scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^3)$ for a matrix of dimension $d$. This computational cost limits the scalability of OFT when applied to very large weight matrices.

The authors address this limitation using block-diagonal parametrization, which reduces the computational complexity by decomposing the orthogonal matrix into smaller independent blocks. For an orthogonal matrix with size $d\times d$, the number of parameters is $d(d-1)/2$, resulting in a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. For an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix, the number of parameters becomes $d(d/r-1)/2$, resulting in a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. While this strategy effectively mitigates scalability concerns, it introduces additional biases and limits flexibility, as each block operates independently on a subset of dimensions.

The challenge of performing matrix inversion in a differentiable and computationally efficient manner remains an open problem. Finding ways to speed up this operation while maintaining exact orthogonality would further improve OFT's applicability to larger models and would be a valuable contribution to the field.

Additionally, the parameter efficiency of OFT is largely dependent on the block-diagonal structure, which introduces a trade-off between efficiency and expressiveness. While the block-diagonal approach reduces the number of trainable parameters, it also constrains the set of orthogonal transformations that can be represented, potentially limiting the method's ability to capture certain types of adaptations. Finding ways to improve parameter efficiency without introducing additional bias or limiting flexibility remains an important open problem for future research.

# Future Directions: Compositionality of Orthogonal Matrices

Beyond addressing current limitations, OFT opens up several intriguing research directions that could have significant implications for how we think about model finetuning and knowledge composition. One particularly exciting avenue concerns the compositionality of orthogonal matrices.

The mathematical properties of orthogonal matrices create a unique opportunity for compositionality that is not available in other finetuning approaches. When two orthogonal matrices are multiplied together, their product remains an orthogonal matrix. Specifically, if $\bm{R}_1$ and $\bm{R}_2$ are orthogonal matrices, then $\bm{R}_1 \bm{R}_2$ is also orthogonal. This closure property under multiplication suggests that orthogonal matrices learned through separate OFT finetuning tasks could potentially be combined to create models that preserve knowledge from multiple downstream tasks simultaneously.

Consider a scenario where one OFT-finetuned model specializes in subject-driven generation for a particular category of objects, while another OFT-finetuned model specializes in controllable generation using a specific type of control signal. If both models are produced through orthogonal transformations of the same pretrained weights, their respective orthogonal matrices could potentially be multiplied together to produce a combined transformation that enables both capabilities in a single model. Whether this composition operation would actually preserve the knowledge and capabilities from both downstream tasks remains an open question and an exciting direction for future investigation.

This compositionality property is fundamentally different from other finetuning approaches like LoRA, where additive weight updates from multiple tasks cannot be meaningfully combined through simple operations. The structured nature of orthogonal transformations creates a unique algebraic framework that may enable new forms of model composition that were not previously possible.

Furthermore, understanding the theoretical properties of orthogonal matrix composition could yield insights into how knowledge is structured within neural networks. If composed orthogonal matrices successfully preserve knowledge from multiple tasks, it would suggest that different types of knowledge can be encoded in orthogonal subspaces of the parameter space. Conversely, if composition fails to preserve knowledge, it would raise interesting questions about the interference between different adaptations and how such interference could be mitigated.

The exploration of compositionality in OFT also connects to broader questions about multi-task learning, continual learning, and modular neural network architectures. The ability to compose multiple specialized models into a single unified model without retraining would have substantial practical implications for deploying machine learning systems that need to handle diverse tasks and use cases.
