\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does GSPO use length normalization in the sequence-level importance ratio?
Question:
The GSPO paper defines the sequence-level importance ratio as:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i|x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

The paper mentions that this length normalization is needed to reduce variance and control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range. However, I'm struggling to understand the precise reasoning.

Without the length normalization, the sequence-level importance ratio would be the product of all token-level ratios, which could become extremely large or small depending on sequence length. But isn't this the correct importance weight for the sequence? Why is adding the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ exponent theoretically justified rather than just a practical trick?

Also, how does this affect the gradient computation? The gradient derivation shows that the length-normalized importance ratio multiplies the average of log-likelihood gradients across tokens. What would change if we didn't use length normalization?
Answer:
The length normalization in GSPO serves both theoretical and practical purposes that become clear when examining the gradient computation and the clipping mechanism.

**The core issue: products vs. geometric means**

The sequence-level importance ratio is the product of token-level ratios:

$$\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i|x)} = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$$

Without normalization, this product grows exponentially with sequence length. For a response of 1000 tokens where each token has an average ratio of 1.01, the sequence-level ratio would be approximately $1.01^{1000} \approx 20,959$—an extreme value that would dominate the gradient signal.

The $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ exponent transforms this product into a **geometric mean** of the token-level ratios. Geometric means are scale-invariant and bounded, making the importance ratio comparable across responses of different lengths.

**Why this matters for the clipping mechanism**

GSPO applies clipping to the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ at the sequence level. The paper explicitly states that without length normalization, "the importance ratios of responses with different lengths will require varying clipping ranges." With a unified numerical range for $s_i(\theta)$, a single clipping threshold (e.g., $1 \pm \varepsilon$) works consistently across all responses regardless of length.

**Effect on gradient computation**

The gradient derivation shows:

$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$$

The length-normalized importance ratio multiplies the **average** of per-token log-likelihood gradients. Without normalization, the unbounded importance ratio would dominate the gradient magnitude, causing training instability—especially for longer responses. The geometric mean keeps the ratio close to 1 when the policy hasn't drifted far, allowing the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ to properly weight the gradient direction.

**Theoretical justification**

The paper emphasizes that importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples from the behavior distribution. At the sequence level, this is meaningful—we have multiple sequence samples per batch. The length-normalized ratio correctly captures "how much the policy has drifted per token on average" rather than amplifying this drift by sequence length. This aligns with the paper's principle that "the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward."
