\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does the GSPO paper claim that GRPO misapplies importance sampling?
Question:
I'm reading the GSPO paper, which argues that GRPO has a fundamental flaw in its use of importance sampling. The paper states that importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution for the importance weight to effectively correct for distributional mismatch.

However, I'm confused about why this invalidates GRPO's token-level importance weights. In standard policy gradient methods like PPO, we use importance weights at the action level (which maps to tokens in the language model setting). Each token's importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$ seems like a valid importance ratio for correcting the distribution mismatch at that timestep.

Why does the paper claim this is a "misapplication"? Is the argument that since we only have one sample per next-token distribution, the importance weight cannot fulfill its role? But isn't this how all on-policy RL with importance sampling works?
Answer:
The GSPO paper's argument is more nuanced than simply claiming token-level importance sampling is invalid. The core issue is a **mismatch between the unit of reward and the unit of optimization**, combined with how token-level importance weights introduce unequal gradient weighting within a sequence.

## The Key Argument

The paper doesn't dispute that importance sampling works in standard RL settings. Instead, it identifies a structural problem specific to the LLM setting where:

1. **Rewards are sequence-level**: We get one reward $r(x, y_i)$ for the entire response, not per-token rewards
2. **Importance weights are token-level**: GRPO applies different weights to different tokens in the same sequence

The critical insight comes from the **gradient analysis** in Section 4.2. Compare the gradients:

**GRPO gradient:**
$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO} \propto \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$$

**GSPO gradient:**
$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} \propto \mathbb{E}\left[ s_i(\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$$

In GRPO, each token's gradient is weighted by its *own* importance ratio. These weights can vary dramatically—some tokens might have ratios near 0, others near $1+\varepsilon$ or higher. Since all tokens share the same advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ (computed from sequence-level reward), this unequal weighting is arbitrary and introduces instability. The paper notes these weights "can vary among $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ (for $\widehat{A}_{i} > 0$) or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ (for $\widehat{A}_{i} < 0$)" and are "not negligible."

## Why "Misapplication"?

The paper frames this as a misapplication because importance sampling corrects for distribution mismatch between the sampling and target distributions. When the target distribution is over **sequences** (since that's where rewards are defined), the correction should operate at the sequence level. Applying token-level corrections doesn't properly account for the fact that the *entire sequence*—not individual tokens—is what deviates from the target distribution.

The sequence-level importance weight $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$ captures how far the sampled response deviates from what the current policy would produce, which naturally aligns with sequence-level rewards. GSPO then applies this single weight uniformly to all tokens, avoiding the arbitrary per-token weight variations that cause instability in GRPO.

### Why does GSPO use length normalization in the sequence-level importance ratio?
Question:
The GSPO paper defines the sequence-level importance ratio as:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i|x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

The paper mentions that this length normalization is needed to reduce variance and control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range. However, I'm struggling to understand the precise reasoning.

Without the length normalization, the sequence-level importance ratio would be the product of all token-level ratios, which could become extremely large or small depending on sequence length. But isn't this the correct importance weight for the sequence? Why is adding the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ exponent theoretically justified rather than just a practical trick?

Also, how does this affect the gradient computation? The gradient derivation shows that the length-normalized importance ratio multiplies the average of log-likelihood gradients across tokens. What would change if we didn't use length normalization?
Answer:
The length normalization in GSPO serves both theoretical and practical purposes that become clear when examining the gradient computation and the clipping mechanism.

**The core issue: products vs. geometric means**

The sequence-level importance ratio is the product of token-level ratios:

$$\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i|x)} = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$$

Without normalization, this product grows exponentially with sequence length. For a response of 1000 tokens where each token has an average ratio of 1.01, the sequence-level ratio would be approximately $1.01^{1000} \approx 20,959$—an extreme value that would dominate the gradient signal.

The $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ exponent transforms this product into a **geometric mean** of the token-level ratios. Geometric means are scale-invariant and bounded, making the importance ratio comparable across responses of different lengths.

**Why this matters for the clipping mechanism**

GSPO applies clipping to the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ at the sequence level. The paper explicitly states that without length normalization, "the importance ratios of responses with different lengths will require varying clipping ranges." With a unified numerical range for $s_i(\theta)$, a single clipping threshold (e.g., $1 \pm \varepsilon$) works consistently across all responses regardless of length.

**Effect on gradient computation**

The gradient derivation shows:

$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$$

The length-normalized importance ratio multiplies the **average** of per-token log-likelihood gradients. Without normalization, the unbounded importance ratio would dominate the gradient magnitude, causing training instability—especially for longer responses. The geometric mean keeps the ratio close to 1 when the policy hasn't drifted far, allowing the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ to properly weight the gradient direction.

**Theoretical justification**

The paper emphasizes that importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples from the behavior distribution. At the sequence level, this is meaningful—we have multiple sequence samples per batch. The length-normalized ratio correctly captures "how much the policy has drifted per token on average" rather than amplifying this drift by sequence length. This aligns with the paper's principle that "the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward."

### Why do clipping ranges differ by orders of magnitude between GSPO and GRPO?
Question:
In the experiments section, the GSPO paper mentions setting clipping ranges to 3e-4 and 4e-4, while GRPO uses 0.2 and 0.27. This is a difference of about three orders of magnitude.

The paper briefly explains that this is due to the "distinct definitions of importance ratios," but I'm not clear on why exactly this leads to such dramatically different numerical scales.

In GRPO, the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ measures the probability ratio of a single token. In GSPO, the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ uses the geometric mean of token ratios. Intuitively, the geometric mean should be somewhere around 1 for typical policy updates, similar to individual token ratios.

Why then is there such a massive difference in the appropriate clipping ranges? Shouldn't both types of importance ratios be centered around 1 when the policy hasn't changed much?
Answer:
The key insight is that the sequence-level importance ratio in GSPO has **much lower variance** than token-level ratios in GRPO, which is a direct consequence of how the geometric mean aggregates information across tokens.

The GSPO importance ratio is defined as:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log w_{i,t}(\theta) \right)$$

This is the geometric mean of all token-level ratios in the sequence. By the law of large numbers, averaging reduces variance. Specifically, if each $\log w_{i,t}$ has variance $\sigma^2$ around its mean, then the variance of $\log s_i(\theta)$ is $\sigma^2 / |y_i|$.

For typical response lengths of hundreds or thousands of tokens, this means $s_i(\theta)$ is **much more tightly concentrated around 1** than individual token ratios $w_{i,t}$. The distribution of $s_i(\theta)$ is essentially a narrow spike near 1, whereas token-level ratios can vary substantially from token to token—even when the policy hasn't changed much overall.

Therefore, to achieve comparable clipping behavior (i.e., clipping samples that deviate meaningfully from the expected distribution), GSPO requires proportionally tighter clipping ranges. If responses are 1,000 tokens long on average, the standard deviation of $s_i(\theta)$ would be roughly $\sqrt{1000} \approx 32$ times smaller than that of $w_{i,t}$, explaining why GSPO's clipping ranges are roughly $1/30$ to $1/100$ the size of GRPO's ranges.

This variance reduction is precisely what the paper means by "distinct definitions of importance ratios"—the sequence-level geometric mean fundamentally changes the numerical scale of the quantity being clipped, not just its semantic meaning.

### What is 'expert-activation volatility' in MoE models and how does GSPO resolve it?
Question:
The GSPO paper mentions that when training Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models with GRPO, they encountered "expert-activation volatility" that prevents normal convergence. Specifically, they note that "roughly 10% of the experts activated under the new policy $\pi_\theta$ are different from those under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$."

I'm trying to understand:

1. Why does this expert switching happen more frequently during RL training compared to regular training?

2. Why does switching experts invalidate the token-level importance ratios? The paper seems to suggest that when different experts are activated, $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$ becomes unreliable. But shouldn't the importance ratio still correctly capture the probability ratio regardless of which expert produced it?

3. How does GSPO's sequence-level approach avoid this problem? The paper says GSPO "focuses only on sequence likelihood and is not sensitive to individual token likelihood," but the sequence likelihood is still computed from the same MoE model. Why doesn't expert switching affect sequence-level likelihoods?
Answer:
**Understanding Expert-Activation Volatility in MoE RL Training**

Let me address each of your questions in turn.

## 1. Why does expert switching happen more frequently during RL training?

During RL training, the policy undergoes more aggressive gradient updates compared to standard pretraining or supervised fine-tuning. In MoE models, the router network determines which expert processes each token, and this routing is highly sensitive to parameter changes. When using GRPO's token-level importance weights, the gradients become noisy and unstable (as the paper discusses in Section 3), which causes the routing patterns to shift dramatically between gradient updates. The paper notes this affects roughly `$10\%$` of experts after each update in the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B model, and the effect becomes more pronounced in deeper models.

## 2. Why does switching experts invalidate token-level importance ratios?

You raise a good point—the mathematical ratio `$\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$` does compute a valid probability ratio. However, the issue is that when different experts are activated for the same token position under `$\pi_\theta$` versus `$\pi_{\theta_{old}}$`, these probabilities are being computed by **fundamentally different computational pathways**.

The importance sampling framework assumes we're comparing how two *related* distributions weight the same sample. But when expert A processes a token under `$\pi_{\theta_{old}}$` and expert B processes it under `$\pi_\theta$`, we're essentially comparing outputs from different sub-networks. This creates large, erratic fluctuations in the token-level importance ratios `$w_{i,t}(\theta)$`, which—as Sections 3 and 4.2 explain—introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences and destabilizes training.

## 3. How does GSPO's sequence-level approach avoid this problem?

GSPO defines the importance ratio at the sequence level:
`$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i|x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$$`

This is effectively the **geometric mean** of all token-level importance ratios. When expert switching causes some token probabilities to spike upward and others to drop, these fluctuations average out in the sequence-level calculation.

Critically, as the gradient analysis in Section 4.2 shows, GSPO applies this single importance weight `$s_i(\theta)$` uniformly to all tokens in a response. In contrast, GRPO assigns potentially wildly different weights `$\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$` to each token, creating the instability the paper describes.

The paper's key insight is that "the MoE model always maintains its language modeling capability"—even if individual token probabilities fluctuate due to routing changes, the overall sequence likelihood `$\pi_\theta(y|x)$` remains relatively stable because the model's fundamental ability to assign probability to sequences is preserved. This allows GSPO to sidestep the expert-activation volatility problem entirely, eliminating the need for workarounds like Routing Replay.

### How does GSPO-token differ from standard GRPO if the importance ratios are numerically equal?
Question:
The GSPO paper introduces a token-level variant called GSPO-token with the importance ratio:

$$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})]}$$

where $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ denotes stop-gradient. The paper notes that since $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})]}$ equals 1 numerically, $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$.

This confuses me:

1. If $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ equals $s_i(\theta)$ numerically, what's the point of this definition? Why not just use $s_i(\theta)$ directly?

2. The gradient derivation shows that the stop-gradient operations change which terms receive gradients. But if the numerical values are the same, what exactly does the stop-gradient accomplish here?

3. How is GSPO-token fundamentally different from GRPO's approach? Both apply something at the token level, but GSPO-token apparently doesn't have the same instability issues. What's the key distinction?
Answer:
The distinction between GSPO-token and GRPO lies not in the numerical values but in **how gradients are computed and distributed across tokens**. Let me clarify each of your questions.

## Why the stop-gradient design matters

The key insight is that numerical equality does not imply gradient equality. The stop-gradient operations fundamentally change the computational graph, determining which terms receive gradient flow.

Consider the GSPO-token importance ratio:

$$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})]}$$

While the second factor numerically equals 1, the stop-gradient on the denominator means gradients only flow through the numerator. As the paper derives, this yields:

$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta) \propto s_i(\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$$

Notice that **all tokens in response $y_i$ share the same importance weight $s_i(\theta)$**—the sequence-level importance ratio.

## The fundamental difference from GRPO

In contrast, GRPO's gradient is:

$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) \propto \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$$

where $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$ varies **independently for each token**.

As the paper emphasizes, these unequal per-token weights in GRPO can vary wildly (in the range $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative ones), and their impact accumulates over long sequences, leading to instability and potential model collapse.

## Why this avoids GRPO's instability

The core issue identified in Section 3 is that GRPO's token-level importance weights fail to perform proper distribution correction—they're based on single samples from each next-token distribution, introducing high-variance noise. GSPO-token sidesteps this by:

1. Using the **sequence-level** importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ (a geometric mean across all tokens) as a uniform weight for all tokens in the response
2. Ensuring the gradient for each token is weighted equally rather than by potentially extreme per-token ratios

This design maintains the flexibility of token-level advantage customization (allowing $\widehat{A}_{i,t} \neq \widehat{A}_{i}$) while avoiding the instability of per-token importance weighting. As the paper notes, when $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$ for all tokens, GSPO-token is numerically identical to standard GSPO in both objective and gradient—yet retains the option for finer-grained control when needed (e.g., in multi-turn RL scenarios).

### Why does clipping more tokens lead to better training efficiency in GSPO?
Question:
In Section 5.2, the GSPO paper reports a counterintuitive finding: GSPO clips about two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO, yet achieves better training efficiency. The paper states:

> "This counter-intuitive finding — that clipping a much larger fraction of tokens leads to superior training efficiency — further indicates that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient for sample exploitation."

I'm not following this argument. If GSPO clips more samples, doesn't it mean GSPO is throwing away more training data? How can using fewer samples for gradient estimation be more efficient?

Also, why does GSPO clip so many more tokens? Is it because the clipping thresholds (3e-4 and 4e-4) are much smaller than GRPO's (0.2 and 0.27), so more samples fall outside the clipping range? Or is there something about the distribution of sequence-level importance ratios that leads to more clipping?
Answer:
The key to understanding this counterintuitive result lies in recognizing that "more clipping" doesn't mean "throwing away useful information"—it means "more aggressively filtering out noisy samples that would degrade the gradient estimate."

## Why GSPO Clips More Tokens

There are two factors here. First, GSPO uses much tighter clipping thresholds: the paper uses $[1 - 3\times 10^{-4}, 1 + 4\times 10^{-4}]$ for GSPO versus $[0.8, 1.27]$ for GRPO. But more fundamentally, GSPO clips at the **sequence level** while GRPO clips at the **token level**. This means when GSPO determines a response is "too off-policy" and clips it, **every token in that entire response is excluded from gradient computation**. The paper explicitly notes that "adjusting the clipping ranges does not alter the disparity in magnitude," indicating this isn't simply a hyperparameter artifact—the clipping unit itself is the driver.

The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$ has a different distribution than token-level ratios, being a geometric mean that typically exhibits lower variance and clusters more tightly around $1$. The tighter clipping thresholds are appropriate for this distribution.

## Why Fewer (Clipped) Samples Leads to Better Efficiency

The core insight is that GRPO's token-level importance weights are fundamentally unreliable for their intended purpose. The paper explains in Section 3 that importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples from the behavior distribution to correct for distributional mismatch. GRPO's per-token weight $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$ is based on a **single sample** from each next-token distribution, failing to provide meaningful distributional correction and introducing high-variance noise.

Consider the gradient comparison from Section 4.2: GRPO weights each token's gradient by its individual importance ratio, creating unequal weights across tokens that "can vary among $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$." These disparate weights accumulate unpredictably over long sequences. GSPO, by contrast, weights all tokens in a response equally by the sequence-level ratio.

When GSPO clips a sequence, it's identifying that sequence as providing an unreliable gradient estimate. Using such samples wouldn't add useful information—it would inject noise. The paper's argument is precisely that GRPO's apparent "efficiency" in using more tokens is illusory: those additional tokens contribute gradients corrupted by the token-level importance weight artifacts. GSPO's higher clipping rate reflects a more principled filtering of unreliable samples, and the remaining unclipped sequences provide cleaner, more effective gradient signals that translate into faster, more stable learning.

### How should I interpret the gradient analysis comparing GSPO and GRPO?
Question:
The GSPO paper derives gradients for both methods to highlight their differences. For GSPO (omitting clipping), the gradient is:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i|x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) \right]$$

And for GRPO:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) \right]$$

The paper concludes that GSPO "weights all the tokens in a response equally" while GRPO applies "unequal weights." But I'm confused:

1. In GSPO, the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is multiplied as a scalar factor outside the token average. Each token's gradient still receives the same weight. In GRPO, each token's gradient is weighted by its own importance ratio. Why is uniform weighting inherently better?

2. The paper claims GRPO's unequal weights "can vary among $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$" and that this "can lead to unpredictable consequences." But isn't this variation exactly what importance sampling is supposed to do—weight each token's contribution by how likely it is under the current vs. old policy?

3. Why doesn't the GSPO importance ratio introduce bias by weighting all tokens equally regardless of how much each token's probability has changed?
Answer:
The key insight from the paper is that the distinction isn't about "uniform weighting is better" in an absolute sense, but about **correctly applying importance sampling principles** to match the reward structure.

## The Core Issue: Misapplication of Importance Sampling

The paper's fundamental critique of GRPO lies in how importance sampling weights are applied. Standard importance sampling requires averaging over *multiple samples* from the behavior distribution for the weight $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ to effectively correct distributional mismatch.

In GRPO, the token-level importance ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$ is computed from a **single sample** at each token position. This doesn't perform proper distribution correction—it introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences.

## Addressing Your Questions

**1. Why is GSPO's weighting scheme correct?**

GSPO computes the importance ratio at the **sequence level**: $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. When taking the gradient of $\log \pi_\theta(y_i|x) = \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$, the importance weight naturally distributes uniformly across all tokens in the gradient. This isn't artificially imposed—it emerges from the mathematics of differentiating the sequence-level objective.

The uniform weighting arises because you're optimizing the *sequence likelihood*, and the gradient of sequence log-likelihood is the sum of token-level gradients. This correctly aligns with the fact that the reward is granted to the entire sequence.

**2. The GRPO variation isn't proper importance sampling**

The variation in token-level weights isn't the "intended behavior" of importance sampling. Since each $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is based on a single sample from $\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot|x, y_{i,<t})$, it cannot perform meaningful distribution correction. Instead, these fluctuating weights inject noise: when $\widehat{A}_i > 0$, weights can vary in $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$, and when $\widehat{A}_i < 0$, in $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$. Over long sequences, this accumulated noise destabilizes training.

**3. GSPO doesn't introduce bias**

GSPO's importance ratio correctly corrects for the off-policy distribution at the sequence level, which is where the reward signal originates. The gradient derivation shows:
$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}} = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i|x)} \widehat{A}_i \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_i|x)\right]$$

This is the proper importance sampling formulation—the sequence-level weight correctly reweights samples from $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$ to approximate expectations under $\pi_\theta$. The uniform distribution to tokens is a mathematical consequence, not an arbitrary design choice.

## Summary

The paper's principle is that **the optimization objective unit should match the reward unit**. Since rewards are sequence-level, importance sampling should operate at the sequence level. GSPO achieves this correctly; GRPO's token-level importance weights are theoretically misapplied, creating training instability rather than proper variance reduction.

### What is 'Routing Replay' and why does GSPO eliminate the need for it?
Question:
The GSPO paper describes a technique called "Routing Replay" used for MoE models with GRPO. The description says:

> "we cache the activated experts in $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$ and 'replay' these routing modes in $\pi_\theta$ when computing the importance ratios."

I'm confused about several aspects:

1. What does it mean to "replay" routing modes? Is this forcing the new policy to use the same experts as the old policy for the same tokens?

2. How does this relate to computing importance ratios? The paper says sharing the same activated network ensures $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$ and $\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$ are comparable, but why would different experts make them incomparable?

3. The paper mentions Routing Replay "can limit the actual capacity of the MoE model." Why is this the case?

4. Why does GSPO eliminate the need for this technique? The sequence likelihood is still computed by routing through different experts for different tokens. If expert routing is volatile, wouldn't that affect the sequence likelihood too?
Answer:
## Understanding Routing Replay and GSPO's Solution

Let me address your questions systematically based on the paper's explanation in Section 5.3.

### What "Replay" Means

Yes, your understanding is correct. "Replaying" routing modes means forcing the new policy $\pi_\theta$ to use the exact same experts for each token that were activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ during the original rollout. When computing the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$, both numerator and denominator are computed through the same activated sub-network, ensuring a meaningful comparison.

### Why Different Experts Make Importance Ratios Incomparable

This is the key insight. In MoE models, the routing decision (which expert processes which token) is itself part of the policy's stochastic behavior. After a gradient update, even for the exact same token with identical context, the new policy might route through different experts than the old policy—what the paper calls "expert-activation volatility." The paper notes that with the Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, roughly 10% of activated experts change after each gradient update.

When this happens, you're comparing likelihoods from two *different* networks: $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$ might be computed through Expert A while $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$ was computed through Expert B. The importance ratio then becomes meaningless—it reflects routing changes rather than meaningful policy shifts, introducing high-variance noise that the paper identifies as a core instability source.

### Why Routing Replay Limits MoE Capacity

The paper explicitly states that Routing Replay's practice of "reusing routing modes...can limit the actual capacity of the MoE model." The reason is intuitive: by forcing the model to use cached routing decisions rather than allowing it to dynamically route tokens to the most appropriate experts based on its current parameters, you're artificially constraining the model's ability to learn better routing strategies. The sparse activation pattern is frozen from the old policy rather than being optimized freely.

### Why GSPO Eliminates the Need

This is the elegant part. GSPO's key innovation is defining importance ratios at the *sequence level* rather than the token level:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

The paper explains that "GSPO focuses only on the sequence likelihood and is not sensitive to the individual token likelihood." While expert routing may change for individual tokens, these local fluctuations tend to average out across the entire sequence. As the paper states, "since the MoE model always maintains its language modeling capability, the sequence likelihood will not fluctuate drastically."

The mathematical reason is that the sequence likelihood is a product over all tokens. Routing changes that cause some tokens' likelihoods to fluctuate up and others down will largely cancel when aggregated into a sequence-level ratio. GSPO's sequence-level importance ratio thus remains stable and meaningful even when expert-activation volatility occurs, fundamentally resolving the problem without needing to constrain the routing mechanism.
