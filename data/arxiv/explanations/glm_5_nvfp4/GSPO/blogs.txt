# From Tokens to Sequences: How GSPO Solves the Stability Crisis in LLM Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning has become the engine behind the remarkable reasoning capabilities of modern language models. From competition-level mathematics to complex programming tasks, large-scale RL training has pushed models like Qwen3 to new heights. But there's been a lurking problem: training instability that can lead to catastrophic, irreversible model collapse.

The culprit? A fundamental flaw in how popular algorithms like GRPO handle importance sampling. In this post, we'll dive into why token-level importance ratios are problematic and how Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) fixes this by shifting the optimization unit from tokens to sequences.

## The Stability Crisis in LLM RL Training

When training large language models with reinforcement learning, stability is everything. Current state-of-the-art algorithms like GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) have enabled impressive results, but they suffer from severe stability issues when scaled to gigantic models. The result is often model collapse—a catastrophic failure where the model degrades irreversibly.

What's particularly frustrating is that once collapse occurs, there's often no recovering. Reverting to previous checkpoints, tuning hyperparameters, extending generation length, or switching RL queries—none of it helps. This suggests a fundamental problem in the algorithmic design itself.

## The Core Problem: Token-Level Importance Sampling

To understand the issue, we need to look at how GRPO works. In RL training, we typically sample responses from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and then update the current policy $\pi_\theta$. This creates an "off-policy" learning setting, where we need to correct for the distribution mismatch between the old and current policies.

The standard approach uses importance sampling weights. GRPO defines the importance ratio at the token level:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$$

This looks reasonable at first glance, but there's a critical flaw. The principle of importance sampling tells us:

$$\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)} \, f(z) \right]$$

This expectation relies on averaging over multiple samples from the behavior distribution. GRPO's token-level importance weight, however, is based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution. Instead of correcting the distributional mismatch, it introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients.

This noise accumulates over long sequences and gets amplified by the clipping mechanism, eventually leading to model collapse.

## The Mismatch Between Reward and Optimization

There's another perspective that reveals the same problem. In language model RL training, rewards are assigned to entire sequences, not individual tokens. A verifier scores a complete response $y$ to a query $x$, yielding a reward $r(x, y) \in [0, 1]$.

Yet GRPO applies off-policy correction at the token level. This creates a fundamental mismatch: the unit of optimization objective doesn't match the unit of reward.

This observation points to a core principle: **the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward**. Since rewards are granted at the sequence level, the importance weight should also be defined at the sequence level.

## GSPO: Sequence-Level Optimization

GSPO addresses this by defining the importance ratio based on sequence likelihood:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

The length normalization (raising to the power of $1/|y_i|$) is crucial. Without it, likelihood changes from just a few tokens could cause dramatic fluctuations in the importance ratio. The normalization also ensures that responses of different lengths can use the same clipping range.

GSPO's optimization objective becomes:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min \left( s_{i}(\theta)  \widehat{A}_{i},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i} \right) 
\right]$$

The advantage $\widehat{A}_{i}$ is computed by normalizing rewards across a group of responses to the same query, matching the sequence-level approach.

## Gradient Analysis: Why GSPO Is More Stable

The mathematical difference becomes clearest when we look at the gradients. In GRPO, each token's gradient is weighted by its own importance weight:

$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO} (\theta) \propto \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} 
\nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$$

These token-level weights can vary dramatically—they range from near zero to unbounded values. This unequal weighting introduces unpredictable dynamics as training progresses.

In contrast, GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally:

$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) \propto \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_{i} 
\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$$

The sequence-level importance weight applies uniformly across all tokens, eliminating the instability factor introduced by GRPO's unequal token weighting.

## Special Benefits for Mixture-of-Experts Models

GSPO's sequence-level approach is particularly valuable for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. These models have a unique stability challenge: expert-activation volatility. After gradient updates, the experts activated for the same response can change significantly—for a 48-layer MoE model, roughly 10% of activated experts differ between the old and new policies.

This volatility makes token-level importance ratios fluctuate wildly in GRPO. Previous workarounds like "Routing Replay"—caching and reusing expert routing patterns—added complexity and limited model capacity.

GSPO sidesteps this entirely. Because it focuses on sequence likelihood rather than individual token likelihoods, it's not sensitive to which specific experts are activated. The sequence likelihood remains stable as long as the model maintains its language modeling capability. This eliminates the need for complex stabilization strategies entirely.

## Results: Efficiency and Stability

Empirical results on models like Qwen3-30B-A3B demonstrate GSPO's advantages. Training proceeds stably throughout, with continuous performance improvements as compute scales. GSPO achieves better training accuracy and benchmark performance than GRPO under the same computational budget.

Perhaps counter-intuitively, GSPO clips significantly more tokens than GRPO—about two orders of magnitude more in terms of clipping fractions. Yet it still achieves superior training efficiency. This reveals that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient. Clipping more tokens actually means using cleaner learning signals.

## Implications for RL Infrastructure

GSPO also has practical benefits for RL infrastructure. In production systems, training engines (like Megatron) and inference engines (like vLLM) often have precision discrepancies. Standard practice is to recompute likelihoods with the training engine to ensure consistency.

But since GSPO operates on sequence-level likelihoods rather than token-level likelihoods, it's more tolerant of precision discrepancies. This opens the possibility of using likelihoods directly from inference engines, avoiding expensive recomputation—especially valuable for partial rollout and multi-turn RL scenarios.

## Conclusion

The shift from tokens to sequences in GSPO represents more than an incremental improvement—it's a fundamental fix to a core algorithmic flaw in GRPO. By aligning the importance sampling with the actual unit of reward, GSPO provides a mathematically sound foundation for stable, efficient RL training.

For practitioners scaling up LLM reinforcement learning, GSPO offers a path forward: stable training without model collapse, efficient use of compute, and natural compatibility with MoE architectures. The remarkable improvements in Qwen3 models stand as testament to its effectiveness.



# Taming the Experts: Why GSPO is a Game-Changer for Mixture-of-Experts Models

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the engine behind the latest leap in Large Language Model (LLM) capabilities. Models like the Qwen3 series rely on large-scale RL to develop deep reasoning skills for complex tasks. However, as models grow larger and sparser, particularly with Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, maintaining training stability has become a critical challenge. A new algorithm, Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), offers a robust solution to these stability issues, specifically addressing the unique difficulties of training sparse MoE models.

## The Instability of MoE Reinforcement Learning

Mixture-of-Experts models are highly efficient because they activate only a subset of their parameters (the "experts") for any given token. However, this sparse activation introduces a unique source of instability during RL training known as **expert-activation volatility**.

In algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the training objective relies on token-level importance ratios. This ratio compares the probability of a token under the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$ against the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$$

This works well for dense models, but in MoE models, the experts activated for a specific token can shift significantly after just a few gradient updates. For instance, in a 48-layer MoE model, roughly 10% of the activated experts can change between the old and new policies for the same response. This fluctuation causes the token-level importance ratios to swing wildly, invalidating the gradient estimates and leading to training divergence or catastrophic model collapse.

## The "Routing Replay" Workaround

Previously, researchers attempted to fix this volatility with a technique called **Routing Replay**. This strategy caches the activated experts from the old policy and forces the new policy to "replay" or reuse these same routing patterns when calculating the importance ratios.

While Routing Replay stabilizes the training by ensuring consistent network paths, it comes at a cost. It introduces additional memory and communication overhead and artificially constrains the model's capacity by preventing it from exploring new expert combinations. It was a necessary but cumbersome bandage for a fundamental algorithmic issue.

## GSPO: A Sequence-Level Solution

GSPO solves expert-activation volatility at the root by changing how the importance ratio is calculated. Instead of comparing token probabilities, GSPO computes the importance ratio based on the **sequence likelihood**:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

This approach aligns the optimization objective with the sequence-level reward. Because the importance ratio is now derived from the likelihood of the entire response, GSPO is no longer sensitive to the specific experts activated for individual tokens.

## Eliminating the Need for Workarounds

The shift to sequence-level optimization has profound implications for MoE training. Because GSPO focuses on the overall sequence likelihood rather than individual token probabilities, it is inherently robust to the shifting expert activations that plague GRPO. The sequence likelihood does not fluctuate drastically even if the underlying experts change, as long as the model maintains its language modeling capability.

Consequently, GSPO eliminates the need for Routing Replay entirely. It stabilizes MoE training naturally without artificial constraints, allowing the model to leverage its full capacity and simplifying the underlying RL infrastructure. This stability and efficiency were key factors in the success of the latest Qwen3 models, proving that a theoretically grounded algorithmic change is superior to complex engineering workarounds when scaling RL for sparse architectures.



# Beyond Performance: How GSPO Simplifies the Infrastructure of Large-Scale RL

When we discuss new reinforcement learning algorithms for large language models (LLMs), the conversation usually revolves around performance metrics: higher pass rates on math benchmarks, better code generation, or faster training convergence. The recent paper introducing Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) certainly delivers on these fronts, contributing to the impressive results seen in the Qwen3 models. However, focusing solely on performance misses a critical part of the story.

Hidden within the algorithmic details of GSPO is a significant engineering advantage: it simplifies RL infrastructure. By shifting from token-level to sequence-level optimization, GSPO offers a robust solution to the precision discrepancies that plague large-scale training systems.

## The Precision Mismatch Problem

In modern LLM training pipelines, we typically decouple the training engine (e.g., Megatron) from the inference engine (e.g., vLLM or SGLang). Inference engines are optimized for speed, while training engines are optimized for differentiation and distributed memory.

However, due to differences in kernel implementations and floating-point accumulation strategies, the log-likelihoods calculated by an inference engine often differ slightly from those calculated by the training engine. For algorithms like GRPO, which rely on token-level importance ratios, these tiny discrepancies are a major headache.

Consider the importance ratio in GRPO, defined for each token $y_{i,t}$:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$$

Because this ratio is calculated at the token level, even a minor precision mismatch for a single token can cause the ratio to fluctuate unpredictably. This forces engineers to adopt an expensive workaround: using the training engine to recompute the log-likelihoods of all sampled responses under the old policy to ensure numerical consistency. This recomputation step negates many of the efficiency benefits of using a fast inference engine in the first place.

## Tolerance Through Aggregation

GSPO solves this by fundamentally changing how the importance ratio is calculated. Instead of looking at tokens individually, GSPO defines the importance ratio based on the sequence likelihood:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

This formulation is the geometric mean of the token likelihood ratios. By aggregating over the entire sequence, GSPO averages out the noise. A minor precision error on one specific token is diluted by the stability of the hundreds or thousands of other tokens in the response.

As noted in the paper, sequence-level likelihoods are "much more tolerant of precision discrepancies." This robustness implies that we can trust the likelihoods returned by the inference engine directly, removing the need for expensive recomputation by the training engine.

## Streamlining the Training Pipeline

The implications for infrastructure are profound. By eliminating the need for recomputation, GSPO allows for a more streamlined training loop:

*   **Direct Optimization:** You can use log-probabilities generated by the inference engine directly for the optimization step.
*   **Resource Efficiency:** This saves significant computational cycles that were previously spent re-running inference data through the training model.
*   **Architecture Flexibility:** This is particularly beneficial for disaggregated training-inference frameworks and complex scenarios like partial rollouts or multi-turn RL, where managing state and precision is notoriously difficult.

## A Bonus for Mixture-of-Experts Models

The simplification extends to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models as well. MoE models suffer from "expert-activation volatility," where the specific experts activated for a token can shift after a gradient update. In token-level methods like GRPO, this instability invalidates the importance ratio, requiring a complex stabilization strategy known as "Routing Replay."

Because GSPO focuses on the sequence likelihood, it is less sensitive to which specific expert was activated for an individual token. The sequence likelihood remains stable even if the internal routing fluctuates. As the paper demonstrates, GSPO eliminates the need for Routing Replay entirely, removing yet another layer of infrastructure complexity.

## Conclusion

GSPO is more than just a performance boost; it is an engineering enabler. By aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward, it provides a more stable signal that is inherently resilient to the numerical quirks of modern hardware. For teams building large-scale RL infrastructure, GSPO offers a path to simpler, more efficient pipelines without sacrificing model quality.



# Unlocking Qwen3: The Role of Stable Reinforcement Learning in the Next Generation of AI

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the driving force behind the latest breakthroughs in large language models (LLMs). From solving competition-level mathematics to complex coding tasks, RL allows models to develop sophisticated reasoning capabilities that go far beyond what is possible with pre-training alone. However, as we push models to reason deeper and longer, we hit a critical bottleneck: stability. Training massive models with existing algorithms often leads to catastrophic "model collapse," rendering the model useless.

In the development of the latest Qwen3 models, researchers introduced **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**, a new algorithm designed to tackle these stability challenges head-on. By fixing fundamental flaws in how we optimize language models, GSPO has unlocked new levels of performance and efficiency. Let's dive into why stable RL is the key to the next generation of AI.

## The Instability Problem in Modern RL

To understand GSPO, we first need to look at the algorithms it aims to replace, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO gained popularity as an efficient alternative to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) because it eliminates the need for a heavy "value model," reducing memory overhead. Instead, it calculates advantages by comparing the rewards of different responses within a group.

However, GRPO has a hidden weakness. It relies on **token-level importance ratios**. When training LLMs, we typically generate data with an "old" policy and then update a "new" policy. To correct for this difference, we use an importance ratio—a mathematical weight that adjusts the gradient estimates.

GRPO applies this ratio at the token level, calculating $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$ for every single token generated.

The problem? This violates the basic principles of importance sampling. Importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples to correct for distributional mismatches. Since we only have one sample for each token position during training, this token-level weighting introduces high-variance noise. As models generate longer reasoning chains, this noise accumulates, eventually destabilizing training and causing model collapse.

## Enter GSPO: Optimization at the Sequence Level

GSPO solves this by changing the fundamental unit of optimization. Instead of looking at tokens, GSPO looks at the **sequence**.

The core insight is simple but profound: **the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward**. Since we assign a reward to an entire response, we should perform off-policy correction at the response level.

GSPO defines the importance ratio based on sequence likelihood:
$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

Here, the importance ratio reflects how far the entire response deviates from the old policy, normalized by the response length. This approach allows GSPO to apply clipping to entire responses rather than individual tokens, filtering out "off-policy" samples effectively without introducing gradient noise.

## Why This Matters for Gradient Estimation

The distinction between GSPO and GRPO becomes crystal clear when we look at the gradients.

In GRPO, the tokens in a response are weighted unequally based on their individual importance ratios. Since these ratios can fluctuate wildly—ranging from nearly 0 to over 1—the gradient updates become unpredictable.

In contrast, GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally. The gradient calculation ensures that the learning signal is consistent across the entire sequence. This removes the instability factor inherent in GRPO, leading to smoother convergence and more reliable training dynamics.

## Unlocking Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Training

One of the most significant impacts of GSPO is on **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)** models. MoE architectures are incredibly efficient but notoriously difficult to train with RL.

The challenge lies in "expert-activation volatility." In MoE models, different experts (sub-networks) are activated for different tokens. During RL updates, the specific experts activated for a given token can change drastically between the old and new policies. With GRPO's token-level ratios, this fluctuation invalidates the importance weights, causing training to diverge.

Previous workarounds involved "Routing Replay," a complex strategy that forced the new policy to reuse the old routing decisions. While this stabilized training, it added overhead and limited the model's capacity.

GSPO renders this workaround obsolete. Because GSPO focuses on sequence likelihood, it is not sensitive to which expert was activated for a specific token. It fundamentally resolves the instability issues in MoE training, allowing models like Qwen3-30B-A3B to train efficiently without artificial constraints.

## The Results: Efficiency and Performance

The theoretical advantages of GSPO translate directly into real-world gains. In experiments comparing GSPO to GRPO on the Qwen3-30B-A3B model, GSPO demonstrated:

*   **Superior Stability:** Training proceeded smoothly without the risk of irreversible model collapse.
*   **Better Efficiency:** GSPO achieved higher training accuracy and benchmark performance under the same computational budget.
*   **Simplified Infrastructure:** By eliminating the need for complex stabilization strategies like Routing Replay, GSPO simplifies the RL pipeline.

Perhaps the most compelling evidence is the role GSPO played in the Qwen3 release. The algorithm provided the stable foundation necessary to scale RL training, resulting in remarkable improvements in reasoning and problem-solving capabilities.

## Conclusion

As we demand more from our AI models—longer reasoning, deeper logic, and larger scales—the stability of the training process becomes paramount. GSPO represents a crucial step forward in RL algorithm design. By aligning the optimization objective with the sequence-level nature of language generation, GSPO eliminates the noise and instability that plagued previous approaches.

The success of Qwen3 serves as a powerful validation: stable, efficient algorithms are not just a theoretical nicety—they are the engines that drive the next generation of AI intelligence.
