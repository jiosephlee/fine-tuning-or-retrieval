Chapter 4: # The GSPO Algorithm

# Defining the Sequence-Level Importance Ratio

The cornerstone of Group Sequence Policy Optimization lies in its fundamental rethinking of how importance ratios should be defined and applied in the context of language model training. To understand this innovation, we must first appreciate the theoretical foundation upon which it is built and the critical shortcomings it addresses in prior approaches.

Importance sampling is a statistical technique used to estimate properties of a particular distribution while only having samples generated from a different distribution. In the context of reinforcement learning for language models, this becomes essential because we often need to optimize a policy $\pi_\theta$ using data that was generated from an older version of the policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The principle of importance sampling tells us that the expectation of a function $f$ under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ can be estimated by re-weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$ as follows:

$$\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)} \, f(z) \right].$$

This equation reveals a crucial insight: the importance weight $\frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)}$ corrects for the distributional mismatch between where the samples came from and where we want to evaluate the expectation. However, for this correction to work reliably, we need multiple samples from the behavior distribution—the theoretical guarantees depend on averaging over many samples.

The fundamental problem with previous algorithms like GRPO is that they apply importance weights at the token level. Specifically, GRPO computes the importance weight $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$ at each token position $t$. Since this weight is based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution, it fails to perform the intended distribution-correction role. Instead, it introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients.

GSPO takes a fundamentally different approach by recognizing that in language generation, the sequence-level importance weight $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y | x)}$ has a clear and meaningful theoretical interpretation. This ratio reflects how far the response $y$, which was sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (\cdot | x)$, deviates from what would be expected under the current policy $\pi_{\theta} (\cdot | x)$. This is precisely what we need: a measure of how "off-policy" a particular sequence is.

Consider what this means intuitively. When we sample a response $y$ from the old policy, we want to know whether this response is still representative of what the current policy would produce. If the sequence-level importance ratio is close to 1, the response is still likely under the current policy, and we can confidently use it for training. If the ratio deviates significantly from 1, the response has become too far removed from the current policy's distribution, and we should be cautious about using it for gradient updates.

This sequence-level approach aligns perfectly with how rewards are structured in language model training. The reward $r(x, y)$ is granted to the entire sequence, not to individual tokens. There is no meaningful way to assign credit or blame to specific tokens based solely on the sequence-level reward. Therefore, the unit of optimization—the sequence—should match the unit of reward.

# Length Normalization of Importance Ratios

A naive implementation of sequence-level importance ratios would simply compute $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y | x)}$ directly. However, this approach introduces significant practical challenges that GSPO addresses through a crucial modification: length normalization.

To understand why length normalization is necessary, recall that the likelihood of a sequence is computed as the product of token-level probabilities: $\pi_\theta (y | x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t} )$. When we form the ratio of two such products, even small changes in individual token probabilities can compound dramatically over long sequences. A response with 1000 tokens might have a vastly different importance ratio than a response with 100 tokens, even if the per-token probability changes are similar.

GSPO addresses this by defining the importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ with explicit length normalization:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.$$

This can be equivalently expressed in terms of log probabilities:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right).$$

The geometric interpretation of this formula is insightful: we are computing the geometric mean of the token-level likelihood ratios. This ensures that the importance ratio reflects the average change in likelihood across all tokens, rather than the accumulated product of changes.

Without length normalization, responses of different lengths would have importance ratios on vastly different scales. Long responses would tend to have more extreme importance ratios simply because more token probabilities are being multiplied together. This would make it impossible to use a single clipping range for all responses, as different response lengths would require different thresholds for what constitutes "too far off-policy."

Length normalization keeps the importance ratios within a unified numerical range, allowing GSPO to apply a consistent clipping threshold regardless of response length. It also reduces variance in the training signal, as the importance ratios become more stable and comparable across different samples.

# Sequence-Level Clipping and Optimization

With the properly defined and normalized importance ratio in hand, GSPO constructs its optimization objective. The clipping mechanism, borrowed from PPO, serves to prevent overly aggressive policy updates by limiting how much the policy can change in a single update step.

The GSPO objective function is:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \min \left( s_{i}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i} \right) \right].$$

The key difference from previous algorithms is that the clipping is applied to entire responses rather than individual tokens. When $s_{i}(\theta)$ falls outside the range $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$, the importance ratio is clipped, and the gradient contribution from that response is modified accordingly.

This sequence-level clipping has profound implications for training stability. In GRPO, each token's importance ratio is clipped independently, which can lead to inconsistent gradient updates within a single response. Some tokens might be clipped while others are not, creating a fragmented and potentially contradictory learning signal. GSPO's approach is coherent: either a response is included in the gradient update with its full sequence of tokens, or its contribution is limited by clipping.

The clipping ranges used in GSPO typically differ by orders of magnitude from those in GRPO. In the experiments described in the paper, GSPO uses clipping ranges of approximately $3 \times 10^{-4}$ to $4 \times 10^{-4}$, while GRPO uses ranges around 0.2 to 0.27. This difference reflects the distinct statistical properties of sequence-level versus token-level importance ratios.

An interesting empirical observation from the paper is that GSPO clips significantly more tokens than GRPO—a difference of about two orders of magnitude in clipping fractions. Despite this, GSPO achieves superior training efficiency. This counter-intuitive finding suggests that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and that GSPO's more selective approach provides a cleaner, more reliable learning signal.

# Group-Based Advantage Estimation

GSPO adopts a group-based approach to advantage estimation, similar to GRPO, which elegantly sidesteps the need for a separate value function approximator. This is particularly valuable for large language models, where training and maintaining a value model of comparable size to the policy model would double the memory and computational requirements.

Given a query $x$, GSPO samples $G$ responses from the old policy. Each response $y_i$ receives a reward $r(x, y_i)$ from a verifier. The advantage for each response is then computed relative to the other responses in the same group:

$$\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) }{ \mathrm{std} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) }.$$

This normalization serves several purposes. First, by subtracting the mean reward, it centers the advantages around zero, ensuring that responses with above-average rewards receive positive advantages and responses with below-average rewards receive negative advantages. Second, dividing by the standard deviation scales the advantages to a consistent range, making the optimization less sensitive to the absolute scale of rewards.

The advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ represents how much better or worse response $y_i$ is compared to the typical response in its group. A positive advantage indicates the response is better than average and should be made more likely; a negative advantage indicates the opposite. This relative comparison within a group is a form of self-supervised learning, where the model learns from its own attempts at solving a problem.

Importantly, all tokens in a response $y_i$ share the same advantage value $\widehat{A}_i$. This is consistent with the sequence-level approach: since the reward is assigned to the entire sequence, the credit or blame is distributed equally among all tokens. This stands in contrast to value-based methods like PPO, where each token has a potentially different advantage based on the value function's estimate of expected future rewards.

# GSPO-token: A Variant for Token-Level Advantage Customization

While the sequence-level approach of GSPO is well-suited for many scenarios, there are situations where finer-grained control over advantages is desirable. Multi-turn reinforcement learning, for example, might benefit from assigning different advantages to tokens based on their position or the turn in which they were generated.

To address this need, the paper introduces GSPO-token, a variant that maintains the core benefits of GSPO while allowing token-wise advantage customization. The objective function becomes:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \min \left( s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) \right].$$

The key innovation lies in how the token-level importance ratio $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is defined:

$$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg} \left[ s_{i}(\theta) \right] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] },$$

where $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ denotes the stop-gradient operation, which takes the numerical value but stops gradient propagation. This is equivalent to the `detach` operation in PyTorch.

At first glance, this formula might seem complex, but its purpose becomes clear upon closer examination. The stop-gradient operation ensures that the sequence-level importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ contributes to the importance weighting without introducing its own gradient terms. Similarly, the token-level likelihood ratio $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }$ has a numerical value of exactly 1, since the numerator and denominator are identical.

The consequence is that $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_{i}(\theta)$, the sequence-level importance ratio. This is crucial: it means GSPO-token inherits the stability benefits of GSPO's sequence-level importance weighting while gaining the flexibility to assign different advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to different tokens.

When all tokens in a response share the same advantage (i.e., $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$), GSPO-token is numerically identical to GSPO in terms of the optimization objective, clipping condition, and theoretical gradient. This equivalence means that practitioners can adopt GSPO-token as a drop-in replacement for GSPO, gaining additional flexibility without sacrificing any of GSPO's benefits.

The gradient derivation for GSPO-token reveals its elegant properties:

$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right].$$

Notice that all tokens in the response are weighted equally by the sequence-level importance ratio, and the gradient of each token's log-likelihood is scaled by its corresponding advantage. This is fundamentally different from GRPO, where tokens are weighted by their individual importance weights, which can vary widely and introduce instability.
