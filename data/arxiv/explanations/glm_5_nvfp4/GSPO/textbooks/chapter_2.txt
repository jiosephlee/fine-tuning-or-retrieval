Chapter 2: # Background: Foundations of PPO and GRPO

## Autoregressive Language Models as Policies

To understand the foundations of reinforcement learning for language models, we must first establish how language models are framed within the RL paradigm. In this framework, an autoregressive language model serves as the policy that we seek to optimize. Concretely, a language model parameterized by $\theta$ is defined as a policy $\pi_\theta$. This policy takes a query $x$ as input and generates a response $y$ in an autoregressive manner, meaning each token is generated conditioned on all previously generated tokens.

The notation we use throughout this discussion is as follows: $x$ denotes a query, and $\mathcal{D}$ represents the query set from which queries are sampled. Given a response $y$ to a query $x$, its likelihood under the policy $\pi_\theta$ is denoted as $\pi_\theta (y | x)$. Due to the autoregressive nature of language generation, this sequence likelihood decomposes into the product of token-level conditional probabilities:

$$\pi_\theta (y | x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t} ),$$

where $|y|$ denotes the number of tokens in the response $y$, $y_t$ is the $t$-th token in the sequence, and $y_{<t}$ represents all tokens preceding position $t$. Each factor $\pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t})$ is the probability distribution over the vocabulary for the next token, conditioned on the query and all previously generated tokens.

The quality of a generated response is assessed by a verifier $r$, which scores a query-response pair $(x, y)$ and produces a reward $r(x, y) \in [0, 1]$. This reward signal guides the policy optimization, where the objective is to adjust the model parameters $\theta$ such that the policy generates responses that receive higher rewards. The challenge lies in how to perform this optimization efficiently and stably, which brings us to the algorithmic foundations of Proximal Policy Optimization.

## Proximal Policy Optimization and the Clipping Mechanism

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) represents a landmark algorithm in reinforcement learning, designed to constrain policy updates within a proximal region of the old policy to ensure stable training. The fundamental idea behind PPO is that large, uncontrolled policy updates can destabilize training, leading to catastrophic forgetting or collapse. To address this, PPO introduces a clipping mechanism that limits how far the new policy can deviate from the old policy.

Using samples generated from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, PPO optimizes the following objective:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \min \left( w_{t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{t}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( w_{t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon}\right) \widehat{A}_{t} \right) \right],$$

where the importance ratio of token $y_t$ is defined as:

$$w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t})}.$$

The importance ratio $w_{t}(\theta)$ measures how much the probability of generating token $y_t$ has changed under the new policy $\pi_\theta$ compared to the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ from which the sample was drawn. This ratio is central to off-policy reinforcement learning, as it corrects for the discrepancy between the distribution from which samples were drawn and the distribution we wish to optimize.

The clipping mechanism operates by constraining the importance ratio within a range $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$, where $\varepsilon$ is a hyperparameter controlling the clipping range. When the importance ratio falls outside this range, the objective "clips" the ratio to the boundary value, effectively preventing the policy from being incentivized to make updates that would cause it to deviate too far from the old policy. The $\min$ operator in the objective ensures that the clipped surrogate objective forms a lower bound on the unclipped objective, providing a pessimistic estimate that guards against overly optimistic policy updates.

The advantage $\widehat{A}_{t}$ of token $y_t$ represents how much better or worse the action (token selection) at position $t$ is compared to the expected value under the current policy. This advantage estimation is crucial for guiding the direction and magnitude of policy updates, and in PPO, it is estimated by a separate value model.

## The Reliance on Value Models in PPO

A core practical challenge of PPO lies in its heavy dependence on the value model for advantage estimation. The value model, also known as a critic, estimates the expected cumulative reward from any given state, providing a baseline against which the actual rewards are compared to compute advantages. This comparison allows the algorithm to distinguish between actions that are genuinely good versus those that merely appear good due to stochasticity in the environment.

The reliance on the value model introduces several significant burdens. First, the value model typically has a similar architectural size to the policy model, meaning it has a comparable number of parameters. This effectively doubles the memory footprint during training, as both the policy model and value model must be loaded and maintained simultaneously. For large language models where parameters already number in the billions, this memory burden is considerable.

Second, the computational cost of training is substantially increased. The value model must be trained alongside the policy model, requiring forward passes, backward passes, and parameter updates for both networks. This doubles the per-step computational requirements for the critic-related components, which can become a bottleneck when scaling to larger models and longer training runs.

Third, and perhaps most critically, the algorithmic effectiveness of PPO hinges on the reliability of the value estimates. If the value model produces inaccurate estimates, the computed advantages will be incorrect, leading to misguided policy updates. The value model must learn to accurately predict expected returns across a vast space of possible states (partial sequences), which is inherently challenging. The difficulty is further compounded when scaling to longer responses and more complex tasks, as the value model must maintain accuracy over extended temporal horizons.

Acquiring a reliable value model is not merely a matter of training longer or with more data. The value function approximation problem becomes harder as the space of possible partial sequences grows exponentially with sequence length. Furthermore, the value model must generalize to states not encountered during training, which is particularly challenging when the policy being trained is constantly changing. This interdependence between the policy and value model creates a moving target problem: as the policy improves, the value model must continually adapt to provide accurate estimates under the new policy distribution.

## Group Relative Policy Optimization and Group-Based Advantages

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) emerged as an algorithm designed to address the practical challenges of PPO by eliminating the need for a value model. The key insight behind GRPO is that rather than training a separate value model to estimate advantages, one can compute relative advantages by comparing multiple responses to the same query.

In GRPO, instead of generating a single response per query, the algorithm generates $G$ responses, forming a group. The advantage of each response is then computed relative to the other responses in the group. This group-based advantage estimation allows the algorithm to bypass the need for a learned value function entirely.

The GRPO optimization objective is formulated as:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \min \left( w_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( w_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon}\right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) \right],$$

where $G$ is the group size, representing the number of generated responses to each query $x$. The importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ for the $t$-th token in the $i$-th response is:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}.$$

The advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ in GRPO takes a particularly simple form. Notably, all tokens in a given response $y_i$ share the same advantage value:

$$\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) }{ \mathrm{std} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) }.$$

This formulation normalizes the rewards within each group by subtracting the mean reward and dividing by the standard deviation. Responses that receive rewards above the group mean receive positive advantages, while those below the mean receive negative advantages. This relative comparison provides a meaningful signal for policy improvement without requiring a separate value model. The normalization by the standard deviation ensures that the advantage values are on a consistent scale regardless of the absolute magnitude of the rewards.

The elegance of GRPO lies in its simplicity: by generating multiple responses and comparing them against each other, it transforms what would otherwise require a learned function approximation into a simple statistical computation. This approach is particularly well-suited to settings where rewards can be computed for complete responses, such as verifying whether a generated program passes test cases or whether a mathematical derivation arrives at the correct answer.

## Token-Level Importance Ratios in Existing Methods

Both PPO and GRPO share a fundamental characteristic: they compute and apply importance ratios at the token level. This design choice has deep implications for the training dynamics and stability of these algorithms.

The token-level importance ratio $w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t})}$ compares the probability of generating a specific token $y_t$ under the new policy versus the old policy, conditioned on the same prefix. In PPO and GRPO, this ratio is computed independently for each token position and used to weight the corresponding token's contribution to the overall objective.

The objective functions of both algorithms average over all token positions in a response. In PPO, this appears as $\frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}$, averaging over all tokens. Similarly, GRPO employs $\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}$, averaging over all responses in the group and all tokens within each response.

This token-level approach raises a subtle but important consideration. The clipping mechanism operates on each token's importance ratio independently. When a token's importance ratio falls outside the clipping range, that specific token's contribution to the gradient is modified. However, the clipping patterns can vary significantly across different positions in a sequence, creating heterogeneous treatment of different parts of the same response.

The use of token-level importance ratios becomes particularly relevant in the context of large-scale training. As model sizes grow and response lengths increase, practitioners typically partition large batches of rollout data into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates. This procedure introduces an off-policy learning setting, where responses are sampled from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ rather than the current policy $\pi_\theta$ being optimized. The importance ratios are meant to correct for this distributional mismatch, enabling the use of samples from one distribution to estimate gradients for another.

Understanding this token-level formulation is essential for appreciating the innovations introduced by sequence-level approaches. The fundamental question that arises is whether the unit of optimization—tokens—properly matches the unit of reward—complete sequences. This mismatch between token-level optimization and sequence-level rewards motivates the exploration of alternative formulations that align these units more coherently.
