Chapter 1: # Introduction: The Role of Reinforcement Learning in Scaling LLMs

# The Paradigm Shift: Using RL to Enhance Reasoning in LLMs

Reinforcement learning has emerged as a pivotal paradigm for scaling language models, fundamentally transforming how we approach the development of artificial intelligence systems. In recent years, the field has witnessed a remarkable evolution from purely supervised learning approaches to sophisticated reinforcement learning frameworks that enable language models to develop deeper reasoning capabilities. This shift represents more than just a technical adjustment; it constitutes a fundamental reimagining of how large language models can be trained to tackle increasingly complex cognitive tasks.

Through large-scale reinforcement learning, language models develop the capability to tackle sophisticated problems that were previously considered the exclusive domain of human experts. Competition-level mathematics and programming represent two domains where this advancement has been particularly pronounced. The key insight driving this paradigm shift is that reinforcement learning enables models to undertake deeper and longer reasoning processes, moving beyond simple pattern matching to engage in genuine problem-solving strategies. When a language model is trained with reinforcement learning, it learns to generate responses that maximize a reward signal, effectively learning which reasoning paths lead to correct solutions and which do not.

The transition to reinforcement learning-based training has enabled models to discover reasoning strategies that are difficult to capture through supervised learning alone. In supervised learning, a model learns to imitate the outputs provided in a training dataset, essentially learning to predict the next token based on patterns observed in human-generated text. While this approach produces fluent and knowledgeable models, it does not inherently teach models to reason through problems systematically. Reinforcement learning, by contrast, provides a framework where models can explore different approaches to problem-solving and receive feedback on their effectiveness, gradually learning to optimize their reasoning processes toward successful outcomes.

This paradigm shift has profound implications for the scalability of intelligence in artificial systems. As computational resources for reinforcement learning training increase, models can engage with more complex problems, explore more diverse solution strategies, and develop more robust reasoning capabilities. The relationship between computational investment and model capability suggests that reinforcement learning provides a viable path toward continued improvement in language model performance, even as the low-hanging fruits of supervised pre-training begin to exhaust their potential for driving further gains.

# The Importance of Stability in Large-Scale RL Training

To successfully scale reinforcement learning with greater computational investment, the foremost prerequisite is maintaining stable and robust training dynamics. Stability in training refers to the consistent and predictable behavior of the learning process over time, where the model's performance improves steadily without sudden degradations or erratic fluctuations. This requirement for stability becomes increasingly critical as the scale of training expands, involving larger models, longer training runs, and more complex optimization landscapes.

The challenge of maintaining training stability is not unique to reinforcement learning for language models, but it manifests in particularly severe forms in this context. Current state-of-the-art reinforcement learning algorithms, exemplified by Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), exhibit severe stability issues when training large language models. These issues become more pronounced as model sizes increase and as the length of generated responses grows, creating conditions where small perturbations in the training process can cascade into significant problems.

The importance of stability stems from several interconnected factors inherent to large-scale reinforcement learning. First, the optimization landscape for large language models is extremely high-dimensional, with billions or even trillions of parameters that must be jointly optimized. Small changes in these parameters can have complex and difficult-to-predict effects on the model's behavior, making it challenging to ensure that each training step moves the model toward improved performance rather than away from it. Second, the nature of reinforcement learning itself introduces variance into the training process, as the algorithm must estimate gradients from sampled trajectories that represent only a tiny fraction of all possible behaviors the model could exhibit.

When training instability occurs, it can manifest in various ways that undermine the effectiveness of the learning process. The gradients used to update the model may become excessively large or point in unproductive directions, causing the model to change its behavior in ways that degrade performance. The distribution of responses generated by the model may shift dramatically from one training iteration to the next, creating a moving target that prevents stable convergence. In the worst cases, these instabilities compound over time, leading to situations where the training process diverges entirely and the model becomes incapable of generating useful outputs.

# Overview of Model Collapse and Irreversible Training Failures

One of the most severe manifestations of training instability is model collapse, a phenomenon where the model's performance catastrophically degrades, often in an irreversible manner. Model collapse in the context of reinforcement learning for language models refers to a situation where the model's ability to generate coherent and useful responses deteriorates to the point where it can no longer perform the tasks it was designed for. Unlike temporary setbacks or temporary performance dips that might be corrected through continued training or hyperparameter adjustments, true model collapse represents a fundamental breakdown of the learning process from which recovery is typically impossible.

The paper identifies that current state-of-the-art reinforcement learning algorithms often result in catastrophic and irreversible model collapse when training large language models. This observation highlights a critical vulnerability in existing approaches: once collapse occurs, resuming training is unavailing, even when reverting to a previous checkpoint and meticulously tuning hyperparameters. Neither adjusting the clipping ranges that control how far policy updates can deviate, nor extending the generation length to provide more context, nor switching the reinforcement learning queries to different problems can restore the model to a functional state once collapse has occurred.

The root cause of model collapse can be traced to fundamental issues in the algorithmic design of methods like GRPO. Specifically, the instability stems from the misapplication and invalidation of importance sampling weights in the algorithm's design. Importance sampling is a statistical technique used to estimate properties of a target distribution using samples drawn from a different behavior distribution. In reinforcement learning, importance sampling weights correct for the difference between the distribution under which samples were generated and the distribution defined by the current policy being optimized.

When importance sampling weights are improperly applied, they introduce high-variance training noise into the gradient estimates used to update the model. This noise progressively accumulates with increased response length, as the errors compound across the sequence of tokens generated by the model. The clipping mechanism designed to prevent excessive policy updates can further amplify these problems, ultimately precipitating model collapse. The interaction between high-variance noise accumulation and the clipping mechanism creates a feedback loop where small errors lead to larger errors, eventually overwhelming the training process and causing irreversible degradation in model quality.

Understanding model collapse is essential for anyone working to develop or apply reinforcement learning methods to large language models. The irreversibility of collapse means that preventive measures must be built into the algorithm design from the outset; it is not sufficient to detect and correct collapse after it occurs. This understanding motivates the development of new algorithms that address the fundamental sources of instability rather than merely treating their symptoms.

# Introduction to Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) represents a new reinforcement learning algorithm specifically designed to address the stability challenges inherent in training large language models. The algorithm introduces several key innovations that distinguish it from previous approaches and provide a more stable foundation for large-scale reinforcement learning. GSPO is described as a stable, efficient, and performant algorithm that achieves superior training characteristics compared to existing methods.

The key innovation of GSPO lies in its theoretically grounded definition of importance ratios based on sequence likelihood. Unlike previous algorithms that compute importance ratios at the token level—comparing the probability of each individual token under the current and old policies—GSPO defines the importance ratio based on the complete sequence likelihood. This approach aligns with the basic principle of importance sampling, which requires that the importance weight correctly captures the relationship between the target distribution and the behavior distribution. By computing importance ratios at the sequence level, GSPO ensures that the weight reflects how far a complete response sampled from the old policy deviates from the distribution defined by the current policy.

GSPO computes normalized rewards as the advantages of multiple responses to a query, ensuring alignment between sequence-level rewarding and optimization. This approach maintains the efficiency benefits of group-based advantage estimation while avoiding the pitfalls of token-level importance weighting. The sequence-level approach to clipping, rewarding, and optimization creates a coherent framework where each component operates on the same unit of analysis—the complete sequence rather than individual tokens.

The empirical evaluation of GSPO demonstrates its significant superiority over GRPO in training stability, efficiency, and performance. Perhaps most notably, GSPO has inherently resolved the stability challenges in the reinforcement learning training of large Mixture-of-Experts models. Mixture-of-Experts models, which route different inputs through different subsets of model parameters, introduce additional complexity to training due to the volatility of expert activation patterns. GSPO's sequence-level approach proves robust to these fluctuations, eliminating the need for complex stabilization strategies that were previously required.

Beyond stability improvements, GSPO shows potential for simplifying reinforcement learning infrastructure. Because the algorithm operates on sequence-level likelihoods rather than token-level likelihoods, it can be more tolerant of precision discrepancies between training and inference engines. This tolerance opens possibilities for more efficient training pipelines that avoid the computational overhead of recomputing likelihoods with the training engine. The merits of GSPO have contributed to remarkable improvements in the latest Qwen3 models, demonstrating its practical effectiveness at scale. As reinforcement learning continues to scale to larger models and more complex tasks, algorithms like GSPO that provide robust and stable foundations will be essential for realizing the full potential of this training paradigm.
