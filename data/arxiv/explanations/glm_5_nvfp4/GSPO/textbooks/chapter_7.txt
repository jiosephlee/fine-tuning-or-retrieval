Chapter 7: # Advanced Applications: MoE Training and Infrastructure

As we have established the theoretical foundations and algorithmic innovations of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), we now turn our attention to its practical advantages in real-world deployment scenarios. This chapter examines how GSPO addresses critical challenges in training Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models and simplifies the design of reinforcement learning infrastructure. These practical benefits stem directly from GSPO's sequence-level approach and represent significant advances in the scalability of large language model training.

# Stability Challenges in Mixture-of-Experts Models

Mixture-of-Experts architectures have emerged as a powerful paradigm for scaling language models while maintaining computational efficiency. Unlike dense models where all parameters are activated for every input, MoE models employ sparse activation patterns where only a subset of experts are routed to for any given token. This sparse activation provides substantial computational savings but introduces unique challenges when combined with reinforcement learning.

The fundamental stability challenge in MoE RL training arises from what we term expert-activation volatility. In dense models, the network architecture remains fixed across training steps; the same neurons process the same inputs, albeit with updated weights. In MoE models, however, the routing mechanism determines which experts are activated for each token, and this routing is itself a learned function that changes during training. As the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is updated through gradient descent, the routing decisions can shift, causing different experts to be activated for the same input sequence.

This volatility becomes particularly problematic in the reinforcement learning context. Consider what happens during a typical RL iteration: a batch of responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ is generated from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, which activates certain experts for each token position. After gradient updates, the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ may route tokens differently, activating a different set of experts even when processing the identical token sequence. Our empirical observations with the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model revealed that after each RL gradient update, roughly $10\%$ of the experts activated under the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ differ from those activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ for the same rollout sample. This phenomenon becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models where routing decisions compound across layers.

The implications for token-level importance sampling are severe. The importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$ computed in algorithms like GRPO assumes that the probability ratio meaningfully reflects the policy change. However, when the experts being compared come from different routing configurations—essentially comparing outputs from different sub-networks—this ratio loses its interpretation as a meaningful importance weight. The ratio becomes a mixture of genuine policy shift and routing volatility, introducing high-variance noise into gradient estimates that compounds throughout training.

# Expert-Activation Volatility and Its Impact on Token-Level Ratios

To understand why expert-activation volatility is so detrimental to token-level importance ratios, we must examine the computational structure of MoE models more closely. In a typical MoE layer, each token $y_{i,t}$ is routed to a subset of experts based on a learned gating function. The output for that token is a weighted combination of the selected expert outputs:

$$\text{output}(y_{i,t}) = \sum_{k \in \text{selected}} g_k(y_{i,t}) \cdot E_k(y_{i,t})$$

where $g_k$ is the gating weight for expert $E_k$. The probability $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})$ is computed through multiple such MoE layers, meaning that each token's likelihood depends on a cascade of routing decisions.

When computing the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$, we are implicitly assuming that both the numerator and denominator are computed through compatible network paths. However, expert-activation volatility violates this assumption. If the routing under $\pi_{\theta}$ activates experts that differ from those activated under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, the ratio becomes:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}^{(E_{\text{new}})}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}^{(E_{\text{old}})}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$$

where the superscripts indicate potentially different expert configurations. This ratio no longer serves as a valid importance weight because the numerator and denominator sample from different computational paths. The importance sampling principle requires that the behavior distribution adequately covers the target distribution's support, but when routing changes, this coverage assumption is violated.

The practical consequence is that token-level importance ratios fluctuate drastically and unpredictably during MoE training with GRPO. These fluctuations introduce high-variance noise into the gradient estimates, as discussed in the gradient analysis of GRPO:

$$\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})} \nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})\right]$$

The unequal token weights $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$, which vary between $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ depending on the advantage sign, are further destabilized by routing-induced fluctuations. This accumulation of variance through long sequences and repeated gradient updates ultimately prevents normal convergence of RL training.

# Eliminating the Need for Routing Replay Strategies

Before GSPO, practitioners developed workarounds to address MoE instability. The most notable of these is the Routing Replay training strategy, which we examine here both to understand its mechanism and to appreciate how GSPO eliminates the need for such complex interventions.

The Routing Replay strategy operates by caching the activated experts during rollout generation under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and "replaying" these routing modes when computing importance ratios. Specifically, when calculating $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$, the method forces $\pi_{\theta}$ to use the same expert activations that were cached from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ rather than computing new routing decisions. By ensuring that both the numerator and denominator share the same activated network paths for each token, the importance ratio regains stability, and optimization proceeds on consistent activated networks across gradient updates.

While Routing Replay enabled GRPO training of MoE models to converge properly, it introduced several practical drawbacks. First, caching and replaying routing modes incurs additional memory overhead, as the expert activation patterns must be stored for all tokens across all samples. Second, it increases communication costs in distributed training, where routing information must be synchronized across devices. Third, and perhaps most fundamentally, Routing Replay artificially constrains the model's capacity by preventing it from exploring new expert configurations during training. The model is forced to use historical routing patterns rather than adapting its routing to the evolving policy.

GSPO eliminates the dependency on Routing Replay entirely, representing a fundamental rather than symptomatic solution to the expert-activation volatility problem. The key insight is that GSPO focuses only on sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)$ rather than individual token likelihoods $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})$. The sequence-level importance ratio:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

aggregates information across all tokens in the response. While individual token probabilities may fluctuate due to routing volatility, the MoE model maintains its overall language modeling capability, and the sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)$ does not fluctuate drastically. The sequence-level probability serves as a smoothed signal that is robust to local routing variations.

This robustness stems from the mathematical properties of the sequence likelihood. Since $\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})$, the sequence likelihood effectively averages over the variability in individual token probabilities. Routing changes that increase some token probabilities while decreasing others tend to partially cancel out in the product, making the sequence-level probability a more stable quantity than any individual token probability.

By fundamentally resolving the expert-activation volatility issue, GSPO not only simplifies and stabilizes the training process but also allows the model to leverage its full capacity without artificial constraints. The model is free to explore different expert configurations as the policy evolves, enabling more expressive and adaptive behavior.

# Infrastructure Simplification: Handling Precision Discrepancies

Beyond MoE-specific benefits, GSPO provides significant advantages for RL infrastructure design, particularly in handling precision discrepancies between training and inference engines. Understanding this benefit requires appreciating the practical architecture of large-scale RL systems.

In production RL training, there typically exists a separation between training engines and inference engines. Training engines like Megatron are optimized for gradient computation and parameter updates, while inference engines like SGLang and vLLM are optimized for fast generation with features like continuous batching and PagedAttention. These engines may differ in their numerical precision, memory layout, and implementation details, leading to subtle discrepancies in computed probabilities.

Under token-level importance sampling as used in GRPO, these precision discrepancies become problematic. The importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$ is sensitive to small changes in individual token probabilities. A discrepancy of even $0.001$ in a token probability can compound across a long sequence, and more importantly, the clipping mechanism may behave differently depending on whether the ratio is computed with training-engine or inference-engine probabilities.

The standard practice to address this has been to recompute all likelihoods using the training engine. When responses are generated by the inference engine under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, the training engine must recompute $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})$ for all tokens to ensure consistency with how $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})$ is computed during gradient calculation. This recomputation adds substantial computational overhead and complicates the training pipeline.

GSPO fundamentally changes this equation. Since GSPO uses only sequence-level likelihoods for optimization, and sequence-level likelihoods are significantly more tolerant of precision discrepancies, it becomes possible to directly use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine for optimization. The intuition is similar to that for MoE stability: small precision differences that affect individual token probabilities tend to average out in the sequence-level likelihood.

This tolerance has practical implications for several scenarios. In partial rollout, where generation is split across multiple inference calls, avoiding recomputation reduces latency and computational cost. In multi-turn RL, where interactions span multiple conversation turns, the efficiency gains compound. In training-inference disaggregated frameworks, where training and inference are deployed on different hardware, the reduced sensitivity to precision discrepancies simplifies system design and reduces engineering complexity.

The sequence-level approach thus not only improves training stability and efficiency but also simplifies the overall RL infrastructure, reducing the need for complex workarounds and enabling more flexible deployment strategies.

# The Future of Scalable RL with GSPO

Looking forward, GSPO establishes a robust and scalable algorithmic foundation that positions the field for continued advancement in large-scale RL training with language models. The empirical results demonstrate that GSPO can deliver continuous performance improvement through increasing training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending the generation length. This scalability is crucial as the field pursues more capable models through larger-scale training.

The success of GSPO in contributing to the exceptional improvements in the Qwen3 models validates its practical efficacy. More importantly, the algorithm addresses fundamental issues that previously limited scaling. The instability of GRPO, exacerbated by response length and model scale, created a ceiling on how far RL training could be pushed. GSPO's sequence-level approach removes this ceiling, enabling training to proceed stably throughout extended optimization.

The infrastructure simplifications enabled by GSPO also have implications for future research directions. As models grow larger and training setups become more complex—with techniques like training-inference disaggregation, multi-turn interactions, and partial rollouts becoming standard—the reduced complexity of GSPO's requirements becomes increasingly valuable. Researchers and practitioners can focus on capability improvements rather than working around fundamental algorithmic limitations.

The elimination of specialized strategies like Routing Replay for MoE training opens new possibilities for sparse model architectures. Without the overhead and capacity constraints imposed by such workarounds, MoE models can be trained more efficiently and can leverage their full representational capacity. This is particularly significant as MoE architectures continue to scale to larger sizes.

GSPO represents a return to principled algorithm design in RL for language models. By aligning the importance sampling weights with the sequence-level nature of rewards and language generation, GSPO provides a theoretically grounded approach that proves more stable and efficient in practice. As the field continues to scale RL and push the boundaries of model capabilities, GSPO serves as a scalable algorithmic cornerstone that enables continued advancement. The fundamental advances in intelligence that result from such scaling efforts depend critically on having stable, efficient, and performant algorithms—precisely what GSPO provides.
