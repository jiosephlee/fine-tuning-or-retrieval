\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 6: # Experiments and Empirical Results

# Experimental Setup: Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base and Benchmarks

To rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), the authors designed a comprehensive experimental framework that compares GSPO against Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the established baseline algorithm for reinforcement learning with large language models. The experimental setup was carefully constructed to ensure fair comparison and to assess multiple dimensions of algorithmic performance, including training stability, efficiency, and final model capabilities.

The primary model used in these experiments is a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base. This model architecture is particularly significant because it represents a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) design, which introduces unique stability challenges during reinforcement learning training. The MoE architecture employs sparse activation, where only a subset of expert networks are activated for each token during forward propagation. This sparsity, while computationally efficient, creates additional complexity for gradient-based optimization compared to dense models. The choice of this model thus provides a stringent testbed for evaluating whether GSPO can address the stability issues that plague existing algorithms when training large, sparse models.

The evaluation methodology employed multiple benchmarks to comprehensively assess model capabilities across different domains. For mathematical reasoning, the experiments utilized AIME'24 (American Invitational Mathematics Examination), reporting average Pass@1 scores computed over 32 independent samplings. This metric captures the model's ability to correctly solve competition-level mathematics problems in a single attempt, providing a rigorous measure of mathematical reasoning capability. For coding abilities, two benchmarks were employed: LiveCodeBench (covering the period from October 2024 to February 2025) with average Pass@1 computed over 8 samplings, and CodeForces which reports Elo ratings. These coding benchmarks evaluate the model's practical programming skills across diverse problem types and difficulty levels, with the CodeForces Elo rating providing a competitive ranking measure that accounts for the difficulty of problems solved.

The training procedure followed a standard regime where each batch of rollout data was partitioned into four mini-batches for gradient updates. This multi-epoch usage of generated data is a common practice to improve sample efficiency, but it introduces an off-policy learning setting that necessitates careful importance sampling treatment. For GSPO, the left and right clipping ranges were set to $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$, respectively. For GRPO, these values were set to $0.2$ and $0.27$, representing clipping ranges that were carefully tuned to ensure fair comparison. The substantial difference in clipping ranges between the two algorithms reflects their fundamentally different definitions of importance ratios—GSPO operates on sequence-level importance ratios while GRPO operates on token-level importance ratios—necessitating different numerical scales for effective clipping.

A critical distinction in the experimental setup concerns the treatment of Mixture-of-Experts models. GRPO necessitates the Routing Replay training strategy for normal convergence during MoE reinforcement learning. This strategy caches the activated experts under the old policy and enforces the same routing patterns when computing importance ratios under the new policy. In contrast, GSPO was found to obviate the need for this additional stabilization mechanism entirely. This difference in required infrastructure not only affects implementation complexity but also has implications for computational overhead and model capacity utilization during training.

# Training Stability and Performance Curves

The experimental results demonstrate that GSPO achieves remarkably stable training dynamics throughout the reinforcement learning process. Training with GSPO proceeds stably without exhibiting the catastrophic model collapse that frequently afflicts GRPO training on large language models. This stability is particularly noteworthy given the challenging setting of training Mixture-of-Experts models, where the combination of sparse activation patterns and reinforcement learning gradients has historically produced severe convergence issues.

A key finding from the experimental evaluation is that GSPO enables continuous performance improvement through scaling computational investment. Specifically, the results show that increasing training compute leads to sustained gains in both training reward and benchmark performance. This continuous improvement is facilitated by three primary mechanisms: regularly updating the query set to expose the model to diverse problem distributions, extending the generation length to enable more thorough reasoning processes, and maintaining stable gradient updates throughout extended training runs. The ability to continue scaling training without encountering stability plateaus or collapse is essential for pushing the capability boundaries of language models through reinforcement learning.

The performance curves reveal that GSPO consistently outperforms GRPO across all evaluated benchmarks. On AIME'24, the mathematical reasoning benchmark, models trained with GSPO achieve higher Pass@1 scores compared to their GRPO counterparts. Similar improvements are observed on LiveCodeBench and CodeForces, demonstrating that the benefits of GSPO generalize across different task domains. The consistency of these improvements across mathematical reasoning and coding tasks suggests that GSPO provides fundamental algorithmic advantages rather than task-specific optimizations.

The stability advantages of GSPO have direct practical implications for model development workflows. With GRPO, practitioners frequently encounter scenarios where training collapses irreversibly, necessitating rollback to earlier checkpoints and extensive hyperparameter tuning to recover training stability. The paper notes that once collapse occurs in GRPO, resuming training proves unavailing even when reverting to a previous checkpoint and meticulously tuning hyperparameters such as clipping ranges, extending generation length, or switching the reinforcement learning queries. GSPO eliminates this failure mode, providing practitioners with confidence that training will proceed reliably to completion.

The successful application of GSPO to the reinforcement learning training of the latest Qwen3 models provides strong evidence for the algorithm's efficacy in unleashing the power of RL scaling for large language models. These production-scale experiments demonstrate that the algorithmic improvements from GSPO translate directly to real-world performance gains, validating the theoretical motivations behind the sequence-level optimization approach.

# Analysis of Clipping Fractions: Why Clipping More Tokens Leads to Better Efficiency

One of the most counter-intuitive findings from the experimental evaluation concerns the relationship between clipping fractions and training efficiency. The analysis reveals a difference of two orders of magnitude in the fractions of clipped tokens between GSPO and GRPO. This dramatic disparity persists even when adjusting clipping ranges, indicating a fundamental difference in how the two algorithms distribute their clipping operations across training samples.

In GSPO, the sequence-level clipping approach means that when an entire response is deemed too far from the old policy's distribution, all tokens in that response are simultaneously clipped and excluded from gradient estimation. This results in a higher overall fraction of tokens being affected by clipping operations. Naively, one might expect that clipping more tokens would reduce training efficiency by discarding more potentially useful training signal. However, the empirical results demonstrate the opposite: GSPO achieves superior training efficiency compared to GRPO despite clipping significantly more tokens.

This counter-intuitive finding provides important insight into the nature of gradient estimation in reinforcement learning for language models. The explanation lies in understanding what the clipping mechanism actually accomplishes and how it interacts with gradient noise. In GRPO, the token-level clipping mechanism operates on each position independently, meaning that some tokens within a response may be clipped while others remain unclipped. The unclipped tokens continue to contribute gradients, but these gradients are weighted by potentially unreliable token-level importance ratios. The paper establishes that these token-level importance ratios introduce high-variance noise into the training process.

The key insight is that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient for sample exploitation. The token-level importance weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$ at each position fails to properly correct for the distributional mismatch between the old and current policies because importance sampling relies on averaging over multiple samples to effectively perform distribution correction. Since this weight is based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution, it introduces noise rather than providing meaningful off-policy correction.

GSPO's approach of clipping entire sequences provides a more principled mechanism for handling off-policy samples. When a sequence has drifted too far from the old policy's distribution—as indicated by the sequence-level importance ratio exceeding the clipping bounds—the entire sequence is excluded from gradient computation. This prevents the propagation of noisy gradient estimates while ensuring that when sequences are included, their contribution to the gradient reflects the coherent optimization of an entire response. The sequence-level importance ratio $\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$ provides a meaningful measure of how far the sampled response deviates from the current policy, properly aligning with the principles of importance sampling.

This analysis reframes the purpose of clipping from a constraint to be minimized to a quality filter that improves the signal-to-noise ratio of gradient estimates. GSPO's higher clipping fraction indicates that it more aggressively filters out samples that would contribute noisy gradients, while preserving the samples that provide reliable learning signals. The result is that even though fewer tokens contribute to gradient updates, those that do contribute provide higher-quality gradient information, leading to more efficient training overall.

# Comparison of Training Efficiency and Compute Consumption

Beyond the qualitative advantages in training stability, the experimental evaluation provides quantitative evidence for GSPO's superior training efficiency. The results demonstrate that GSPO achieves better training accuracy and benchmark performance compared to GRPO when operating under identical constraints of training compute and consumed queries. This efficiency advantage means that practitioners can achieve equivalent model performance with fewer computational resources, or alternatively, achieve superior performance with the same computational budget.

The training efficiency improvements stem from multiple factors identified in the paper. First, GSPO's gradient weighting scheme treats all tokens within a response equally, as derived in the gradient analysis. In the GSPO gradient expression, the importance ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$ applies uniformly to all tokens in the sequence. This contrasts with GRPO, where tokens are weighted according to their respective token-level importance weights $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$. These unequal weights can vary across ranges like $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages, introducing instability that accumulates unpredictably as training progresses.

The efficiency advantage is particularly pronounced for Mixture-of-Experts models, where GSPO eliminates the need for the Routing Replay training strategy. Routing Replay, while enabling GRPO to train MoE models successfully, incurs substantial overhead. The strategy requires caching the activated experts under the old policy and enforcing these same routing patterns when computing importance ratios. This caching imposes additional memory requirements, and the enforcement of historical routing patterns requires additional communication overhead in distributed training settings. Furthermore, by constraining the model to replay historical routing patterns, Routing Replay can limit the actual capacity utilization of the MoE model, as experts cannot dynamically adapt their activation patterns during training.

GSPO fundamentally resolves the expert-activation volatility issue that necessitates Routing Replay. The sequence-level approach focuses on the sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$ rather than individual token likelihoods $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})$. Because the MoE model maintains its overall language modeling capability even as expert activation patterns shift, the sequence likelihood does not fluctuate drastically across gradient updates. This insensitivity to routing changes allows GSPO to train normally without requiring artificial constraints on expert activation, enabling the model to leverage its full capacity without artificial constraints.

The paper also identifies infrastructure efficiency benefits from GSPO's sequence-level approach. In practice, precision discrepancies between training engines (such as Megatron) and inference engines (such as SGLang and vLLM) typically necessitate recomputing likelihoods of sampled responses using the training engine. However, because GSPO uses only sequence-level likelihoods rather than token-level likelihoods for optimization, the algorithm is much more tolerant of precision discrepancies. This tolerance potentially enables direct use of likelihoods returned by the inference engine, avoiding the computational overhead of recomputation with the training engine. This benefit is particularly valuable in scenarios like partial rollout and multi-turn reinforcement learning, and in training-inference disaggregated frameworks where computational resources are distributed across specialized hardware.
