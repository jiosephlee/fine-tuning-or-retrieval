\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 3: # Motivation: The Instability of Token-Level Optimization

# The Off-Policy Learning Setting in LLM Training

Reinforcement learning for large language models operates under unique constraints that fundamentally shape the design of training algorithms. To understand why GSPO represents a necessary departure from previous approaches, we must first examine the practical realities of training large language models at scale.

When training language models with reinforcement learning, the computational demands are immense. Modern language models contain billions or even trillions of parameters, and generating responses requires substantial memory and compute. To maximize hardware utilization, practitioners employ large rollout batch sizes—generating many responses simultaneously across multiple devices. However, this creates a practical challenge: computing gradients over extremely large batches is computationally prohibitive.

The standard solution to this problem is to partition a large batch of rollout data into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates. This approach allows efficient use of available memory while maintaining reasonable batch sizes for each gradient step. However, this seemingly innocuous engineering decision introduces a fundamental theoretical complication: the training becomes inherently off-policy.

To understand what off-policy means in this context, consider the sequence of events during training. First, responses are generated by sampling from a policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, which represents the model's parameters at the time of generation. These responses are stored and then processed in mini-batches for gradient computation. Crucially, by the time a later mini-batch is processed, the model parameters $\theta$ have already been updated multiple times based on earlier mini-batches. The responses in the later mini-batch were sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, but the gradient updates are being applied to a different policy $\pi_{\theta}$.

This mismatch between the sampling distribution and the optimization target is what defines the off-policy setting. The samples were drawn from one distribution (the old policy), but we wish to optimize a different distribution (the current policy). This is not merely a theoretical concern—it has profound implications for how we must construct our optimization objectives.

The off-policy nature of the training explains why algorithms like PPO and GRPO incorporate clipping mechanisms. These mechanisms are designed to prevent samples that are too far from the current policy's distribution from contributing to gradient estimates. When a sample is highly off-policy, meaning the current policy would have assigned very different probabilities to that sample than the old policy did, directly using such samples in gradient estimation can lead to unstable and unpredictable updates. The clipping mechanism constrains the policy update to remain within a proximal region of the old policy, hence the name Proximal Policy Optimization.

However, as we will see, the clipping mechanism itself interacts problematically with how importance sampling is applied in GRPO, creating instabilities that compound over long sequences and repeated updates.

# The Principle of Importance Sampling

To understand the fundamental flaw in GRPO's design, we must first establish what importance sampling is and how it should correctly be applied. Importance sampling is a statistical technique that allows us to estimate properties of a target distribution using samples drawn from a different behavior distribution.

The core idea of importance sampling is mathematically elegant. Suppose we wish to compute the expectation of some function $f$ under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$, but we only have samples available from a different behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. The importance sampling identity provides a way to correct for this distributional mismatch through re-weighting:

$$\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)} \, f(z) \right].$$

This equation states that if we want to compute the expected value of $f(z)$ when $z$ is drawn from $\pi_\text{tar}$, we can instead draw samples from $\pi_\text{beh}$ and weight each sample by the ratio $\frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)}$, which is called the importance weight. This weight compensates for samples that are more likely under the target distribution than the behavior distribution (weight greater than 1) and samples that are less likely (weight less than 1).

However, a crucial condition for importance sampling to be effective is that we must average over multiple samples from the behavior distribution. When $N$ is large, the empirical average of the re-weighted function values converges to the true expectation. The importance weight $\frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)}$ can effectively correct for the distributional mismatch only when we have multiple samples to average over.

This requirement for multiple samples is not merely a technical detail—it is fundamental to why importance sampling works. Consider what happens with a single sample. If we draw just one sample $z$ from $\pi_\text{beh}$ and compute $\frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)} f(z)$, we get a single weighted estimate. But this single estimate can be arbitrarily far from the true expectation. The importance weight can be very large or very small depending on where the sample happened to fall, and with only one sample, we have no way to average out this variability.

The variance of importance sampling estimates is directly related to the variance of the importance weights themselves. When $\pi_\text{tar}$ and $\pi_\text{beh}$ are similar distributions, the importance weights tend to be close to 1, and the variance is manageable. But when the distributions differ significantly, some samples will have very large importance weights, leading to high-variance estimates that require many samples to converge reliably.

# The Invalidity of Token-Level Importance Weights

With the principle of importance sampling established, we can now examine why GRPO's approach is fundamentally flawed. GRPO applies importance weights at the token level, computing the importance ratio for each token position:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}.$$

At first glance, this might seem reasonable. After all, language models generate text token by token, and each token is sampled from a conditional distribution given the preceding context. Why shouldn't we apply importance sampling to correct for each token's distribution?

The problem lies in the fundamental principle established above: importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples to effectively correct for distributional mismatch. At each token position $t$, GRPO has only a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from the next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x, y_{i, <t})$. This single sample cannot fulfill the averaging requirement that makes importance sampling valid.

Consider what this means in practice. For each position in the sequence, we compute an importance weight based on a single token sample. This weight is supposed to correct for the distributional difference between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, but with only one sample, it cannot perform this correction reliably. Instead of reducing variance as proper importance sampling would, these token-level weights introduce additional variance into the gradient estimates.

This variance is not merely theoretical—it has been empirically observed to cause catastrophic model collapse during training. The collapse is so severe that once it occurs, resuming training proves futile even when reverting to a previous checkpoint and meticulously tuning hyperparameters. The paper explicitly notes that strategies such as adjusting clipping ranges, extending generation length, or switching the RL queries all fail to recover from this collapse.

The invalidity of token-level importance weights reveals a fundamental mismatch in GRPO's design. Importance sampling is being applied where it cannot be effective—single token samples cannot provide the averaging necessary for valid importance weight computation. Yet these weights are being used to scale gradients, introducing noise that accumulates over training.

# Accumulation of Variance in Long Sequences

The problems with token-level importance weights do not remain isolated—they compound destructively over the course of long sequences. Modern language models, especially those trained for reasoning tasks, generate responses that can span thousands of tokens. Each token position introduces its own importance weight, and each of these weights contributes to the overall noise in the gradient estimate.

To understand how this accumulation works, consider the gradient of the GRPO objective:

$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right].$$

Notice that each token's gradient is weighted by its own importance weight. These weights are unequal across tokens and can vary in the range $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages. The impact of these unequal weights accumulates over the sequence length $|y_i|$.

When responses are short, the accumulated variance might be tolerable. But as response length increases—a necessity for tackling sophisticated reasoning tasks—the variance grows proportionally. Each token adds another potentially noisy weight to the gradient estimate. For responses of several thousand tokens, the accumulated noise becomes substantial.

This accumulation effect interacts problematically with the clipping mechanism. The clipping is designed to prevent extreme updates from highly off-policy samples, but it operates independently at each token position. The combined effect of many clipped or nearly-clipped importance weights, each introducing its own variance, creates unpredictable gradient behavior.

The paper's observation of model collapse in GRPO training is a direct consequence of this variance accumulation. The noise introduced by token-level importance weights progressively corrupts the gradient estimates, leading to unstable updates that eventually cause the model to diverge irrecoverably.

Furthermore, this problem is exacerbated by the trend toward longer responses in modern language model training. As models are trained to undertake deeper and longer reasoning processes, response lengths naturally increase. GRPO's instability thus becomes more severe precisely when it is most problematic—when training models for the most challenging tasks.

# The Core Principle: Aligning the Unit of Optimization with the Unit of Reward

The failures of token-level importance sampling point to a deeper principle that should guide algorithm design: the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward. This principle emerges clearly when we consider how rewards are assigned in language model training.

In typical reinforcement learning for language models, rewards are granted to entire sequences, not individual tokens. A verifier $r$ evaluates a query-response pair $(x, y)$ and produces a reward $r(x, y)$. This reward reflects the quality of the complete response—whether it correctly solves a mathematical problem, produces working code, or provides a helpful answer. The reward does not distinguish between individual tokens within the response; it evaluates the sequence as a whole.

Given that rewards are sequence-level, it follows naturally that optimization should also operate at the sequence level. Yet GRPO applies importance sampling weights at the token level, creating a fundamental misalignment. The advantage $\widehat{A}_{i}$ computed from sequence-level rewards is broadcast to all tokens in the response, but each token is then weighted differently by its own importance weight. This means tokens within the same response, all receiving the same reward signal, are optimized with different weighting schemes determined by their individual importance ratios.

This misalignment is not merely inelegant—it causes practical training instability. The paper explicitly states that the failure of token-level importance weights points to this core principle: applying off-policy correction at the token level is problematic when rewards are granted at the sequence level.

GSPO's design directly embodies this principle. By defining the importance ratio at the sequence level as $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$, GSPO ensures that the unit of optimization matches the unit of reward. The clipping mechanism operates on entire responses rather than individual tokens, and all tokens within a response receive equal weight in the gradient computation.

This alignment has both theoretical and practical benefits. Theoretically, sequence-level importance sampling has a clear meaning—it reflects how far the response sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ deviates from what $\pi_{\theta}$ would have produced. This aligns naturally with sequence-level rewards. Practically, eliminating the per-token importance weight variance removes a major source of training instability, enabling stable training even with long responses and large models.

The principle of aligning optimization with reward units extends beyond the specific algorithm design. It suggests that future reinforcement learning algorithms for language models should carefully consider at what granularity rewards are assigned and ensure that importance sampling, clipping, and optimization all operate at compatible levels. When these components are misaligned, as in GRPO's token-level importance weights with sequence-level rewards, the resulting instability can undermine training even when other aspects of the algorithm are well-designed.
