Chapter 2: # The Standard RLHF Pipeline

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as the predominant methodology for steering large language models to align with human preferences. While unsupervised pre-training provides language models with broad world knowledge and reasoning capabilities, achieving precise control over their behavior remains challenging due to the unsupervised nature of their training. The RLHF pipeline addresses this challenge by incorporating human judgments about the relative quality of model outputs, thereby instilling desired behaviors into the model. Understanding the standard RLHF pipeline is essential for appreciating the innovations that Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) brings to preference learning. This chapter provides a comprehensive technical examination of the three-stage RLHF process, including the mathematical formulations that underpin reward learning and policy optimization.

#

## Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to Create a Reference Model

The RLHF pipeline begins with supervised fine-tuning, which serves as the foundation for all subsequent stages. Large-scale unsupervised language models are trained on vast datasets containing text generated by humans with diverse goals, priorities, and skill levels. While this pre-training endows models with broad capabilities, the resulting behavior may not align with specific downstream requirements. The supervised fine-tuning stage addresses this by adapting the pre-trained model to the particular task of interest, whether that be dialogue, summarization, or instruction following.

During this first phase, a pre-trained language model undergoes fine-tuning using supervised learning on high-quality data relevant to the downstream task. This process produces what is denoted as the reference model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$. The SFT model serves as the starting point for all subsequent stages in the RLHF pipeline, providing a baseline policy that already exhibits reasonable task-specific behavior before preference learning begins. The quality of this initial fine-tuning is crucial, as it determines the distribution from which preference data will be sampled and establishes the reference policy against which later policy updates will be measured.

The SFT model essentially learns to imitate human demonstrations of high-quality responses, providing a strong prior for the types of outputs that are generally appropriate for the task. This supervised initialization is particularly important because it places the model in a reasonable region of the policy space before the more complex preference learning stages begin. Without this supervised foundation, the subsequent reward modeling and reinforcement learning stages would have to operate starting from a much weaker baseline, potentially making the entire pipeline less stable and effective.

#

## Stage 2: Reward Modeling Using the Bradley-Terry Preference Model

Once the SFT model has been established, the second phase focuses on learning a reward function that captures human preferences. This stage begins by using the SFT model to generate candidate responses. Specifically, the SFT model is prompted with various inputs $x$ to produce pairs of answers $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)$. These paired responses are then presented to human labelers who express their preferences by selecting which response they prefer. When the human labeler chooses a preferred response, this is denoted as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ represents the preferred completion and $y_l$ represents the dispreferred completion from among the pair $(y_1, y_2)$.

The fundamental assumption underlying reward modeling is that these human preferences are generated by some latent reward model $r^*(y, x)$ that we do not have direct access to. The goal is to learn a parameterized approximation of this latent reward function that accurately reflects human preferences. To formalize the relationship between rewards and preferences, RLHF relies on preference models from the ranking literature. The Bradley-Terry model is a popular choice, though more general Plackett-Luce ranking models are also compatible with the framework when multiple ranked answers are available.

The Bradley-Terry model provides a probabilistic framework for modeling pairwise preferences. It stipulates that the human preference distribution $p^*$ can be expressed in terms of the underlying reward function. Specifically, the probability that response $y_1$ is preferred over response $y_2$ given input $x$ is:

$$p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.$$

This formulation elegantly captures the intuition that the probability of preferring one response over another should increase as the difference in their rewards increases. When both responses have equal reward, the model assigns equal probability to each being preferred. As the reward of one response becomes much larger than the other, the probability of preferring that response approaches one.

#

## Maximum Likelihood Estimation for Fitting the Reward Function

Given a static dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled from the preference distribution $p^*$, the reward model parameters are estimated via maximum likelihood. The reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ is parameterized by $\phi$, and the optimization problem is framed as binary classification over preference pairs.

The negative log-likelihood loss for the reward model is:

$$\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l))\bigr],$$

where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic function. This loss function encourages the reward model to assign higher values to preferred completions $y_w$ compared to dispreferred completions $y_l$. The logistic function transforms the reward difference into a probability, and the loss penalizes the model when it assigns low probability to the observed preference ordering.

In practice, for language models, the reward network $r_\phi(x, y)$ is typically initialized from the SFT model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)$. A linear layer is added on top of the final transformer layer to produce a single scalar prediction representing the reward value. This architectural choice allows the reward model to leverage the rich representations learned during pre-training and supervised fine-tuning, while adding minimal additional parameters for reward prediction.

To ensure a reward function with lower variance and better numerical properties, prior works normalize the rewards such that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$. This normalization helps stabilize training and prevents the reward model from drifting to extreme values.

#

## Stage 3: RL Fine-Tuning Using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

The third and final stage of the RLHF pipeline uses the learned reward function to fine-tune the language model policy through reinforcement learning. During this phase, the reward model provides feedback signals that guide the language model toward generating higher-quality responses as judged by human preferences.

The optimization objective balances two competing goals: maximizing the expected reward while preventing the policy from deviating too far from the reference policy. This is formulated as:

$$\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_\phi(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\bigl[\pi_\theta(y \mid x) \mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)\bigr],$$

where $\beta$ is a hyperparameter controlling the strength of the constraint on policy deviation, and $\pi_\text{ref}$ denotes the reference policy, typically the initial SFT model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$. In practice, the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ is also initialized from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$, ensuring the optimization begins from a reasonable starting point.

Due to the discrete nature of language generation, this objective is not directly differentiable, necessitating the use of reinforcement learning algorithms. The standard approach constructs the reward function as:

$$r(x, y) = r_\phi(x, y) - \beta(\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)),$$

and optimizes this using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). PPO is a policy gradient method that uses a clipped objective to prevent excessively large policy updates, which helps maintain training stability.

#

## The KL-Divergence Constraint to Prevent Reward Hacking and Maintain Diversity

The KL-divergence constraint in the RLHF objective serves a crucial role in ensuring the quality and reliability of the trained model. This constraint is essential for multiple interconnected reasons that fundamentally affect the success of preference learning.

First, the constraint prevents the model from deviating too far from the distribution on which the reward model was trained. The reward model is trained on preference data generated by the SFT model, and its accuracy is only reliable within a certain neighborhood of this distribution. If the policy were allowed to drift arbitrarily far from the reference policy, it might generate responses that fall outside this reliable region, causing the reward model to provide inaccurate or meaningless feedback.

Second, the constraint maintains generation diversity and prevents mode collapse. Without such a constraint, the reinforcement learning optimization might discover responses that exploit quirks or errors in the reward model to achieve high scores without actually being preferred by humans. This phenomenon, known as "reward hacking," could cause the policy to collapse to a narrow set of responses that achieve high reward through spurious features rather than genuine quality.

The parameter $\beta$ explicitly controls the trade-off between maximizing reward and staying close to the reference policy. A higher value of $\beta$ imposes a stronger constraint, keeping the policy closer to the reference but potentially limiting the achievable reward improvement. A lower value allows more aggressive optimization but increases the risk of the problems mentioned above.

## Complexity and Instability Issues Inherent in the RLHF Pipeline

Despite producing models with impressive capabilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than standard supervised learning. This complexity manifests in several ways that create practical challenges for practitioners.

The pipeline involves training multiple language models in sequence: first the SFT model, then the reward model, and finally the policy through reinforcement learning. Each stage introduces its own hyperparameters, potential failure modes, and computational requirements. The need to sample from the language model policy during the reinforcement learning stage adds significant computational overhead, as this sampling must occur repeatedly throughout training.

The actor-critic algorithms commonly used for RLHF, such as PPO, can exhibit instability during training. One source of instability relates to the normalization of rewards and value function estimation. The theoretical objective includes a partition function term that acts as a soft value function of the reference policy. While this term does not affect the optimal solution, omitting it can cause the policy gradient to have high variance, making learning unstable. Prior works have attempted to accommodate this through learned value functions or Monte Carlo estimates, but these approaches introduce their own optimization difficulties.

Additionally, the reward model and policy optimization stages are coupled in ways that can amplify errors. If the reward model contains systematic biases or inaccuracies, the policy optimization will exploit these to maximize reward, potentially leading to behaviors that humans find undesirable. The delicate balance required between reward maximization and KL-divergence minimization further complicates hyperparameter tuning, as the optimal settings may vary significantly across different tasks and datasets.

These complexity and stability issues motivate the search for simpler approaches to preference learning. Direct Preference Optimization addresses many of these challenges by eliminating the need for explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning, instead optimizing a single maximum likelihood objective that directly captures human preferences.
