Chapter 1: # Introduction to Language Model Alignment

The advent of large-scale unsupervised language models represents one of the most significant developments in artificial intelligence. Models such as PaLM, GPT-3, and LLaMA, trained on vast corpora of text data, demonstrate remarkable capabilities that emerge from the simple objective of predicting the next token. These models acquire broad world knowledge, develop reasoning abilities, and can perform a diverse array of tasks without explicit supervision. However, the very nature of their unsupervised training creates a fundamental challenge: achieving precise control over their behavior. The data on which these models are trained reflects the full diversity of human expression, including both desirable and undesirable patterns. Humans who generated this training data operated with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skill levels, not all of which we wish our AI systems to emulate. This disconnect between what language models learn and what we want them to produce motivates the central problem of alignment—steering these powerful models toward behaviors that are safe, helpful, and controllable.

## The Capabilities and World Knowledge of Unsupervised Large Language Models

Large language models undergo a training process that is both remarkably simple and surprisingly effective. Through self-supervised learning on massive text datasets, these models learn to predict the next word in a sequence, a task that requires them to internalize patterns in language, facts about the world, and even certain reasoning abilities. The scale of training data and model parameters enables capabilities that were not explicitly programmed into the systems. Models like PaLM, GPT-3, and LLaMA can perform tasks ranging from question answering to code generation, from translation to creative writing, often without any task-specific training.

The knowledge encoded within these models is vast and diverse. Having been exposed to text spanning virtually every domain of human knowledge, the models develop representations that capture relationships between concepts, facts about the world, and the structure of language itself. This unsupervised acquisition of knowledge is both a strength and a vulnerability. The strength lies in the breadth of capabilities that emerge without explicit engineering. The vulnerability lies in the indiscriminate nature of this learning—the model does not distinguish between high-quality and low-quality information, between helpful behaviors and harmful ones, between accurate facts and widespread misconceptions. The model's objective during training is simply to predict what text would appear next, not to produce text that is safe, helpful, or aligned with human values.

## The Challenge of Controlling Model Behavior and Selecting Desired Responses

The difficulty in controlling language model behavior stems directly from the unsupervised nature of their training. Consider a concrete example from the domain of programming assistance. We might want an AI coding assistant to understand common programming mistakes—this knowledge is valuable for identifying and correcting errors. However, when generating code, we want the model to exhibit high-quality coding ability, which may be relatively rare in its training data. The model has learned from all code it has seen, including bugs, anti-patterns, and poor practices. Without intervention, the model might produce code that reflects the average quality of its training data rather than the best practices we desire.

A similar problem arises with factual accuracy and misconceptions. Suppose a particular misconception is believed by a significant portion of the population. A language model trained on general internet text might have been exposed to this misconception extensively. We might want the model to be aware that this belief exists—to understand what people think—but we certainly do not want the model to present the misconception as true in proportion to its prevalence in the training data. The challenge is that the model's knowledge encompasses both the truth and the misconception, and without explicit guidance, the model may not reliably distinguish between them.

The fundamental issue is one of selection. Language models possess vast knowledge and abilities, but not all of this learned content is desirable to reproduce. The goal of alignment is to select from the model's capabilities those responses and behaviors that humans find safe, helpful, and appropriate. This requires going beyond the unsupervised training paradigm to inject human judgment about what constitutes good behavior.

## Introduction to Alignment: Safety, Helpfulness, and Steerability

Alignment refers to the process of steering a model's behavior to match human intentions and values. The concept encompasses several interconnected goals. Safety means ensuring that the model does not produce harmful outputs—whether through generating dangerous instructions, perpetuating biases, or engaging in deceptive behavior. Helpfulness means that the model provides responses that are genuinely useful to users, addressing their questions and needs effectively. Steerability means that users can reliably control the model's behavior through prompts and instructions, directing it toward desired outputs rather than receiving unpredictable responses.

The importance of alignment becomes clear when we consider the deployment of language models in real-world applications. An AI system that can be prompted to produce harmful content, that provides unhelpful or incorrect information, or that ignores user instructions is not merely a technical inconvenience—it represents a potential source of harm. As language models become more capable and more widely deployed, the stakes of misalignment increase. Selecting the desired responses and behaviors from the model's broad capabilities is crucial to building AI systems that are genuinely useful and trustworthy.

Achieving alignment is not a simple matter of filtering outputs or adding rules. The model's behavior emerges from complex interactions between its training data, architecture, and prompting. Effective alignment requires modifying the model's underlying behavior, not just its outputs, through principled training procedures that instill desired patterns of response.

## The Role of Human Preferences in Refining Model Behavior

A key insight in the development of aligned language models is that human preferences provide a powerful signal for training. Rather than attempting to specify rules for every possible situation—a hopelessly complex task given the open-ended nature of language—researchers have found it more effective to collect human judgments about the relative quality of model outputs. These preferences, aggregated across many examples, provide a training signal that captures human values in a practical and scalable manner.

The collection of human preferences typically involves presenting human labelers with pairs of model outputs and asking which one they prefer. For example, given a prompt asking for a summary of a document, two different summaries might be generated, and a human would indicate which summary is better. These pairwise comparisons are often easier for humans to provide than generating expert demonstrations from scratch. A person may find it straightforward to judge that one response is better than another even if they would have difficulty writing a perfect response themselves.

This preference data becomes the foundation for training models that align with human judgment. The intuition is that by learning from examples of what humans prefer, the model internalizes patterns of behavior that lead to preferred outputs. This approach has proven effective across a range of tasks including translation, summarization, story-telling, and instruction-following, improving model performance in ways that correlate with human judgment of quality.

## Overview of Existing Approaches: Supervised Fine-Tuning and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

Existing methods for aligning language models to human preferences generally follow a multi-stage pipeline. The first stage, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), takes a pre-trained language model and fine-tunes it on high-quality data for the downstream task of interest, such as dialogue or summarization. This produces a model denoted as $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ that has been adapted to the target domain through standard supervised learning. The SFT stage provides a reasonable starting point, but it does not directly incorporate preference information.

The second major approach is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). RLHF begins with the SFT model and proceeds through additional phases. In the preference sampling and reward learning phase, the SFT model is prompted to generate pairs of responses. Human labelers express preferences between these responses, producing data in the form $(x, y_w, y_l)$ where $x$ is the prompt, $y_w$ is the preferred completion, and $y_l$ is the dispreferred completion. These preferences are assumed to be generated by some latent reward function $r^*(x, y)$ that reflects true human preferences, though this function is unknown.

To model these preferences, a common choice is the Bradley-Terry model, which stipulates that the probability of preferring one response over another follows the form:

$$p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.$$

Given a dataset of preference comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, a reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ is trained to maximize the likelihood of the observed preferences. This is formulated as a binary classification problem with the negative log-likelihood loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l))\right],$$

where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic function. The reward model is typically initialized from the SFT model with a linear layer added to produce a scalar reward prediction.

Once the reward model is trained, the RL fine-tuning phase optimizes the language model policy to maximize the expected reward while staying close to the reference model. This is formulated as:

$$\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(y \mid x)}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi_\theta(y \mid x) \mid\mid \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\right],$$

where $\beta$ controls the deviation from the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (typically the SFT model), and the KL-divergence constraint prevents the model from drifting too far from the distribution on which the reward model was trained. Due to the discrete nature of language generation, this objective is not differentiable and is typically optimized using reinforcement learning algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

While RLHF has produced models with impressive capabilities, it has significant drawbacks. The pipeline is complex, involving training multiple models and sampling from the language model policy during training, which incurs substantial computational costs. The instability of reinforcement learning algorithms makes the training process difficult to tune and replicate. These challenges motivate the search for simpler, more direct approaches to training language models from human preferences—approaches that avoid the complexity of explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning while still achieving alignment with human values.
