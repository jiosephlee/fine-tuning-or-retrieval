\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why is the KL divergence constraint necessary in RLHF?
Question:
The paper mentions that the RL fine-tuning phase optimizes:

$$\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\mid\mid \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\right]$$

They say the constraint prevents the model from drifting too far from the distribution where the reward model is accurate, and prevents mode-collapse. But I'm confused about two things:

1. Why would the reward model be inaccurate far from its training distribution? Isn't it a neural network that should generalize?

2. What exactly is mode-collapse in this context, and why would maximizing reward without the KL constraint cause it?
Answer:
Great questions! Let me address both of your concerns about the KL divergence constraint in RLHF.

**Why the reward model becomes inaccurate far from its training distribution**

The key insight is that the reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ is trained on preference data generated from the SFT model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$. Specifically, the training pairs $(y_1, y_2)$ are sampled from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)$, and human labelers provide preferences between these samples.

While neural networks can generalize, they often generalize poorly to inputs far outside their training distribution. If the policy $\pi_\theta$ drifts significantly from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ during RL optimization, it may start generating outputs $y$ that occupy regions of the output space the reward model never saw during training. In these out-of-distribution regions, the reward model's predictions become unreliable—it may assign spuriously high scores to outputs that humans would actually rate poorly.

Without the KL constraint, the optimization process would exploit these "blind spots" in the reward model, finding outputs with inflated reward estimates rather than genuinely preferred outputs. This is sometimes called "reward hacking" or "reward over-optimization."

**What mode-collapse means in this context**

Mode-collapse in language generation refers to the policy losing diversity and converging to produce only a narrow set of high-reward outputs. Without the KL constraint, the optimization would simply maximize:

$$\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(y \mid x)}[r_\phi(x, y)]$$

If a particular response $y^*$ receives high reward, the optimization has no incentive to maintain probability mass on other valid responses. The policy could collapse to assigning nearly all probability to $y^*$ (or a small set of high-reward responses), causing the model to generate repetitive, narrow outputs regardless of the input context.

The KL constraint $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta(y \mid x) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)]$ counteracts this by penalizing deviations from the reference policy, which maintains diversity since $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ was trained as a general language model capable of producing varied outputs across the full distribution of reasonable responses.
