\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does the partition function $Z(x)$ cancel out in the DPO derivation?
Question:
I'm reading the DPO paper and trying to understand the key derivation. The paper shows that the optimal policy under KL-constrained reward maximization is:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

Then they rearrange to get:

$$r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

When substituting this into the Bradley-Terry preference model, they claim the partition function cancels. I understand the Bradley-Terry model depends on reward differences, but I'm not seeing exactly how the math works out. Can someone walk through why $\beta \log Z(x)$ disappears?
Answer:
The partition function cancels because the Bradley-Terry model depends on the **difference** between rewards for two completions, and $\beta \log Z(x)$ is independent of the response $y$.

Let me walk through the derivation. Starting from the reparameterization of the reward function (Equation 5 in the paper):

$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

When we compute the reward difference between two responses $y_1$ and $y_2$:

$$r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2) = \left[\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right] - \left[\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right]$$

The $\beta \log Z(x)$ term appears in both rewards with the **same sign** and **same value** (since $Z(x)$ only depends on $x$, not on which response we're evaluating). They cancel directly:

$$r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}$$

Now substituting into the Bradley-Terry preference model (Equation 1):

$$p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\left(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2)\right)$$

Since the preference probability only depends on this difference, the partition function doesn't affect preference predictions at all.

This connects to a deeper insight in the paper: reward functions are only identifiable up to an equivalence class. As Definition 1 states, two rewards $r(x,y)$ and $r'(x,y)$ are equivalent if $r(x,y) - r'(x,y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$ depending only on $x$. Adding the partition function term creates an equivalent reward that induces identical preferences and the same optimal policy. The partition function is essentially an "unidentifiable" component of the reward that the Bradley-Terry model cannot and does not need to determine.

### What is the intuition behind the DPO gradient weighting?
Question:
The paper derives the gradient of the DPO loss as:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))\left[\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)\right]\right]$$

They mention the weight $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))$ is "higher when the reward estimate is wrong." I understand the sigmoid outputs a value between 0 and 1, but I'm confused about why this particular weighting scheme prevents model degeneration. Why couldn't we just use a simpler objective that directly increases $\pi(y_w)$ and decreases $\pi(y_l)$ without this weighting?
Answer:
The key intuition is that the sigmoid weighting acts as an **adaptive stopping mechanism** that prevents over-optimization on examples where the model has already learned the correct preference ordering.

Let me unpack this. The weight $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$ equals:

$$\sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w|x)}\right) = \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w|x)}\right)$$

This sigmoid evaluates to approximately:
- **Close to 1** when $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) \gg \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)$: the model incorrectly assigns much higher reward to the dispreferred response
- **Close to 0** when $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) \ll \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)$: the model correctly ranks the preferred response higher

The critical insight is that **a naive objective without this weighting would continue pushing** $\pi(y_w)$ **up and** $\pi(y_l)$ **down indefinitely**, even when the model already correctly orders them. This leads to model degeneration because:

1. **Probability mass collapse**: The model can drive preferred completions toward probability 1 and dispreferred toward 0, causing the policy to become overly confident and lose diversity

2. **KL constraint violation**: Without the weighting, the objective ignores the implicit KL constraint. The weighting naturally enforces this constraint because once the model correctly orders completions with sufficient margin, updates cease

3. **Distribution shift**: Aggressive optimization can push the policy far from $\pi_\text{ref}$, the distribution where the preference comparisons were collected, harming generalization

The paper explicitly validates this in their experiments with the **Unlikelihood** baseline, which uses a simpler objective without the adaptive weighting. They find this causes the language model to degenerate (referenced in Appendix Table 3).

The DPO weighting emerges naturally from the Bradley-Terry preference model—since DPO maximizes likelihood under this model, the gradient automatically includes this weighting term. It's not an arbitrary design choice but a mathematical consequence of properly modeling human preferences.

### What does it mean for two reward functions to be "equivalent"?
Question:
In Section 5.1, the paper defines an equivalence relation between reward functions: two reward functions $r(x,y)$ and $r'(x,y)$ are equivalent if $r(x,y) - r'(x,y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$.

Lemma 1 states that equivalent reward functions induce the same preference distribution under the Bradley-Terry model, and Lemma 2 states they induce the same optimal policy. 

I think I understand Lemma 1 intuitively since Bradley-Terry only cares about reward differences. But why does this equivalence relation partition reward functions into classes? And more importantly, why does Theorem 1 say that all reward classes can be represented as $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$? Doesn't this eliminate the $f(x)$ term entirely?
Answer:
The equivalence relation partitions reward functions because it satisfies the three axioms of an equivalence relation. Two reward functions $r$ and $r'$ are equivalent when $r(x,y) - r'(x,y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$ depending only on $x$. This is reflexive ($r - r = 0$, and $0$ is a valid function of $x$), symmetric (if $r - r' = f(x)$, then $r' - r = -f(x)$), and transitive (if $r - r' = f(x)$ and $r' - r'' = g(x)$, then $r - r'' = f(x) + g(x)$). By standard mathematical theory, any equivalence relation partitions a set into disjoint equivalence classes.

Your second question gets at the heart of the DPO insight. The reparameterization $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ doesn't eliminate the $f(x)$ term—it *selects* a specific canonical representative from each equivalence class.

Here's how it works. Given any reward function $r(x,y)$, Eq. (4) in the paper shows the optimal policy is:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$$

where $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$ is the partition function. Rearranging gives:

$$r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x)$$

The term $\beta\log Z(x)$ depends only on $x$, so it's exactly the $f(x)$-type term that defines the equivalence class. The proof of Theorem 1 defines a projection that subtracts this term:

$$f(r; \pi_{\text{ref}}, \beta)(x,y) = r(x,y) - \beta\log Z(x)$$

This projected reward is still in the same equivalence class as $r$ (we just subtracted a function of $x$), and it now has the canonical form $\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$.

So DPO's reparameterization doesn't constrain what rewards can be learned—it just picks a convenient representative from each class where the partition function normalizes to 1, making $\pi$ a valid probability distribution. This is why the paper says the reparameterization "does not constrain the class of learned reward models" while making the optimal policy analytically tractable.

### Why is the KL divergence constraint necessary in RLHF?
Question:
The paper mentions that the RL fine-tuning phase optimizes:

$$\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\mid\mid \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\right]$$

They say the constraint prevents the model from drifting too far from the distribution where the reward model is accurate, and prevents mode-collapse. But I'm confused about two things:

1. Why would the reward model be inaccurate far from its training distribution? Isn't it a neural network that should generalize?

2. What exactly is mode-collapse in this context, and why would maximizing reward without the KL constraint cause it?
Answer:
Great questions! Let me address both of your concerns about the KL divergence constraint in RLHF.

**Why the reward model becomes inaccurate far from its training distribution**

The key insight is that the reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ is trained on preference data generated from the SFT model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$. Specifically, the training pairs $(y_1, y_2)$ are sampled from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)$, and human labelers provide preferences between these samples.

While neural networks can generalize, they often generalize poorly to inputs far outside their training distribution. If the policy $\pi_\theta$ drifts significantly from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ during RL optimization, it may start generating outputs $y$ that occupy regions of the output space the reward model never saw during training. In these out-of-distribution regions, the reward model's predictions become unreliable—it may assign spuriously high scores to outputs that humans would actually rate poorly.

Without the KL constraint, the optimization process would exploit these "blind spots" in the reward model, finding outputs with inflated reward estimates rather than genuinely preferred outputs. This is sometimes called "reward hacking" or "reward over-optimization."

**What mode-collapse means in this context**

Mode-collapse in language generation refers to the policy losing diversity and converging to produce only a narrow set of high-reward outputs. Without the KL constraint, the optimization would simply maximize:

$$\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(y \mid x)}[r_\phi(x, y)]$$

If a particular response $y^*$ receives high reward, the optimization has no incentive to maintain probability mass on other valid responses. The policy could collapse to assigning nearly all probability to $y^*$ (or a small set of high-reward responses), causing the model to generate repetitive, narrow outputs regardless of the input context.

The KL constraint $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta(y \mid x) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)]$ counteracts this by penalizing deviations from the reference policy, which maintains diversity since $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ was trained as a general language model capable of producing varied outputs across the full distribution of reasonable responses.

### How does DPO avoid reinforcement learning entirely?
Question:
The main claim of the DPO paper is that it eliminates the need for reinforcement learning. But looking at the objective they optimize:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$

This looks like supervised learning - just a binary cross-entropy loss. But PPO also uses supervised-style updates with the policy gradient. What exactly makes DPO "not RL"? Is it just that we don't need to sample from the policy during training, or is there something more fundamental about the optimization landscape?
Answer:
The key distinction lies in whether you need to sample from the policy during training to estimate gradients. Let me explain what makes DPO fundamentally different from PPO-style RL.

**What makes PPO "RL" despite supervised-style updates?**

PPO optimizes the objective:
$$\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_\phi(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\bigl[\pi_\theta \| \pi_\text{ref}\bigr]$$

The critical word is "expectation over $y \sim \pi_\theta$." This expectation must be estimated by sampling from the *current* policy during training. Even though PPO uses a surrogate loss that looks like supervised learning, you're computing advantages from on-policy samples, making it a policy gradient method.

**What DPO does instead:**

DPO starts from the same constrained objective but makes a key mathematical observation. The optimal policy for this objective has the form:
$$\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

The partition function $Z(x)$ is intractable. However, DPO inverts this relationship:
$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

When substituting into the Bradley-Terry preference model, the partition function **cancels** because it only depends on $x$ (not $y$):
$$p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l \mid x)}\right)$$

This yields a closed-form expression where preference probability depends only on policy parameters, not on an explicit reward model.

**The fundamental difference:**

You now have a fixed dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x, y_w, y_l)\}$ and a loss function that directly maps policy parameters to likelihood. No sampling from $\pi_\theta$ is required—you simply compute log-probabilities of the fixed completions $y_w$ and $y_l$ and optimize via gradient descent.

As the paper notes, the gradient is:
$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\beta\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))\big[\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_l \mid x)\big]\bigg]$$

This is computed entirely from the fixed preference dataset—no policy rollouts, no advantage estimation, no value function learning. The optimization landscape is that of maximum likelihood estimation, not policy gradient optimization.

### Why does the reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ need to be $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$?
Question:
In Section 4, the paper mentions that when $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ is available, they initialize $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi^{\text{SFT}}$. When it's not available, they fine-tune on preferred completions to create the reference model.

I understand the KL constraint requires a reference policy, but why specifically should it be the SFT model? The paper mentions mitigating "distribution shift between the true reference distribution which is unavailable, and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$." What is this true reference distribution, and why does using a different reference cause problems?
Answer:
The "true reference distribution" refers to the policy that was actually used to sample the completions in the preference dataset. In the standard RLHF pipeline described in Section 3, the SFT model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ generates pairs of completions $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)$, which are then labeled by humans to create the preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$.

The issue arises from how DPO derives its objective. Starting from the KL-constrained RL objective in Eq. 3:
$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]$$

The optimal policy takes the form (Eq. 4):
$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

This derivation, combined with the Bradley-Terry preference model, leads to the DPO loss (Eq. 7):
$$\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$

The implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ is defined relative to $\pi_\text{ref}$. If the preference data was sampled from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ but we use a different $\pi_\text{ref}$, the implicit reward model becomes miscalibrated—it's essentially an importance weighting problem where the wrong baseline distorts the relative preferences learned from the data.

Intuitively, DPO learns by comparing how much more likely $\pi_\theta$ makes preferred responses versus dispreferred ones, *relative to* the reference policy. If the reference policy assigns systematically different probabilities to responses compared to the distribution those responses were sampled from, the learned preference signal gets distorted.

When $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ is unavailable, the paper recommends fine-tuning on preferred completions to approximate it. This brings $\pi_\text{ref}$ closer to the sampling distribution, minimizing the distribution shift and ensuring the implicit reward model more accurately reflects the preferences encoded in the data.

### How does Theorem 1 guarantee we don't lose generality?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that all reward classes consistent with Plackett-Luce models can be represented as:

$$r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$$

The proof sketch defines a projection operator $f(r; \pi_{\text{ref}}, \beta)$ that normalizes the reward with the partition function. Then it claims this produces a member of the equivalence class with the desired form.

I'm struggling to follow this argument. How does showing that $f$ produces one member of the equivalence class prove that ALL reward classes can be represented this way? What if there are reward functions outside these classes?
Answer:
The key to understanding this theorem lies in recognizing what the equivalence relation means and how the projection operator works.

Two reward functions are equivalent if they differ by a function of only $x$. Under the Plackett-Luce preference model, equivalent rewards produce identical preference distributions because the model only depends on reward *differences* between completions:

$$p(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2))$$

If $r'(x,y) = r(x,y) + f(x)$, then $r'(x,y_1) - r'(x,y_2) = r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)$, so the preference probabilities are unchanged.

Now, for **any** reward function $r$, the proof constructs a specific member of its equivalence class. The projection operator:

$$f(r; \pi_{\text{ref}}, \beta)(x,y) = r(x,y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$$

subtracts the log partition function, which depends **only on $x$**. By definition, this produces a reward in the same equivalence class as $r$.

The crucial step: using Eq. (5) from the paper, the optimal policy induced by $r$ is:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$$

Substituting this into the projected reward yields:

$$f(r; \pi_{\text{ref}}, \beta)(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$$

So for **every** reward function $r$, there exists an equivalent reward with the DPO parameterization. Since equivalence classes partition the space of all reward functions, every class has at least one representative of this form.

Your concern about "reward functions outside these classes" misunderstands the structure: every reward function belongs to exactly one equivalence class, and the proof shows each class contains a DPO-form representative. We don't lose generality because we're not restricting which rewards can be learned—we're just selecting a particular canonical form from each class that makes the optimal policy analytically tractable.

### What causes instability in actor-critic algorithms for RLHF?
Question:
In Section 5.2, the paper discusses instability of PPO and connects it to the control-as-inference framework. They derive an objective:

$$\max_{\pi_\theta}\mathbb{E}\left[r_\phi(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x, y)\right) - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\right]$$

They call the middle term the "soft value function" and say that without it, "the policy gradient could have high variance." Why does this normalization term reduce variance? And why is it difficult to estimate with a learned value function in practice?
Answer:
The instability stems from the variance-reducing role of the soft value function and the practical challenges of estimating it.

**Why the normalization term reduces variance**

The soft value function $V(x) = \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)$ acts as a baseline that depends only on the prompt $x$, not on the response $y$. In policy gradient methods, the gradient scales with $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ multiplied by some signal. Without the baseline, this signal is the raw reward $r_{\phi}(x,y)$, which can vary dramatically across different sampled responses. By subtracting $V(x)$, we center the reward signal: responses with rewards above average for that prompt receive positive weight, and those below receive negative weight. This is analogous to how value function baselines reduce variance in standard REINFORCE—the baseline absorbs variability attributable to the state rather than the action, yielding a lower-variance advantage-like signal.

**Why estimation is difficult in practice**

The soft value function requires marginalizing over all possible responses $y$, computing a partition function. In language modeling, the response space is combinatorially large—exponentially large in sequence length—making exact computation intractable. A learned value function would need to approximate this sum through sampling or training a separate critic network. This introduces several problems: the critic must be trained jointly with the policy, creating potential instability when its estimates are inaccurate early in training; Monte Carlo estimates of the partition function can be high-variance themselves; and an inaccurate baseline can actually *increase* variance rather than reduce it. Prior work has used single-sample Monte Carlo estimates with human completions as a crude approximation, but this is inherently noisy.

**How DPO avoids this issue**

DPO's reparameterization elegantly sidesteps this problem. By expressing the reward directly in terms of the policy ratio $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$ (up to the partition function), the Bradley-Terry preference model only requires reward *differences*, causing the partition function to cancel exactly. No baseline or value function estimation is needed.
