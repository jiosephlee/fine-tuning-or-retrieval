\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

# From RLHF to DPO: Simplifying the Path to Aligned AI

Large unsupervised language models (LMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities, but steering their behavior to be safe and helpful remains a significant challenge. The current gold standard for aligning these models with human intent is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). While effective, RLHF is a complex, often unstable pipeline that involves training multiple models and optimizing a language model with reinforcement learning. In this post, we explore Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a method proposed by Rafailov et al. that simplifies this process significantly by eliminating the need for reinforcement learning entirely.

## The RLHF Bottleneck

To understand the innovation of DPO, we first need to look at why RLHF is so cumbersome. The standard RLHF pipeline typically involves three distinct stages:

1.  **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** A pre-trained model is fine-tuned on high-quality human demonstrations.
2.  **Reward Modeling:** The SFT model generates pairs of responses for a given prompt, and human labelers rank them. A reward model is then trained to predict which response humans prefer.
3.  **RL Fine-Tuning:** The language model (the policy) is fine-tuned using reinforcement learning (specifically algorithms like PPO) to maximize the reward predicted by the reward model, while a KL-divergence constraint prevents it from drifting too far from the original SFT model.

The third step is the bottleneck. RL algorithms like PPO are computationally expensive, unstable to train, and require significant hyperparameter tuning. Furthermore, the reward model is just an approximation; optimizing the policy against it using RL can lead to "reward hacking," where the model finds ways to maximize the reward signal without actually satisfying the human preference.

## Enter Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

The authors of the paper introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that achieves the same goal as RLHF but with a simple classification objective. Instead of fitting a reward model and then using RL to optimize the policy, DPO directly optimizes the policy using the preference data.

The key insight relies on a mathematical reparameterization. The standard RLHF objective aims to maximize the reward while staying close to a reference policy (the SFT model). It turns out that the optimal solution to this constrained optimization problem has a closed-form solution. 

Specifically, the optimal policy $\pi_r$ for a reward function $r$ is given by:

$$ \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right) $$

Here, $\pi_\text{ref}$ is the reference policy, $\beta$ is a parameter controlling the KL-divergence constraint, and $Z(x)$ is a partition function that ensures the probabilities sum to 1. While calculating $Z(x)$ is intractable in practice, the authors realized that by rearranging this equation, they could express the reward function in terms of the optimal policy:

$$ r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) $$

## Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model

This rearrangement is the core of DPO. Human preference data is usually modeled using the Bradley-Terry model, which describes the probability that one response is preferred over another based on the difference in their rewards. When the authors plugged their expression for the reward function into the preference model, the intractable partition function $Z(x)$ cancelled out.

This is a crucial result. It means we can define the likelihood of human preference data directly in terms of the policy $\pi$, without ever needing to know the partition function or explicitly train a reward model. This transformation turns the RL problem into a simple binary cross-entropy loss problem.

The resulting loss function, $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$, increases the likelihood of the preferred responses and decreases the likelihood of the dispreferred responses. However, it does so with a dynamic weighting factor. This weight is higher when the model currently ranks the dispreferred response higher than the preferred one—essentially penalizing the model more for mistakes it is currently making. This prevents the model from degenerating, a common issue with naive probability-ratio objectives.

## Performance and Simplicity

The paper demonstrates that DPO is not just theoretically elegant but also practically effective. In experiments across three diverse tasks—controlled sentiment generation, summarization, and single-turn dialogue—DPO performed as well as or better than PPO-based RLHF.

In the controlled sentiment generation task, DPO achieved a better trade-off between maximizing reward and minimizing KL-divergence from the reference policy compared to PPO. In summarization and dialogue tasks, DPO models were preferred by GPT-4 evaluators (correlated with human judgments) at similar or higher rates than PPO-trained models. Notably, DPO achieved these results without significant hyperparameter tuning and was more robust to changes in sampling temperature.

## Conclusion

DPO represents a significant step forward in making language model alignment accessible. By recognizing that the optimal policy inherently contains the information of a reward function, DPO bypasses the unstable and complex RL loop entirely. It replaces the RLHF pipeline with a simple, stable, and computationally efficient classification objective. This simplification lowers the barrier to training aligned models, potentially enabling wider adoption and experimentation in the field of AI safety and alignment.
