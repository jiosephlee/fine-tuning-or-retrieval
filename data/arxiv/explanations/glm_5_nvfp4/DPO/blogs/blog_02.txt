\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

# The Hidden Math: How DPO Turns Your Policy into a Reward Model

The title of the paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" isn't just clever marketing—it's a mathematical fact. Hidden within the equations is a reparameterization trick that transforms how we think about aligning language models with human preferences. Let's unpack the "secret" that makes DPO work.

## The RLHF Problem: A Two-Stage Headache

Traditional Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) follows a winding path. First, you train a reward model on human preference data, essentially teaching a neural network to predict which responses humans prefer. Then, you use reinforcement learning—typically PPO—to optimize your language model to maximize this learned reward, while adding a KL-divergence constraint to prevent the model from drifting too far from its original behavior.

The objective looks like this:

$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]$$

This works, but it's complex. You're fitting one model (the reward model), then training another (the policy) using that first model's predictions as ground truth. The process is unstable, computationally expensive, and involves sampling from the language model during training.

## The Key Insight: An Analytical Mapping

DPO's breakthrough comes from a simple question: what if we could skip the reward model entirely?

The starting point is a well-known result in control theory. The optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem has a closed-form solution:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

where $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is the partition function that normalizes the distribution.

Here's the problem: computing $Z(x)$ requires summing over all possible completions, which is intractable for language models. This is why standard RLHF uses RL algorithms—they sidestep the need to compute this normalization.

But DPO takes a different approach. Instead of trying to compute $Z(x)$, the authors ask: what if we rearrange the equation to express the reward in terms of the policy?

Taking the logarithm and doing some algebra:

$$r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

This looks like it still has the intractable $Z(x)$ term. But here's where the magic happens.

## The Bradley-Terry Model Saves the Day

Human preferences are typically modeled using the Bradley-Terry model, which says that the probability of preferring response $y_1$ over $y_2$ depends only on the difference in rewards:

$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\sigma\bigl(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2)\bigr)$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic function.

When we substitute our reparameterized reward expression into this model, something beautiful happens: the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out completely! Why? Because $Z(x)$ only depends on the prompt $x$, not the response $y$. So when we compute the difference $r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2)$, the $\beta \log Z(x)$ terms subtract away.

What remains is a preference model expressed entirely in terms of policies:

$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}$$

This means we can fit the optimal policy directly from preference data, without ever explicitly computing a reward.

## The DPO Objective: A Simple Classification Loss

With the preference probability expressed in terms of the policy, we can now write down a maximum likelihood objective. For a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ containing prompts $x$ and pairs of preferred $y_w$ and dispreferred $y_l$ responses:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$

This is just binary cross-entropy loss—the same loss used for training reward models in standard RLHF. The key difference is that we're training the policy directly, not an intermediate reward model.

The implicit reward defined by the policy is: $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$

Your language model $\pi_\theta$ is secretly a reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$.

## What Does the DPO Update Actually Do?

Looking at the gradient of the DPO loss provides an intuitive understanding:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\beta\mathbb{E} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{higher weight when reward estimate is wrong}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{increase likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{decrease likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg]$$

The gradient does two things: it increases the likelihood of preferred completions and decreases the likelihood of dispreferred ones. But crucially, the update is weighted by how wrong the implicit reward model is. If the model already assigns much higher reward to the preferred response, the weight is small. If the model incorrectly prefers the dispreferred response, the weight is large.

This dynamic weighting is essential. The paper shows that a naive version without this weighting causes the language model to degenerate.

## Why This Doesn't Constrain What We Can Learn

A natural concern arises: does this reparameterization limit the class of reward functions we can represent? The paper proves that it doesn't.

The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows that for any reward function $r(x, y)$, there exists an equivalent reward function (in the same equivalence class under the Bradley-Terry model) that can be written as:

$$r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$$

for some policy $\pi$. Two reward functions are considered equivalent if they differ only by a function of $x$ (which doesn't affect preference probabilities). This means DPO selects a specific reward function from each equivalence class—one that makes the optimal policy analytically tractable.

## The Payoff

This mathematical maneuver eliminates the need for reinforcement learning entirely. DPO directly optimizes what we ultimately care about—the policy—using a simple supervised learning objective. The experiments confirm that DPO performs as well as or better than PPO-based RLHF across sentiment modulation, summarization, and dialogue tasks, while being substantially simpler to implement.

The "secret" is that we've been making the problem harder than it needs to be. By recognizing that the optimal policy and the reward model are two sides of the same coin, DPO cuts out the middleman and solves the alignment problem directly.
