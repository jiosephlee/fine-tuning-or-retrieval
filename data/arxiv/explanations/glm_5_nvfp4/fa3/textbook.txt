# Introduction and Motivation

# The Role of Attention in the Transformer Architecture

The Transformer architecture has become the foundation of modern deep learning, powering large language models, computer vision systems, and multimodal AI applications. At the heart of this architecture lies the attention mechanism, a computational primitive that enables the model to dynamically weigh the importance of different parts of the input when producing each output. Understanding attention is essential not only for grasping how Transformers work but also for appreciating why optimizing attention computation has become a critical research direction.

In the context of Transformers, attention allows each position in a sequence to attend to all other positions, capturing long-range dependencies that were difficult to model with previous architectures like recurrent neural networks. Given query, key, and value matrices $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, where $N$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the head dimension, the attention output is computed as:

$$\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}, \quad \mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}, \quad \mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d},$$

where $\mathrm{softmax}$ is applied row-wise and $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ is a scaling factor. This formulation reveals a fundamental characteristic of attention: for every query, we compute similarity scores with all keys, producing an $N \times N$ attention matrix. This means that each position can potentially influence every other position, which is the source of attention's expressive power but also its computational challenge.

# The Quadratic Scaling Challenge in Sequence Length

The attention mechanism constitutes the primary computational bottleneck in Transformers because computing the self-attention scores of queries and keys has quadratic scaling in the sequence length. This quadratic scaling arises directly from the matrix multiplication $\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$, which produces an $N \times N$ matrix. When the sequence length $N$ doubles, the computation required for this operation increases by a factor of four.

To understand the implications of this quadratic scaling, consider that modern large language models routinely process sequences of thousands of tokens. For a sequence of length 8,192, the attention matrix contains over 67 million entries. If we extend this to 32,768 tokens—a length that enables processing longer documents—the attention matrix balloons to over one billion entries. This rapid growth in computational requirements creates a practical ceiling on the context length that models can efficiently process.

The backward pass compounds this challenge further. During training, computing gradients through attention requires five matrix multiplications compared to two in the forward pass, meaning the backward pass is approximately 2.5 times more expensive in terms of floating-point operations. This computational burden has motivated significant research into making attention faster, particularly for long-context scenarios.

# Long-Context Applications and Their Promise

The demand for longer context lengths stems from a diverse array of applications that could fundamentally transform what AI systems can accomplish. When attention can efficiently scale to longer sequences, it unlocks new capabilities across multiple domains.

In natural language processing, scaling attention to longer contexts enables modeling and reasoning over multiple long documents simultaneously. This capability is essential for tasks like legal document analysis, scientific literature review, and comprehensive code understanding across large codebases. Current models are limited by their context windows, forcing them to either truncate inputs or employ complex retrieval mechanisms. Efficient long-context attention would allow models to genuinely "read" and reason over entire documents rather than processing them piecemeal.

Beyond text, longer context windows open doors to new modalities. High-resolution images contain far more information than can be captured in standard patch-based representations used by vision Transformers. Similarly, video processing requires attending across many frames—each frame itself being a high-dimensional input—to capture temporal dependencies and motion patterns. Audio processing with Transformers, as in the Conformer architecture, also benefits from longer sequence modeling to capture extended acoustic context.

Emerging applications further underscore the importance of long-context attention. User interaction systems that maintain conversation history over extended dialogues require attending to potentially thousands of previous turns. Similarly, AI agents that plan and execute actions over long horizons need to maintain awareness of their entire action history to make coherent decisions. Each of these applications is currently constrained by the computational cost of attention, motivating the development of more efficient algorithms.

# Evolution of Attention Optimization

The research community has pursued multiple approaches to address attention's computational bottleneck, broadly falling into categories of approximation methods, software optimization, and alternative architectures.

Approximation methods attempt to reduce the computational complexity below quadratic while preserving acceptable accuracy. These approaches include sparse attention patterns that limit each position to attend to only a subset of other positions, low-rank approximations that exploit structure in the attention matrix, and kernel-based methods that reformulate attention as a linear operation. While these methods can dramatically reduce computation, they often sacrifice model quality or require careful tuning.

Software optimization approaches, in contrast, aim to accelerate exact attention computation without sacrificing accuracy. These methods focus on optimizing how attention is implemented on hardware, particularly GPUs. The key insight is that the standard implementation of attention, which materializes the full $N \times N$ attention matrix to global memory (also called High Bandwidth Memory or HBM), incurs substantial memory bandwidth costs that can dominate the overall runtime.

Alternative architectures have also emerged that aim to replace attention entirely with mechanisms having linear complexity. Architectures like RWKV, RetNet, and Mamba attempt to capture long-range dependencies through different computational primitives, often drawing on ideas from recurrent neural networks or state space models. While promising, these approaches require rethinking model design and training procedures, whereas optimizing attention directly benefits the vast existing ecosystem of Transformer-based models.

# FlashAttention and FlashAttention-2: Foundational Ideas and Limitations

FlashAttention represented a breakthrough in software optimization for attention. The key innovation was a novel tiling strategy that eliminates intermediate reads and writes to slow global memory by fusing all attention operations into a single GPU kernel. Rather than computing the entire attention matrix and storing it to HBM before proceeding to subsequent operations, FlashAttention processes attention in small tiles that remain in fast on-chip memory (shared memory and registers) throughout the computation. This approach dramatically reduces memory bandwidth requirements, as the expensive $N \times N$ attention matrix never needs to be materialized to global memory.

The mathematical foundation of FlashAttention relies on a local version of the softmax reduction. Since softmax involves exponentials that can cause numerical overflow, standard implementations subtract the row maximum before exponentiating. FlashAttention showed that this reduction could be computed incrementally across tiles: as each tile of the attention scores is computed, it can be immediately incorporated into the running maximum and the running sum of exponentials. This insight enables computing attention in a streaming fashion without ever materializing the full attention matrix.

FlashAttention-2 further refined this approach by restructuring the algorithm to parallelize over the sequence length dimension and perform the inner loop of the forward pass over blocks of the key and value matrices. This restructuring improved occupancy—the fraction of GPU resources actively engaged in computation—and improved the distribution of work across the GPU. The result was significantly better performance compared to the original FlashAttention.

However, despite these advances, FlashAttention-2 achieves only 35% utilization on the H100 GPU (based on the Hopper architecture), compared to 80-90% utilization for optimized matrix multiplication kernels. This substantial gap between achieved and theoretical peak performance indicates that significant optimization opportunities remain unexploited. Partially, this gap can be attributed to implementation-level differences, such as not using Hopper-specific instructions when targeting the Tensor Cores. Several efforts, including ThunderKitten and cuDNN 9, have demonstrated that Hopper-specific instructions and tile-based abstractions can speed up attention computation.

# The Gap Between FlashAttention-2 and Hardware Capabilities

More fundamentally, FlashAttention-2's algorithm adheres to a simplified synchronous model that makes no explicit use of two critical hardware features present in modern GPUs: asynchrony and low-precision computation.

Asynchrony emerges from hardware specialization designed to accelerate the most important operations in machine learning workloads. Modern GPUs include dedicated hardware units for specific operations: Tensor Cores perform matrix multiplications, and the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) handles memory loading. These units operate independently from the CUDA cores that perform logic, integer, and floating-point computation. This separation creates opportunities for overlapping different types of work—if the algorithm is designed to exploit it.

Low-precision number formats represent another hardware capability that FlashAttention-2 does not fully leverage. Modern GPUs have specialized hardware for low-precision computation, with formats like FP8 offering double the throughput compared to FP16 or BF16 for the same power and chip area. This trend toward lower precision has continued through GPU generations: FP16 support arrived with Pascal in 2017, BF16 with Ampere in 2020, FP8 with Hopper, and FP4 with Blackwell. The potential performance gains from exploiting these formats are substantial.

The technical challenge lies in redesigning FlashAttention-2 to harness these hardware features effectively. Asynchrony requires overlapping computation between matrix multiplication and softmax, even though one depends on the output of the other—fundamentally sequential dependencies must somehow be parallelized. Low-precision computation requires careful handling to minimize quantization error, especially given that large language models often contain outlier features that make quantization difficult. These challenges motivate the development of a new approach.

# Three Key Contributions of FlashAttention-3

FlashAttention-3 addresses these challenges through three interconnected technical contributions that synthesize into a unified algorithm optimized for modern GPU architectures.

The first contribution is producer-consumer asynchrony through warp-specialization. The algorithm defines a software pipelining scheme that exploits the asynchronous execution of data movement and Tensor Core computation by splitting producers and consumers of data into separate warps. In GPU programming, a warp is a group of 32 threads that execute together in lockstep. By designating some warps as "producers" responsible only for loading data and others as "consumers" responsible only for computation, the algorithm can hide memory latency and instruction issue latency more effectively than a synchronous approach would allow.

The second contribution involves hiding softmax computation under asynchronous block-wise matrix multiplications. The algorithm overlaps the comparatively low-throughput non-GEMM operations involved in softmax—such as floating-point multiply-add and exponential calculations—with the asynchronous WGMMA (Warp-Group Matrix Multiply-Accumulate) instructions that perform matrix multiplication. This requires reworking the FlashAttention-2 algorithm to circumvent the sequential dependencies between softmax and the matrix multiplications. In the two-stage version of the algorithm, while softmax executes on one block of the attention scores matrix, WGMMA executes asynchronously to compute the next block, enabling true instruction-level parallelism.

The third contribution adapts the algorithm for hardware-accelerated low-precision computation using FP8. This requires addressing layout conformance requirements for FP8 WGMMA instructions, which have different constraints than FP16 on how matrix blocks must be arranged in memory. Additionally, to maintain numerical accuracy despite the reduced precision, the algorithm employs block quantization (keeping one scaling factor per block rather than per tensor) and incoherent processing (multiplying queries and keys by a random orthogonal matrix before quantization to "spread out" outlier values). These techniques together enable exploiting FP8 Tensor Cores without sacrificing accuracy.

# Performance Preview: What FlashAttention-3 Achieves

The empirical validation of FlashAttention-3 demonstrates substantial performance improvements on H100 GPUs. With FP16 precision, FlashAttention-3 achieves 1.5-2.0$\times$ speedup over FlashAttention-2 in the forward pass, reaching up to 740 TFLOPs/s. This corresponds to 75% of the theoretical maximum throughput on H100 GPUs—a dramatic improvement from the 35% utilization achieved by FlashAttention-2.

For FP8 precision, FlashAttention-3 reaches close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s (petabytes of floating-point operations per second), demonstrating the potential of low-precision computation. Importantly, FP8 FlashAttention-3 achieves 2.6$\times$ lower numerical error than a baseline FP8 attention implementation, validating that the block quantization and incoherent processing techniques effectively address the accuracy concerns associated with low-precision computation.

These results position FlashAttention-3 competitively against state-of-the-art implementations. For large sequence lengths, FP16 FlashAttention-3 outperforms and FP8 FlashAttention-3 is competitive with NVIDIA's cuDNN library implementation of attention—a vendor-optimized implementation specifically designed for H100 GPUs. The combination of algorithmic innovation and hardware-specific optimization thus enables FlashAttention-3 to significantly narrow the gap between achieved and theoretical peak performance on modern GPU architectures, unlocking new possibilities for long-context applications in deep learning.

# Mathematical Foundations of Attention

## Query, Key, and Value Matrices: Dimensions and Meanings

The attention mechanism is fundamentally built upon three input matrices that serve distinct computational roles. Let us denote these matrices as $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$, corresponding to the query, key, and value matrices, respectively. For a single attention head, each of these matrices has dimensions $\mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, where $N$ represents the sequence length and $d$ represents the head dimension. The sequence length $N$ determines how many tokens or positions are being processed, while the head dimension $d$ controls the richness of the representation at each position.

The nomenclature of these matrices derives from an analogy to information retrieval systems. The query matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ encodes what each position is "looking for" or attending to. Each row of $\mathbf{Q}$ represents a query vector that seeks relevant information from other positions in the sequence. The key matrix $\mathbf{K}$ encodes what each position "offers" or can be matched against. When a query interacts with keys through matrix multiplication, we obtain attention scores that indicate the relevance of each position to each query. Finally, the value matrix $\mathbf{V}$ contains the actual information that will be aggregated based on the attention weights. This separation of keys and values allows the mechanism to retrieve information based on relevance scores computed from queries and keys, while returning content stored in the values.

In the context of multi-head attention (MHA), each head maintains its own set of query, key, and value projections. This means that for a model with $H$ heads, we have $H$ separate triples of matrices $\mathbf{Q}^{(h)}, \mathbf{K}^{(h)}, \mathbf{V}^{(h)}$ for $h = 1, 2, \ldots, H$. Each head can therefore learn to attend to different types of relationships within the input sequence. The outputs from all heads are typically concatenated and projected back to the model dimension through a final linear transformation.

## Forward Pass Computation: $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$

The forward pass of attention involves three sequential matrix operations that transform the input matrices into the output. The computation proceeds as follows:

$$\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}, \quad \mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}, \quad \mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d},$$

where $\mathrm{softmax}$ is applied row-wise and the scaling factor $\alpha$ is typically set to $1/\sqrt{d}$.

The first operation computes the score matrix $\mathbf{S}$ by multiplying the query matrix with the transpose of the key matrix. This yields an $N \times N$ matrix where entry $\mathbf{S}_{ij}$ represents the raw attention score between position $i$ (as a query) and position $j$ (as a key). The scaling factor $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ is crucial for maintaining stable gradients during training. Without this scaling, the dot products in $\mathbf{S}$ would grow large for larger head dimensions, pushing the softmax into regions of extremely small gradients.

The second operation applies the softmax function row-wise to the score matrix, producing the attention weight matrix $\mathbf{P}$. For each row $i$, the softmax function transforms the raw scores into a probability distribution:

$$\mathbf{P}_{ij} = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{N} \exp(\mathbf{S}_{ik})}.$$

This ensures that for each query position $i$, the attention weights across all key positions sum to one. The softmax operation thus converts raw similarity scores into proper attention weights that can be interpreted as the degree to which position $i$ attends to position $j$.

The third and final operation computes the output matrix $\mathbf{O}$ by multiplying the attention weight matrix $\mathbf{P}$ with the value matrix $\mathbf{V}$. Each output vector $\mathbf{O}_i$ (the $i$-th row of $\mathbf{O}$) is thus a weighted combination of all value vectors, where the weights are determined by the attention mechanism:

$$\mathbf{O}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{N} \mathbf{P}_{ij} \mathbf{V}_j.$$

This weighted sum allows each position to aggregate information from all other positions in the sequence, with the attention weights controlling how much each source position contributes.

## Numerical Stability: Subtracting Rowmax for Stable Exponentiation

A critical practical concern when implementing the softmax function is numerical stability. The exponential function $\exp(x)$ grows extremely rapidly for positive arguments and underflows to zero for large negative arguments. When computing $\exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij})$ for large values of $\mathbf{S}_{ij}$, we risk overflow, resulting in numerical instability. Conversely, when all values in a row are large negative numbers, the exponentials may underflow to zero.

The standard solution to this problem is to subtract the maximum value in each row before computing the exponentials. In practice, we compute:

$$\mathbf{S}'_i = \mathbf{S}_i - \max_j(\mathbf{S}_{ij})$$

for each row $i$, and then apply softmax to the adjusted scores:

$$\mathbf{P}_{ij} = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{S}'_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{N} \exp(\mathbf{S}'_{ik})}.$$

This transformation is mathematically equivalent to the original softmax because subtracting a constant from all values in a row does not change the softmax output. To see this, observe that for any constant $c$:

$$\frac{\exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij} - c)}{\sum_{k} \exp(\mathbf{S}_{ik} - c)} = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij}) \cdot \exp(-c)}{\sum_{k} \exp(\mathbf{S}_{ik}) \cdot \exp(-c)} = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij})}{\sum_{k} \exp(\mathbf{S}_{ik})}.$$

By choosing $c = \max_j(\mathbf{S}_{ij})$ for each row, we ensure that the largest value in each row becomes zero, and all other values are negative or zero. This guarantees that all exponentials lie in the range $(0, 1]$, avoiding overflow entirely. The numerical stability is maintained because we are now computing exponentials of values that are bounded from above.

This technique is essential for practical implementations of attention, as the raw scores in $\mathbf{S}$ can vary widely depending on the values in $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$. Without this adjustment, training deep transformer models would frequently encounter numerical instabilities that could halt training or produce incorrect gradients.

## Backward Pass Gradients: $\mathbf{dV}$, $\mathbf{dP}$, $\mathbf{dS}$, $\mathbf{dQ}$, $\mathbf{dK}$ Derivations

During backpropagation, we must compute gradients of a scalar loss function $\phi$ with respect to each of the input matrices. Let us denote $\mathbf{d}(-) = \partial \phi / \partial (-)$ as the gradient with respect to a given tensor. Given the output gradient $\mathbf{dO} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ that flows into the attention layer from subsequent layers, we compute the gradients through the chain rule.

The gradient with respect to the value matrix $\mathbf{V}$ follows from the equation $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. Since this is a matrix multiplication, we have:

$$\mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}.$$

This gradient captures how changes in each value vector affect the loss through its contribution to the weighted sum.

The gradient with respect to the attention weight matrix $\mathbf{P}$ comes from the same matrix multiplication:

$$\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO} \mathbf{V}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}.$$

This gradient indicates how changes in the attention weights affect the output.

The gradient with respect to the score matrix $\mathbf{S}$ requires differentiating through the softmax function:

$$\mathbf{dS} = \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}.$$

The detailed form of $\mathrm{dsoftmax}$ is discussed in the next section.

Finally, the gradients with respect to the query and key matrices follow from the score computation $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$:

$$\mathbf{dQ} = \alpha \mathbf{dS} \mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d},$$
$$\mathbf{dK} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}^\top \mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}.$$

Note the transposition in $\mathbf{dK}$: since $\mathbf{K}$ appears transposed in the forward computation, its gradient involves $\mathbf{dS}^\top$. The scaling factor $\alpha$ propagates through unchanged.

These gradient computations reveal the computational structure of the backward pass: it involves five matrix multiplications (compared to two in the forward pass), making the backward pass approximately $2.5$ times as many floating-point operations as the forward pass when accounting for recomputation strategies.

## The dsoftmax Operation and Its Jacobian Structure

The softmax function has a distinctive Jacobian structure that makes its backward pass somewhat unusual compared to simple element-wise operations. For a vector $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$ and its softmax output $\mathbf{p} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{s})$, the Jacobian matrix $\partial \mathbf{p} / \partial \mathbf{s}$ is not diagonal but has a more complex structure.

The gradient of the softmax function can be expressed as:

$$\mathbf{d}s = (\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{p} \mathbf{p}^\top)\mathbf{d}p,$$

where $\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{p})$ is a diagonal matrix with the elements of $\mathbf{p}$ on the diagonal. This formula captures the interaction between all elements of the softmax output. Expanding this, we can write the individual components:

$$\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial s_j} = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j),$$

where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta (equal to $1$ if $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise).

This structure reveals two important properties. First, the diagonal elements of the Jacobian are $p_i(1 - p_i)$, which shows that the gradient with respect to an input element depends on the output value itself. Second, the off-diagonal elements are $-p_i p_j$, showing that changes in any input element affect all output elements.

The matrix $\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{p} \mathbf{p}^\top$ can be interpreted as the covariance matrix of a multinoulli distribution with probabilities given by $\mathbf{p}$. This reflects the probabilistic interpretation of softmax: it outputs a probability distribution, and the gradient captures how this distribution changes with its inputs.

In the context of attention, the $\mathrm{dsoftmax}$ operation is applied row-wise to the matrix $\mathbf{dP}$ to produce $\mathbf{dS}$. For each row, we independently compute:

$$\mathbf{dS}_i = (\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{P}_i) - \mathbf{P}_i \mathbf{P}_i^\top) \mathbf{dP}_i,$$

where $\mathbf{P}_i$ and $\mathbf{dP}_i$ denote the $i$-th rows of $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{dP}$, respectively. This row-wise independence is crucial for parallel implementation.

## Parallelization Across Heads and Batches

One of the key advantages of the multi-head attention architecture is its inherent parallelizability. The computation parallelizes naturally across multiple dimensions, enabling efficient utilization of modern hardware accelerators.

For multi-head attention, each head maintains its own set of query, key, and value projections. This means that the attention computation for each head is entirely independent of the computations for other heads. As stated in the paper, "each head has its own set of query, key and value projections, and this computation parallelizes across multiple heads and batches to produce the full output tensor."

This parallelization extends to the batch dimension as well. Different samples in a batch can be processed independently since there is no interaction between samples within the attention layer. Thus, the computation exhibits three levels of parallelism: across the batch dimension, across the number of heads, and across the query sequence length.

In the forward pass, this means that all heads and all batch elements can be processed simultaneously, with each computing its own attention output. In the backward pass, the same parallelization applies: "this computation parallelizes across the number of heads and batches for the backward pass of MHA."

This parallel structure makes attention particularly well-suited for GPU execution, where thousands of threads can work simultaneously on independent computations. The FlashAttention family of algorithms further exploits this by parallelizing over the sequence length dimension in FlashAttention-2, with FlashAttention-3 noting that "the forward pass of FlashAttention-3 is embarrassingly parallel in the batch size, number of heads, and query sequence length."

## FLOP Counting for Forward and Backward Passes

Understanding the computational cost of attention requires careful counting of the floating-point operations (FLOPs) involved. The paper provides explicit formulas for this purpose.

For the forward pass, the total FLOPs are given by:

$$\text{FLOPs}_{\text{forward}} = 4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head dimension} \cdot \text{number of heads}.$$

Let us derive this formula. The attention forward pass involves two matrix multiplications. The first computes $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, multiplying an $N \times d$ matrix by a $d \times N$ matrix, which requires $2NdN = 2N^2 d$ FLOPs (counting both multiplications and additions). The second computes $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, multiplying an $N \times N$ matrix by an $N \times d$ matrix, which also requires $2N^2 d$ FLOPs. Summing these gives $4N^2 d$ for a single head, and multiplying by the number of heads gives the total.

When causal masking is applied (as in autoregressive language models), approximately half of the attention matrix entries are masked out. In this case, the FLOP count is divided by 2 to account for the fact that "approximately only half of the entries are calculated."

For the backward pass, the FLOP count is approximately $2.5$ times that of the forward pass. As the paper explains, "we multiply the forward pass FLOPs by 2.5 (since there are 2 matmuls in the forward pass and 5 matmuls in the backward pass, due to recomputation)." The backward pass requires computing gradients for $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{V}$, $\mathbf{P}$, and $\mathbf{S}$, involving five matrix multiplications compared to two in the forward pass.

The quadratic dependence on sequence length ($N^2$) makes attention the computational bottleneck for long sequences. This quadratic scaling is the primary motivation for research into efficient attention approximations and optimized implementations like FlashAttention.

## Memory Access Patterns in Standard Attention Implementation

The memory access patterns of an attention implementation critically determine its performance on modern hardware. Understanding these patterns illuminates why FlashAttention achieves significant speedups over standard implementations.

The paper defines "standard attention" as an implementation that "materializes the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM," referring to high-bandwidth memory, the GPU's global memory. This materialization creates a substantial memory bandwidth bottleneck.

Consider the sequence of operations in standard attention. First, the $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ matrices are read from HBM. The computation $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ produces an $N \times N$ matrix that must be written to HBM. Then this matrix is read back to compute $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, producing another $N \times N$ matrix written to HBM. Finally, $\mathbf{P}$ is read back to compute $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$.

For a sequence length of $N$ and head dimension $d$, the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ each have $N^2$ elements. For long sequences, this quadratic memory footprint becomes prohibitive. Moreover, the repeated reads and writes to HBM create a memory bandwidth bottleneck that limits performance.

The GPU memory hierarchy provides motivation for optimization. As described in the paper, "Global memory (GMEM), also known as HBM, is the off-chip DRAM accessible to all streaming multiprocessors (SMs)." This global memory has the largest capacity but the lowest bandwidth. In contrast, "each SM contains a small on-chip, programmer-managed highly banked cache called shared memory (SMEM)" which offers much higher bandwidth but limited capacity.

The key insight behind FlashAttention was to leverage "a local version of the softmax reduction to avoid these expensive intermediate reads/writes and fuse attention into a single kernel." By keeping intermediate results in the faster shared memory and only writing the final output to global memory, FlashAttention dramatically reduces memory traffic.

The paper notes that "FlashAttention elaborated an approach to speed up attention on GPUs through minimizing memory reads/writes." This optimization is particularly important because modern GPUs are often memory-bandwidth limited rather than compute limited, making memory access patterns a critical factor in overall performance.

# GPU Architecture and Hardware Characteristics

Understanding the hardware characteristics of modern GPUs is essential for optimizing algorithms like FlashAttention-3. This chapter provides a detailed examination of the GPU execution model, focusing on the NVIDIA Hopper architecture that FlashAttention-3 targets. We will explore how the memory hierarchy, thread organization, and specialized hardware units like Tensor Cores and the Tensor Memory Accelerator work together to enable high-performance computation.

## Memory Hierarchy: Global Memory (HBM), L2 Cache, Shared Memory (SMEM), and Register File

The GPU's memory system is organized as a strict hierarchy of data storage locales, with each level characterized by a fundamental trade-off between capacity and bandwidth. At the highest level, global memory (GMEM), also referred to as High Bandwidth Memory (HBM), serves as the primary off-chip DRAM that is accessible to all streaming multiprocessors (SMs) on the GPU. This memory layer provides the largest storage capacity but operates at the slowest access speeds. Global memory is where input tensors and model parameters typically reside when they are first loaded onto the GPU, and it is also where output results are written back for subsequent use by other kernels or for transfer back to the CPU.

Between global memory and the compute units sits the L2 cache, which transparently caches data fetched from global memory. This cache helps amortize the cost of repeated accesses to the same memory locations, acting as a bridge between the slow off-chip DRAM and the faster on-chip resources. The L2 cache is shared across all SMs on the GPU, providing a mechanism for data reuse across different threadblocks when access patterns exhibit locality.

Within each streaming multiprocessor lies shared memory (SMEM), a small but highly efficient on-chip memory that is programmer-managed. Unlike the L2 cache, which operates transparently, shared memory requires explicit loading and storing of data by the programmer. This memory is organized as a highly banked structure, meaning it is divided into multiple memory banks that can be accessed simultaneously, enabling high throughput for well-structured access patterns. Shared memory serves as a scratchpad for intermediate computations, allowing threads within a threadblock to share data without incurring the latency of global memory accesses.

At the finest granularity of the memory hierarchy is the register file, which resides within each SM and provides the fastest possible data access. Each thread has access to a limited number of registers (at most 256 registers per thread in the Hopper architecture) that are private to that thread. Register access is essentially instantaneous compared to other memory levels, making register allocation a critical optimization concern. Intermediate values that are accessed frequently during computation, such as accumulators for matrix operations, should ideally reside in registers to maximize performance.

## Bandwidth vs Capacity Tradeoffs Across Memory Levels

The organizing principle underlying the GPU memory hierarchy is an inverse relationship between capacity and bandwidth: as one moves down the hierarchy from global memory to registers, available storage capacity decreases dramatically, while access bandwidth increases correspondingly. This design philosophy reflects the physical reality that faster memory technologies require more chip area and power per bit of storage, limiting the amount that can be economically incorporated into a processor.

Global memory, with its large capacity (80 GB on the H100 GPU used in FlashAttention-3 experiments), operates at relatively modest bandwidth compared to on-chip memory. Accessing global memory incurs latency measured in hundreds of clock cycles, making it imperative for performance-oriented algorithms to minimize global memory traffic through techniques like tiling and data reuse. When an algorithm reads or writes to global memory frequently, it becomes memory-bandwidth-bound, meaning performance is limited by the rate at which data can be transferred rather than by computational throughput.

Shared memory, in contrast, provides substantially higher bandwidth than global memory. The H100's shared memory bandwidth can be calculated by considering that each SM has 128 bytes per clock cycle of shared memory bandwidth, and with 132 SMs operating at a boost clock of 1830 MHz, this yields substantial aggregate bandwidth. This high bandwidth makes shared memory ideal for storing tiles of data that will be accessed multiple times by threads within a threadblock, such as the blocks of query, key, and value matrices in FlashAttention.

The register file provides the highest bandwidth of all, with essentially zero-latency access, but with severely limited capacity. This creates a fundamental constraint on algorithm design: the working set of frequently-accessed data must fit within the available register budget, or else performance will suffer from register spilling to shared memory or global memory. FlashAttention-3 must carefully balance register usage across multiple pipeline stages, as discussed in the algorithm chapter, where additional buffering for overlapping GEMM and softmax operations increases register pressure.

## Thread Hierarchy: Threads, Warps, Warpgroups, Threadblocks, Clusters, and Grids

Parallel execution on GPUs is organized through a hierarchical threading model that maps logical work units to physical execution resources. At the most fundamental level, individual threads represent the finest granularity of parallel execution. Each thread executes the same kernel code but operates on different data elements, following the Single Instruction Multiple Thread (SIMT) paradigm that underlies GPU computing.

Threads are grouped into warps, which consist of 32 threads that execute in lockstep. The warp is the fundamental scheduling unit on NVIDIA GPUs: when a warp is selected for execution, all 32 threads execute the same instruction simultaneously, though they may operate on different data values. This lockstep execution model has profound implications for algorithm design, as divergent control flow (where different threads in a warp take different execution paths) can severely degrade performance due to thread divergence.

Building upon warps, the Hopper architecture introduces the concept of warpgroups, which consist of four contiguous warps (128 threads total). Warpgroups are particularly relevant for matrix operations, as the WGMMA instruction used to invoke Tensor Cores operates at the warpgroup granularity. This means that a single WGMMA instruction involves the coordinated effort of 128 threads working together to compute a portion of a matrix multiplication.

Threadblocks, also known as Cooperative Thread Arrays (CTAs), represent a coarser level of organization comprising multiple warps. All threads within a threadblock are guaranteed to be co-scheduled on the same streaming multiprocessor, enabling them to share resources like shared memory and to synchronize with each other using barrier instructions. Threadblocks are the primary unit for decomposing a computational problem: when launching a kernel, the programmer specifies the grid dimensions (how many threadblocks) and the block dimensions (how many threads per threadblock).

The Hopper architecture introduces threadblock clusters, which group multiple threadblocks together for co-scheduling on the same Graphics Processing Cluster (GPC). This allows threads across different threadblocks within a cluster to coordinate more efficiently, sharing certain resources and reducing synchronization overhead. At the highest level of the hierarchy, grids encompass all threadblocks launched for a given kernel invocation.

## Relationship Between Memory and Thread Hierarchies

The memory and thread hierarchies are deeply interconnected, with the organization of threads determining which memory resources they can access and how efficiently. This relationship is crucial for understanding how to structure algorithms for optimal performance on GPUs.

Threads within the same threadblock (CTA) share access to the same streaming multiprocessor and can therefore communicate through shared memory. This enables efficient data sharing patterns where threads collaboratively load data from global memory into shared memory, process it, and write results back. In FlashAttention-3, threads within a threadblock work together to load blocks of the query, key, and value matrices into shared memory before performing computations, leveraging this shared memory access to minimize global memory traffic.

At a finer granularity, threads within the same warp can communicate even more efficiently through warp-level primitives like shuffle instructions, which exchange data between registers of different threads without going through shared memory. This capability is exploited in FlashAttention-3 for operations like the in-kernel transpose required for FP8 precision.

Conversely, threads in different threadblocks cannot directly share data through shared memory or registers. Communication between threadblocks must go through global memory, which is substantially slower and requires explicit synchronization mechanisms. This is why algorithms like FlashAttention are designed to be embarrassingly parallel across certain dimensions (like batch size, number of heads, and query sequence length), allowing each threadblock to work independently without needing inter-threadblock communication.

The register file presents the most restrictive sharing model: each thread has its own private registers, inaccessible to other threads. This privacy enables extremely fast access but requires explicit programming when data must be shared. The setmaxnreg instruction in Hopper allows dynamic reallocation of the register budget between warpgroups within a threadblock, enabling asymmetric resource distribution where warpgroups performing heavy computation can obtain more registers than those primarily performing data movement.

## Tensor Cores: Dedicated Hardware for Matrix Multiplication

Modern GPUs incorporate specialized hardware units called Tensor Cores that accelerate matrix multiplication operations, which form the computational backbone of deep learning workloads. Tensor Cores are distinct from the general-purpose CUDA cores that handle logic, integer, and floating-point operations, representing a hardware specialization that reflects the importance of matrix operations in machine learning.

Tensor Cores perform matrix-matrix multiplication and accumulation operations (referred to as HMMA or WGMMA depending on the architecture) much more efficiently than general-purpose compute units. A single Tensor Core operation multiplies two small matrices and adds the result to a third accumulator matrix, all in a single hardware instruction. For example, a typical Tensor Core operation might compute $D = A \times B + C$ where $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ are small matrix fragments.

In the context of attention computation, Tensor Cores are used for the two matrix multiplications that dominate the workload: the multiplication of query and key matrices to produce attention scores ($\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$), and the multiplication of attention weights with the value matrix to produce the output ($\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$). These operations benefit enormously from Tensor Core acceleration, as they involve large matrix multiplications that can be decomposed into Tensor Core-sized tiles.

A significant advancement in the Hopper architecture is that Tensor Core operations are now asynchronous. When a thread issues a WGMMA instruction, the Tensor Core begins executing the operation while the thread can continue with other work. This asynchrony is exposed through the WGMMA (Warp Group Matrix Multiply Accumulate) instruction, which allows warpgroups to initiate matrix multiplication without blocking until completion. This capability is fundamental to FlashAttention-3's design, enabling the overlapping of computation with data movement and other operations.

## Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA): Asynchronous Memory Copy

The Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) is a dedicated hardware unit introduced in the Hopper architecture for performing asynchronous memory transfers between global memory and shared memory. Unlike traditional memory loads that require threads to actively participate in the transfer, TMA offloads the entire copy operation to dedicated hardware, freeing up threads for computation.

TMA operations can transfer multi-dimensional tensor tiles with hardware handling the addressing calculations. This is particularly useful for deep learning workloads where data is naturally organized as multi-dimensional tensors. A single thread can issue a TMA instruction to load a block of data from global memory into shared memory, and then continue with other work while the transfer proceeds in the background.

The asynchronous nature of TMA is crucial for hiding memory latency. In traditional synchronous loading, threads must wait for memory transfers to complete before using the data, creating stalls that waste computational resources. With TMA, threads can issue multiple load operations for different data blocks, perform useful work on data that has already arrived, and only wait when the data is actually needed. This enables a pipelined execution pattern where memory transfers for future iterations proceed concurrently with computation on current data.

FlashAttention-3 exploits TMA by designating producer warps that specialize in issuing TMA load instructions. These producer warps load blocks of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ matrices from global memory into shared memory using TMA, while consumer warps perform the actual attention computations. This division of labor, enabled by TMA's asynchronous operation, allows memory transfers to be overlapped with computation, maximizing hardware utilization.

## WGMMA Instruction: Asynchronous Warpgroup Matrix Multiplication

The WGMMA (Warp Group Matrix Multiply Accumulate) instruction is the Hopper architecture's mechanism for invoking Tensor Core operations at the warpgroup level. Unlike previous generations where matrix multiplication instructions were synchronous, WGMMA operates asynchronously, returning control to the issuing threads immediately while the Tensor Core performs the multiplication in the background.

WGMMA instructions can source their input operands directly from shared memory, eliminating the need to explicitly load operands into registers before computation. This capability, combined with the asynchronous execution model, enables more flexible scheduling of operations. For instance, a thread can issue a WGMMA instruction, then perform other work like updating loop counters or checking synchronization barriers, and only synchronize with the Tensor Core when the result is needed.

The instruction requires all 128 threads in a warpgroup to participate cooperatively. Each thread holds a fragment of the accumulator matrix in its registers, and the Tensor Core operations update these fragments in place. The layout of data in registers follows hardware-specific patterns that the programmer must understand to correctly chain multiple WGMMA operations together.

FlashAttention-3 makes extensive use of WGMMA's asynchrony. The algorithm issues WGMMA instructions without waiting for completion (using "commit but do not wait" semantics in the pseudocode), allowing subsequent operations like softmax computation or additional GEMM initiation to proceed while the Tensor Core works. This overlapping of operations is key to achieving the high utilization rates demonstrated in FlashAttention-3, reaching up to 75% of theoretical peak throughput compared to the 35% utilization of FlashAttention-2.

## FP8 and FP16 Tensor Core Throughput Differences

Low-precision arithmetic has become a cornerstone of efficient deep learning computation, with each generation of GPUs introducing support for smaller number formats that enable higher throughput. The Hopper architecture introduces FP8 (8-bit floating point) support, continuing the progression from FP16 (introduced in Pascal) and BF16 (introduced in Ampere).

The FP8 format uses significantly fewer bits than FP16 or BF16, with typical configurations allocating 4 bits for the exponent and 3 bits for the mantissa (E4M3 format) or 5 bits for the exponent and 2 bits for the mantissa (E5M2 format). This reduced precision allows Tensor Cores to process twice as many elements per clock cycle compared to FP16 or BF16 operations.

Specifically, the WGMMA instruction targeting FP8 Tensor Cores delivers twice the throughput per streaming multiprocessor compared to FP16 or BF16 operations. This translates to substantial performance gains: FlashAttention-3 in FP8 mode achieves close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s (petalops per second) on the H100 GPU, compared to approximately 740 TFLOPs/s in FP16 mode. This near-doubling of raw computational throughput makes FP8 highly attractive for large-scale model training and inference.

However, the throughput advantage of FP8 comes with challenges. The reduced mantissa width (3 bits in E4M3 compared to 10 bits in FP16) means significantly less precision, introducing numerical errors that can accumulate through multiple operations. Large language models often contain outlier features—values that are much larger in magnitude than typical values—which are particularly problematic for quantization to FP8. FlashAttention-3 addresses these accuracy concerns through block quantization and incoherent processing techniques.

Furthermore, while matmul throughput doubles when moving from FP16 to FP8, the throughput of special function units (used for operations like exponentiation in softmax) remains unchanged. The H100 provides 989 TFLOPS of FP16 matmul throughput but only 3.9 TFLOPS of special function throughput. This imbalance means that softmax operations become relatively more expensive in FP8 mode, making the overlapping techniques in FlashAttention-3 even more important.

## Layout Constraints for Low-Precision Operations: mn-major vs k-major

Understanding memory layout constraints is essential for correctly utilizing low-precision Tensor Cores. The layout of matrix operands in shared memory determines whether WGMMA instructions can efficiently access them, and these constraints differ between FP16 and FP8 precision modes.

Consider a general matrix multiplication $C = A \times B^\top$ where $A$ is an $M \times K$ matrix and $B$ is an $N \times K$ matrix. The layout of these matrices can be classified based on which dimension is contiguous in memory. An operand is termed mn-major if it is contiguous in the outer $M$ or $N$ dimension, meaning that elements along the $M$ dimension (for matrix $A$) or $N$ dimension (for matrix $B$) are stored consecutively in memory. Conversely, an operand is k-major if it is contiguous in the inner $K$ dimension.

For FP16 WGMMA operations, both mn-major and k-major input operands are accepted for matrices stored in shared memory. This flexibility simplifies algorithm design, as matrices can be laid out in their natural configuration without requiring explicit transposition. However, FP8 WGMMA imposes stricter constraints: only the k-major format is supported for shared memory operands.

This constraint has significant implications for attention computation. In standard attention, the value matrix $\mathbf{V}$ is typically stored contiguous in the head dimension (equivalent to the $K$ dimension in the second matmul $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$). However, to satisfy FP8 WGMMA's k-major requirement, $\mathbf{V}$ needs to be contiguous in the sequence length dimension instead. This mismatch requires either preprocessing $\mathbf{V}$ with a transpose operation or performing an in-kernel transpose after loading tiles into shared memory.

FlashAttention-3 implements an in-kernel transpose using LDSM (load matrix) and STSM (store matrix) instructions, which efficiently transpose data between shared memory and registers. The algorithm also addresses layout differences between the FP32 accumulator output of the first WGMMA and the expected register layout for the FP8 operand of the second WGMMA, using byte permute instructions to transform the accumulator format appropriately.

## Warp-Specialization: Producer-Consumer Execution Model

Warp-specialization is a programming technique that leverages hardware support for asynchronous execution to divide warps within a threadblock into distinct roles. In the producer-consumer model, producer warps specialize in data movement operations while consumer warps specialize in computation, with each role issuing only the instructions relevant to its function.

This separation offers several advantages. First, it simplifies the compiler's task of generating optimal instruction schedules, as the instruction sequences for each warp type are more homogeneous. Producer warps issue TMA instructions and manage synchronization barriers, while consumer warps issue WGMMA instructions for Tensor Core operations and perform the associated arithmetic. Second, it enables more effective latency hiding: while consumer warps are performing computation, producer warps can independently issue memory transfers for future data blocks.

FlashAttention-3 implements warp-specialization by designating specific warpgroups as producers or consumers. Producer warpgroups issue TMA loads to bring blocks of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ from global memory into shared memory. Consumer warpgroups then perform the attention computation using this data. Synchronization barriers ensure that consumers wait for data to be available before accessing it, and producers wait for buffer space to be freed before loading new data.

The asynchronous nature of both TMA and WGMMA is essential for this model to work effectively. Producer warps issue TMA loads that proceed asynchronously, allowing them to continue issuing additional loads without blocking. Consumer warps issue WGMMA instructions that also execute asynchronously, enabling them to overlap computation with waiting for subsequent data blocks. The careful orchestration of these asynchronous operations, managed through synchronization barriers and circular buffer management, enables FlashAttention-3 to achieve high hardware utilization.

## Dynamic Register Reallocation with setmaxnreg

Register allocation presents a fundamental challenge in GPU programming: the register file is a limited resource, and different operations have different register requirements. In the warp-specialized model, consumer warps performing matrix multiplication typically need more registers for accumulators and intermediate results than producer warps that primarily issue memory operations.

The Hopper architecture introduces the setmaxnreg instruction, which enables dynamic reallocation of registers between warpgroups within a threadblock. This allows the GPU to redistribute the register budget based on the actual needs of each warpgroup. Warpgroups performing heavy computation (like the consumer warpgroups in FlashAttention-3) can obtain a larger share of the register file, while warpgroups primarily performing data movement (like producer warpgroups) can operate with fewer registers.

This dynamic reallocation occurs at runtime, allowing a single kernel to adapt its resource allocation based on the computational pattern. At kernel launch, threads can deallocate registers if they will be in a producer role, or reallocate additional registers if they will be consumers. The setmaxnreg instruction provides the mechanism for this reallocation, with the hardware managing the redistribution of physical register resources.

In FlashAttention-3, the algorithm pseudocode shows producer warpgroups deallocating "a predetermined number of registers" while consumer warpgroups reallocate "a predetermined number of registers as a function of number of consumer warps." This asymmetric distribution ensures that consumer warpgroups have sufficient register capacity for the GEMM accumulators and softmax intermediate values, while producer warpgroups—which only need registers for managing TMA operations—operate with minimal register overhead.

This dynamic reallocation capability is particularly important for the 2-stage and 3-stage pipelining algorithms in FlashAttention-3, which require additional registers to buffer intermediate results across pipeline stages. Without the ability to give consumer warpgroups more registers, these sophisticated algorithms would face even greater pressure on the register budget, potentially forcing register spilling and degrading performance.

# From Standard Attention to FlashAttention

## Standard Attention: Materializing Intermediate S and P Matrices to HBM

The attention mechanism lies at the heart of the Transformer architecture, serving as the primary means by which the model captures dependencies between different positions in a sequence. To understand the optimization journey from standard attention to FlashAttention, we must first establish the mathematical formulation of attention and examine how a naive implementation operates on GPU hardware.

Consider the query, key, and value input sequences associated with a single attention head, denoted as $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, where $N$ represents the sequence length and $d$ represents the head dimension. The attention computation proceeds through a series of matrix operations. First, we compute the attention scores by taking the scaled dot product between the query and key matrices:

$$\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N},$$

where $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ serves as the scaling factor to prevent the dot products from growing too large. The resulting matrix $\mathbf{S}$ has dimensions $N \times N$, meaning its size grows quadratically with the sequence length. This quadratic scaling is fundamental to understanding why attention becomes a computational bottleneck for long sequences.

Next, we apply the softmax function row-wise to convert the attention scores into attention weights:

$$\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}.$$

The softmax operation exponentiates each element and normalizes each row so that the weights sum to one. In practice, to prevent numerical instability when computing exponentials of large values, we subtract the row maximum from each element before exponentiating. Finally, we compute the output by weighting the values according to the attention weights:

$$\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}.$$

In what the FlashAttention paper terms **standard attention**, this computation proceeds in a straightforward manner by materializing—meaning explicitly computing and storing—the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to High Bandwidth Memory (HBM), which is the GPU's off-chip DRAM also referred to as global memory. The implementation computes $\mathbf{S}$ first and writes the entire $N \times N$ matrix to HBM. Then it reads $\mathbf{S}$ back from HBM to compute $\mathbf{P}$, and writes $\mathbf{P}$ back to HBM. Finally, it reads $\mathbf{P}$ from HBM one more time to compute the output $\mathbf{O}$.

This approach of materializing intermediate results follows the conventional programming paradigm where each operation is implemented as a separate kernel launch. However, this straightforward implementation carries significant costs when the sequence length grows large. The $N \times N$ matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ require substantial memory capacity, and more critically, the repeated reads and writes to HBM create a substantial performance bottleneck.

## Memory Bandwidth Bottleneck in Standard Implementation

Understanding why standard attention performs poorly requires examining the memory hierarchy of modern GPUs. GPU memories are organized as a hierarchy of data locales, with capacity inversely related to bandwidth. At the top level sits global memory (GMEM) or HBM, which is the off-chip DRAM accessible to all streaming multiprocessors (SMs). While HBM provides large capacity, its bandwidth is limited compared to on-chip memories. Data from GMEM gets transparently cached into an on-chip L2 cache, which provides faster access but with limited capacity. Each SM contains a small on-chip, programmer-managed cache called shared memory (SMEM), which offers much higher bandwidth than HBM. At the finest level, each SM has a register file that provides the fastest access but with the most limited capacity.

The key insight is that memory bandwidth, not computational throughput, often becomes the limiting factor for attention calculations. While modern GPUs like the H100 can achieve theoretical peak throughputs of nearly 989 TFLOPS for FP16 matrix multiplication, the actual memory bandwidth is orders of magnitude lower. When standard attention materializes the $N \times N$ matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM, it incurs enormous memory traffic: writing $\mathbf{S}$ (an $N \times N$ matrix), reading $\mathbf{S}$ back for the softmax, writing $\mathbf{P}$ (another $N \times N$ matrix), and reading $\mathbf{P}$ back for the final matrix multiplication with $\mathbf{V}$.

For a sequence length of 8192, each of these $N \times N$ matrices contains approximately 67 million elements. In FP16, each element occupies 2 bytes, meaning each matrix requires about 134 MB of storage. The memory bandwidth bottleneck compounds when we consider that modern LLMs often process much longer sequences. The attention computation scales quadratically in sequence length not just in computation but also in memory traffic when using standard attention, making it impractical for long-context applications such as processing multiple long documents, analyzing large codebases, handling high-resolution images, or managing long conversation histories.

## FlashAttention Key Insight: Tiling and Fusing Operations

FlashAttention emerged as a breakthrough approach to eliminate the memory bandwidth bottleneck by fundamentally rethinking how attention is computed on GPUs. The key insight behind FlashAttention is to leverage a tiling strategy that keeps intermediate computations entirely within the fast on-chip memory, avoiding expensive reads and writes to HBM for the $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ matrices.

Rather than computing the full $\mathbf{S}$ matrix at once, FlashAttention divides the query, key, and value matrices into smaller blocks or tiles that fit within the SM's shared memory and register file. The algorithm processes these tiles one at a time, computing partial results for each tile and accumulating them to produce the final output. Crucially, FlashAttention fuses all of the attention operations—the matrix multiplication to compute scores, the softmax normalization, and the weighted value aggregation—into a single GPU kernel.

This fusion eliminates intermediate reads and writes to slow global memory. Instead of materializing $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM, FlashAttention keeps these intermediate results entirely within the on-chip memory hierarchy. The only HBM accesses are reading the input matrices $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ at the beginning and writing the output $\mathbf{O}$ at the end. This dramatically reduces memory traffic from $O(N^2)$ reads and writes to just $O(Nd)$ for reading inputs and writing outputs.

The tiling strategy works by dividing the query matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ into blocks along the sequence dimension. For each block of queries $\mathbf{Q}_i$, the algorithm iterates through blocks of keys and values, computing partial attention results and accumulating them. Since the blocks are small enough to fit in shared memory, all intermediate computations happen on-chip without touching HBM.

## Online Softmax: Local Reduction Without Materializing Full Matrix

A critical challenge that FlashAttention must overcome is computing the softmax without having access to the entire $\mathbf{S}$ matrix at once. The softmax function requires knowing the maximum value in each row for numerical stability and the sum of exponentials for normalization. In standard attention, these statistics are computed after the entire $\mathbf{S}$ matrix has been generated, but FlashAttention processes $\mathbf{S}$ block by block.

The solution lies in what FlashAttention calls **online softmax**, which is a local version of the softmax reduction. This technique allows the algorithm to compute and update running statistics—the row-wise maximum and the sum of exponentials—incrementally as it processes each block of keys. The mathematics works out such that we can maintain these statistics correctly without ever seeing the full matrix.

Specifically, when processing a new block $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$ (the attention scores for query block $i$ and key block $j$), FlashAttention updates the running maximum $m_i$ and the running sum $\ell_i$ for each row in the query block. If the new block contains a larger maximum value than previously observed, we adjust the running sum accordingly to account for the change in the normalization baseline. This involves rescaling previously accumulated results.

The algorithm stores the old maximum $m_i^{\mathrm{old}}$ and computes the new maximum $m_i = \mathrm{max}(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}, \mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}))$. Then it computes the local softmax values $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \mathrm{exp}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i)$ and updates the running sum $\ell_i = \mathrm{exp}(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i) \ell_i + \mathrm{rowsum}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)})$. The $\mathrm{exp}(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i)$ term rescales the previously accumulated sum when the maximum increases.

Similarly, the output accumulation must be rescaled when the maximum changes. Before adding the new contribution $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$ to the running output sum, the algorithm rescales the existing accumulation by $\mathrm{exp}(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i)$. After processing all key blocks, a final normalization by $\ell_i$ produces the correct output.

This online softmax computation is mathematically equivalent to computing softmax on the full matrix, but it enables block-wise processing without materializing $\mathbf{S}$ or $\mathbf{P}$. The derivation showing that this procedure correctly computes the attention output can be found in the FlashAttention-2 paper.

## FlashAttention-2 Improvements: Parallelizing Over Sequence Length

While FlashAttention made significant strides in reducing memory traffic, FlashAttention-2 introduced algorithmic restructuring to improve GPU utilization and handle work distribution more effectively. The original FlashAttention parallelized the computation across the batch size and number of heads, with each threadblock processing one attention head for one batch element. The inner loop then iterated through blocks of keys and values.

FlashAttention-2 restructured this parallelization strategy. In addition to parallelizing over batch size and number of heads, it also parallelizes over the sequence length dimension for the query matrix. This means more threadblocks can be launched concurrently, improving the utilization of the GPU's streaming multiprocessors. The inner loop of the forward pass was restructured to iterate over blocks of the key and value matrices, rather than having each threadblock responsible for a single query block.

This restructuring addresses a fundamental challenge in GPU programming: ensuring that enough work is distributed across the hardware to keep all execution units busy. GPUs achieve high throughput through massive parallelism, but this requires having sufficient parallel work to distribute across the available resources. By parallelizing over the sequence length dimension, FlashAttention-2 creates more independent work items that can be scheduled across the GPU's many SMs.

## Occupancy and Work Distribution Optimizations

The concept of **occupancy** in GPU programming refers to the ratio of active warps to the maximum number of warps that can be resident on an SM. Higher occupancy generally enables better hiding of memory and instruction latencies, as the SM can switch between warps when one is waiting for memory or computational results.

FlashAttention-2's parallelization strategy improves occupancy by creating more independent threadblocks. With the original FlashAttention, the total number of threadblocks was limited to the batch size times the number of heads, which might not be enough to fully utilize all SMs on modern GPUs, especially for smaller batch sizes. By parallelizing over the sequence length dimension as well, FlashAttention-2 generates many more threadblocks, providing better opportunities for the GPU scheduler to keep all SMs busy.

The work distribution in FlashAttention-2 also takes into account the causal masking commonly used in autoregressive language models. With causal masking, each query position can only attend to key positions that come before it, creating a triangular attention pattern. FlashAttention-2 adjusts its work distribution to account for this asymmetry, reducing redundant computation for positions that are masked out.

These improvements enabled FlashAttention-2 to achieve significantly better performance than the original FlashAttention. However, as hardware continued to evolve, new limitations emerged that prevented FlashAttention-2 from fully utilizing the capabilities of newer GPU architectures.

## Why FlashAttention-2 Achieves Only 35% Utilization on H100

Despite FlashAttention-2's optimizations, the paper observes that it achieves only about 35% utilization on NVIDIA's Hopper H100 GPU, compared to optimized matrix multiplication kernels that achieve 80-90% utilization. This significant gap points to fundamental limitations in how FlashAttention-2's algorithm was designed relative to modern hardware capabilities.

Part of this underutilization can be attributed to implementation-level differences. FlashAttention-2 was originally designed and optimized for the Ampere architecture, and when running on Hopper GPUs, it may not use Hopper-specific instructions that can better leverage the new hardware features. Work such as ThunderKitten and cuDNN 9 has demonstrated that Hopper-specific instructions and tile-based abstractions can speed up attention computation. However, the utilization gap cannot be fully explained by implementation differences alone.

The more fundamental issue is that FlashAttention-2's algorithm was designed with a simplified synchronous execution model in mind. The algorithm proceeds through its computation in a sequential manner, with each operation waiting for the previous one to complete before proceeding. While this simplifies reasoning about correctness, it fails to exploit the asynchronous execution capabilities present in modern hardware like Hopper.

## Synchronous Execution Model Limitation

FlashAttention-2 adheres to what the paper describes as a synchronous execution model. In this model, operations are issued and completed in a sequential order, with explicit synchronization points ensuring that dependent operations wait for their inputs to be ready.

Consider the inner loop of FlashAttention-2's attention computation: it first computes the attention scores $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^T$, waits for this computation to complete, then performs the softmax operations including finding the maximum, computing exponentials, and normalizing. After the softmax, it computes the weighted value aggregation and waits for completion before moving to the next block.

This sequential execution means that at any given time, only one type of operation is actively using the GPU's specialized hardware units. While the Tensor Cores are computing matrix multiplications, the other hardware units sit idle. Conversely, while the CUDA cores are computing the softmax operations, the Tensor Cores are not being utilized. This serial execution fails to maximize the hardware's potential throughput.

Modern GPUs like Hopper are designed as throughput-oriented processors that rely on concurrency and asynchrony to hide memory and execution latencies. Different hardware units can operate independently and simultaneously. However, FlashAttention-2's algorithm structure does not expose opportunities for overlapping these different types of work, leading to underutilization of the GPU's full capabilities.

## Missing Exploitation of Asynchrony and Low-Precision Hardware Features

Two key hardware features that FlashAttention-2 does not exploit are asynchrony and low-precision computation. Understanding these features reveals why a redesign was necessary for FlashAttention-3.

**Asynchrony** in modern GPUs results from hardware specialization to accelerate the most important operations in machine learning workloads. The Tensor Cores, specialized hardware units for matrix multiplication, operate asynchronously from the rest of the CUDA cores that perform logic, integer, and floating-point computation. Similarly, the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) in Hopper provides asynchronous memory copying between global memory and shared memory.

This asynchrony means that different operations can proceed in parallel without waiting for each other. For example, while Tensor Cores are performing matrix multiplication, the multi-function units can simultaneously compute exponentials for the softmax. Similarly, while computation proceeds on the SM, the TMA can be loading the next block of data from global memory. However, FlashAttention-2's algorithm makes no explicit use of these asynchronous capabilities.

**Low-precision number formats** represent another hardware capability that FlashAttention-2 does not fully exploit. Modern GPUs have specialized hardware units for accelerating low-precision computation. The Hopper architecture, for instance, includes FP8 Tensor Cores that can deliver twice the throughput compared to FP16 or BF16 Tensor Cores. This follows a trend of introducing new precision formats: FP16 in Pascal (2017), BF16 in Ampere (2020), FP8 in Hopper, and FP4 in the upcoming Blackwell architecture.

Low precision is a proven technique to achieve double or quadruple throughput for the same power and chip area. However, using low precision requires careful handling to minimize quantization error, especially when outlier features in large language models can be orders of magnitude larger than typical values. FlashAttention-2 was not designed to leverage these low-precision capabilities.

The technical challenge that FlashAttention-3 addresses is redesigning the attention algorithm to make use of these hardware features. Asynchrony requires overlapping computation between matrix multiplication and softmax even though one depends on the output of the other—a dependency that must be carefully managed through pipelining techniques. Low precision requires implementing quantization strategies that maintain accuracy despite the reduced numerical precision, particularly in the presence of outliers that make quantization difficult.

These limitations of FlashAttention-2—its synchronous execution model and lack of support for low-precision computation—motivated the development of FlashAttention-3, which introduces new techniques to exploit asynchrony through warp-specialization and GEMM-softmax pipelining, and supports FP8 computation with block quantization and incoherent processing to maintain accuracy.

# Producer-Consumer Asynchrony and Warp-Specialization

Modern GPU architectures have evolved to include specialized hardware units that can execute independently of one another, creating opportunities for sophisticated scheduling strategies that dramatically improve computational throughput. This chapter explores how FlashAttention-3 exploits these hardware capabilities through a technique called warp-specialization, which fundamentally reorganizes how work is distributed across the execution units of a GPU. By understanding this approach, you will gain insight into how attention mechanisms can be optimized to achieve near-peak hardware utilization on contemporary accelerators.

# The Concept of Warp-Specialization

The traditional approach to GPU programming treats all threads within a cooperative thread array (CTA) as essentially equivalent workers, each capable of performing any combination of data movement and computation. While this uniform view simplifies programming, it fails to leverage the heterogeneous nature of modern GPU hardware. Warp-specialization represents a paradigm shift in this thinking: rather than treating all warps identically, the warps of a CTA are explicitly divided into distinct roles—producers that handle data movement and consumers that perform computation.

This division of labor is motivated by the architectural reality of GPUs like NVIDIA's Hopper architecture. The execution units on these chips are not monolithic; instead, they comprise specialized hardware designed for specific operations. The Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) is a dedicated unit optimized for moving data between global memory (HBM) and shared memory (SMEM). The Tensor Cores, accessed through the warpgroup matrix multiply accumulate (WGMMA) instruction, are specialized for matrix multiplication operations. These units operate asynchronously with respect to each other and with respect to the general-purpose CUDA cores that handle logic, integer, and floating-point operations.

The key insight behind warp-specialization is that by assigning warps to exclusively perform either data movement or computation, we can improve the compiler's ability to generate optimal instruction schedules. When a warp's role is clearly defined, the compiler can arrange its instructions without concern for conflicts or dependencies that might arise from mixing different types of operations. Furthermore, because the specialized hardware units can operate concurrently, a well-designed warp-specialized kernel can keep multiple units busy simultaneously, thereby hiding the latencies inherent in each type of operation.

The term "warp-specialized" thus refers to kernels where the thread hierarchy is explicitly organized around the specialized hardware units available on the GPU. Producer warps issue instructions primarily to the TMA for loading data, while consumer warps issue instructions primarily to the Tensor Cores for matrix operations. This clean separation allows each type of warp to maximize its utilization of a particular hardware resource without interference from the other.

# Producer Warpgroup: Handling TMA Loads from HBM to SMEM

In the FlashAttention-3 algorithm, the producer warpgroup is responsible for orchestrating all data movement from high-bandwidth memory (HBM), also called global memory, into shared memory (SMEM). This seemingly simple task is critical to achieving high performance, as memory bandwidth often represents the primary bottleneck in attention computations. The producer warpgroup leverages the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA), a dedicated hardware unit introduced in the Hopper architecture specifically designed for efficient asynchronous memory transfers.

The TMA differs fundamentally from traditional memory load operations. Rather than requiring threads to individually load data elements, the TMA can transfer entire multidimensional tensors from global memory to shared memory with a single instruction. This capability significantly reduces the instruction overhead associated with data loading. Moreover, TMA operations are truly asynchronous: once issued, the TMA handles the entire transfer independently, freeing the issuing thread to perform other work or issue additional transfers.

The producer warpgroup in FlashAttention-3 begins its operation by deallocating a predetermined number of registers using the `setmaxnreg` instruction. This deallocation is strategically important because it frees register resources that the consumer warpgroup will later need. Since the producer's primary task is issuing TMA loads—a responsibility that conceptually requires only a single thread—allocating many registers to the producer would be wasteful. The deallocation ensures that register pressure is minimized where it matters least.

Following register deallocation, the producer issues a TMA load to bring the query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ from HBM into shared memory. This initial load must complete before any computation can begin, so the producer commits to a barrier synchronization to notify the consumer that the query data is available. The concept of "committing" here refers to signaling completion of an asynchronous operation to other warps that may be waiting on that data.

After the initial query load, the producer enters its main loop. For each iteration $j$ from $0$ to $T_c - 1$ (where $T_c$ is the number of key/value blocks), the producer must first wait for the appropriate stage of a circular shared memory buffer to be consumed by the previous iteration. This waiting ensures that the producer does not overwrite data that the consumer is still processing. Once the buffer stage is free, the producer issues TMA loads for both $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$, loading these key and value blocks into the designated stage of the shared memory buffer. Upon completion of these loads, the producer again commits to notify the consumer that new data is available.

The beauty of this approach lies in the pipelining it enables. Because TMA loads are asynchronous, the producer can issue a load and immediately move to prepare for subsequent operations without waiting for the transfer to complete. The hardware handles the actual data movement in the background, and the producer simply ensures that the consumer is notified when the data arrives. This asynchrony allows memory transfers to be overlapped with computation, hiding the latency that would otherwise stall the entire kernel.

# Consumer Warpgroup: Executing WGMMA Computations

While the producer warpgroup handles data movement, the consumer warpgroup bears responsibility for all computational work. The consumer warpgroup's primary tools are the WGMMA (warpgroup matrix multiply accumulate) instructions, which invoke the Tensor Cores to perform matrix multiplication operations. These instructions represent the computational engine of FlashAttention-3, and understanding their characteristics is essential to appreciating the algorithm's design.

WGMMA instructions on the Hopper architecture differ significantly from their predecessors on earlier GPU generations. Most notably, WGMMA operations are asynchronous, meaning they return control to the issuing warp before the computation completes. This asynchrony is crucial because it allows the consumer to initiate matrix operations and then perform other work—such as softmax computations or rescaling operations—while the Tensor Cores execute the matrix multiply. The consumer can later wait for the WGMMA completion when the result is actually needed.

The WGMMA instruction can source its input operands directly from shared memory, eliminating the need to first move data into registers before computation. This capability is indicated by the "SS" prefix in SS-GEMM, meaning both operands come from shared memory, or the "RS" prefix in RS-GEMM, meaning one operand is in registers and one in shared memory. The distinction matters because it affects how data must be arranged and when the consumer can access computed results.

Upon initialization, the consumer warpgroup first reallocates registers using `setmaxnreg`. This reallocation obtains a larger share of the register file, which is necessary because the consumer must store intermediate results including the output accumulator $\mathbf{O}_i$, the log-sum-exp values $\ell_i$, and the maximum values $m_i$ used for numerical stability. The consumer initializes $\mathbf{O}_i$ to zero, $\ell_i$ to zero, and $m_i$ to negative infinity, preparing for the accumulation across all key-value blocks.

The consumer then waits for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ to be available in shared memory. Once the query block is loaded, the consumer enters its main loop, processing each key-value pair in sequence. For each iteration, the consumer waits for $\mathbf{K}_j$ to be loaded, then computes the score matrix $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^T$ using an SS-GEMM operation. The consumer must commit and wait for this GEMM to complete before proceeding because the softmax computation depends on its output.

After computing the scores, the consumer performs the local softmax computation: computing the row maximum, updating the global maximum $m_i$, computing the rescaled exponential values $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \exp(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i)$, and updating the log-sum-exp accumulator $\ell_i$. The consumer then waits for $\mathbf{V}_j$ to be loaded before computing $\mathbf{O}_i = \text{diag}(\exp(m_i^{\text{old}} - m_i))^{-1} \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$ using an RS-GEMM operation.

Once both GEMMs and softmax are complete, the consumer releases the shared memory buffer stage, allowing the producer to load the next block of data. After all iterations complete, the consumer performs a final rescaling $\mathbf{O}_i = \text{diag}(\ell_i)^{-1} \mathbf{O}_i$ and writes the results to HBM.

# Circular SMEM Buffer with s Stages

A critical component of the warp-specialized design is the circular shared memory buffer, which enables efficient pipelining between the producer and consumer warpgroups. This buffer is organized as $s$ stages, where each stage can hold one complete set of data needed for a single iteration of the consumer's computation loop. The circular nature of the buffer means that once all $s$ stages are filled, the producer wraps around to the first stage and begins overwriting the oldest data.

The purpose of staging the buffer is to decouple the producer's data loading from the consumer's computation. Without a multi-stage buffer, the producer would have to wait for the consumer to completely finish processing one block of data before loading the next block, resulting in significant idle time. With $s$ stages, the producer can load ahead, filling subsequent buffer stages while the consumer processes earlier ones. This overlapping of data movement and computation is what enables the high utilization that FlashAttention-3 achieves.

The value of $s$ represents a trade-off. A larger $s$ allows more aggressive pipelining and better hiding of memory latency, but it also requires more shared memory. Since shared memory is a limited resource—typically on the order of tens to hundreds of kilobytes per streaming multiprocessor—the number of stages must be chosen carefully. In practice, the number of stages is often determined by the block sizes used for the query, key, and value matrices, as well as the total shared memory available on the device.

In Algorithm 1 from the paper, the circular buffer is managed through explicit synchronization. The producer waits for stage $(j \mod s)$ to be consumed before loading new data into it. This modulo operation implements the circular indexing, ensuring that after stage $s-1$, the producer returns to stage $0$. Similarly, after the consumer finishes processing the data in a given stage, it releases that stage, signaling to the producer that the buffer slot is now free for new data.

The pipeline object mentioned in the algorithm manages the barrier synchronization between producers and consumers. This object tracks which stages are currently in use and which are available, coordinating access to ensure correctness. The synchronization primitives ensure that the consumer never reads data that hasn't been fully loaded, and the producer never overwrites data that hasn't been consumed.

# Barrier Synchronization between Producers and Consumers

Correct execution of a warp-specialized kernel requires careful coordination between the producer and consumer warpgroups. Since both warps access the same shared memory buffer, and since their operations proceed asynchronously, we need synchronization mechanisms to ensure that data dependencies are respected. Barrier synchronization provides this coordination, ensuring that producers do not overwrite data before consumers have finished using it, and that consumers do not read data before producers have finished writing it.

The synchronization model employed by FlashAttention-3 is based on acquire-release semantics. When a producer issues TMA loads into a buffer stage, it performs a "commit" operation once those loads complete. This commit operation signals that the data in that buffer stage is now valid and ready for consumption. The consumer, waiting on the corresponding barrier, can then acquire access to the data and proceed with its computation.

Conversely, when the consumer finishes processing a buffer stage, it releases that stage, signaling that the buffer slot is now free. The producer, which may be waiting for a free buffer slot, can then acquire permission to write new data. This bidirectional synchronization ensures that neither warp races ahead of the other in a way that would compromise data integrity.

The implementation of these barriers uses hardware-supported synchronization primitives. In CUDA, the `bar.sync` instruction provides the necessary functionality, allowing threads to wait until a specified number of threads have arrived at the barrier. For warp-specialized kernels, the barriers are typically set up to synchronize between warpgroups rather than individual threads, since the warpgroup is the fundamental unit of execution for both TMA and WGMMA operations.

An important aspect of the barrier design is that it enables overlap while maintaining correctness. The producer's main loop, for instance, will not issue waits for the first $s$ iterations because the circular buffer starts empty. During these initial iterations, the producer can fill the buffer without blocking, allowing it to get ahead of the consumer. Once the buffer is full, subsequent producer iterations must wait for the consumer to catch up, but the buffering provided by the $s$ stages ensures that such waiting is minimized under normal operating conditions.

# Register Allocation and Deallocation with setmaxnreg

The Hopper architecture introduces a powerful new capability for dynamic register allocation: the `setmaxnreg` instruction. This instruction allows a kernel to dynamically reallocate registers between warpgroups at runtime, enabling the asymmetric distribution of register resources that warp-specialized kernels require. Understanding how this instruction is used in FlashAttention-3 illuminates why such flexibility is valuable.

In a traditional GPU kernel, all warps are allocated the same number of registers at kernel launch time. This uniform allocation is simple but suboptimal when different warps have different computational needs. In FlashAttention-3, the producer warpgroup has minimal register requirements—it primarily issues TMA loads, and conceptually only a single thread is needed for this task. Allocating the default 255 registers per thread to the producer would waste valuable register file capacity.

The consumer warpgroup, by contrast, has substantial register requirements. It must store the output accumulator $\mathbf{O}_i$, intermediate results from GEMM operations, softmax computation results, and various scalar values for tracking statistics like $m_i$ and $\ell_i$. The two-stage pipelining described later in this chapter increases these requirements further, as the consumer must maintain additional buffers like $\mathbf{S}_{\text{next}}$.

The `setmaxnreg` instruction resolves this mismatch. At the beginning of execution, the producer warpgroup deallocates a predetermined number of registers, effectively giving them up to the system. The consumer warpgroup then reallocates these registers, increasing its allocation beyond the default. This transfer is asymmetric: the consumer receives more registers while the producer makes do with fewer.

The number of registers to reallocate is determined as a function of the number of consumer warps and the specific computational requirements of the algorithm. For example, with larger block sizes $B_r$ and $B_c$, more intermediate data must be stored, requiring more registers. The reallocation amount must be carefully tuned to avoid register spilling—where data that should live in registers is instead written to local memory—while also ensuring that total register usage within the CTA stays within the hardware limit of 65,536 registers per SM.

This dynamic reallocation capability is unique to Hopper-class GPUs and represents a significant advantage for warp-specialized kernels. Prior architectures required all warps to use the same static register allocation, forcing compromises between the needs of different warps. With `setmaxnreg`, FlashAttention-3 can tailor resource allocation to the actual requirements of each warpgroup, maximizing overall efficiency.

# Algorithm Walkthrough for Warp-Specialized Forward Pass

Having examined the individual components, we now synthesize them into a complete understanding of the warp-specialized forward pass algorithm. Algorithm 1 from the paper provides the formal specification, and walking through its execution reveals how all the pieces fit together.

The algorithm operates at the CTA level, meaning each cooperative thread array processes one tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ of the query matrix to compute the corresponding tile $\mathbf{O}_i$ of the output. The forward pass is embarrassingly parallel across batch size, number of heads, and query sequence length, so multiple CTAs can execute independently without coordination.

Execution begins with the initialization of a pipeline object that manages the $s$-stage circular SMEM buffer. This object encapsulates the barrier synchronization state needed to coordinate producer and consumer access to the buffer.

The algorithm then branches based on whether the executing code is in the producer or consumer warpgroup. For the producer, the first action is to deallocate registers using `setmaxnreg`. The producer then issues a TMA load to bring $\mathbf{Q}_i$ from HBM to shared memory. Upon completion, it commits to a barrier, notifying the consumer that $\mathbf{Q}_i$ is available. The producer then enters its main loop, iterating over all $T_c$ key-value blocks. For each iteration $j$, it waits for buffer stage $(j \mod s)$ to be free, issues TMA loads for $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ into that stage, and commits to notify the consumer.

For the consumer, execution begins by reallocating registers. The consumer initializes $\mathbf{O}_i = 0$, $\ell_i = 0$, and $m_i = -\infty$ in on-chip storage (registers and shared memory). It waits for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ to be loaded, then enters its main loop. For each iteration $j$, the consumer waits for $\mathbf{K}_j$ to be loaded, computes $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^T$ via SS-GEMM, commits and waits for completion. It then computes the local softmax: storing the old maximum $m_i^{\text{old}}$, computing the new maximum $m_i = \max(m_i^{\text{old}}, \text{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}))$, computing rescaled exponentials $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \exp(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i)$, and updating $\ell_i$. After waiting for $\mathbf{V}_j$ to load, it computes $\mathbf{O}_i = \text{diag}(\exp(m_i^{\text{old}} - m_i))^{-1} \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$ via RS-GEMM. Finally, it releases the buffer stage for the producer.

After all iterations, the consumer performs the final rescaling $\mathbf{O}_i = \text{diag}(\ell_i)^{-1} \mathbf{O}_i$, computes $L_i = m_i + \log(\ell_i)$ for numerical stability in the backward pass, and writes $\mathbf{O}_i$ and $L_i$ to HBM.

The correctness of this algorithm follows from the local softmax reduction technique introduced in FlashAttention. By maintaining running statistics $m_i$ and $\ell_i$, the algorithm can correctly compute the softmax over all keys without ever materializing the full $N \times N$ attention matrix in memory.

# Pingpong Scheduling: Alternating GEMM and Softmax across Warpgroups

The warp-specialization approach described thus far already provides significant performance improvements by overlapping data movement with computation. However, the algorithm can be pushed further through a technique called pingpong scheduling, which exploits the existence of multiple consumer warpgroups to overlap different types of computation.

To understand the motivation for pingpong scheduling, we must examine the throughput characteristics of different operations on modern GPUs. The H100 SXM5 GPU achieves 989 TFLOPS of FP16 matrix multiplication throughput through its Tensor Cores. However, special function units that compute exponentials—necessary for the softmax operation—achieve only about 3.9 TFLOPS of throughput. This massive disparity (over 250×) means that the exponential computation, while representing a small fraction of total operations, can consume a significant portion of the execution time.

For attention with head dimension 128 in FP16, there are 512× more matmul FLOPS than exponential operations, but the exponential has 256× lower throughput, meaning exponential computation can take roughly 50% of the time compared to matmul. With FP8, the situation is even worse: matmul throughput doubles while exponential throughput remains constant.

Pingpong scheduling addresses this imbalance by ensuring that exponential computation happens while Tensor Cores are busy with matrix operations. The key insight is that with multiple consumer warpgroups, we can schedule them such that while one warpgroup performs softmax (using the multi-function units), another warpgroup performs GEMM (using the Tensor Cores).

The scheduling works as follows. Synchronization barriers (bar.sync instructions) are used to force the GEMM operations of warpgroup 1 to be scheduled before the GEMM operations of warpgroup 2. As a result, while warpgroup 1's GEMMs are running on the Tensor Cores, warpgroup 2 must wait. During this waiting period, warpgroup 2 can perform its softmax operations on the multi-function units. Then the roles swap: warpgroup 2's GEMMs execute while warpgroup 1 performs softmax. This alternation—like a pingpong ball going back and forth—gives the technique its name.

In practice, the scheduling is not as clean as the idealized description suggests. Compiler optimizations, hardware scheduling quirks, and varying execution times all cause deviations from perfect alternation. Nevertheless, empirical results show that pingpong scheduling improves performance substantially—for instance, increasing throughput from 570 TFLOPS to 620-640 TFLOPS for FP16 forward pass with head dimension 128 and sequence length 8192.

# Overcoming Throughput Imbalance between Matmul and Exponential Operations

The throughput imbalance between matrix multiplication and exponential operations represents one of the fundamental challenges in optimizing attention computations. Understanding this imbalance and the strategies to overcome it is crucial for achieving high utilization on modern hardware.

The root cause of the imbalance lies in hardware specialization. Tensor Cores are highly optimized circuit blocks designed specifically for matrix multiplication, achieving extraordinary throughput through massive parallelism. The multi-function units that handle operations like exponential, sine, cosine, and square root are general-purpose units that cannot match this specialization. While this design is economically rational—matrix multiplication dominates the computational profile of most machine learning workloads—it creates challenges for operations like attention that intersperse matmul with other computations.

The ratio of compute time for matmul versus exponential depends on several factors. For head dimension $d$, each attention head computes matrices of size $N \times d$ for queries, keys, and values. The first GEMM ($\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^T$) requires $O(N^2 d)$ operations, as does the second GEMM ($\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$). The softmax operation requires $O(N^2)$ exponential computations. Thus the ratio of matmul FLOPS to exponential operations is proportional to $d$.

With head dimension 128, this ratio favors matmul by 128× in operation count. But the throughput ratio of roughly 256× means exponential operations consume disproportionately more time. The effective time spent on exponential relative to matmul is approximately $256 / 128 = 2$ when both are measured in terms of operation counts, meaning exponential computation could take twice as much time as matmul in the worst case.

Several strategies mitigate this imbalance. First, the two-stage pipelining described earlier in the chapter overlaps softmax with the second GEMM, ensuring that exponential operations occur during time that would otherwise be idle while waiting for the second matmul to complete. Second, pingpong scheduling extends this overlap across warpgroups. Third, for larger head dimensions like 256, the ratio of matmul to exponential operations doubles, making the throughput imbalance less severe.

The design of FlashAttention-3 explicitly accounts for these factors. The algorithm structure is arranged to maximize overlap between high-throughput Tensor Core operations and lower-throughput multi-function unit operations. When implementing attention on new hardware, similar analysis should inform algorithmic design: understanding the throughput characteristics of each operation type and structuring computation to minimize idle time across all hardware units.

# Handling Multi-Query Attention and Grouped Query Attention Variants

FlashAttention-3 supports attention variants beyond standard multi-head attention, specifically multi-query attention (MQA) and grouped query attention (GQA). These variants have become increasingly important in large language model design because they reduce the memory and computation required for the key and value projections, enabling more efficient inference.

In standard multi-head attention, each head has its own separate key and value projections. If the model has $H$ heads, then there are $H$ different $\mathbf{K}$ matrices and $H$ different $\mathbf{V}$ matrices. Multi-query attention simplifies this by using a single key and value projection shared across all heads—that is, only one $\mathbf{K}$ and one $\mathbf{V}$ for all queries. Grouped query attention is an intermediate approach where queries are divided into groups, and each group shares key and value projections.

The warp-specialized algorithm handles these variants by adjusting the tensor indexing for loading $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ blocks. Rather than loading separate key-value blocks for each head, the producer warpgroup loads from the shared key-value tensors and makes this data available to all heads that need it. This avoids duplicating $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ in HBM, which would waste memory bandwidth—a critical concern for inference workloads where memory bandwidth is often the primary bottleneck.

The computational structure of the algorithm remains largely unchanged. The consumer warpgroup still computes attention scores for each query head, but the key and value data it uses comes from the shared projections. The SS-GEMM and RS-GEMM operations proceed identically; only the addresses from which data is loaded differ.

From a correctness perspective, MQA and GQA require careful attention to how the attention scores are computed. Since multiple query heads attend to the same key and value projections, the effective attention computation has different characteristics than standard multi-head attention. The FlashAttention-3 algorithm handles this transparently: by adjusting indices into the key and value tensors, the same computational kernel correctly implements these variants without algorithmic changes.

The support for these variants in FlashAttention-3 follows the approach established in FlashAttention-2, demonstrating that the warp-specialized design does not fundamentally change how these attention variants are handled. The efficiency benefits of warp-specialization apply equally to standard attention, multi-query attention, and grouped query attention, as the underlying computational pattern—matrix multiplications interleaved with softmax—remains the same.

# Intra-Warpgroup Overlapping: GEMM-Softmax Pipelining

# The Sequential Dependency Problem in Attention Computation

To understand the motivation for intra-warpgroup overlapping, we must first examine the inherent sequential dependencies that exist within the attention algorithm's inner loop. In the standard formulation of FlashAttention, each iteration of the main loop processes a block of the key and value matrices, computing attention scores and accumulating results. The operations within each iteration follow a strict ordering that presents challenges for parallelization.

Consider the sequence of operations required to process a single block. First, a matrix multiplication computes the attention scores $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^T$, where $\mathbf{Q}_i$ is a tile of the query matrix and $\mathbf{K}_j$ is a tile of the key matrix. This first GEMM operation produces scores that must then undergo softmax normalization. The softmax operation, specifically the local softmax variant used in FlashAttention, requires computing the row-wise maximum and the exponential of the scores. Critically, this softmax computation depends entirely on the output of the first GEMM—the scores $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$ must be available before any exponential or normalization can proceed.

Following the softmax, a second GEMM multiplies the normalized attention weights $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ with a tile of the value matrix: $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$. This second matrix multiplication cannot begin until the softmax has produced $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$. Thus, we observe a chain of dependencies: the first GEMM must complete before softmax can begin, and softmax must complete before the second GEMM can begin. In the baseline FlashAttention-2 algorithm, explicit wait statements serialize these operations, forcing each to complete before the next can commence.

This serialization is problematic because it leaves hardware resources underutilized. While the Tensor Cores are busy computing one GEMM, the multi-function units that handle exponential calculations sit idle. Conversely, during softmax computation, the powerful Tensor Cores remain dormant. The sequential nature of these dependencies, when executed naively, creates a situation where specialized hardware units take turns being active, rather than working concurrently to maximize throughput.

# The Throughput Gap: Understanding Hardware Asymmetry

The imperative to overlap GEMM and softmax operations becomes clear when we examine the stark throughput disparity between these two classes of operations on modern GPUs. The H100 SXM5 GPU exemplifies this hardware asymmetry. For FP16 matrix multiplication operations, the H100 delivers an impressive 989 TFLOPS of computational throughput. This massive number-crunching capability is made possible by the Tensor Cores, specialized hardware units designed specifically for accelerating matrix operations common in deep learning workloads.

In contrast, the special function units responsible for computing exponentials and other transcendental functions operate at dramatically lower throughput. The H100's special function units deliver approximately 3.9 TFLOPS for operations like exponential. This figure is derived from the CUDA programming guide's specification that each streaming multiprocessor can perform 16 special function operations per clock cycle. Multiplying this by 132 SMs and the 1830 MHz boost clock speed yields the 3.9 TFLOPS figure. The throughput gap between matmul and exponential operations spans roughly 256x—a factor of 256 more operations can be performed per unit time on the Tensor Cores compared to the special function units.

To appreciate the practical impact of this asymmetry, consider the attention forward pass with FP16 precision and a head dimension of 128. In this configuration, there are approximately 512 times more matmul FLOPS than exponential operations. However, since the exponential throughput is 256 times lower, the exponential computations can consume roughly 50% of the total cycle time compared to matmul. The situation becomes even more dire with FP8 precision, where the matmul throughput doubles to nearly 2 PFLOPS while the exponential throughput remains unchanged at 3.9 TFLOPS. This further widens the gap and makes overlapping even more critical.

Since the exponential calculations are performed by a completely separate hardware unit—the multi-function unit—ideal execution would schedule these calculations precisely when the Tensor Cores are engaged in matrix multiplication. The goal is to achieve concurrent utilization of both specialized units, effectively hiding the latency of the lower-throughput exponential operations behind the higher-throughput matmul operations. This observation motivates the intra-warpgroup overlapping technique, which seeks to exploit the asynchronous nature of WGMMA instructions to achieve precisely this kind of concurrent execution.

# The Two-Stage Pipelining Algorithm

The two-stage pipelining algorithm represents a sophisticated approach to breaking the sequential dependencies that constrain attention computation. The fundamental insight is that while dependencies exist within a single iteration, we can pipeline across iterations by maintaining additional buffers in registers. This allows the softmax computation from one iteration to overlap with GEMM computations from subsequent iterations.

The algorithm maintains two sets of score buffers: $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ for the current iteration and $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ for the next iteration. By computing ahead the first GEMM of iteration $j+1$ while still processing iteration $j$, we create the opportunity for overlapping. Specifically, while the second GEMM of iteration $j$ executes on the Tensor Cores, the softmax computation for iteration $j+1$ can proceed on the multi-function units. The key innovation is issuing WGMMA instructions without immediately waiting for their completion, allowing subsequent instructions to execute asynchronously.

The algorithm begins by loading $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and $\mathbf{K}_0$ into shared memory and computing the initial score matrix $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_0^T$ using WGMMA. After this first computation, the algorithm enters the main loop. Within each iteration $j$ of the main loop, the algorithm issues the first WGMMA to compute $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_{j}^T$ but critically does not wait for completion. Instead, it proceeds to wait for $\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ to be loaded and then issues the second WGMMA for the previous iteration: $\mathbf{O}_{i} = \mathbf{O}_{i} + \tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$.

At this point, the algorithm has two WGMMAs in flight—the first from the current iteration and the second from the previous iteration. The algorithm then waits specifically for the first WGMMA to complete before proceeding with softmax computation on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$. After waiting for the second WGMMA and rescaling $\mathbf{O}_i$, the algorithm releases the appropriate buffer stages and copies $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ to $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ for the next iteration. This careful orchestration ensures that the exponential computations required for softmax occur while Tensor Core operations are in progress.

# Breaking Dependencies Through Additional Register Buffers

The ability to pipeline across iterations hinges on maintaining state for multiple iterations simultaneously. The two-stage pipeline requires allocating additional registers to store $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$, the score matrix for the upcoming iteration. This buffer allows the algorithm to look ahead and begin computing the first GEMM of the next iteration while the current iteration's softmax and second GEMM are still in progress.

The register cost of this additional buffer is substantial. Specifically, storing $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ requires register space of size $B_r \times B_c \times \text{sizeof}(\text{float})$ per threadblock, where $B_r$ is the query block size (number of rows in each query tile) and $B_c$ is the key block size (number of rows in each key tile). For typical configurations, this can amount to a significant fraction of the available register budget per threadblock.

The presence of this additional buffer fundamentally changes the dependency structure. Rather than waiting for each operation to complete before beginning the next, the algorithm can issue operations speculatively for future iterations. The softmax of iteration $j$ now depends on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, which was computed in iteration $j-1$, while the first GEMM of iteration $j$ computes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ that will be used in iteration $j+1$. This decoupling of production and consumption of score matrices across iterations is what enables the overlapping of computation.

# WGMMA Commit Without Wait: Asynchronous Execution

Central to the intra-warpgroup overlapping technique is the asynchronous nature of the WGMMA instruction on Hopper GPUs. Unlike previous architectures where Tensor Core operations were largely synchronous from the programmer's perspective, Hopper's WGMMA instruction operates asynchronously. When a WGMMA is issued, the instruction commits the operation to the Tensor Core but does not necessarily wait for completion before allowing subsequent instructions to execute.

In the two-stage pipelining algorithm, this asynchrony is exploited through the distinction between "commit" and "wait" operations. After issuing a WGMMA, the algorithm can specify whether to wait for completion or proceed immediately. The pseudocode in Algorithm 2 of the paper shows this distinction: certain WGMMA calls are followed by "Commit but do not wait," while others are followed by explicit waits. This selective waiting is crucial for achieving overlap.

Consider the sequence within the main loop. The first WGMMA computes $\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_{j}^T$ and is committed without waiting. The algorithm then issues the second WGMMA for $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$, also without waiting. At this point, both Tensor Core operations are in flight. The algorithm then explicitly waits for the first WGMMA to complete before computing softmax on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$. This wait is necessary because softmax requires the actual values of $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$. However, during the time between issuing the first WGMMA and waiting for its completion, the multi-function units can execute the exponential operations needed for softmax.

# Detailed Algorithm Walkthrough

To solidify understanding, let us trace through the algorithm step by step. The algorithm begins with initialization: $\mathbf{O}_i$ is initialized to zero, and the log-sum-exp values $\ell_i$ and $m_i$ are initialized to zero and negative infinity respectively. The algorithm waits for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and $\mathbf{K}_0$ to be loaded into shared memory, then computes the first score matrix $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_0^T$.

After computing $m_i$, $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, and $\ell_i$ based on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, the algorithm enters the main loop starting at iteration $j=1$. At the start of each main loop iteration, the algorithm waits for $\mathbf{K}_j$ to be loaded. It then computes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_{j}^T$ using WGMMA, committing without waiting. This begins the first GEMM of the current iteration asynchronously.

Next, the algorithm waits for $\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ to be loaded and issues the second WGMMA: $\mathbf{O}_{i} = \mathbf{O}_{i} + \tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$, again committing without waiting. Now both WGMMAs are executing on the Tensor Cores. The algorithm then explicitly waits for the first WGMMA to complete, at which point $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ is available. The softmax computation proceeds, computing $m_i$, $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{next}}$, and $\ell_i$ based on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$. During this softmax computation, the second WGMMA is still potentially executing on the Tensor Cores, achieving the desired overlap.

After waiting for the second WGMMA and rescaling $\mathbf{O}_i$, the algorithm releases the buffer stages for reuse by the producer warps. Finally, $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ is copied to $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, and the loop proceeds to the next iteration. The epilogue handles the final iteration separately, computing the last second GEMM and performing final rescaling before writing the output to HBM.

# Compiler Reordering Challenges

While the pseudocode presented in Algorithm 2 represents an idealized execution order, practical implementation faces challenges from compiler optimization. The NVCC compiler, which translates CUDA C++ code into machine instructions, often rearranges instructions for optimization purposes. This reordering can disrupt the carefully crafted sequence of WGMMA and non-WGMMA operations that enable overlapping.

The compiler's instruction scheduling is designed to maximize various metrics such as register usage, instruction throughput, and latency hiding. However, the compiler does not have complete knowledge of the programmer's intent regarding overlapping between different hardware units. Left to its own devices, the compiler might reorder instructions in ways that serialize operations rather than overlapping them, defeating the purpose of the pipelining scheme.

Despite this challenge, analysis of the generated SASS (assembly) code confirms that the compiler does generate overlapped code as expected. This suggests that with appropriate code structure and potentially explicit synchronization hints, the desired overlapping behavior can be achieved. Programmers implementing such algorithms must be attentive to the generated assembly to verify that their intended overlapping is actually being realized in practice.

# Register Pressure Analysis

The two-stage pipeline algorithm imposes increased register pressure compared to the baseline algorithm. The additional buffer $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ requires register space that could otherwise be used for other purposes. This creates a tension between the benefits of pipelining and the costs of register usage.

Register spilling—the process of moving register contents to slower memory when register capacity is exceeded—must be minimized for optimal performance. When registers spill to shared memory or global memory, the resulting memory traffic can negate the performance gains from overlapping. Therefore, careful attention must be paid to register allocation when implementing the pipelining algorithm.

The trade-off manifests in the choice of block sizes. Larger block sizes $B_r$ and $B_c$ generally improve arithmetic intensity and reduce the overhead of loop control. However, larger blocks also require more register space for storing intermediate results. The addition of $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ further increases this pressure. In practice, profiling is essential to determine the optimal balance between block size and pipeline depth for a given configuration.

# Trade-offs Between Tile Size and Pipeline Depth

The design space for pipelining algorithms involves a fundamental trade-off between tile size and pipeline depth. Larger tiles increase the amount of computation per iteration, which generally improves efficiency by amortizing loop overhead. However, larger tiles also require more register space for intermediate results and accumulator buffers.

Pipeline depth represents how many iterations ahead the algorithm computes before waiting. The two-stage pipeline maintains one iteration ahead, while a three-stage pipeline would maintain two iterations ahead. Deeper pipelines offer the potential for higher Tensor Core utilization by providing more opportunities for overlapping. However, each additional stage requires additional register buffers for storing intermediate score matrices.

This trade-off is particularly acute because the registers used for pipeline buffers directly compete with the registers needed for larger tiles. For example, doubling the pipeline depth from two stages to three stages would require an additional $\mathbf{S}$ buffer, reducing the register budget available for tile data. This might force the use of smaller tiles, which could offset the benefits of deeper pipelining.

The optimal configuration depends on the specific parameters of the attention computation, including sequence length, head dimension, and hardware characteristics. In practice, empirical tuning and profiling are necessary to navigate this trade-off space and identify the best configuration for each use case.

# Three-Stage Pipelining Variant

Building upon the two-stage algorithm, a natural extension is the three-stage pipelining variant. This approach would further overlap the second GEMM with softmax operations, potentially achieving even higher Tensor Core utilization. The three-stage variant would maintain two lookahead buffers, allowing computation to proceed two iterations ahead.

In a three-stage pipeline, the algorithm would compute $\mathbf{S}$ for iterations $j+1$ and $j+2$ while processing the softmax and second GEMM for iteration $j$. This creates additional opportunities for hiding the latency of exponential operations behind Tensor Core computations. Theoretically, this could reduce the idle time of specialized hardware units even further.

However, the three-stage variant comes with increased costs. Each additional stage requires another register buffer for storing intermediate score matrices. The register pressure from a three-stage pipeline would be approximately 50% higher than the two-stage version. This increased pressure might necessitate smaller tile sizes or cause register spilling, either of which could degrade performance.

The trade-off between pipeline depth and tile size becomes even more critical for the three-stage variant. While the theoretical potential for overlap is greater, the practical realization of this potential depends on whether sufficient register capacity is available. Detailed evaluation and benchmarking are necessary to determine whether the three-stage variant provides net benefits for specific configurations.

# SASS Code Analysis Verifying Intended Overlapping Behavior

Validation of the pipelining algorithm's effectiveness requires examining the generated machine code. SASS (Streaming Assembly) is the low-level instruction set executed by NVIDIA GPUs. By analyzing the SASS code generated from the pipelining algorithm, we can verify that the intended overlapping behavior is actually being realized.

The analysis confirms that the compiler generates code where WGMMA instructions and non-WGMMA operations are interleaved as intended. Specifically, the SASS code shows that exponential operations (computed by the multi-function units) are scheduled during periods when WGMMA instructions are in progress on the Tensor Cores. This confirms that the asynchronous nature of WGMMA is being exploited correctly.

This verification is important because it demonstrates that the algorithmic innovations translate into actual hardware behavior. Without such analysis, it would be possible to write code that appears correct at the CUDA level but fails to achieve the desired overlapping due to compiler intervention. The SASS analysis provides confidence that the pipelining algorithm is indeed exploiting hardware capabilities as designed.

# Low-Precision Attention with FP8

Modern deep learning has witnessed a consistent trend toward lower numerical precision, driven by the substantial performance benefits that come with reduced bit-widths. The progression from FP32 to FP16, then to BF16, and now to FP8 represents a systematic effort to maximize computational throughput while minimizing memory bandwidth requirements. This chapter explores the techniques required to implement attention mechanisms in FP8 precision, focusing on the hardware constraints, layout transformation challenges, and accuracy preservation methods that make this precision level practical for large language models.

## The FP8 Format and Hardware Benefits

The FP8 format represents a significant departure from traditional floating-point representations used in deep learning. Specifically, the e4m3 variant of FP8 allocates 4 bits to the exponent and 3 bits to the mantissa, leaving one bit for the sign. This allocation represents a dramatic reduction compared to FP16 or BF16, which both use 5 bits for the exponent and 10 or 7 bits for the mantissa, respectively. The reduced mantissa precision means that FP8 can represent fewer distinct values within its dynamic range, making it more susceptible to quantization errors. However, this trade-off is often acceptable given the substantial performance benefits.

The primary motivation for adopting FP8 precision is the hardware throughput advantage. Modern GPU architectures like NVIDIA's Hopper provide specialized Tensor Core units that can execute FP8 matrix multiplications at double the throughput of FP16 or BF16 operations. Specifically, the WGMMA (Warp Group Matrix Multiply Accumulate) instruction can target the FP8 Tensor Cores on Hopper GPUs to deliver $2\times$ the throughput per streaming multiprocessor (SM) compared to FP16 or BF16. This doubling of computational throughput translates directly to faster attention computation, which is particularly valuable given that attention constitutes the primary computational bottleneck in Transformer architectures.

The empirical results demonstrate the magnitude of these performance gains. FlashAttention-3 achieves close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s when operating in FP8 precision, compared to approximately 740 TFLOPs/s in FP16. This represents a throughput increase of roughly 1.6$\times$ in practice, approaching but not quite achieving the theoretical 2$\times$ due to various overheads and bottlenecks in the complete attention pipeline. Nevertheless, these speedups are substantial and make FP8 an attractive option for training and inference with large language models.

## Layout Constraints and the K-Major Requirement

While FP8 offers compelling performance benefits, it introduces strict constraints on how data must be organized in memory for efficient Tensor Core utilization. Understanding these constraints requires first establishing some terminology for describing matrix layouts.

Consider a GEMM operation that multiplies matrices $A \times B^{\top}$, where $A$ has dimensions $M \times K$ and $B$ has dimensions $N \times K$. An operand (either $A$ or $B$) is said to be \emph{mn-major} if it is contiguous in the outer $M$ or $N$ dimension, meaning that elements along that dimension are stored consecutively in memory. Conversely, an operand is \emph{k-major} if it is contiguous in the inner $K$ dimension.

The critical difference between FP16 and FP8 WGMMA instructions lies in their layout flexibility. For FP16 WGMMA, both mn-major and k-major input operands are accepted when the operands reside in shared memory (SMEM). This flexibility gives the programmer considerable freedom in organizing data. However, FP8 WGMMA is significantly more restrictive: only the k-major format is supported for SMEM operands. This constraint has profound implications for how attention must be implemented in FP8.

The attention mechanism requires two matrix multiplications: first computing $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$ to obtain attention scores, then computing $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$ to produce the output. For the second GEMM, the $\mathbf{V}$ matrix must be k-major to satisfy the FP8 WGMMA constraint. This means $\mathbf{V}$ must be contiguous in the sequence length dimension (the $K$ dimension in the GEMM formulation).

## The Value Tensor Layout Challenge

The layout constraint creates a practical problem because the input tensors $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ are typically organized with the head dimension as the contiguous dimension in memory. This is the natural layout produced by most Transformer implementations, where the head dimension follows the batch and sequence dimensions in the tensor shape. However, to satisfy the k-major constraint for the second GEMM in FP8, $\mathbf{V}$ must instead be contiguous in the sequence length dimension.

This mismatch between the natural input layout and the required FP8 layout necessitates a transformation. The naive approach would be to transpose $\mathbf{V}$ in global memory as a preprocessing step. This could be implemented either by fusing the transpose operation with the epilogue of a preceding operation (such as rotary embedding), or by calling a standalone preprocessing transpose kernel. However, fusing with rotary embedding is difficult to integrate into standard libraries, and a standalone transpose kernel is wasteful in memory-bound scenarios like inference, where the additional memory transfers would negate much of the speedup from FP8.

## In-Kernel Transpose Using LDSM and STSM

FlashAttention-3 solves the layout challenge through an in-kernel transpose operation, performed after loading tiles of $\mathbf{V}$ into shared memory. This approach leverages specialized GPU instructions designed for efficient matrix transposition.

The LDSM (load matrix) and STSM (store matrix) instructions enable a warp of threads to collectively load data from shared memory to registers (RMEM) and store data from registers to shared memory, respectively, at a granularity of 128 bytes. These instructions are particularly valuable because they can perform transposition during the memory copy operation itself. In the PTX documentation, LDSM and STSM are described as copying $8 \times 8$ matrices with 16-bit entries. For FP8 precision, two 8-bit entries can be packed together to utilize these instructions, though this requires careful handling because the transpose variants of LDSM and STSM cannot split packed 8-bit entries, necessitating certain register movements between the load and store operations.

The efficiency of LDSM and STSM makes them ideal for execution in the producer warpgroup. This choice is strategic: the producer warpgroup is responsible for data movement, and the register efficiency of these instructions means they can be performed without overwhelming the available register resources. Furthermore, after the first iteration of the attention loop, the transpose of the next $\mathbf{V}$ tile can be scheduled to execute in the "shadow" of the two WGMMA operations that involve the preceding $\mathbf{V}$ tile and the current $\mathbf{K}$ tile. This overlapping hides the latency of the transpose operation behind the dominant GEMM computation.

## FP32 Accumulator and FP8 Operand Layout Mismatch

Beyond the input layout constraints, FP8 attention faces another challenge related to how intermediate results are organized in registers. When performing GEMM operations, the output is accumulated in FP32 precision to maintain numerical accuracy, even when the inputs are in FP8. However, the layout of this FP32 accumulator differs from the layout expected for an FP8 operand when it is used as input to a subsequent GEMM.

The FP32 accumulator of an FP8 WGMMA follows a specific register layout, with entries held in registers per thread in a particular order. For example, a fragment of this layout might organize values as $\{$d0, d2, d4, d6, d1, d3, d5, d7$\}$. However, the FP8 WGMMA expects its operand A (when held in registers) to follow a different ordering, such as $\{$d0, d1, d4, d5, d2, d3, d6, d7$\}$.

To bridge this mismatch, FlashAttention-3 uses byte permute instructions to transform the first WGMMA's accumulator into a format suitable for the second WGMMA. Specifically, the register order is permuted by changing the sequence to $\{$d0, d1, d4, d5, d2, d3, d6, d7$\}$, and this permutation is replicated over every 8 bytes. In terms of the logical shape of the $\mathbf{P}$ tile (the intermediate attention weights), this operation permutes columns; for instance, columns 0, 1, 8, and 9 might become the first four columns.

Crucially, the in-kernel transpose provides additional freedom that simplifies this transformation. The transpose can be arranged to write out a matching row permutation of the $\mathbf{V}$ tile, which eliminates the need for shuffle instructions to change register ownership across threads. This elegant solution ensures that the second WGMMA computes the correct output tile while satisfying both the FP8 layout constraints and the natural flow of data through the attention computation.

## Outlier Values and the Quantization Challenge

The reduced mantissa precision of FP8 (only 3 bits) makes it highly sensitive to the distribution of values being quantized. While this is problematic for any quantization task, it is particularly challenging for large language models, which typically exhibit outlier values that are much larger in magnitude than most other values in the activation tensors. These outliers create a fundamental tension: using a single scaling factor that accommodates the outliers forces most values to be quantized with very low precision, while using a scaling factor optimized for the majority of values would cause the outliers to overflow or saturate.

The standard approach of per-tensor scaling, where one scalar is kept per tensor for $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$, is insufficient for handling the outlier phenomenon in LLMs. This motivates the need for more sophisticated quantization strategies that can adapt to the local statistics of different regions of the tensor.

## Block Quantization: Local Scaling Factors

Block quantization addresses the outlier challenge by using per-block scaling factors instead of per-tensor scaling. For each of the input tensors $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$, the tensor is split into blocks of size $B_r \times d$ or $B_c \times d$, where $B_r$ and $B_c$ are the query and key block sizes used in the tiled attention algorithm. Each block is then quantized separately with its own scaling factor.

This approach is more naturally compatible with the FlashAttention algorithm, which already operates on blocks of the input tensors. As the algorithm processes each block, it can account for the block's scaling factor at essentially no additional computational cost—the scaling is absorbed into the existing operations.

An important practical benefit is that block quantization can be fused with operations that immediately precede attention, such as rotary embedding. Since rotary embedding is memory-bandwidth bound, the additional computation required for block quantization does not slow down the overall pipeline. This fusion is transparent to the user and integrates cleanly into existing model architectures.

## Incoherent Processing: Smoothing the Distribution

While block quantization helps adapt to local variations in magnitude, it does not directly address the fundamental problem that outliers create in the value distribution. Incoherent processing provides an orthogonal technique that modifies the distribution itself before quantization.

The key insight behind incoherent processing is that multiplying the query and key matrices by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ does not change the attention output. Since $\mathbf{M}$ is orthogonal, we have $\mathbf{M} \mathbf{M}^\top = I$, and therefore:

$$(\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{M}) (\mathbf{K} \mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{M} \mathbf{M}^\top \mathbf{K}^\top = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$$

This mathematical identity means we can freely transform $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by $\mathbf{M}$ without affecting the attention computation. The benefit comes from how this transformation affects the value distribution: each entry of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}$ or $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}$ is a random sum of entries from the original $\mathbf{Q}$ or $\mathbf{K}$. This averaging effect "spreads out" the outliers, reducing their magnitude and creating a more uniform distribution that is amenable to quantization.

The computational cost of applying $\mathbf{M}$ would be prohibitive if implemented naively, as matrix multiplication has $O(d^2)$ complexity. However, by choosing $\mathbf{M}$ to be the product of random diagonal matrices with entries $\pm 1$ and a Hadamard matrix, the transformation can be computed in $O(d \log d)$ time. The Hadamard matrix has a recursive structure that enables fast multiplication using the Fast Walsh-Hadamard Transform, similar to how the Fast Fourier Transform exploits the structure of the Fourier matrix. Like block quantization, this incoherent processing can be fused with the rotary embedding operation, adding no extra computational overhead in practice.

## Achieving Numerical Accuracy: The Combined Effect

The combination of block quantization and incoherent processing yields substantial improvements in numerical accuracy. Empirical validation demonstrates that FlashAttention-3 in FP8 with these techniques achieves 2.6$\times$ lower root mean squared error (RMSE) compared to a baseline FP8 attention implementation that uses per-tensor scaling.

To validate these accuracy claims under realistic conditions, the evaluation uses a distribution designed to simulate the outlier features observed in large language models. The entries of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ are generated from a mixture distribution: a standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ combined with a sparse outlier term $\mathcal{N}(0, 100) \cdot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001)$. This means that 0.1\% of entries have their values augmented by a term with standard deviation 10, creating outliers that are approximately 10 times larger than typical values.

Under this challenging distribution, the baseline FP8 attention with per-tensor scaling suffers significant accuracy degradation. The FP8 FlashAttention-3 with block quantization and incoherent processing maintains accuracy much closer to higher-precision implementations, validating that these techniques effectively address the fundamental challenges of FP8 quantization for attention in large language models.

# Experimental Validation and Performance Analysis

Having developed the algorithmic innovations of FlashAttention-3, we now turn to the critical question of empirical validation. This chapter examines the experimental methodology and results that demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed techniques. Understanding these results requires not only appreciating the raw performance numbers but also grasping the careful experimental design that ensures fair comparisons and meaningful conclusions.

## Benchmarking Setup: H100 SXM5 GPU Configuration

The empirical evaluation of FlashAttention-3 was conducted on the NVIDIA H100 80GB SXM5 GPU, representing the state-of-the-art Hopper architecture that introduces the hardware features central to this work. This specific GPU model is significant because it embodies the architectural innovations—Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA), asynchronous WGMMA instructions, and FP8 Tensor Cores—that FlashAttention-3 is designed to exploit.

The H100 SXM5 features 132 streaming multiprocessors (SMs) with a boost clock of 1830 MHz, yielding substantial computational throughput. For FP16 matrix multiplication operations, the theoretical peak performance reaches 989 TFLOPS, while FP8 operations can achieve approximately twice this rate at close to 1.98 PFLOPS. These theoretical maximums provide important context for interpreting the utilization rates reported in the experimental results.

The benchmarking methodology follows established conventions for attention kernel evaluation. The experiments vary the sequence length across a range from 512 to 16,384 tokens, with batch sizes adjusted to maintain a constant total of 16k tokens across all configurations. This approach ensures that the workload remains substantial enough to measure sustained throughput rather than being dominated by kernel launch overheads. The hidden dimension was fixed at 2048, with head dimensions tested at 64, 128, and 256, corresponding to 32, 16, and 8 attention heads respectively.

The FLOPs calculation for the forward pass follows the standard formula:
$$\text{FLOPs}_{\text{forward}} = 4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head dimension} \cdot \text{number of heads}.$$

This formula accounts for the two matrix multiplications in the attention computation: first computing $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and then $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. For causal masking scenarios, where approximately half the attention matrix entries are masked, the FLOPs count is divided by two. The backward pass, which requires five matrix multiplications due to gradient computations and recomputation strategies, is calculated by multiplying the forward FLOPs by a factor of 2.5.

## Comparison Baselines: PyTorch Standard, FlashAttention-2, Triton Implementation, and cuDNN

To properly contextualize the performance of FlashAttention-3, the evaluation compares against several carefully selected baselines that represent different levels of optimization sophistication. Each baseline serves a specific purpose in demonstrating where FlashAttention-3's improvements originate.

The PyTorch standard implementation represents the naive approach to attention computation, materializing the full attention matrix $\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ in high bandwidth memory (HBM). This implementation, while correct and easy to understand, suffers from substantial memory bandwidth limitations due to the quadratic memory footprint. It serves as the baseline that illustrates the fundamental need for memory-efficient attention algorithms.

FlashAttention-2, the immediate predecessor to FlashAttention-3, represents the previous state-of-the-art in exact attention computation. It employs the tiling strategy that avoids materializing intermediate matrices, parallelizes effectively over the sequence length dimension, and has been highly optimized for the Ampere architecture. However, as the authors observe, FlashAttention-2 achieves only approximately 35% utilization on H100 GPUs, compared to 80-90% utilization typical of optimized matrix multiplication kernels. This gap motivates the architectural innovations in FlashAttention-3.

The Triton implementation of FlashAttention-2 provides an important intermediate baseline. Triton is a domain-specific language for writing GPU kernels that can automatically utilize architecture-specific instructions. This baseline demonstrates whether simple adoption of Hopper-specific instructions alone can close the performance gap, or whether deeper algorithmic innovations are required. The experimental results show that while the Triton version improves over the original FlashAttention-2, it still falls short of FlashAttention-3's performance.

Finally, NVIDIA's cuDNN library provides a vendor-optimized implementation that has been specifically tuned for H100 GPUs. This closed-source library represents the best efforts of hardware engineers with intimate knowledge of the architecture. Comparing against cuDNN serves two purposes: it establishes an upper bound on what highly optimized vendor software can achieve, and it validates that FlashAttention-3's open-source implementation can match or exceed vendor-optimized code—a significant achievement for reproducible research.

## FP16 Forward Pass Results: 1.5-2.0x Speedup and Up to 740 TFLOPs/s

The FP16 forward pass results demonstrate substantial performance improvements over all baselines. FlashAttention-3 achieves speedups ranging from 1.5x to 2.0x compared to FlashAttention-2, with the exact improvement depending on the specific configuration parameters such as sequence length, head dimension, and whether causal masking is applied.

The peak performance reaches 740 TFLOPs/s, which corresponds to approximately 75% of the theoretical maximum FP16 throughput on the H100 GPU. This represents a dramatic improvement over FlashAttention-2's 35% utilization, effectively doubling the computational efficiency. To appreciate this achievement, consider that the gap between FlashAttention-2's 35% and typical GEMM kernels' 80-90% utilization was precisely the motivation for this work. Closing this gap required fundamentally rethinking how attention computation maps to the hardware execution model.

The speedup varies with sequence length due to the nature of the attention computation. At shorter sequence lengths, the overhead of kernel launch and the limited opportunity for overlapping operations reduce the relative benefits of asynchronous execution. As sequence lengths increase, the ratio of computation to overhead improves, and the pipelining techniques become more effective at hiding memory and instruction latencies. For medium to long sequences (1k tokens and above), FlashAttention-3's advantages become most pronounced.

Compared to the standard PyTorch implementation, FlashAttention-3 achieves speedups of 3x to 16x depending on the configuration. The larger improvements occur at longer sequence lengths where the standard implementation's quadratic memory footprint becomes increasingly prohibitive. These results underscore the practical importance of memory-efficient attention for enabling long-context applications.

## FP16 Backward Pass Results: 1.5-1.75x Speedup

The backward pass of attention computation presents additional optimization challenges compared to the forward pass, yet FlashAttention-3 maintains substantial performance improvements here as well. The speedup over FlashAttention-2 ranges from 1.5x to 1.75x in FP16 precision.

The backward pass is more complex because it requires computing gradients with respect to all three input matrices $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$. As detailed in the background section, this involves five matrix multiplications: computing $\mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO}$, $\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO}\mathbf{V}^\top$, applying the softmax derivative, and then computing $\mathbf{dQ} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{dK} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}^\top\mathbf{Q}$. This increased computational complexity, combined with the data dependencies between operations, creates different optimization opportunities and challenges than the forward pass.

The slightly lower speedup compared to the forward pass (1.5-1.75x versus 1.5-2.0x) reflects these additional complexities. However, the consistent improvements across both passes demonstrate that the algorithmic innovations of FlashAttention-3—warp-specialization, GEMM-softmax pipelining, and low-precision support—generalize effectively to the gradient computation setting.

## FP8 Performance: Close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s

FP8 precision represents a significant frontier for attention optimization, offering potentially double the throughput of FP16 by leveraging the specialized FP8 Tensor Cores in the Hopper architecture. FlashAttention-3 achieves performance approaching 1.2 PFLOPs/s in FP8, demonstrating near-optimal utilization of the hardware's low-precision capabilities.

This performance level represents approximately 60% of the theoretical FP8 peak throughput on the H100. While this utilization rate is lower than the 75% achieved in FP16, it still represents a substantial achievement given the additional challenges of FP8 computation. The FP8 Tensor Cores operate at double the throughput of FP16 units, but extracting this performance requires overcoming the layout conformance constraints discussed earlier.

The FP8 implementation must contend with the k-major constraint on input operands, necessitating in-kernel transposition of the value matrix tiles. Additionally, the layout mismatch between FP32 accumulators and FP8 operands requires careful register permutation. These additional operations introduce overhead that partially offsets the raw throughput advantage of FP8 Tensor Cores.

The comparison between FP16 and FP8 performance reveals interesting trade-offs. For head dimension 64, FlashAttention-3 FP8 outperforms FP16. However, for head dimensions 128 and 256, FP8 achieves parity with FP16 in cases without causal masking, but falls behind in cases with causal masking. This behavior relates to the persistent kernel design: the FP16 implementation includes a persistent kernel and load balancing strategy, while the FP8 version does not, creating a disadvantage for FP8 in certain workload configurations.

## Comparison with Vendor-Optimized cuDNN Library

Perhaps the most striking validation of FlashAttention-3's effectiveness is its performance relative to NVIDIA's cuDNN library—a vendor-optimized, closed-source implementation specifically tuned for H100 GPUs. For medium and long sequences (1k tokens and above), FlashAttention-3 matches or exceeds cuDNN's performance in FP16.

This comparison is significant for several reasons. First, it demonstrates that the algorithmic innovations developed through open research can match the efforts of hardware vendors with privileged knowledge of architectural details. Second, it validates that FlashAttention-3's approach correctly identifies and exploits the key performance bottlenecks on Hopper GPUs. Third, it provides confidence that further optimization within the FlashAttention-3 framework is possible, as the algorithm has not yet hit fundamental hardware limits.

The competitive performance with cuDNN holds across various configurations. For non-causal attention, FlashAttention-3 performs comparably to cuDNN across different head dimensions. For causal attention, the results are similarly competitive, though the specific performance characteristics depend on the interaction between causal masking and the kernel's work distribution strategy.

It is worth noting that cuDNN's FP8 implementation outperforms FlashAttention-3 FP8 for small sequence lengths and causal masking scenarios. This gap is attributed to the lack of a persistent kernel design in the FP8 implementation of FlashAttention-3—a limitation the authors identify for future work.

## Ablation Study: Contribution of Warp-Specialization

To isolate the contribution of individual optimization techniques, the authors conduct ablation studies that selectively enable or disable specific features. These experiments are crucial for understanding which innovations drive the observed performance improvements and for validating the theoretical motivations behind each technique.

Warp-specialization represents a fundamental reorganization of how work is distributed among GPU threads. Rather than having all warps perform similar operations, the approach divides warps into producers (responsible for data movement via TMA) and consumers (responsible for computation via WGMMA). This specialization allows each warp type to be optimized for its specific role, with producers deallocating registers to maximize available shared memory and consumers reallocating registers to accommodate larger computation buffers.

The ablation experiments confirm that warp-specialization contributes measurably to the overall speedup. By decoupling data movement from computation, the technique enables more effective hiding of memory latency. The producer warps can issue TMA loads asynchronously, prefetching data for upcoming iterations while consumer warps continue processing the current iteration. This overlapping of memory transfers with computation reduces the time the GPU spends waiting for data.

## Ablation Study: Contribution of 2-Stage Pipelining

The 2-stage GEMM-softmax pipelining technique represents a more granular optimization that operates within the consumer warpgroup. The ablation study specifically tests this technique by comparing performance with and without the pipelined execution.

The results show a clear improvement from 570 TFLOPs/s without pipelining to 661 TFLOPs/s with the 2-stage pipeline—an improvement of approximately 16%. This speedup directly validates the insight that non-GEMM operations (particularly the exponential functions required for softmax) represent a significant bottleneck due to their relatively low throughput on modern GPUs.

To recall the hardware characteristics, the H100 provides 989 TFLOPS of FP16 matmul throughput but only 3.9 TFLOPS for special functions like exponential. While the attention computation has 512 times more matmul FLOPs than exponential operations (for head dimension 128), the exponential's 256x lower throughput means it can consume up to 50% of the execution time. By overlapping softmax computation with asynchronous WGMMA execution, the pipelining technique effectively hides this bottleneck.

The combined ablation results—warp-specialization and pipelining together—demonstrate that these techniques are complementary. Each addresses a different source of inefficiency: warp-specialization targets memory latency, while pipelining targets compute throughput imbalances. Together, they transform FlashAttention from a synchronous, memory-bound algorithm into an asynchronous, compute-efficient one.

## Numerical Error Validation Methodology: FP64 Reference

Beyond performance, the accuracy of computed results is paramount for practical deployment. FlashAttention-3 introduces numerical validation experiments to ensure that the algorithmic optimizations do not compromise result quality.

The validation methodology uses a reference implementation in FP64 (double precision) as ground truth. FP64 provides approximately 15-16 significant decimal digits of precision, making it effectively exact for the numerical ranges encountered in attention computation. By comparing outputs against this FP64 reference, the experiments measure the numerical error introduced by lower-precision computations.

Root mean squared error (RMSE) serves as the primary accuracy metric. RMSE captures the average magnitude of errors across all output elements, providing a single scalar measure of numerical deviation:
$$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N \cdot d}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(O_{ij} - O_{ij}^{\text{ref}})^2},$$
where $O$ is the computed output and $O^{\text{ref}}$ is the FP64 reference.

This methodology provides a fair comparison across different implementations because all methods are evaluated against the same ground truth using the same metric. The experiments compare FlashAttention-2, FlashAttention-3, and the standard PyTorch implementation to isolate the effects of different algorithmic approaches on numerical accuracy.

## Simulating Outlier Features with Mixed Gaussian Distribution

A particular concern for low-precision attention is the presence of outlier features—values that are significantly larger in magnitude than typical elements. Large language models are known to exhibit such outliers, which create challenges for quantization because they can saturate the limited dynamic range of FP8 format.

To evaluate robustness against outliers, the authors design a synthetic data distribution that mimics this phenomenon. The entries of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ are generated according to:
$$x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) + \mathcal{N}(0, 100) \cdot \text{Bernoulli}(0.001).$$

This distribution can be understood as follows: the base value follows a standard normal distribution with zero mean and unit variance. However, with probability 0.1%, an additional term is added that follows a normal distribution with standard deviation 10. This creates a heavy-tailed distribution where most values are moderate (standard deviation 1), but approximately 0.1% of values are outliers approximately 10 times larger than typical.

This synthetic distribution serves two purposes. First, it provides a controlled setting where outlier behavior can be studied systematically, unlike real data where outliers may be unpredictable. Second, it stress-tests the quantization strategies, revealing how different approaches handle the dynamic range challenges that outliers create.

## FP16 Accuracy: 1.7x Lower RMSE than Standard Attention

The FP16 numerical accuracy results reveal an important finding: both FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3 achieve 1.7x lower RMSE compared to the standard PyTorch implementation. This improvement in accuracy, alongside the dramatic speedup, demonstrates that efficient algorithms need not sacrifice numerical quality.

The accuracy advantage stems from how intermediate results are stored. In the standard implementation, the attention scores $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and the softmax output $\mathbf{P} = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$ are materialized in HBM at FP16 precision. This introduces rounding errors at each stage that accumulate through the computation.

In contrast, FlashAttention implementations keep intermediate softmax results in FP32, performing the rescaling operations at higher precision. Although the inputs and outputs remain FP16, the critical reduction operations (computing row-wise maximums and sums for softmax) maintain FP32 precision throughout. This design choice, inherited from FlashAttention-2 and maintained in FlashAttention-3, provides numerical benefits without performance cost since these operations occur on-chip.

The fact that FlashAttention-3 maintains the same accuracy as FlashAttention-2 confirms that the new algorithmic techniques—asynchronous execution, pipelining, and warp-specialization—do not introduce numerical degradation. The reordered execution does not change which arithmetic operations are performed, only when they are scheduled relative to each other.

## FP8 Accuracy: 2.6x Better than Baseline Per-Tensor Quantization

The FP8 accuracy results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed quantization techniques. FlashAttention-3 in FP8, using block quantization and incoherent processing, achieves 2.6x lower RMSE than a baseline FP8 attention implementation using per-tensor scaling.

To understand this improvement, consider the quantization challenge. The FP8 E4M3 format uses only 3 bits for the mantissa and 4 bits for the exponent, providing limited precision and dynamic range compared to FP16. Per-tensor quantization—using a single scale factor for the entire $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, or $\mathbf{V}$ tensor—struggles when outliers exist, as the scale must accommodate the largest values, leaving little precision for typical values.

Block quantization addresses this by assigning separate scale factors to blocks of size $B_r \times d$ for $\mathbf{Q}$ and $B_c \times d$ for $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$. This local adaptation allows each block to use a scale appropriate for its value range, rather than being constrained by outliers elsewhere in the tensor.

Incoherent processing provides complementary benefits by redistributing outlier values. Multiplying $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ (implemented efficiently as the product of random diagonal matrices and a Hadamard matrix) spreads large values across multiple dimensions. Since each entry of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}$ or $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}$ is a weighted sum of original entries, outliers are diluted across many elements. Critically, this transformation preserves the attention output: $(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ because $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = \mathbf{I}$.

The combination of these techniques—achieving 2.6x error reduction—validates that careful algorithmic design can mitigate FP8's precision limitations, enabling practical use of low-precision attention without unacceptable accuracy degradation.

## Effect of Sequence Length and Head Dimension on Performance

The performance characteristics of FlashAttention-3 vary systematically with sequence length and head dimension, reflecting the interaction between algorithmic design and hardware characteristics.

Sequence length affects performance through several mechanisms. At short sequences (512 tokens), kernel launch overhead and the limited size of computation per kernel reduce utilization. As sequence length increases, the ratio of useful computation to overhead improves, and the pipelining techniques become more effective at hiding latencies. The benefits of FlashAttention-3's optimizations become most pronounced at sequence lengths of 1k tokens and above, where sustained throughput approaches the measured peaks.

For very long sequences, the attention computation remains compute-bound rather than memory-bound, allowing FlashAttention-3 to maintain high utilization. This contrasts with standard attention implementations, which become memory-bound at long sequences due to quadratic memory access patterns.

Head dimension affects the balance between different operations. Larger head dimensions increase the relative cost of matrix multiplications compared to softmax operations, since the GEMM FLOPs scale linearly with head dimension while softmax FLOPs depend only on sequence length. This shifts the bottleneck and affects how effectively the GEMM-softmax pipelining can hide non-GEMM operations.

The experiments show that FlashAttention-3 performs well across head dimensions of 64, 128, and 256, though the specific speedup factors vary. The head dimension also affects register pressure and shared memory usage, influencing optimal tile sizes and pipeline configurations. The implementation must balance these factors to achieve good performance across different model architectures.

# Discussion, Limitations, and Future Directions

## Summary of Core Techniques: Asynchrony, Overlapping, and Low-Precision

FlashAttention-3 represents a significant advancement in the optimization of attention computation on modern GPUs, synthesizing three principal techniques that collectively transform how we approach kernel design for deep learning workloads. These techniques—exploiting asynchrony through warp-specialization, overlapping computation with data movement, and leveraging hardware support for low-precision arithmetic—each address fundamental bottlenecks that previous implementations could not overcome.

The first technique, producer-consumer asynchrony through warp-specialization, fundamentally restructures how work is distributed across the execution units of a GPU. In traditional GPU programming models, threads within a cooperative thread array (CTA) typically perform similar operations, with implicit synchronization points that can lead to idle time. FlashAttention-3 instead divides warps into distinct producer and consumer roles. Producer warps are dedicated to data movement operations, specifically loading tiles of the query, key, and value matrices from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) into shared memory using the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA). Consumer warps, conversely, focus exclusively on computation, executing matrix multiplications on the Tensor Cores and performing the softmax normalization. This separation exploits the asynchronous nature of modern GPU hardware, where specialized units like the TMA and Tensor Cores operate independently of the main CUDA cores. By dedicating warps to specific tasks, the compiler can generate more optimal instruction schedules, and the runtime can better hide memory and instruction issue latencies.

The second technique, hiding softmax operations under asynchronous block-wise general matrix multiplications (GEMMs), addresses a critical throughput imbalance in attention computation. On the H100 GPU, Tensor Core operations can achieve approximately 989 TFLOPS for FP16 matrix multiplication, while special function units that compute exponentials for softmax achieve only about 3.9 TFLOPS. This disparity means that even though the attention computation involves far more floating-point operations for matrix multiplication than for exponential calculations, the exponential operations can consume a substantial fraction of the total execution time—up to 50% for typical configurations. The insight behind this technique is that the softmax computation, which depends on the output of one matrix multiplication and produces input for another, can be pipelined across iterations of the main loop. Specifically, while the consumer warpgroup executes the second matrix multiplication $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$ for iteration $j$, it can simultaneously prepare the softmax computation for iteration $j+1$. This interleaving is enabled by the asynchronous WGMMA (warp-group matrix multiply accumulate) instruction on Hopper GPUs, which allows the Tensor Cores to continue executing while other operations proceed. The two-stage and three-stage pipeline variants of this technique represent trade-offs between overlapping depth and register pressure.

The third technique, hardware-accelerated low-precision computation, exploits the FP8 Tensor Cores available on Hopper GPUs to nearly double the achievable throughput compared to FP16 or BF16. The FP8 format (specifically the e4m3 variant) uses 4 bits for the exponent and 3 bits for the mantissa, providing half the precision of FP16. However, utilizing FP8 for attention requires overcoming several challenges. First, the WGMMA instruction has specific layout constraints: for FP8 operands, only k-major format is supported (where the inner K dimension is contiguous in memory), whereas FP16 accepts both mn-major and k-major layouts. This necessitates in-kernel transposition of the value matrix tiles using specialized instructions like LDSM and STSM. Second, the accumulator layout for FP8 WGMMA differs from the operand layout expected for subsequent operations, requiring register permutation via byte permute instructions. Most critically, FP8 precision introduces significant numerical error, particularly for models with outlier features—values that are much larger in magnitude than typical activations. To address this, FlashAttention-3 employs block quantization, where each block of the query, key, and value matrices has its own scaling factor rather than using a single per-tensor scale, and incoherent processing, which multiplies queries and keys by a random orthogonal matrix before quantization to spread out the effect of outliers.

## Closing the Gap Between Actual and Theoretical GPU Performance

The most compelling achievement of FlashAttention-3 is its dramatic improvement in hardware utilization, bringing actual performance much closer to theoretical limits. To appreciate this achievement, we must understand what constitutes theoretical peak performance and why previous implementations fell short. The H100 SXM5 GPU has a theoretical maximum of 989 TFLOPS for FP16 Tensor Core operations. FlashAttention-2, despite being highly optimized, achieved only about 35% of this peak—approximately 350 TFLOPS. This gap between actual and theoretical performance represents a substantial opportunity for optimization.

FlashAttention-3 closes this gap impressively, achieving up to 740 TFLOPS for FP16 computation, which corresponds to approximately 75% utilization of the theoretical peak. For FP8 computation, where the theoretical peak doubles to approximately 1978 TFLOPS (close to 2 PFLOPS), FlashAttention-3 achieves close to 1.2 PFLOPS. This represents a 1.5 to 2.0× speedup over FlashAttention-2 for the forward pass and a 1.5 to 1.75× speedup for the backward pass. Compared to a standard PyTorch attention implementation, FlashAttention-3 can be 3 to 16× faster depending on the sequence length and configuration.

The significance of closing this utilization gap extends beyond mere benchmark improvements. When a GPU kernel achieves low utilization, it indicates that hardware resources are sitting idle for significant portions of execution time—memory bandwidth is underutilized, Tensor Cores are waiting for operands, or control flow overhead dominates useful computation. By achieving 75% utilization, FlashAttention-3 demonstrates that careful attention to hardware characteristics can extract nearly all available performance from the device. This is particularly important for attention computation, which is the primary computational bottleneck in transformer models. Every improvement in attention efficiency directly translates to faster training and inference for large language models and enables longer context lengths that unlock new capabilities.

The performance comparison with vendor-optimized libraries is particularly noteworthy. FlashAttention-3 not only matches but in many cases exceeds the performance of NVIDIA's cuDNN library, which is closed-source and specifically optimized for H100 GPUs. For medium and long sequences (sequence length 1024 and above), FlashAttention-3 outperforms cuDNN's attention implementation. This demonstrates that algorithmic innovations and careful low-level programming can achieve performance that equals or exceeds vendor-optimized code, even without access to proprietary hardware documentation.

## Limitations: Optimization for LLM Inference

Despite its impressive achievements, FlashAttention-3 has several limitations that represent opportunities for future work. The first significant limitation concerns optimization for large language model inference workloads. While FlashAttention-3 excels at attention computation during training, where the forward and backward passes process large batches of data, inference presents a different set of challenges that the current implementation does not fully address.

During inference, particularly for autoregressive generation where tokens are produced one at a time, the attention computation has different characteristics than during training. The key-value cache grows with each generated token, and the query sequence length is often much shorter—frequently just one token. This creates a memory-bound rather than compute-bound workload, where the efficiency of reading and updating the key-value cache becomes more important than raw compute throughput. The current FlashAttention-3 algorithm, optimized primarily for training scenarios with longer query sequences, may not be optimal for these inference patterns.

Furthermore, inference workloads often involve batching strategies like continuous batching, where multiple sequences at different generation stages are processed together. These dynamic batching patterns require different scheduling and memory management strategies than the static batching used during training. Optimizing for inference would require modifications to the kernel design, potentially including specialized code paths for single-token queries, better integration with key-value cache management, and load balancing strategies for variable-length sequences within a batch.

## Limitations: FP8 Kernel Lacks Persistent Kernel Design

A second technical limitation specifically affects the FP8 variant of FlashAttention-3. The FP16 implementation employs a persistent kernel design with load balancing strategies, while the FP8 implementation does not. Understanding this limitation requires explaining what persistent kernels are and why they matter for performance.

In a traditional GPU kernel launch model, the GPU schedules cooperative thread arrays (CTAs) as resources become available. When a kernel completes, a new kernel is launched. The overhead of kernel launch and the potential for suboptimal scheduling can impact performance, particularly for workloads with varying amounts of work across different CTAs. A persistent kernel design takes a different approach: kernels remain resident on the GPU, and work items are dynamically scheduled to available resources. This allows for better load balancing, as CTAs that finish early can take on additional work, and it amortizes launch overhead across many work items.

For FlashAttention-3, the FP16 implementation uses a persistent kernel design where the thread blocks dynamically distribute work across the query sequence length dimension. This load balancing strategy ensures that all GPU resources remain utilized even when some attention heads or batches have less work than others. The FP8 implementation, due to the additional complexity of handling the layout transformations and quantization, does not include this persistent design.

The practical consequence of this limitation is observable in benchmark results. For small sequence lengths and with causal masking enabled, the FP8 FlashAttention-3 performs less competitively against the FP8 cuDNN kernels. Causal masking means that only the lower triangular portion of the attention matrix is computed, which changes the workload distribution across CTAs. Without a persistent kernel design, some CTAs may have significantly more work than others, leading to load imbalance and idle resources. Addressing this limitation would require integrating persistent kernel patterns into the FP8 implementation while maintaining the layout transformations needed for FP8 WGMMA compliance.

## Future Work: Understanding Low-Precision Attention in Large-Scale Training

An important direction for future research involves understanding the effects of low-precision attention computation in large-scale training scenarios. While FlashAttention-3 demonstrates that FP8 attention can achieve 2.6× lower numerical error than baseline FP8 attention through block quantization and incoherent processing, the implications of these errors for large language model training remain an open question.

Large-scale training involves running optimization algorithms over billions of parameters and trillions of tokens. Numerical errors that appear negligible in isolated attention computations can potentially accumulate over millions of training steps, affecting model convergence, final accuracy, or the emergence of specific capabilities. Conversely, low-precision training has been shown to work well in many scenarios, and the extra throughput from FP8 computation might enable training larger models or longer sequences that would otherwise be impractical.

The block quantization and incoherent processing techniques introduced in FlashAttention-3 provide a foundation for exploring these questions. Block quantization adapts to local variation in the magnitude of activations, providing better dynamic range utilization than per-tensor scaling. Incoherent processing addresses the challenge of outlier features—activation values that are orders of magnitude larger than typical values—by spreading their influence across multiple entries through multiplication with a random orthogonal matrix. However, understanding how these techniques interact with training dynamics, whether certain model architectures are more sensitive to low-precision attention, and whether additional techniques are needed for stable training at scale requires extensive empirical investigation.

Moreover, future hardware generations like NVIDIA's Blackwell architecture will support even lower precision formats like FP4. The techniques developed for FlashAttention-3 may need extension or modification for these new formats, and the trade-offs between precision, throughput, and training stability may shift. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for the continued scaling of large language models.

## Applicability to Other Hardware Accelerators

Although FlashAttention-3 is described and evaluated specifically on NVIDIA's Hopper architecture, the algorithmic principles are broadly applicable to other hardware accelerators that provide similar capabilities. The paper explicitly notes that the algorithm "is operative for any GPU architecture with sufficiently robust asynchronous execution and low-precision capabilities."

The three core techniques—warp-specialization for producer-consumer asynchrony, overlapping non-GEMM operations with asynchronous GEMMs, and hardware-accelerated low-precision computation—require specific hardware features to be effective. These include: specialized units for memory loading that operate asynchronously from compute units, asynchronous matrix multiplication units (like Tensor Cores), sufficient register file capacity to support multiple pipeline stages, and low-precision compute units with appropriate layout handling.

Modern AI accelerators increasingly provide these features. AMD's MI300 series, Google's TPUs, and various startup accelerators all include specialized matrix multiplication units and are moving toward lower precision support. The specific instructions, layouts, and constraints differ across architectures, but the fundamental principle of exploiting asynchrony to hide latency and overlapping computation across independent execution units applies broadly.

The warp-specialization concept, in particular, represents a general pattern for throughput optimization. When different operations execute on different hardware units with different latencies, separating them into producer and consumer roles allows for better scheduling and reduces the overhead of managing dependencies. Similarly, the pipelining approach that overlaps softmax computation with matrix multiplication applies whenever non-matrix-multiply operations are a significant fraction of total runtime.

Understanding these algorithmic principles and how they map to specific hardware features is valuable beyond implementing attention. The same techniques apply to other operations in neural networks—normalization layers, activation functions, custom operators—that involve combinations of memory access, specialized computation, and matrix operations. The lessons from FlashAttention-3 provide a template for optimizing other kernel types on current and future hardware.

## Integration with PyTorch and Hugging Face Ecosystems

For any optimization to have practical impact, it must be accessible to practitioners. FlashAttention-3 is designed with integration into existing machine learning ecosystems as a priority. The authors commit to open-sourcing the implementation under a permissive license and integrating it with both PyTorch and Hugging Face libraries.

This integration strategy matters for several reasons. First, the vast majority of large language model development happens using these frameworks. PyTorch provides the tensor operations and automatic differentiation infrastructure, while Hugging Face's transformers library provides implementations of model architectures, pre-trained weights, and training pipelines. An optimized attention implementation that requires significant code changes or cannot be used with existing models would see limited adoption.

The integration approach for FlashAttention-3 follows the pattern established by its predecessors. The FlashAttention kernels expose the same interface as standard attention, allowing them to be dropped into existing models without modification. The automatic differentiation machinery in PyTorch handles the backward pass, and the kernel itself handles the fused computation of gradients. For Hugging Face models, this means that a simple import change or configuration flag can enable the faster attention implementation.

Beyond basic compatibility, deep integration enables additional optimizations. When the attention kernel is aware of the broader computation graph, it can potentially fuse with adjacent operations. The paper mentions that block quantization can be fused with rotary embedding computations, which commonly precede attention in transformer models. Similarly, incoherent processing involves multiplying by a Hadamard matrix, which can also be fused. These fusion opportunities reduce memory bandwidth requirements and improve overall efficiency.

The open-source release under a permissive license ensures that the implementation can be used, modified, and incorporated into both research projects and commercial products without licensing restrictions. This broadens the potential impact and enables community contributions to further improve and extend the implementation.

## Broader Lessons for GPU Kernel Optimization

Beyond the specific techniques for attention, FlashAttention-3 provides broader lessons about effective GPU kernel optimization in the modern hardware landscape.

The first lesson is the importance of understanding hardware execution models at a deep level. The improvements in FlashAttention-3 come not from algorithmic changes to attention itself—the mathematical computation is unchanged—but from adapting the implementation to hardware features that previous versions did not fully exploit. Understanding that Tensor Cores operate asynchronously, that the TMA can load memory independently of compute, and that FP8 has specific layout requirements enabled the optimizations. This pattern—optimizing not the algorithm but its mapping to hardware—applies broadly across computational kernels.

The second lesson concerns the evolution of hardware specialization and the programming techniques it enables. Early GPU programming treated the device as a homogeneous array of execution units. Modern GPUs are increasingly heterogeneous, with specialized units for specific operations. Effective optimization requires understanding this heterogeneity and structuring code to keep all units busy. Warp-specialization is one programming pattern that addresses this challenge, but similar thinking applies whenever specialized hardware units are present.

The third lesson involves the interplay between numerical precision and performance. Lower precision formats offer higher throughput but introduce errors that must be managed. The choice of precision is not simply a performance versus accuracy trade-off—it requires understanding the structure of the computation and developing techniques to mitigate precision loss. Block quantization and incoherent processing are specific to attention, but the principle of adapting precision handling to computation structure applies broadly.

The fourth lesson concerns the importance of memory layout and data movement. Much of the complexity in the FP8 implementation stems from layout requirements of the WGMMA instruction. Understanding how data is arranged in memory and registers, and how layout affects what instructions can be executed efficiently, is fundamental to GPU optimization. The transpose operations and register permutations in FlashAttention-3 exemplify the type of low-level data manipulation that high-performance kernels require.

Finally, FlashAttention-3 demonstrates the value of co-design between algorithms and hardware. Rather than treating hardware as a fixed target and algorithms as adjustable, effective optimization often involves understanding what the hardware does well and designing algorithms to match. The attention computation was not redesigned—the sequence of matrix multiplications and softmax remains—but the implementation structure was redesigned around hardware characteristics. This co-design mindset, considering algorithm and hardware together rather than separately, characterizes the most effective optimization work.
