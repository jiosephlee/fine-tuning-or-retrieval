# The Comeback Kid: How xLSTM is Challenging Transformers for AI Supremacy

For nearly a decade, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network was the undisputed king of sequence modeling. From generating text and handwriting to powering the AI behind AlphaStar and OpenAI Five, LSTMs were the backbone of deep learning success stories. However, the rise of Transformers in 2017, with their parallelizable self-attention mechanisms, dethroned LSTMs, rendering them seemingly obsolete in the era of Large Language Models (LLMs). 

But a recent paper titled "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory" asks a provocative question: What if we modernized LSTMs with today's techniques and scaled them to billions of parameters? The result is xLSTM, a revamped architecture that not only addresses the historical flaws of LSTMs but challenges the supremacy of Transformers and State Space Models.

## Why LSTMs Fell Behind

To understand why xLSTM is a breakthrough, we first need to understand why LSTMs were left behind. Despite their success, traditional LSTMs suffered from three critical limitations:

1.  **Inability to revise storage decisions:** Once an LSTM decided to store something in its memory cell, it struggled to overwrite that value when a more relevant piece of information appeared later.
2.  **Limited storage capacity:** LSTMs compress information into scalar cell states. This bottleneck limits their ability to handle complex information retrieval, especially for rare tokens.
3.  **Lack of parallelizability:** The "hidden-to-hidden" connections (memory mixing) required sequential processing, making training slow compared to the fully parallelizable Transformer.

These limitations paved the way for Transformers. However, Transformers come with their own trade-offs, specifically quadratic complexity with respect to sequence length. xLSTM aims to get the best of both worlds: the linear complexity of recurrent models and the performance of Transformers.

## The xLSTM Upgrade: Exponential Gating and New Memories

The authors of the paper introduced two major modifications to the classic LSTM architecture to overcome its limitations: exponential gating and modified memory structures.

### Exponential Gating

The original LSTM used sigmoid gates, which output values between 0 and 1. This bounded nature limited the model's ability to make sharp storage decisions. xLSTM introduces exponential gating, where the input and forget gates use exponential activation functions.

This change allows the gates to take on much larger positive values, enabling the model to "write" to memory more decisively. If the model detects a highly relevant piece of information, the exponential input gate can blast it into memory, effectively solving the problem of revising storage decisions. To prevent numerical instability from these large values, the authors implement a normalizer state and stabilizer state to keep the computations grounded.

### New Memory Structures: sLSTM and mLSTM

The paper introduces two distinct LSTM variants based on how they handle memory:

*   **sLSTM (Scalar Memory):** This variant retains the scalar memory of the original LSTM but adds exponential gating and a new "memory mixing" mechanism. It allows for multiple heads (similar to Transformers), where memory mixing occurs within heads but not across them. This structure excels at state tracking and solving formal language tasks that stump models like Transformers.
*   **mLSTM (Matrix Memory):** This is perhaps the most significant innovation. Instead of a scalar memory cell, mLSTM uses a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. It stores key-value pairs using a covariance update rule: $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. This dramatically increases storage capacity. Crucially, because mLSTM removes hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections (no memory mixing), it is fully parallelizable, allowing it to train as fast as a Transformer.

## Building the Architecture

Just as Transformers stack attention layers, xLSTM stacks these new blocks into deep architectures. The authors use residual blocks—specifically, post up-projection blocks for sLSTM and pre up-projection blocks for mLSTM—to create the final xLSTM network.

An xLSTM architecture typically combines these blocks, denoted as xLSTM[$a$:$b$], where $a$ is the ratio of mLSTM blocks and $b$ is the ratio of sLSTM blocks. This mixture allows the model to leverage the parallelizable, high-capacity storage of mLSTM alongside the powerful state-tracking capabilities of sLSTM.

## Does It Actually Work?

The experimental results are striking. The authors trained xLSTM on 15B and 300B tokens from the SlimPajama dataset and compared it against heavy hitters like Llama (Transformer), Mamba (State Space Model), and RWKV (RNN).

In language modeling perplexity, xLSTM outperformed all baselines. But the advantages go beyond just raw accuracy:

*   **Scaling Laws:** xLSTM shows favorable scaling behavior, with performance curves suggesting it will continue to outperform Transformers and State Space Models as model size increases.
*   **Long Context:** Unlike many models that struggle when inference sequences are longer than training sequences, xLSTM maintains low perplexity even when extrapolating to context lengths of 16k.
*   **Efficiency:** Thanks to its recurrent nature, xLSTM has linear complexity with respect to sequence length and constant memory requirements. In throughput tests, xLSTM achieved significantly higher batch sizes and throughput compared to Llama, which suffers from quadratic memory growth.

## The Verdict

The paper concludes that xLSTM performs "favorably when compared to state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models." By fixing the storage revision issues with exponential gating and boosting capacity with matrix memory, the authors have successfully modernized the LSTM for the era of billion-parameter models.

While Transformers defined the last era of AI, xLSTM proves that the story isn't over for recurrent networks. With linear complexity, high throughput, and superior performance on benchmarks like PALOMA, xLSTM is positioning itself as a serious competitor to the Transformer throne. The comeback kid might just be ready to rule again.



# Memory Upgraded: From Scalar Cells to Matrix Memories in xLSTM

For years, Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTMs) were the workhorses of sequence modeling. They powered early language models, translated languages, and even controlled AI agents in complex games like StarCraft II and Dota 2. But when Transformers arrived with their parallelizable self-attention mechanism, LSTMs fell behind. One fundamental bottleneck held them back: their memory capacity. A recent paper titled "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory" tackles this limitation head-on by reimagining how LSTMs store information.

## The Scalar Memory Bottleneck

Traditional LSTMs store information in scalar memory cells. Each cell maintains a single numerical value $c_t$ that gets updated through gating mechanisms. The update rule looks like this:

$$c_t = f_t \cdot c_{t-1} + i_t \cdot z_t$$

Here, the forget gate $f_t$ determines how much of the previous memory to keep, the input gate $i_t$ controls how much new information $z_t$ to write, and the result is... a single scalar value.

Think of it like trying to summarize an entire document on a single sticky note. At each time step, the LSTM must decide what to keep and what to discard, compressing everything into that one number. This creates a severe storage limitation. When information needs to be recalled later—especially rare tokens in language modeling—there simply is not enough capacity to retain it all.

The xLSTM paper demonstrates this problem through "Rare Token Prediction" experiments on Wikitext-103. Traditional LSTMs perform significantly worse on tokens that appear infrequently because their limited storage capacity cannot accommodate the long tail of the vocabulary.

## Enter Matrix Memory: The mLSTM

The solution proposed in xLSTM is elegant: upgrade from scalar memory to matrix memory. The mLSTM variant replaces the scalar cell state $c \in \mathbb{R}$ with a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$.

This is not just a dimensional upgrade—it fundamentally changes how information is stored and retrieved. Instead of storing a single value, mLSTM stores associations between vectors using a covariance update rule:

$$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \cdot \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \cdot \mathbf{v}_{t} \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

Here, $\mathbf{k}_t$ is a key vector and $\mathbf{v}_t$ is a value vector. The outer product $\mathbf{v}_{t} \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ creates a matrix that captures the association between these two pieces of information. Later, when you want to retrieve information, you use a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$$

This matrix-vector multiplication retrieves the stored value associated with the most similar key.

## From Notepad to Filing Cabinet

The analogy in the title captures the essence of this upgrade. A traditional LSTM with scalar memory is like trying to keep track of everything on a single notepad. You can write, erase, and rewrite, but the page fills up quickly, and you must constantly decide what information is worth keeping.

mLSTM's matrix memory is more like a filing cabinet with cross-referenced folders. You can store key-value pairs—putting documents (values) in labeled folders (keys)—and later retrieve them by searching with the right query. The matrix structure $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ provides $d^2$ storage slots instead of just one, dramatically increasing capacity.

## The Parallelization Bonus

There is another critical advantage to mLSTM's design: it abandons "memory mixing," the recurrent connections between hidden states that force sequential processing. Traditional LSTMs must process tokens one at a time because each hidden state depends on the previous one. This makes them slow on modern hardware optimized for parallel computation.

mLSTM, by removing these hidden-hidden connections, becomes fully parallelizable. The matrix memory update and retrieval operations can be computed efficiently using standard matrix operations that GPUs excel at. The paper notes that mLSTM can be parallelized analogously to FlashAttention, making it practical for large-scale training.

## Evidence of Improved Capacity

The paper provides compelling evidence that matrix memory solves the capacity problem. On the Multi-Query Associative Recall task—where models must memorize and retrieve key-value pairs from sequences—xLSTM models with matrix memory significantly outperform other recurrent architectures.

Even more impressively, when tested on sequences longer than those seen during training, xLSTM maintains strong performance. This length extrapolation capability suggests the model is not just memorizing patterns but actually storing information in a structured, retrievable way.

## A Balanced Architecture

The full xLSTM architecture combines two variants: sLSTM (with scalar memory and exponential gating) and mLSTM (with matrix memory). The sLSTM handles tasks requiring "state tracking"—remembering the current state of a process—thanks to its memory mixing capabilities. The mLSTM provides the high-capacity storage needed for language modeling. The paper uses notations like xLSTM[7:1] to indicate the ratio of mLSTM to sLSTM blocks in the architecture.

## Conclusion

By upgrading from scalar cells to matrix memories, xLSTM addresses one of the fundamental limitations that held LSTMs back in the Transformer era. The matrix memory provides dramatically higher storage capacity, enables parallel processing, and—when combined with other innovations like exponential gating—allows LSTMs to compete with state-of-the-art architectures like Transformers and State Space Models.

The scaling experiments in the paper suggest that as models grow larger, xLSTM's advantages become more pronounced. Sometimes, the best way forward is to revisit and upgrade the foundational ideas that worked so well in the first place.



# Why Exponential Gating Changes Everything for Neural Network Memory

For decades, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture reigned supreme in sequence modeling. From generating text to controlling nuclear fusion reactors, LSTMs were the backbone of deep learning success stories. However, they eventually ceded ground to Transformers. One specific weakness that plagued LSTMs was their inability to effectively "change their minds." The xLSTM paper addresses this head-on with a simple but powerful modification: **exponential gating**.

## The Problem: Sticky Memories

To understand why exponential gating matters, we have to look at a fundamental limitation of traditional LSTMs: the inability to revise storage decisions.

Imagine an LSTM is scanning a sequence of vectors looking for the one most similar to a reference vector—a task the paper refers to as "Nearest Neighbor Search." The model finds a decent match early on and stores it. As it continues scanning, it finds a much better match. Ideally, the model should overwrite the previous memory with this new, superior information.

Traditional LSTMs struggle with this. The culprit is the sigmoid activation function used for the gates.

In a standard LSTM, the input gate $i_t$ and forget gate $f_t$ use the sigmoid function $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+\exp(-x))$. This squashes values strictly between 0 and 1. The cell state update looks like this:

$$c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$$

Here, $z_t$ is the new candidate value. Because $i_t$ is capped at 1, the influence of the new value is limited. The network has to rely on the forget gate $f_t$ shrinking the old memory close to zero to make room for the new input. This balancing act often results in a "sticky" memory where the model fails to fully overwrite the past when a stronger signal arrives.

## The Solution: Exponential Gating

The xLSTM architecture replaces the bounded sigmoid gates with exponential gates. For the input gate, the activation becomes:

$$i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$$

where $\tilde{i}_t$ is the raw input logit. This changes the game entirely. Unlike sigmoid gates, exponential gates are unbounded. They can become arbitrarily large.

When the model encounters a highly relevant input (like a better vector match in our example), the input gate $i_t$ can spike to a massive value. This allows the new information $z_t$ to overwhelm the existing memory state $c_{t-1}$, effectively forcing the network to "revise" its storage decision instantly.

## Taming the Explosion: Normalization and Stabilization

Of course, letting gate values run off to infinity is dangerous for numerical stability. A massive input gate could cause activations to explode during training.

To handle this, xLSTM introduces a **normalizer state**. The cell state is no longer the final output; it is normalized by a running track of the gate strengths.

The system maintains a normalizer state $n_t$ alongside the cell state:

$$n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$$

The output of the memory cell (before the output gate) is the ratio of the cell state to this normalizer:

$$\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}$$

This creates a self-correcting mechanism. If the input gate $i_t$ becomes huge to emphasize a new input, the normalizer $n_t$ also grows by the same proportion. This keeps the output stable while allowing the new input to dominate the history.

Additionally, the authors use a stabilizer state to prevent overflow during computation, ensuring that the exponential function remains numerically robust even when the input logits are large.

## A New Era for Recurrence

By swapping sigmoid for exponential activation and adding proper normalization, xLSTM gives recurrent networks the ability to be decisive. They can now aggressively update their internal state when new, contradictory information arrives.

This fixes a core deficiency that held LSTMs back, allowing xLSTM to outperform state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models on various benchmarks. It turns out that the key to better memory wasn't just adding more parameters—it was giving the network the mathematical freedom to forget the past when it needs to.



# Faster, Leaner, Smarter: Why xLSTM Could Be the Key to AI on the Edge

For the past few years, the Transformer architecture has been the undisputed king of deep learning. From Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 to state-of-the-art computer vision systems, the self-attention mechanism has driven incredible breakthroughs. However, this prowess comes with a hefty price tag: quadratic computational complexity. As sequence lengths increase, the memory and compute costs balloon, making Transformers difficult to deploy in resource-constrained environments.

Enter **xLSTM** (Extended Long Short-Term Memory). A recent paper introduces this new architecture, arguing that we shouldn't count LSTMs out just yet. By modernizing the classic architecture with exponential gating and matrix memories, xLSTM offers a compelling alternative that performs on par with or better than Transformers—but with a fraction of the resource footprint.

## The Transformer's Achilles' Heel

To understand why xLSTM matters, we first need to look at why Transformers struggle at the edge. The core issue is the self-attention mechanism, which compares every token in a sequence to every other token. This results in a computational complexity of $O(N^2)$ with respect to the sequence length $N$.

Furthermore, Transformers typically require a Key-Value (KV) cache that grows linearly with the sequence length during inference. For industrial applications or edge devices with limited VRAM, this is a significant bottleneck. You either have to truncate your context or invest in expensive, power-hungry hardware.

## Linear Scaling and Constant Memory

The primary advantage of xLSTM is its efficiency profile. According to the research, xLSTM networks boast **linear computation complexity** and **constant memory complexity** regarding the sequence length.

Unlike a Transformer, which must store the history of all past inputs in a growing cache, xLSTM compresses the history into a fixed-size state. This "compressive memory" means the memory requirements remain constant regardless of how long the input sequence is. The paper explicitly notes that this makes xLSTM "well suited for industrial applications and implementations on the edge."

## How It Works: sLSTM and mLSTM

The authors achieved this by addressing the historic limitations of traditional LSTMs—specifically their limited storage capacity and inability to revise storage decisions. They introduced two new variants:

*   **sLSTM (Scalar LSTM):** This variant uses exponential gating to allow the model to revise its storage decisions more effectively. It maintains memory mixing (connections between hidden states), which is crucial for solving complex state-tracking problems, though this requires a specialized fast CUDA kernel for speed.
*   **mLSTM (Matrix LSTM):** This variant trades scalar memory for a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. It uses a covariance update rule to store key-value pairs. Crucially, mLSTM eliminates hidden-hidden connections, making it fully parallelizable on GPUs.

By stacking these blocks into residual architectures, xLSTM combines the best of both worlds: the expressivity of modern architectures and the efficiency of recurrent neural networks.

## Inference Speed and Throughput

The practical benefits of xLSTM's architecture are visible in the benchmarks. The authors compared xLSTM against Llama (Transformer), Mamba (State Space Model), and RWKV-4 (RNN) on generation times and throughput.

The results highlight a stark difference in scaling. While Transformer generation time scales quadratically with sequence length, xLSTM scales linearly. This allows xLSTM to maintain low latency even for very long contexts.

Furthermore, because xLSTM uses constant memory, it can handle much higher batch sizes than Transformers. In throughput tests, xLSTM achieved significantly higher throughput than Llama models, which were limited by their ever-growing KV cache. For real-world deployment—where serving multiple requests simultaneously is key—this efficiency translates directly into lower costs and higher availability.

## No Compromise on Performance

Efficiency usually implies a trade-off in model quality, but the paper suggests otherwise. When scaled to billions of parameters and trained on 300B tokens from the SlimPajama dataset, xLSTM models outperformed Transformers (Llama) and State Space Models (Mamba) on validation perplexity and most downstream tasks.

Notably, xLSTM excels at "state tracking" problems—tasks like evaluating code or tracking entities in a long narrative. The paper points out that models without memory mixing (like standard SSMs and Transformers) struggle with tasks requiring strict state tracking, such as the parity task. Thanks to the sLSTM component, xLSTM retains the ability to handle these complex logic flows that often trip up other efficient architectures.

## The Future of Edge AI?

While Transformers have dominated the headlines, xLSTM serves as a reminder that recurrent architectures have untapped potential. By modernizing the LSTM with exponential gating and matrix memories, the authors have created a model that is leaner and faster without sacrificing intelligence.

For the industry, this is a significant development. As AI moves from the cloud to the "edge"—running on phones, embedded systems, and local servers—architectures with constant memory complexity will become increasingly vital. xLSTM offers a promising path forward: a model that is smart enough for complex language tasks but lean enough to run where Transformers cannot.



# Scaling Laws Showdown: What Happens When LSTMs Grow to Billions of Parameters?

For years, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture was the undisputed king of sequence modeling. However, the rise of Transformers—powered by self-attention and massive parallelization—pushed LSTMs to the sidelines. Transformers scaled well; LSTMs, with their limitations in storage capacity and inability to revise storage decisions, did not.

But a recent paper titled "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory" asks a compelling question: what if we fix the limitations of LSTMs and scale them to the size of modern Large Language Models (LLMs)? The paper introduces **xLSTM**, an architecture that extends the classic LSTM with exponential gating and matrix memory. The results from their scaling experiments suggest that the era of LSTM dominance might not be entirely over.

## The Experimental Setup

To evaluate xLSTM's potential, the authors conducted rigorous experiments comparing it against state-of-the-art architectures. They trained models on two distinct scales:
1.  **15B Tokens:** A standard comparison using 350M parameter models.
2.  **300B Tokens:** A large-scale experiment using model sizes ranging from 125M to 1.3B parameters.

The comparisons pitted xLSTM against heavy hitters like Llama (Transformer), Mamba (State Space Model), RWKV, and others. The primary metric for comparison was validation perplexity, which measures how well a probability model predicts a sample (lower is better).

## The 15B Token Baseline

The initial experiments on 15B tokens served as a comprehensive baseline. The authors compared xLSTM against various architecture classes, including Transformers (GPT-3, Llama), State Space Models (H3, Mamba), and Recurrent Neural Networks (RWKV, HGRN).

The results were clear. xLSTM outperformed all existing methods in validation perplexity. This wasn't just a marginal improvement; it demonstrated that the architectural changes—specifically the exponential gating and the new memory structures—were effective. Ablation studies confirmed that these new components were critical, attributing the performance gains to both the enhanced gating mechanisms and the increased storage capacity of the matrix memory.

## The Scaling Laws Showdown

The most exciting results came from the large-scale experiments on 300B tokens. This is where the "scaling laws" come into play. The authors assessed the power-law scaling behavior of xLSTM against Llama, Mamba, and RWKV-4 across model sizes of 125M, 350M, 760M, and 1.3B parameters.

### Better Offsets, Better Performance
Scaling laws typically dictate that model performance improves predictably as parameters increase. The experiments revealed that while all models follow similar scaling slopes, xLSTM exhibits a significantly better offset.

In terms of validation perplexity, xLSTM variants (specifically xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1]) consistently achieved the lowest scores across all model sizes. The performance hierarchy was distinct: RWKV-4 performed worst, followed by Llama and Mamba. xLSTM sat comfortably at the top, outperforming Mamba by a margin similar to how Mamba outperforms Llama.

### Downstream Task Performance
The paper also evaluated performance on downstream tasks, specifically common sense reasoning benchmarks. Here, xLSTM continued to shine. In the vast majority of tasks and across all model sizes, xLSTM was the best method. Mamba occasionally outperformed xLSTM on specific tasks like the ARC Challenge, but xLSTM maintained a dominant overall lead.

Furthermore, when tested on the PALOMA language benchmark (covering 571 text domains), xLSTM demonstrated superior generalization. xLSTM[1:0] achieved lower perplexity than Mamba in 99.5% of the text domains and outperformed Llama in 85.1% of them.

## Beyond Perplexity: Context and Speed

Scaling isn't just about loss curves; it's about how the model handles real-world constraints like context length and inference speed.

### Extrapolating to Long Contexts
Transformers often struggle to extrapolate beyond the sequence length they were trained on. The authors tested this by training models on a context length of 2048 and evaluating them on contexts up to 16384. While other models saw their perplexity spike, xLSTM maintained low perplexities, suggesting it handles long-range dependencies effectively without the quadratic complexity of full attention.

### Inference Efficiency
Because xLSTM has linear computational complexity with respect to sequence length (unlike the quadratic complexity of standard Transformers), it offers distinct advantages at inference time. The experiments showed that xLSTM scales linearly in generation time, similar to Mamba and RWKV. More importantly, due to its constant memory footprint, xLSTM can handle much higher batch sizes than Transformer models like Llama, resulting in significantly higher throughput.

## Conclusion

So, how far do LSTMs get when scaled to billions of parameters? The paper's conclusion is optimistic: "At least as far as current technologies like Transformers or State Space Models."

The scaling laws indicate that xLSTM does not just match current state-of-the-art models; it offers a favorable offset that suggests it could outperform them as model sizes continue to grow. By addressing the fundamental limitations of the original LSTM—through exponential gating and matrix memory—the authors have successfully revitalized a classic architecture for the modern era of large-scale modeling.
