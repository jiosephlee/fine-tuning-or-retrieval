# Introduction: The Resurgence of LSTMs

# The Historical Impact of Long Short-Term Memory in Deep Learning

The Long Short-Term Memory architecture, introduced by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber in the 1990s, represents one of the most influential innovations in the history of deep learning. At its core, LSTM addressed a fundamental problem that plagued recurrent neural networks: the vanishing gradient problem. This problem occurs when gradients either shrink exponentially during backpropagation through time, making it impossible for the network to learn long-range dependencies, or grow uncontrollably, leading to unstable training. The LSTM solution was elegant in its simplicity yet profound in its implications, introducing two key concepts that would define the architecture: the constant error carousel and gating mechanisms.

The constant error carousel is the foundational idea that enables LSTMs to maintain information over extended time periods. The mathematical formulation captures this elegantly:

$$c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t, \quad h_t = o_t \, \psi(c_t)$$

Here, the cell state $c_t$ is updated additively rather than multiplicatively, which prevents gradients from vanishing during backpropagation. The input gate $i_t$ controls how much new information flows into the cell, the forget gate $f_t$ determines how much of the previous cell state is retained, and the output gate $o_t$ governs what information is output as the hidden state $h_t$. The function $\psi$ normalizes or squashes the cell state, keeping values bounded.

The impact of LSTMs on practical applications cannot be overstated. In the realm of text generation, LSTMs enabled machines to produce coherent, contextually appropriate prose, moving beyond simple n-gram models to capture genuine linguistic structure. Handwriting generation systems built on LSTMs could mimic human writing styles with remarkable fidelity. Perhaps most notably, LSTMs formed the backbone of the first Large Language Models, demonstrating that neural networks could learn rich representations of language when provided with sufficient data and computational resources.

The success of LSTMs extended far beyond natural language processing. In sequence-to-sequence translation, LSTMs enabled the encoding of source language sentences into fixed-dimensional vectors that could then be decoded into target languages. Computer program evaluation became possible through LSTMs' ability to track program state and execution flow. Image captioning systems used LSTMs to generate natural language descriptions of visual content, bridging the modalities of vision and language.

Scientific and engineering applications benefited enormously from LSTM capabilities. Rainfall-runoff modeling and hydrological models for flooding warnings demonstrated that LSTMs could capture complex physical relationships in environmental systems. In reinforcement learning, LSTMs achieved state-of-the-art performance in complex domains: the AlphaStar model for StarCraft II, the OpenAI Five model for Dota 2, and controllers for magnetic confinement in nuclear fusion all relied on LSTM architectures.

A particularly compelling aspect of LSTM success is their ability to learn meaningful abstractions. Research showed that LSTMs adeptly extract semantic information and store it in their memory cells. Evidence for this emerged through various studies identifying specialized neurons within trained LSTM networks: number and syntax neurons that tracked numerical and grammatical properties, linguistic neurons that encoded specific linguistic features, and sentiment neurons that captured emotional content. These findings suggested that LSTMs weren't merely memorizing patterns but were developing interpretable representations of language structure.

# The Shift Towards Transformers and Self-Attention Mechanisms

Despite the remarkable successes of LSTMs across diverse applications, a fundamental shift occurred in 2017 with the introduction of the Transformer architecture. The Transformer, proposed by Vaswani and colleagues, introduced a radically different approach to sequence modeling based on parallelizable self-attention mechanisms. This innovation marked what the xLSTM paper describes as "the dawn of a new era" in sequence modeling and natural language processing.

The key advantage of Transformers over LSTMs lies in their ability to process sequences in parallel rather than sequentially. In a standard LSTM, processing must proceed token by token through the sequence, as each hidden state depends on the previous one. This sequential dependency creates a computational bottleneck, particularly on modern hardware like GPUs that excel at parallel operations. Transformers, by contrast, can attend to all positions in a sequence simultaneously through their self-attention mechanism, enabling dramatic speedups in training and inference.

The self-attention mechanism computes relationships between all pairs of positions in a sequence, allowing the model to directly capture long-range dependencies without the need to propagate information through intermediate time steps. While LSTM gradients must travel through many computational steps to connect distant positions, Transformers can make these connections in a single operation. This direct connectivity, combined with parallel computation, enabled Transformers to scale to unprecedented model sizes.

The scalability of Transformers led directly to the modern era of Large Language Models. GPT-3, ChatGPT, GPT-4, and numerous other models demonstrated that increasing model size, training data, and computational resources yielded predictable improvements in language modeling capability. These models exhibited emergent abilities—capabilities that appeared suddenly at certain scales rather than improving gradually. The Transformer architecture's ability to efficiently utilize massive computational resources made these advances possible.

The success of Transformers established a new paradigm: rather than carefully designing architectures to incorporate inductive biases about sequential data, the field increasingly relied on the self-attention mechanism's flexibility combined with massive scale. This approach proved remarkably effective, and for several years, it appeared that LSTMs and other recurrent architectures had been rendered obsolete for large-scale language modeling applications.

# Identifying the Three Main Limitations of Standard LSTMs

The xLSTM paper provides a systematic analysis of why LSTMs fell behind Transformers, identifying three fundamental limitations that constrained their performance and scalability. Understanding these limitations is essential for appreciating the innovations introduced in xLSTM and the potential for recurrent architectures to compete with Transformers.

The first limitation is the inability to revise storage decisions. Once an LSTM commits to storing information in its cell state, reversing or modifying that decision proves difficult. The paper illustrates this through a Nearest Neighbor Search problem: given a reference vector, the model must scan a sequence to find the most similar vector and output its associated value at the sequence's end. When a more similar vector is discovered partway through the sequence, the LSTM struggles to revise its stored value. This limitation stems from how the forget gate operates—once information is decayed or overwritten, it cannot be recovered, and the sigmoid-based gating provides limited flexibility for abrupt changes in storage strategy.

The second limitation concerns limited storage capacities. Standard LSTMs compress all relevant information into scalar cell states. Even when multiple memory cells are combined into a vector, each cell represents a single scalar value that must encode whatever information is relevant for future predictions. This constraint becomes particularly problematic for tasks requiring memorization of many distinct pieces of information. The paper demonstrates this through Rare Token Prediction experiments on Wikitext-103, showing that LSTMs perform significantly worse on tokens that appear infrequently in the training data. These rare tokens require dedicated storage capacity that scalar memories cannot efficiently provide.

The third limitation is the lack of parallelizability due to memory mixing. Memory mixing refers to the hidden-hidden connections that link hidden states from one time step to the next. These recurrent connections require sequential processing: the hidden state at time $t$ cannot be computed until the hidden state at time $t-1$ is available. This sequential dependency is fundamental to how LSTMs process information, but it creates a substantial computational bottleneck on hardware designed for parallel execution. While Transformers process entire sequences simultaneously, LSTMs must process tokens one by one, limiting their computational efficiency at scale.

These three limitations—inability to revise storage decisions, limited storage capacity, and lack of parallelizability—created a performance gap between LSTMs and Transformers that widened as models scaled to larger sizes and more demanding applications. The question became whether these limitations were inherent to the recurrent paradigm or whether they could be addressed through architectural innovations.

# The Research Goal: Scaling LSTMs to Billions of Parameters

Against this backdrop of LSTM limitations and Transformer dominance, the xLSTM paper poses a central research question: "How far do we get in language modeling when scaling LSTMs to billions of parameters, leveraging the latest techniques from modern LLMs, but mitigating known limitations of LSTMs?"

This question reframes the relationship between recurrent architectures and modern language modeling. Rather than accepting that Transformers have superseded LSTMs, the xLSTM authors ask whether the limitations of LSTMs can be systematically addressed to unlock competitive performance at scale. The approach is methodical: identify the specific mechanisms limiting LSTM performance, design targeted modifications to overcome each limitation, and evaluate whether these modifications enable LSTMs to compete with state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models.

The research agenda involves two primary innovations. First, the introduction of exponential gating with appropriate normalization and stabilization techniques addresses the limitation of revising storage decisions. Exponential gates provide different dynamics than sigmoid gates, enabling more flexible control over information storage and retrieval. Second, modifications to the LSTM memory structure yield two new variants: sLSTM with a scalar memory and scalar update but new memory mixing capabilities, and mLSTM with a matrix memory and a covariance update rule that enables full parallelization.

The matrix memory in mLSTM directly addresses the storage capacity limitation. Rather than storing information in scalar cell states, mLSTM maintains a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that can store key-value pairs through a covariance update rule. This dramatically increases the model's capacity to memorize and retrieve information, addressing the rare token prediction problem that challenged standard LSTMs.

The parallelization of mLSTM addresses the efficiency limitation. By removing memory mixing—hidden-hidden recurrent connections—mLSTM can be reformulated in a parallel version similar to techniques used in FlashAttention or other efficient attention implementations. This enables mLSTM to leverage GPU parallelism in ways that standard LSTMs cannot.

The integration of these LSTM extensions into residual block backbones yields xLSTM blocks, which are then residually stacked into complete xLSTM architectures. This architectural framework follows the design principles of modern deep learning, using pre-LayerNorm residual connections as employed in contemporary Large Language Models.

The ultimate goal extends beyond matching Transformer performance. The xLSTM approach aims to demonstrate that recurrent architectures, when properly designed and scaled, can offer distinct advantages over attention-based models. These advantages include linear computational complexity and constant memory requirements with respect to sequence length—properties that make xLSTM particularly well-suited for industrial applications and edge deployment where computational resources are constrained.

# Foundations: The Original LSTM Architecture

The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture represents one of the most influential developments in the history of deep learning. Introduced in the 1990s, LSTMs addressed fundamental limitations that had plagued recurrent neural networks, particularly the vanishing gradient problem. Understanding the original LSTM architecture provides essential background for appreciating the extensions and modifications introduced in modern variants. This chapter provides a comprehensive review of the standard LSTM architecture, establishing the baseline knowledge required for understanding subsequent developments in sequence modeling.

## The Constant Error Carousel and Gating Mechanisms

The central innovation of the LSTM lies in two key ideas: the constant error carousel and gating. These mechanisms work in concert to enable the network to learn long-range dependencies in sequential data, overcoming the fundamental limitations of earlier recurrent architectures.

The constant error carousel refers to the additive update structure of the cell state. In traditional recurrent neural networks, information is transformed through repeated applications of non-linear functions, causing gradients to either vanish or explode during backpropagation through time. The LSTM circumvents this issue through a clever architectural choice: the cell state is updated additively rather than being directly transformed. The equation governing this update is:

$$c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$$

This deceptively simple equation encapsulates the essence of the constant error carousel. The cell state $c_{t-1}$ from the previous time step is preserved and scaled by the forget gate $f_t$, rather than being passed through a non-linear transformation. The new information $z_t$ is added to this preserved state, moderated by the input gate $i_t$. This additive structure ensures that the gradient can flow backward through time without being repeatedly multiplied by potentially small derivatives, which is the root cause of vanishing gradients.

The gating mechanism provides the network with fine-grained control over information flow. Gates act as learnable filters that determine what information should be stored, forgotten, or output at each time step. The use of sigmoid activation functions for gates ensures that their outputs are bounded between zero and one, allowing them to act as multiplicative filters. A gate value close to zero effectively blocks information flow, while a value close to one allows information to pass through largely unimpeded.

The beauty of the gating mechanism lies in its adaptability. The gates are not fixed thresholds but rather learnable parameters that are computed based on the current input and the previous hidden state. This means the network learns over time what information is relevant to keep and what can be discarded, adapting its behavior to the specific requirements of the task at hand. The forget gate, for instance, learns to identify when previously stored information is no longer relevant and should be purged from memory.

## Mathematical Formulation of the Standard LSTM Forward Pass

A precise understanding of the LSTM requires examining the complete mathematical formulation of its forward pass. The LSTM maintains a cell state $c_t$ and a hidden state $h_t$ at each time step, which together form the memory of the network. The cell state serves as the internal memory that can maintain information over long time periods, while the hidden state represents the output that is exposed to other layers or used for predictions.

The cell state update follows the equation:

$$c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$$

Here, $f_t$ represents the forget gate, $i_t$ the input gate, and $z_t$ the cell input. The forget gate determines how much of the previous cell state should be retained, while the input gate controls how much new information should be added. The cell input $z_t$ is the candidate information to be stored, computed as:

$$z_t = \varphi(\tilde{z}_t)$$

where $\varphi$ is typically the $\tanh$ activation function, and $\tilde{z}_t$ is the pre-activation:

$$\tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{z} \mathbf{x}_t + r_{z} h_{t-1} + b_{z}$$

The weight vector $\mathbf{w}_{z}$ connects the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ to the cell input, while $r_{z}$ is the recurrent weight connecting the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ to the cell input. The bias term $b_{z}$ provides an additional learnable offset.

The hidden state is derived from the cell state through:

$$h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t$$

where $o_t$ is the output gate and $\tilde{h}_t$ is the normalized cell state:

$$\tilde{h}_t = \psi(c_t)$$

The function $\psi$ serves to squash or normalize the cell state, which would otherwise be unbounded. This is typically implemented as a $\tanh$ function, bounding the normalized state between negative one and positive one. The output gate $o_t$ then determines how much of this normalized state should be exposed as the hidden state output.

The three gates each follow a similar computational pattern. The input gate is computed as:

$$i_t = \sigma(\tilde{i}_t)$$

with pre-activation:

$$\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{i} \mathbf{x}_t + r_{i} h_{t-1} + b_{i}$$

Similarly, the forget gate follows:

$$f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$$

$$\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{f} \mathbf{x}_t + r_{f} h_{t-1} + b_{f}$$

And the output gate is given by:

$$o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t)$$

$$\tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{o} \mathbf{x}_t + r_{o} h_{t-1} + b_{o}$$

In all three cases, $\sigma$ denotes the sigmoid function:

$$\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-x)}$$

This function smoothly maps its input to the range $(0, 1)$, making it ideal for gating purposes. Each gate receives input from both the current external input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$, allowing the network to make context-dependent decisions about information flow.

## The Role of the Input, Forget, and Output Gates

Each gate in the LSTM architecture serves a distinct and crucial purpose. Understanding the role of each gate provides insight into how the LSTM manages its internal memory and controls information flow.

The input gate $i_t$ controls the extent to which new information flows into the cell state. When the input gate is close to zero, the cell blocks new information from being stored, preserving its existing content. When the input gate is close to one, the network readily accepts new information. This mechanism allows the LSTM to be selective about what it learns, focusing on inputs that are relevant to the task while ignoring irrelevant noise. The input gate essentially answers the question: "Is the current input worth remembering?"

The forget gate $f_t$, introduced after the original LSTM formulation, provides the complementary ability to remove information from the cell state. By scaling the previous cell state, the forget gate determines how much of the existing memory should be retained. A forget gate value close to one preserves the existing memory largely intact, while a value close to zero effectively erases the cell state, making room for entirely new information. This is particularly important for tasks where the network must learn to forget information that is no longer relevant, such as when processing a new sentence in a paragraph or a new sequence element. The forget gate answers the question: "Should I keep what I previously remembered?"

The output gate $o_t$ controls the extent to which the cell state influences the hidden state and, consequently, the network's output. Even if valuable information is stored in the cell state, it only affects the network's behavior if the output gate allows it to pass through. This provides an additional layer of control, enabling the network to maintain internal state that may be useful later but should not influence current predictions. The output gate essentially decides: "Should I reveal my memory right now?"

The interaction between these gates creates a sophisticated memory management system. Consider processing a long document: the input gate might open to store important names or concepts, the forget gate might close to preserve this information across many time steps, and the output gate might selectively expose relevant memories when answering questions about the document. This coordinated gating enables the LSTM to maintain and manipulate information over extended sequences.

## Understanding Memory Mixing via Hidden-Hidden Recurrent Connections

A fundamental aspect of the LSTM architecture is the concept of memory mixing, which occurs through the hidden-hidden recurrent connections. In the basic formulation presented above, each gate and the cell input receive input from the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ through recurrent weights. This creates pathways through which information from one memory cell can influence another, enabling complex interactions within the network's memory.

When multiple memory cells are combined in a vector $\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, the architecture naturally extends to use recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ to mix the cell outputs of different memory cells. This means that each memory cell's gates can be influenced not only by its own previous state but also by the hidden states of other cells. This cross-cell communication enriches the representational capacity of the network, allowing it to develop specialized but interconnected memory units.

The memory mixing mechanism serves several important functions. First, it enables the network to develop distributed representations, where information is stored across multiple cells rather than being confined to individual units. Second, it allows for complex state transitions, where the activation patterns across cells can capture sophisticated patterns in the input sequence. Third, it facilitates the learning of hierarchical representations, where higher-level cells can build upon information stored in lower-level cells.

However, this memory mixing comes with a computational cost: it enforces sequential processing. Because each time step's computation depends on the hidden state from the previous time step, the network cannot process multiple time steps in parallel. This inherent sequentiality, while beneficial for modeling temporal dependencies, creates a limitation in terms of computational efficiency, particularly on modern hardware that excels at parallel computation. This trade-off between representational power and computational efficiency has motivated various extensions and modifications to the original LSTM architecture.

## The Vanishing Gradient Problem and How LSTMs Address It

To fully appreciate the significance of the LSTM architecture, one must understand the vanishing gradient problem that plagued earlier recurrent neural networks and how the LSTM's design specifically addresses this issue.

The vanishing gradient problem arises during training when gradients are propagated backward through time. In traditional recurrent neural networks, the hidden state at time $t$ depends on the hidden state at time $t-1$ through a non-linear transformation. When computing gradients with respect to parameters at earlier time steps, the chain rule requires multiplying derivatives through each time step. If these derivatives are consistently less than one, their product shrinks exponentially as the number of time steps increases. This means that gradients from distant time steps become vanishingly small, making it extremely difficult for the network to learn long-range dependencies.

The LSTM addresses this problem through the constant error carousel. The key insight is that the cell state update $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$ is additive rather than fully transformative. When computing the derivative of the cell state at time $t$ with respect to the cell state at time $t-k$, the gradient flows through the forget gate multiplications rather than through repeated non-linear transformations. If the forget gate maintains values close to one, the gradient can flow backward through time with minimal attenuation.

This is fundamentally different from standard recurrent networks where the hidden state is transformed by a non-linear activation function at each step. Each transformation introduces a derivative that must be multiplied into the gradient chain, and these derivatives are often less than one, causing the gradient to vanish. The LSTM's additive structure, combined with the learned gating, allows the network to maintain gradient flow over arbitrarily long sequences when appropriate.

The gates play a crucial role in this process. During training, the network learns to set the forget gate to values that preserve important information, effectively creating paths where gradients can flow. The input gate controls when new information should be integrated, and the output gate determines when stored information should influence predictions. Together, these mechanisms enable the LSTM to learn which information is worth preserving and for how long, directly addressing the core challenge that motivated its design.

# The sLSTM: Exponential Gating and Memory Mixing

The sLSTM represents a significant advancement over the traditional LSTM architecture, introducing exponential gating as a mechanism to overcome one of the most fundamental limitations of recurrent neural networks: the inability to revise storage decisions effectively. This chapter provides a comprehensive examination of the sLSTM architecture, exploring how exponential gating transforms the dynamics of memory cells, the mathematical formulation that enables stable training, and the new paradigm of memory mixing that emerges from these modifications.

## Introduction to Exponential Gating for Input and Forget Gates

The traditional LSTM architecture employs sigmoid activation functions for its gating mechanisms, where the input gate $i_t$, forget gate $f_t$, and output gate $o_t$ are computed as $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+\exp(-x))$. This sigmoid activation constrains all gate values to the interval $(0, 1)$, which has important implications for how information flows through the network. When the forget gate takes a value close to zero, the cell state is largely erased, and when it approaches one, the previous cell state is preserved. Similarly, the input gate controls how much of the new information $z_t$ is written to the cell state.

However, this seemingly reasonable design choice introduces a critical limitation: the inability to revise storage decisions decisively. Consider the scenario where a network must store a value based on some criterion, such as finding the most similar vector in a nearest neighbor search problem. When a new, more similar vector is encountered, the network must be able to completely replace the previously stored value with the new one. With sigmoid gates, this revision process is gradual and often ineffective because the gates cannot cleanly separate the decision to forget the old value from the decision to store the new one.

The sLSTM addresses this limitation by introducing exponential activation functions for the input and forget gates. Instead of using the sigmoid function, the input gate in sLSTM is computed as $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$, where $\tilde{i}_t$ is the pre-activation value. The forget gate can optionally use either the sigmoid activation $f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ or the exponential activation $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$. This exponential transformation fundamentally changes the behavior of the gating mechanism.

The key insight behind exponential gating is that it allows the network to make sharp, decisive storage decisions. When the input gate's pre-activation $\tilde{i}_t$ is large, the exponential function produces an exponentially large gate value, which can completely dominate the cell state update. This is in stark contrast to the sigmoid function, which saturates at one and can never exceed that bound. With exponential gating, a strong input signal can effectively overwrite any existing content in the cell state, enabling the network to revise its storage decisions as new information becomes available.

The forget gate's role in this revision process is equally important. In the traditional LSTM, the forget gate's maximum value is one, meaning that the previous cell state can be preserved at best, but never amplified. With exponential activation, the forget gate can take values greater than one, which opens up new possibilities for how the network manages its memory. However, the forget gate can also be kept at sigmoid activation when a simpler behavior is desired, providing flexibility in the design of the sLSTM cell.

The effectiveness of exponential gating in solving the storage revision problem is demonstrated through the Nearest Neighbor Search problem, where the network must scan a sequence of vectors to find the one most similar to a reference vector and output its associated value at the end of the sequence. When a more similar vector is found, the sLSTM with exponential gating can effectively revise its stored value, while the traditional LSTM struggles with this revision, leading to higher mean squared error on this task.

## Mathematical Formulation of the sLSTM Forward Pass

The sLSTM forward pass builds upon the traditional LSTM formulation while introducing critical modifications to accommodate exponential gating and proper normalization. The equations governing the sLSTM represent a careful balance between maintaining the constant error carousel property that makes LSTMs effective and enabling the new capabilities that exponential gating provides.

The cell state update in sLSTM follows a similar additive structure to the original LSTM, but with crucial differences in how the gates operate. The cell state at time step $t$ is computed as:

$$c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$$

Here, $c_t$ represents the cell state, $f_t$ is the forget gate, $i_t$ is the input gate, and $z_t$ is the cell input. The cell input $z_t$ is computed by applying an activation function $\varphi$ (typically $\tanh$) to the pre-activation value:

$$z_t = \varphi(\tilde{z}_t)$$

where the pre-activation $\tilde{z}_t$ incorporates both the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$:

$$\tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z h_{t-1} + b_z$$

The weight vector $\mathbf{w}_z$ processes the input, $r_z$ is the recurrent weight connecting the previous hidden state to the cell input, and $b_z$ is the bias term. This formulation preserves the ability of the network to learn both from the current input and from its previous internal state.

The input gate computation in sLSTM employs the exponential activation function:

$$i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$$

where the pre-activation is computed as:

$$\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i h_{t-1} + b_i$$

The forget gate can use either sigmoid or exponential activation:

$$f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t) \text{ OR } \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$$

with the pre-activation:

$$\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f h_{t-1} + b_f$$

The output gate maintains the traditional sigmoid activation:

$$o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t)$$

where:

$$\tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_o h_{t-1} + b_o$$

The critical difference in the sLSTM formulation emerges in how the hidden state is computed. In the original LSTM, the cell state is passed through a squashing function $\psi$ (typically $\tanh$) to produce the hidden state candidate, which is then gated by the output gate: $h_t = o_t \psi(c_t)$. This squashing function is necessary because the cell state can grow unboundedly during training, and the $\tanh$ function ensures that the hidden state remains bounded.

However, in sLSTM, the exponential gates can produce very large values, making the simple squashing approach inadequate. Instead, sLSTM introduces a normalization scheme that properly accounts for the scale of the exponential gates. The hidden state is computed as:

$$h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t$$

where the normalized hidden state candidate $\tilde{h}_t$ is given by:

$$\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$$

This division by a normalizer state $n_t$ is the key innovation that allows sLSTM to handle exponential gates while maintaining stable gradients during backpropagation.

## The Role of the Normalizer State in Handling Exponential Gate Outputs

The introduction of exponential gates necessitates a corresponding mechanism to properly normalize the cell state before it is used to produce the hidden state. Without such normalization, the exponentially large gate values could cause the cell state to grow uncontrollably, leading to numerical instability and degraded learning. The normalizer state $n_t$ provides exactly this normalization mechanism.

The normalizer state is computed recursively in a manner analogous to the cell state, but it tracks the cumulative effect of the gates rather than the gated content. Specifically, the normalizer state update is:

$$n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$$

This equation reveals the intuition behind the normalizer: it accumulates the total "strength" of the gates over time, weighted by the forget gate at each step. When the forget gate is small (indicating that previous information should be forgotten), the previous normalizer value is scaled down, and the current input gate contribution is added. When the forget gate is large, more of the previous normalizer is retained.

To understand why this normalization is necessary, consider what happens when the input gate takes a very large value due to the exponential activation. The cell state update $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$ would cause the cell state to become very large, potentially dominating the cell's output for many subsequent time steps. Without proper normalization, this could lead to numerical overflow and would make it difficult for the network to learn effectively.

The normalized hidden state $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$ effectively computes a weighted average of the stored information. This can be seen by expanding the cell state recursively. Each stored value $z_\tau$ is weighted by the product of the input gate at time $\tau$ and all subsequent forget gates from time $\tau+1$ to time $t$. Similarly, the normalizer accumulates these same gate products. The division therefore produces a normalized value that represents the weighted average of all stored inputs, with weights determined by the gate dynamics.

This normalization scheme has an important property: it maintains the ability of the network to store and retrieve information over long time horizons while preventing numerical instability. The cell state can still retain information from many time steps ago if the forget gates remain high, but the hidden state will be properly scaled for use in downstream computations.

The normalizer state also plays a crucial role in gradient flow during backpropagation. In the original LSTM, the constant error carousel ensures that gradients can flow through the cell state without vanishing, provided the forget gates remain close to one. In sLSTM, the normalizer provides a similar benefit for the hidden state computation, ensuring that the gradients remain properly scaled even when the exponential gates produce large values.

## Stabilization Techniques to Prevent Overflow in Exponential Gates

While the exponential activation function provides powerful capabilities for storage revision, it also introduces the risk of numerical overflow. When the pre-activation $\tilde{i}_t$ or $\tilde{f}_t$ becomes too large, the exponential function can produce values that exceed the floating-point representation, causing overflow and training instability. The sLSTM addresses this challenge through a sophisticated stabilization technique.

The stabilization approach employed by sLSTM is based on the observation that the output of the sLSTM cell is invariant to certain rescalings of the gate values. Specifically, if we scale both the input gate and forget gate by the same factor, and correspondingly scale the previous cell state and normalizer state, the final hidden state output remains unchanged. This property allows us to rescale the gate values dynamically to keep them within a manageable range.

The stabilization technique introduces a stabilizer state $m_t$ that tracks the maximum of the log-gate values over time. The stabilizer state is computed as:

$$m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$$

This equation can be understood as follows: the stabilizer state accumulates the log of the forget gate values (plus the previous stabilizer value) while tracking the maximum between this accumulated value and the log of the current input gate. When the input gate becomes very large, the stabilizer state increases to match it, and when the accumulated forget gate product becomes large, the stabilizer increases accordingly.

Using this stabilizer state, the stabilized input gate and forget gate are computed as:

$$i'_t = \exp(\log(i_t) - m_t) = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)$$

$$f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)$$

These stabilized gate values are then used in the forward pass instead of the original exponential gate values. The key insight is that subtracting $m_t$ from the exponent ensures that at least one of the gates (the one that contributed to the maximum) will have a value of one, while all other values will be at most one. This keeps the gate values bounded and prevents overflow.

The mathematical justification for this stabilization approach is provided in the appendix of the original paper, where it is shown that replacing $f_t$ by $f'_t$ and $i_t$ by $i'_t$ in the forward pass does not change the output of the network, nor does it change the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters. This means that the stabilization is a numerical trick that improves training stability without affecting the mathematical properties of the model.

To see why the output is unchanged, consider the cell state update with the stabilized gates. The cell state becomes:

$$c_t = f'_t c_{t-1} + i'_t z_t$$

Substituting the stabilized gate expressions and factoring out $\exp(-m_t)$:

$$c_t = \exp(-m_t) \left[ \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}) c_{t-1} + \exp(\log(i_t)) z_t \right]$$

The normalizer state update follows the same pattern, and when we compute the hidden state as $h_t = o_t (c_t / n_t)$, the scaling factor $\exp(-m_t)$ cancels out, leaving the output unchanged.

This stabilization technique is essential for practical training of sLSTM networks. Without it, the exponential gates would frequently overflow during training, especially in deep networks or when training on long sequences. The stabilization allows sLSTM to reap the benefits of exponential gating while maintaining numerical stability.

## The Concept of Multiple Heads and Memory Mixing Within the sLSTM

The sLSTM architecture extends beyond single memory cells to support multiple memory cells and multiple heads, creating rich possibilities for how information is processed and stored within the network. This extension builds upon the original LSTM's capability to have multiple memory cells but introduces new dynamics through exponential gating and the head-based organization.

In the original LSTM, multiple memory cells can be combined into a vector $\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$, allowing the use of recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ to mix the cell outputs of different memory cells. This memory mixing enables different memory cells to influence each other, creating more complex representations than would be possible with isolated cells. The sLSTM preserves and extends this capability.

Multiple memory cells in sLSTM enable memory mixing via recurrent connections $\mathbf{R}_z$, $\mathbf{R}_i$, $\mathbf{R}_f$, and $\mathbf{R}_o$ from the hidden state vector $\mathbf{h}$ to the memory cell input $\mathbf{z}$ and the gates $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{f}$, and $\mathbf{o}$ respectively. These recurrent connections allow each memory cell to be influenced not only by its own previous state but also by the states of other memory cells. This creates a form of communication between cells that can capture more complex patterns in the data.

The sLSTM introduces a new organizational structure through the concept of heads. The sLSTM can have multiple heads, with memory mixing occurring within each head but not across heads. This design choice creates a balance between the flexibility of allowing memory cells to interact and the efficiency of keeping some cells independent. Within each head, the memory cells share information through the recurrent connections, allowing them to cooperate in processing information. Between heads, the memory cells operate independently, allowing the network to maintain separate processing streams.

The introduction of heads for sLSTM, together with exponential gating, establishes a new way of memory mixing that differs qualitatively from the original LSTM. The exponential gates create dynamics where information can be more decisively stored and retrieved, and this influences how the memory cells within a head interact. When a strong input gate signal is received in one cell, it can create a cascade effect through the recurrent connections, influencing how other cells in the same head update their states.

This memory mixing capability is particularly important for tasks that require state tracking, such as evaluating computer programs or tracking entities in long narratives. The ability of memory cells within a head to influence each other allows the network to maintain more complex state representations than would be possible with isolated cells. The exponential gating further enhances this capability by allowing the network to make sharp transitions between states, which is essential for many sequential processing tasks.

It is worth noting that the memory mixing in sLSTM comes at a computational cost: because the hidden-hidden connections require sequential processing, the sLSTM cannot be fully parallelized like the mLSTM variant. However, the paper demonstrates through experimental results that this sequential nature is precisely what enables sLSTM to solve state tracking problems that parallelizable architectures cannot handle effectively. The memory mixing allows sLSTM to be more expressive than state space models and transformers for certain classes of problems, making it a valuable component in the xLSTM architecture even though it is not as computationally efficient as the mLSTM.

# The mLSTM: Matrix Memory and Parallelization

## The Limitation of Scalar Memory and the Motivation for Matrix Memory

The original Long Short-Term Memory architecture, despite its tremendous success across diverse applications, faces a fundamental constraint in its memory structure. The standard LSTM compresses information into scalar cell states, where each memory cell stores a single scalar value $c \in \mathbb{R}$. This design creates a bottleneck in storage capacity that becomes particularly problematic when models must handle complex sequences with rich informational content. The limitation manifests clearly in tasks requiring the model to maintain information about rare tokens or to store multiple distinct pieces of information simultaneously.

Consider the Rare Token Prediction task as a concrete example of this limitation. When evaluating perplexity on Wikitext-103 across partitions of different token frequencies, traditional LSTMs exhibit significantly worse performance on rare tokens compared to frequent ones. This degradation occurs precisely because the scalar memory cells cannot adequately encode the diversity of information needed to make accurate predictions about infrequently occurring tokens. The model must compress an ever-growing amount of contextual information into a fixed number of scalar values, inevitably leading to information loss.

The mLSTM addresses this fundamental limitation by replacing the scalar memory cell with a matrix memory structure. Rather than storing information in a single scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$, the mLSTM maintains a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This change represents a quadratic increase in storage capacity, as a $d$-dimensional matrix contains $d^2$ elements compared to just $d$ scalar cells in an equivalent standard LSTM. With this expanded memory, the model can capture and retain substantially more information about the sequence history, enabling better performance on tasks requiring extensive memory capacity.

The matrix memory also introduces a fundamentally different approach to information retrieval. In the scalar LSTM, the hidden state is computed directly from the cell state through a squashing function and output gating. In contrast, the mLSTM retrieves information through matrix multiplication: given a query vector $\mathbf{q}$, the retrieved information is computed as $\mathbf{C}\mathbf{q}$. This key-value-query structure, borrowed from Transformer terminology, allows for more sophisticated and selective information access patterns, where different queries can extract different aspects of the stored information from the same memory matrix.

## Introduction to the Covariance Update Rule and Bidirectional Associative Memories

The matrix memory structure of mLSTM draws inspiration from the theoretical framework of Bidirectional Associative Memories (BAMs), a class of neural network architectures developed in the 1970s by researchers including Kohonen, Anderson, and Nakano. Bidirectional Associative Memories were designed to store and retrieve associations between pairs of patterns, providing a mathematical foundation for understanding how information can be stored in matrix-structured memories.

In the mLSTM framework, the storage problem is formulated as follows: at time $t$, the model receives a key vector $\mathbf{k}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and a value vector $\mathbf{v}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ that should be stored together. Later, at time $t + \tau$, a query vector $\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ should be able to retrieve the stored value $\mathbf{v}_t$ that corresponds to the most similar key. This key-value-query paradigm mirrors the attention mechanism in Transformers and provides a principled approach to memory storage and retrieval.

The covariance update rule provides the mathematical mechanism for storing key-value pairs in the matrix memory. The rule specifies that when storing a new key-value pair, the memory matrix is updated as:

$$\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_{t} \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

This update rule adds the outer product of the value and key vectors to the existing memory matrix. The outer product $\mathbf{v}_{t} \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ creates a rank-one matrix that encodes the association between the key and value. When a layer normalization is applied before projecting inputs to keys and values, these vectors have zero mean, making this a true covariance update.

The covariance update rule has strong theoretical foundations. Research by Sejnowski and Dayan established that this update rule is optimal for achieving maximal separability of retrieved binary vectors, which is equivalent to maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio in the retrieved information. This optimality property ensures that the mLSTM memory structure stores information in a way that facilitates accurate retrieval. While higher separability is possible when limiting retrieval to pairwise interactions with quadratic complexity—as in standard attention mechanisms—the covariance update rule provides a balanced trade-off between storage capacity and computational efficiency.

The connection between the covariance update rule and Fast Weight Programmers further illuminates the theoretical heritage of mLSTM. Fast Weight Programmers, introduced by Schmidhuber in 1992, used similar outer product updates to rapidly modify network weights. Later work by Ba and others extended this approach by incorporating constant decay rates and learning rates. The mLSTM integrates these ideas into the LSTM framework by using the forget gate as a decay rate and the input gate as a learning rate, while the output gate scales the retrieved vector.

## Mathematical Formulation of the mLSTM Forward Pass

The mLSTM forward pass synthesizes the matrix memory structure with the gating mechanisms that made the original LSTM successful. The complete forward pass equations integrate the covariance update rule with exponential gating and normalization to create a powerful and stable memory system.

The cell state update extends the covariance update rule with gated control:

$$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

The forget gate $f_t$ controls how much of the previous memory matrix is retained, analogous to exponential decay in continuous systems. The input gate $i_t$ controls how strongly the new key-value association is written to memory. This gated formulation allows the network to selectively forget outdated information and selectively store new information, capabilities that are crucial for processing long sequences where relevance changes over time.

The key, value, and query vectors are computed from the input through learned projections:

$$\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q$$
$$\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k$$
$$\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v$$

The scaling factor $1/\sqrt{d}$ applied to the key projection ensures proper normalization of the dot products used in retrieval, preventing numerical instabilities that could arise from high-dimensional vectors. Each of these projections transforms the input into a different role: the key determines where information is stored, the value determines what information is stored, and the query determines how information is retrieved.

The input and forget gates in mLSTM employ exponential activation functions to enable the revision of storage decisions:

$$i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \quad \tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{i} \mathbf{x}_t + b_{i}$$
$$f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t) \text{ OR } \exp(\tilde{f}_t), \quad \tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{f} \mathbf{x}_t + b_{f}$$

The exponential activation allows gates to reach values much larger than one, enabling the network to strongly overwrite previous memory contents when necessary. This is crucial for tasks like the Nearest Neighbor Search problem, where the model must be able to replace a stored value when a more similar vector is found later in the sequence. The standard LSTM with sigmoid gates struggles with this revision task because sigmoid outputs are bounded between zero and one, limiting the network's ability to decisively update stored information.

The output gate is a vector rather than a scalar, allowing different dimensions of the hidden state to be gated independently:

$$\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t), \quad \tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t = \mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{o}} \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_{\mathbf{o}}$$

This vector-valued output gate provides finer control over which aspects of the retrieved information are passed to the next layer.

## Normalizer State Implementation for Matrix Memory

A critical innovation in the mLSTM is the introduction of a normalizer state that ensures stable and well-calibrated outputs from the matrix memory. The normalizer state addresses a fundamental challenge in retrieval from matrix memories: the retrieved values must be properly scaled to prevent numerical instabilities and ensure meaningful comparisons across different queries.

The normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$ tracks the weighted sum of key vectors, accumulating the keys that have been written to memory:

$$\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t$$

This parallel structure to the cell state update ensures that the normalizer state reflects the same gating decisions that control the memory matrix. Each key is weighted by the input gate at its time of writing and subsequently decayed by all future forget gates, exactly matching how the corresponding value-key outer product is weighted in the cell state.

The hidden state computation uses the normalizer state to properly scale the retrieved information:

$$\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t, \quad \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}}$$

The numerator $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ performs the actual retrieval by querying the memory matrix. The denominator normalizes this retrieval by the inner product between the query and the normalizer state. This normalization accounts for the varying strengths of the gates across time steps, ensuring that retrieved values are properly calibrated regardless of how strongly information was written to memory.

The denominator includes two important safeguards. First, the absolute value $|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|$ ensures that the normalization is always positive, preventing sign flips that could occur if the dot product were negative. Second, the maximum with a threshold value (typically 1.0) prevents division by values close to zero, which could cause numerical instabilities or unbounded outputs. This thresholding technique has been used in related work such as Retention and provides robust behavior even when the query is nearly orthogonal to the accumulated normalizer state.

The intuition behind this normalization is that it computes a weighted average of stored values, where the weights are determined by the similarity between the query and each stored key. The normalizer state effectively tracks the total weight, allowing the retrieval to produce values that are comparable across different queries and different points in the sequence. Without normalization, the magnitude of retrieved values could vary dramatically depending on the history of gate values, making it difficult for subsequent layers to interpret the retrieved information consistently.

## Enabling Parallelization: The Removal of Hidden-Hidden Connections

One of the most significant design decisions in mLSTM is the removal of hidden-hidden connections, also known as memory mixing. This change fundamentally alters the computational properties of the model and enables efficient parallelization, addressing one of the key limitations that prevented traditional LSTMs from scaling to the sizes of modern Large Language Models.

In the original LSTM and in the sLSTM, the hidden state from the previous time step $h_{t-1}$ directly influences the computation of the current time step through recurrent weight connections. The cell input, input gate, forget gate, and output gate all receive the previous hidden state as input:

$$\tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{z} \mathbf{x}_t + r_{z} h_{t-1} + b_{z}$$

This recurrent dependency creates a sequential bottleneck: the computation for time step $t$ cannot begin until the computation for time step $t-1$ has completed. For sequences of length $L$, this necessitates $L$ sequential operations, which becomes prohibitively slow for long sequences and large models. Transformers overcome this limitation by using self-attention, which can be computed in parallel across all positions, but this comes at the cost of quadratic memory complexity.

The mLSTM achieves the best of both worlds by removing the hidden-hidden connections entirely. Notice that in the mLSTM equations, the gates and projections depend only on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and learned parameters, with no dependence on previous hidden states:

$$\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{i} \mathbf{x}_t + b_{i}$$
$$\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{f} \mathbf{x}_t + b_{f}$$
$$\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q$$
$$\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k$$
$$\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v$$
$$\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t = \mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{o}} \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_{\mathbf{o}}$$

The only recurrent dependencies are in the cell state and normalizer state updates. Crucially, these updates follow a linear recurrence pattern that can be parallelized using established techniques. The recurrence:

$$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

can be reformulated using associative scan operations, similar to how FlashAttention parallelizes attention computation and how Mamba parallelizes state space model computations. This reformulation allows all time steps to be computed in $O(\log L)$ sequential steps rather than $O(L)$, providing dramatic speedups for long sequences.

The removal of memory mixing does come at a cost: without hidden-hidden connections, the model cannot perform certain types of state tracking that require the hidden state to be transformed between time steps. This is why the xLSTM architecture typically combines mLSTM blocks with sLSTM blocks—the sLSTM provides memory mixing capabilities for tasks requiring state tracking, while the mLSTM provides efficient parallelizable computation and large memory capacity. Empirically, this combination works well, with architectures like xLSTM[7:1] using predominantly mLSTM blocks supplemented by sLSTM blocks.

The trade-off between memory mixing and parallelization reflects a fundamental tension in recurrent neural network design. Memory mixing enables more expressive hidden state dynamics, allowing the model to perform complex state tracking operations that are essential for tasks like evaluating computer programs or tracking entities through long narratives. However, memory mixing enforces sequential processing, limiting training and inference speed. The mLSTM chooses to prioritize computational efficiency while still maintaining strong memory capabilities through its matrix structure, and the broader xLSTM architecture combines both approaches to achieve both expressiveness and efficiency.

# Constructing the xLSTM Architecture

Having introduced the two fundamental building blocks of the extended Long Short-Term Memory framework—the sLSTM with its scalar memory and exponential gating, and the mLSTM with its matrix memory and parallelizable structure—we now turn to the question of how these components are assembled into a complete neural network architecture. The transition from individual memory cells to a full-scale language model requires careful architectural design that draws upon decades of insights from deep learning research. This chapter explores the design principles behind xLSTM blocks, the theoretical motivation for their structure, and the practical considerations that govern their assembly into complete architectures.

# Design of xLSTM Blocks: Residual Block Backbones

The fundamental unit of the xLSTM architecture is the xLSTM block, which serves as a module that non-linearly summarizes the past in a high-dimensional space. This design choice is not arbitrary but stems from a fundamental requirement of sequence modeling: the ability to separate different histories or contexts. When processing a sequence, the model must be able to distinguish between different possible past contexts in order to correctly predict the next element, whether that be a token in language modeling or a value in time series prediction. The separation of histories is thus the prerequisite for accurate next-step prediction.

The xLSTM block architecture follows the residual block design pattern, which has become a cornerstone of modern deep learning. Residual blocks, introduced in the context of very deep convolutional networks, address the challenge of training extremely deep networks by introducing skip connections that allow gradients to flow more easily through the network during backpropagation. The xLSTM framework adopts this proven design pattern, integrating either the sLSTM or mLSTM variants into residual block modules to create what are termed xLSTM blocks.

The structure of these blocks reflects a balance between preserving the temporal processing capabilities of the LSTM variants and enabling efficient training through residual connections. Each xLSTM block operates on an input and produces an output that, combined with the input through a residual connection, forms the input to the next block. This design ensures that even as information passes through many layers of processing, the original signal is preserved, allowing the model to learn modifications to the input rather than complete transformations. The residual structure also facilitates the training of very deep networks, which is essential for scaling to the billions of parameters required for competitive large language models.

# Post Up-Projection Blocks for sLSTM vs. Pre Up-Projection Blocks for mLSTM

The xLSTM framework employs two distinct residual block architectures, each tailored to the specific characteristics of the memory variant it contains. These architectures differ in the order and placement of the projection operations relative to the recurrent processing.

For xLSTM blocks containing an sLSTM, the architecture uses a post up-projection design. In this configuration, the sequence is first processed non-linearly by the sLSTM in the original dimensional space, summarizing the past information. The output of this processing is then linearly mapped into a higher-dimensional space, where a non-linear activation function is applied. Finally, the result is linearly mapped back to the original dimensional space. This design—non-linear summary in the original space, followed by up-projection, activation, and down-projection—mirrors the architecture commonly used in Transformers. The post up-projection block is depicted in the left panel of Figure 1 in the paper and the third column of Figure 2, with a more detailed version shown in Figure 5 of the appendix.

For xLSTM blocks containing an mLSTM, the architecture instead employs a pre up-projection design. Here, the input is first linearly mapped to a higher-dimensional space. The non-linear summary of the past, performed by the mLSTM, takes place in this high-dimensional space. After the recurrent processing completes, the result is linearly mapped back to the original dimensional space. This design pattern is similar to that used in State Space Models and reflects a key insight: the matrix memory of the mLSTM operates more effectively when given access to a higher-dimensional representation space.

The rationale for this architectural distinction lies in the different memory structures of the two variants. The mLSTM's matrix memory, which stores key-value associations through a covariance update rule, benefits from operating in a higher-dimensional space because its memory capacity scales with the dimensionality. By performing the mLSTM computation in an expanded space, the model can store richer representations and retrieve more nuanced information. In contrast, the sLSTM, with its scalar memory and memory mixing capabilities, does not derive the same benefit from pre up-projection. The post up-projection design allows the sLSTM to perform its temporal processing in the original space, where the hidden-hidden connections can effectively mix information across memory cells.

# Application of Cover's Theorem for Non-Linear Separation

The architectural design of xLSTM blocks is grounded in a fundamental theoretical result from pattern recognition theory: Cover's Theorem. This theorem, established in 1965, provides a powerful justification for projecting data into higher-dimensional spaces before applying non-linear transformations. Cover's Theorem states that patterns that are non-linearly embedded into a higher-dimensional space are more likely to be linearly separable than patterns remaining in their original lower-dimensional space.

To understand the significance of this theorem for xLSTM architecture design, consider the task facing the model during sequence processing. At each time step, the model must predict the next element based on the history of the sequence observed so far. Different possible histories lead to different predictions, and the model must be able to distinguish between these histories to make accurate predictions. In other words, the model must separate different histories or contexts in its representation space.

Cover's Theorem suggests that this separation task becomes easier when the representations are first non-linearly transformed and projected into a higher-dimensional space. The xLSTM blocks implement exactly this principle. The non-linear processing performed by the sLSTM or mLSTM creates a non-linear embedding of the sequence history. The up-projection operations then map these embedded representations into a higher-dimensional space where linear separation becomes more feasible.

The practical implication is that the xLSTM block architecture is designed to transform the input representations in a way that maximizes the model's ability to distinguish between different sequence histories. By combining the non-linear temporal processing of the LSTM variants with dimensionality-expanding projections, the architecture creates a representation space where different contexts can be more readily separated. This separation is what enables accurate prediction of the next sequence element, whether that be a token in language modeling or any other sequential prediction task.

# Residual Stacking and the Use of Pre-LayerNorm

With individual xLSTM blocks designed, the question becomes how to assemble multiple blocks into a complete architecture. The xLSTM framework adopts the residual stacking approach that has become standard in contemporary deep learning, particularly for large language models.

Residual stacking refers to the practice of connecting multiple residual blocks in sequence, with each block receiving the sum of the input and output from the previous block. This approach, pioneered in ResNet architectures and subsequently adopted for Transformers, enables the training of very deep networks by providing pathways for gradient flow during backpropagation. Without residual connections, gradients must flow through all the non-linear transformations in each layer, which can lead to vanishing or exploding gradients in very deep networks. With residual connections, gradients can flow directly through the skip connections, bypassing the potentially problematic transformations in each block.

The xLSTM architecture employs the pre-LayerNorm variant of residual stacking, which has become the dominant approach in contemporary large language models. In pre-LayerNorm, layer normalization is applied before the main transformation in each block, rather than after. Specifically, for each xLSTM block, the input is first normalized using layer normalization, then processed by the block's main transformation (the up-projection, LSTM processing, and down-projection sequence), and finally added to the original input through a residual connection.

This pre-LayerNorm design offers several advantages over the alternative post-LayerNorm approach. First, it provides more stable gradients during training, as the normalization is applied to the input before the main transformation rather than to the output. Second, it enables more effective gradient flow through the residual connections, as the skip connection path does not pass through any normalization. These advantages become increasingly important as the depth of the network increases, making pre-LayerNorm essential for training the very deep networks required for large language models.

The complete xLSTM architecture is thus constructed by residually stacking multiple xLSTM blocks, each with its own internal structure tailored to the specific LSTM variant it contains. This construction follows the pattern established by ResNets and Transformers, both of which demonstrated the effectiveness of residual stacking for training deep networks. The paper notes that this approach has enabled deep convolutional networks and Transformers, which form the foundation of large language models like GPT-3, ChatGPT, GPT-4, and many others.

# Notation for xLSTM Architectures and Block Ratios

With the ability to mix sLSTM-based and mLSTM-based blocks in a single architecture, a notation system is needed to specify the composition of different block types. The xLSTM framework introduces a concise notation that captures the ratio of mLSTM-based to sLSTM-based blocks.

The notation $\text{xLSTM}[a:b]$ specifies that the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks is $a/b$. For example, $\text{xLSTM}[7:1]$ indicates that out of every eight blocks in the architecture, seven are mLSTM-based blocks and one is an sLSTM-based block. For a common total block number of 48, which was used in many of the paper's experiments, this ratio translates to 42 mLSTM-based blocks and 6 sLSTM-based blocks.

The notation also supports architectures composed entirely of one block type. $\text{xLSTM}[1:0]$ denotes an architecture consisting only of mLSTM-based blocks, while $\text{xLSTM}[0:1]$ denotes an architecture consisting only of sLSTM-based blocks. The notation $\text{xLSTM}[1:1]$ indicates an equal mix of both block types.

Throughout the experimental evaluation presented in the paper, this notation is used consistently to specify the architecture configuration. For all experiments, the post up-projection block design is used for sLSTM-based blocks, while the pre up-projection block design is used for mLSTM-based blocks. This consistent pairing reflects the architectural principles discussed earlier in this chapter, where the block design is matched to the characteristics of the memory variant it contains.

The choice of block ratio is a hyperparameter that can be tuned for specific applications. The experiments in the paper explore various ratios to understand the contribution of each block type. For instance, in synthetic tasks testing exponential gating and memory mixing, the paper compares $\text{xLSTM}[0:1]$, $\text{xLSTM}[1:0]$, and $\text{xLSTM}[1:1]$ configurations. In the large-scale language modeling experiments training on 300B tokens, the paper primarily evaluates $\text{xLSTM}[1:0]$ and $\text{xLSTM}[7:1]$ configurations, finding that $\text{xLSTM}[1:0]$ performs particularly well on PALOMA language tasks with lower perplexity than competing methods in 568 out of 571 text domains compared to Mamba, and 486 out of 571 domains compared to Llama.

# Implementation and Computational Efficiency

# Complexity Analysis: Linear Computation and Constant Memory

Understanding the computational complexity of sequence models is essential for practitioners who wish to deploy these models in real-world applications. The xLSTM architecture offers significant advantages over Transformer-based models in terms of both time and space complexity, making it particularly attractive for applications where efficiency matters.

Transformers, which have dominated the landscape of large language models, suffer from quadratic complexity with respect to sequence length. This quadratic scaling arises from the self-attention mechanism, where each token must attend to every other token in the sequence, resulting in $O(L^2)$ computational and memory costs for a sequence of length $L$. As sequence lengths grow, these costs become prohibitive, limiting the practical context windows that can be processed efficiently.

In stark contrast, xLSTM networks exhibit linear computation complexity and constant memory complexity with respect to the sequence length. This fundamental difference stems from the recurrent nature of LSTM-based architectures, where processing each new token requires only a fixed amount of computation that does not depend on the total sequence length. The model maintains a fixed-size internal state that summarizes all past information, and this state is updated incrementally as new inputs arrive.

The linear time complexity $O(L)$ means that doubling the sequence length roughly doubles the computation time, rather than quadrupling it as would occur with Transformers. More importantly, the constant memory complexity $O(1)$ with respect to sequence length is a game-changer for practical deployments. While Transformers must store an ever-growing cache of key-value pairs that scales linearly with context length, xLSTM maintains a fixed-size memory state regardless of how long the sequence becomes.

This compressive memory property makes xLSTM particularly well-suited for industrial applications and implementations on edge devices. Edge computing scenarios, such as mobile phones or embedded systems, have strict memory constraints that often make Transformer deployment impractical. The ability of xLSTM to process arbitrarily long sequences with bounded memory usage opens new possibilities for deploying sophisticated language models in resource-constrained environments.

The practical benefits of this complexity profile are demonstrated in the experimental results, where xLSTM shows linear scaling in text generation time compared to the quadratic scaling observed in Transformer architectures like Llama. Furthermore, because xLSTM maintains constant memory, it can utilize much higher batch sizes during inference, resulting in superior throughput compared to Transformer models that are constrained by their growing key-value caches.

# Trade-offs in mLSTM Matrix Memory Computations

The matrix memory architecture of mLSTM introduces an important trade-off between memory capacity and computational complexity that practitioners must carefully consider when designing their models. Understanding this trade-off is crucial for making informed decisions about model architecture and hardware requirements.

The mLSTM extends the traditional scalar memory cell to a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, where $d$ is the hidden dimension. This matrix memory dramatically increases the storage capacity compared to scalar memory cells, enabling the model to store and retrieve more complex patterns and associations. However, this increased capacity comes at a computational cost: operations on the matrix memory involve $d \times d$ matrices, resulting in $O(d^2)$ complexity for each memory update and retrieval operation.

The key insight is that this computational expense is independent of the sequence length. While each individual update operation is more expensive than in scalar memory architectures, the overall complexity still scales linearly with sequence length, as opposed to the quadratic scaling of attention mechanisms. The trade-off is therefore between memory capacity (favored by larger $d$) and per-step computational cost (which grows with $d^2$).

An important practical consideration is that the matrix memory itself does not require learnable parameters. The memory matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ is dynamically constructed through the covariance update rule, where key-value pairs are stored through outer product operations. This means that increasing the memory capacity by increasing the hidden dimension $d$ does not increase the number of model parameters dedicated to memory storage. The parameters only appear in the projections that produce the keys, values, and queries.

The experimental evidence suggests that this trade-off is favorable in practice. Despite the $d \times d$ matrix operations, the computational overhead has only a minor effect on wall-clock time. This is because these operations can be highly parallelized on GPUs using standard matrix multiplication routines, which are among the most optimized operations on modern hardware. The parallel nature of these computations allows GPUs to amortize the cost across thousands of cores.

Practitioners should note that the matrix memory size scales with the square of the hidden dimension, which creates pressure on GPU memory resources. However, since this memory is bounded and does not grow with sequence length, it remains manageable even for very long sequences. The current implementation handles contexts up to 16k tokens without issues, demonstrating that the memory capacity is sufficient for many practical applications.

# Parallelization Strategies for mLSTM

One of the key advantages of mLSTM over traditional LSTM architectures is its amenability to parallelization, which enables efficient training and inference on modern GPU hardware. Understanding why mLSTM can be parallelized while sLSTM cannot reveals fundamental insights about the structure of these architectures.

The parallelization of mLSTM is made possible by the absence of memory mixing—specifically, the lack of hidden-hidden recurrent connections. In traditional LSTMs and in sLSTM, the hidden state at time step $t$ directly influences the computation at time step $t+1$ through recurrent connections, creating a strict sequential dependency that cannot be easily parallelized. Each time step must wait for the completion of the previous time step.

In contrast, mLSTM abandons these hidden-hidden connections. The query, key, and value vectors at each time step are computed independently from the input, without depending on the previous hidden state. This structural choice breaks the sequential dependency chain and allows the core recurrence to be reformulated in a parallel version.

The mathematical foundation for this parallelization lies in the associative property of the mLSTM recurrence. The update rule for the cell state is:

$$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

Similarly, the normalizer state follows:

$$\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t$$

These recurrences can be viewed as a form of linear recurrence that admits a parallel scan algorithm, similar to approaches used in FlashAttention and Gated Linear Attention (GLA). The key insight is that these operations can be decomposed into binary associative operations that can be computed in parallel using a tree-based reduction pattern.

The parallelization strategy proceeds as follows. First, all the gate values, queries, keys, and values are computed in parallel across all time steps, as these depend only on the inputs and not on previous states. Second, a parallel prefix sum (scan) algorithm is applied to combine these values according to the recurrence relations. This scan operation has $O(\log L)$ parallel depth, meaning that the computation can be completed in logarithmic time when sufficient parallel processors are available.

This parallelization approach is analogous to FlashAttention in spirit, though the specific operations differ. Both approaches recognize that certain recurrence structures can be parallelized by reformulating them as associative operations that admit efficient parallel algorithms. The result is that mLSTM can be trained efficiently on modern GPUs, with computation times that scale much more favorably than sequential implementations.

It is worth noting that while the theoretical parallelization is well-established, practical implementations require careful optimization. The current mLSTM CUDA kernels, while functional, are noted to be approximately four times slower than highly optimized implementations like FlashAttention or the scan operations used in Mamba. There remains significant room for optimization, potentially following the techniques pioneered by FlashAttention to reduce memory transfers and improve GPU utilization.

# CUDA Implementation and Memory Optimizations for sLSTM

The implementation of sLSTM presents unique challenges due to its inherent sequential nature, requiring careful engineering to achieve acceptable performance on modern GPU hardware. Understanding these implementation details provides valuable insights into the practical considerations of deploying recurrent architectures at scale.

The fundamental challenge in implementing sLSTM efficiently is the presence of memory mixing—the hidden-hidden connections that create sequential dependencies across time steps. Unlike mLSTM, which can be parallelized through associative scan operations, sLSTM requires processing each time step in strict sequence. The hidden state $h_{t-1}$ must be computed before it can be used to compute the gates and cell input at time step $t$. This sequential dependency fundamentally limits the parallelism that can be extracted from the computation.

Despite this limitation, significant optimizations can be achieved through careful CUDA implementation. The key strategy is to maximize parallelism within each time step while accepting the sequential constraint across time steps. Within a single time step, the computations for different hidden dimensions and different heads can proceed in parallel, utilizing the massive parallelism available on modern GPUs.

The implementation employs GPU memory optimizations that extend to the register level. Register-level optimization is the finest granularity of GPU memory optimization, keeping frequently accessed values in fast register memory rather than slower shared or global memory. This is particularly important for the recurrent weights and intermediate values that are accessed repeatedly during the sequential scan through time steps.

The exponential gating mechanism in sLSTM introduces numerical stability challenges that must be carefully handled in the implementation. The stabilizer state $m_t$ tracks the maximum of the log-domain gate values, and this information is used to normalize the exponential gates to prevent overflow or underflow. Specifically:

$$m_t = \max \left( \log ( f_t ) + m_{t-1}, \log ( i_t ) \right)$$

The stabilized input and forget gates are then computed as:

$$i'_t = \exp \left( \tilde{i}_t - m_t \right)$$
$$f'_t = \exp \left( \log \left( f_t \right) + m_{t-1} - m_t \right)$$

These stabilization operations must be carefully implemented to maintain numerical precision while minimizing computational overhead. The implementation ensures that replacing the original gate values with these stabilized values does not change either the network output or the gradients with respect to the parameters.

The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of these optimizations. Despite the sequential nature of sLSTM, the fast CUDA implementation achieves performance that is typically less than two times slower than the fully parallel mLSTM implementation. This is a significant achievement, as naive implementations of sequential recurrent networks can easily be orders of magnitude slower than their parallel counterparts.

The practical implications are substantial: sLSTM can be included in xLSTM architectures without creating an unacceptable performance bottleneck. The combination of sLSTM and mLSTM blocks allows practitioners to benefit from the unique capabilities of sLSTM—particularly its ability to perform state tracking through memory mixing—while maintaining reasonable computational efficiency. This enables architectures like xLSTM[7:1], which combines mLSTM's parallelizable matrix memory with sLSTM's expressive memory mixing in a balanced configuration.

# Experimental Evaluation and Performance Analysis

The experimental validation of xLSTM represents a comprehensive effort to demonstrate that the architectural innovations—exponential gating and matrix memory structures—translate into meaningful performance improvements across a diverse set of tasks. The experimental methodology is designed to address the central question posed by the authors: how far can LSTMs go in language modeling when scaled to billions of parameters while leveraging modern techniques? The experiments are structured progressively, beginning with synthetic tasks that isolate specific capabilities, moving through systematic comparisons on established language modeling benchmarks, and culminating in large-scale experiments that position xLSTM alongside state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models.

# Synthetic Tasks: Testing State Tracking and Memory Capacity

The synthetic experiments serve a crucial purpose: they provide controlled environments where specific architectural properties can be tested in isolation. Rather than immediately throwing models at complex language modeling tasks, where performance depends on numerous interacting factors, synthetic tasks allow for precise attribution of capabilities to architectural choices. The xLSTM paper focuses on two primary synthetic experiment categories: formal language tasks that test state tracking capabilities, and associative recall tasks that probe memory capacity.

The formal language experiments draw inspiration from prior work demonstrating that Transformers and State Space Models face fundamental limitations in state tracking. The authors implement and extend formal language tasks to enable multi-length training for length extrapolation. These tasks test whether models can learn and apply grammatical rules that require maintaining internal state over potentially long sequences. The key insight being tested is whether exponential gating with memory mixing—the signature innovation of sLSTM—enables solving problems that require tracking computational states, such as counting parentheses or maintaining parity information.

The experimental design compares 2-block architectures across multiple method families. The xLSTM variants tested include xLSTM[$0$:$1$] (pure sLSTM without any mLSTM blocks), xLSTM[$1$:$0$] (pure mLSTM without any sLSTM blocks), and xLSTM[$1$:$1$] (an equal mixture of both block types). These are compared against representative models from each major paradigm: Llama representing Transformers, Mamba representing State Space Models, RWKV and Retention representing modern RNN approaches, Hyena representing linear Transformer variants, and both vanilla LSTM and LSTM wrapped in Transformer blocks. The evaluation metric reports accuracy specifically on tokens relevant to each task, scaled between 0 (random performance) and 1 (perfect performance).

The results reveal a striking pattern: models without memory mixing—such as Transformers and State Space Models—cannot solve regular grammars like the parity task. This finding aligns with theoretical work demonstrating that Transformers and State Space models are fundamentally less powerful than RNNs for certain computational tasks. The sLSTM blocks, with their memory mixing capability enabled by hidden-hidden recurrent connections, successfully solve these state tracking problems. This validates the theoretical motivation for retaining memory mixing despite its computational costs, as it provides computational expressiveness that purely parallelizable architectures cannot achieve.

The second synthetic experiment category addresses memory capacity through the Multi-Query Associative Recall task. In this task, key-value pairs are randomly sampled from a large vocabulary and must be memorized during a sequence for later retrieval. The authors significantly increase the difficulty compared to standard formulations by expanding the number of key-value pairs to 256 and extending context lengths to 2048. This creates substantial pressure on the model's memory capacity, differentiating architectures based on their ability to store and retrieve information.

The experimental comparison for this task includes Llama, Mamba, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, xLSTM[$1$:$1$], and xLSTM[$1$:$0$]. Transformers like Llama serve as a gold standard for this task because their attention mechanism provides memory capacity that grows exponentially with the coding dimension—each token can attend to all previous tokens directly, maintaining perfect recall regardless of sequence length within the context window. The results demonstrate that xLSTM[$1$:$1$] performs best among all non-Transformer models, even for small model sizes. A particularly interesting finding is that including sLSTM blocks does not diminish memory capacity but rather enhances it, which becomes most evident at the most difficult task configuration with 256 key-value pairs.

The extrapolation analyses presented in the appendix provide additional evidence that xLSTM's enhanced memory capacities enable generalization to contexts longer than those seen during training. This is a practically important capability, as deployed models often encounter sequences longer than training sequences, and catastrophic performance degradation on longer inputs would severely limit real-world applicability.

The Long Range Arena benchmark provides a third synthetic evaluation, assessing xLSTM's performance on long sequences and large contexts across diverse tasks. The consistent strong performance across all tasks in this benchmark suggests that the xLSTM architecture efficiently handles different aspects of long context problems, validating that the architectural improvements generalize across task types.

# Language Modeling Benchmarks: SlimPajama and PALOMA Datasets

The synthetic experiments establish that xLSTM's architectural innovations provide the intended capabilities, but the ultimate test for a language model architecture is performance on actual language modeling tasks. The paper presents a systematic progression of language modeling experiments, beginning with moderate-scale training on 15 billion tokens and culminating in large-scale experiments on 300 billion tokens.

The first major language modeling experiment trains various architectures on 15 billion tokens from SlimPajama, a carefully curated dataset, in an auto-regressive setting. The models are evaluated by their perplexity on a held-out validation set. Perplexity measures how surprised a model is by text it hasn't seen—lower perplexity indicates better prediction ability. The comparison spans three major categories: Transformers (including GPT-3 and Llama architectures), State Space Models (including H3 and Mamba), and Recurrent Neural Networks together with linear Transformers (including RWKV-4, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, GLA, HGRN, and HGRN2).

All models are configured to match a GPT-3 architecture with 350 million parameters, having an embedding dimension of 1024 and 24 residual blocks. This careful matching ensures fair comparison, isolating architectural differences from capacity differences. The xLSTM variants tested include xLSTM[$1$:$0$] and xLSTM[$7$:$1$], where the notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$] indicates the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. Thus xLSTM[$7$:$1$] means that out of every eight blocks, seven are mLSTM-based and one is sLSTM-based. For a typical total of 48 blocks, this translates to 42 mLSTM blocks and 6 sLSTM blocks.

The results demonstrate that xLSTM outperforms all existing methods in validation perplexity. The scaling behavior analysis for this experiment further indicates that xLSTM will maintain its performance advantage as model size increases. This finding is significant because it demonstrates that the architectural innovations translate directly into better language modeling, not just improved performance on artificial tasks.

The large-scale experiments increase training to 300 billion tokens from SlimPajama, matching the training scale used in prior work on Mamba and Griffin. This scale better approximates real-world Large Language Model training scenarios. The comparison focuses on one representative from each major method class: RWKV-4 for RNNs, Llama for Transformers, and Mamba for State Space Models. Multiple model sizes are trained: 125 million, 350 million, 760 million, and 1.3 billion parameters.

A critical evaluation dimension is sequence length extrapolation. Models trained on a context length of 2048 are tested on context lengths extending to 16384—eight times the training context. This tests whether models can generalize to longer inputs, a practical requirement for many applications. The results show that xLSTM models maintain low perplexities for longer contexts, while other methods exhibit significant degradation. This finding validates that xLSTM's memory structure generalizes effectively beyond training conditions.

The PALOMA language benchmark provides an even more comprehensive evaluation, testing next-token prediction performance across 571 text domains. These domains span an extraordinary range of language varieties, from formal sources like nytimes.com to community-specific spaces like r/depression on Reddit. This breadth tests whether architectural advantages persist across diverse linguistic contexts.

The PALOMA results are particularly striking. xLSTM[$1$:$0$] achieves lower perplexity than Mamba on 568 out of 571 domains (99.5%), lower perplexity than Llama on 486 out of 571 domains (85.1%), and lower perplexity than RWKV-4 on 570 out of 571 domains (99.8%). This near-universal advantage across domains demonstrates that xLSTM's improvements are not artifacts of particular evaluation sets but represent genuine architectural superiority for language modeling. Interestingly, xLSTM[$1$:$0$] performs better than xLSTM[$7$:$1$] on these language tasks, suggesting that for pure language modeling, the matrix memory of mLSTM provides the dominant benefit.

Downstream task evaluation measures performance on tasks requiring common sense reasoning, providing insight into whether perplexity improvements translate to practical capabilities. The evaluation includes multiple benchmarks, and xLSTM proves to be the best method in the vast majority of tasks across all model sizes. The only exception is the ARC task, where Mamba occasionally performs best. This overall pattern confirms that xLSTM's perplexity advantages reflect genuine improvements in language understanding, not just better probability assignment to tokens.

# Ablation Studies: The Impact of Exponential Gating, Matrix Memory, and Up-Projection Blocks

Understanding why an architecture works is often as important as demonstrating that it works. The ablation studies systematically deconstruct xLSTM, morphing a vanilla LSTM architecture step-by-step into the full xLSTM architecture. This approach isolates the contribution of each component, providing guidance for future architectural development.

The progression begins with integrating vanilla LSTM layers into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones. Pre-LayerNorm refers to applying layer normalization before each sublayer rather than after, a design choice that improves training stability and is standard in contemporary Large Language Models. This integration tests whether modern architectural practices improve LSTM performance even without changing the LSTM cell itself.

The second step extends the architecture to a post up-projection block. In this design, the model first non-linearly summarizes the past in the original space, then linearly projects into a higher-dimensional space, applies a non-linear activation, and linearly projects back. This structure draws on Cover's Theorem, which states that patterns non-linearly embedded in higher-dimensional spaces can more likely be linearly separated. Better separation of different histories enables better prediction of next sequence elements.

The third step adds exponential gating. This modification addresses the inability of standard LSTMs to revise storage decisions—the limitation demonstrated through the Nearest Neighbor Search problem in the introduction. Standard LSTM gates use sigmoid activation functions, which map to values between 0 and 1. Exponential gates can take on arbitrarily large positive values, providing stronger signal when the model decides to store new information and thereby enabling effective revision of previous storage decisions.

The final step adds matrix memory—the covariance update structure of mLSTM. This addresses the limited storage capacity of scalar cell states, allowing information to be stored in a $d \times d$ matrix rather than a scalar. The covariance update rule $\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_{t} \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ stores key-value pairs in a way that supports efficient retrieval via matrix multiplication with a query vector.

The ablation results attribute strong performance improvement to both exponential gating and matrix memory. Neither alone achieves the full performance of xLSTM, indicating that the two innovations address complementary limitations of the original LSTM.

The paper also includes ablation studies on gating mechanisms themselves. Having each gate be learnable and influenced by input has an incremental positive effect, validating that the gating structure—with input, forget, and output gates each computed from input and hidden state—is essential for strong performance. Additional studies on individual backbone components, detailed in the appendix, further illuminate the contribution of design choices within the residual block structure.

# Scaling Laws: Comparing the Performance Trajectory of xLSTM Against Other Architectures

Scaling laws describe how model performance changes as a function of model size and training data quantity. Understanding scaling behavior is crucial for predicting performance at scales too expensive to test directly, informing decisions about whether investing in larger models will yield meaningful improvements.

The paper presents scaling law analyses at both the 15 billion token and 300 billion token training scales. The scaling law plots show perplexity as a function of model size, with different architectures as different curves. At the 15 billion token scale, xLSTM shows favorable scaling behavior compared to all competing methods, with the curve indicating continued advantage at larger scales.

The 300 billion token scaling analysis provides even stronger evidence. All models share similar scaling behavior in the sense that they all follow power-law relationships, but with different offsets. This means that the relative ordering of methods remains consistent as scale increases—RWKV-4 performs worst, followed by Llama and Mamba, with xLSTM achieving the best performance at each scale. The margin between xLSTM and Mamba is similar to the margin between Mamba and Llama, suggesting that xLSTM represents a meaningful step forward comparable to the jump from Transformers to State Space Models.

The consistency of xLSTM's advantage across scales provides confidence that the architectural improvements will continue to pay dividends as models grow larger. This finding is significant for practitioners considering investing in xLSTM-based Large Language Models: the scaling laws suggest that xLSTM will remain competitive with or superior to Transformers and State Space Models even at scales beyond those tested in the paper.

The scaling behavior also validates the theoretical motivation for xLSTM's design. Exponential gating and matrix memory address fundamental limitations of LSTMs, and the persistent scaling advantages suggest that these improvements compound rather than diminish as model capacity increases.

# Inference Speed and Throughput Comparisons

Architecture comparisons often focus on quality metrics like perplexity or task accuracy, but practical deployment also depends on computational efficiency. The paper measures text generation time and maximal throughput, providing insight into how xLSTM performs in production-like conditions.

Text generation time measures wall-clock time to generate sequences of varying lengths. The experiments use batch size 1 with pre-fill 16 tokens—a configuration designed to be maximally favorable for the Transformer baseline, which benefits from the pre-fill computation being parallelizable. The results demonstrate linear scaling of generation time with sequence length for xLSTM, matching other recurrent models like Mamba and RWKV-4. In contrast, the Transformer (Llama) exhibits quadratic scaling with sequence length. This quadratic scaling arises from the attention mechanism's need to compute relationships between all pairs of tokens, while recurrent models like xLSTM only need to process each token once during generation.

Maximal throughput measures how many tokens per second a model can generate when maximally utilizing available computational resources. The constant memory footprint of recurrent models allows them to use much higher batch sizes than Transformers, which must maintain a key-value cache that grows linearly with both sequence length and batch size. The throughput experiments show that xLSTM achieves the highest throughput among compared methods, validating that the architectural advantages translate to practical deployment benefits.

The paper acknowledges current implementation limitations: the CUDA kernels for mLSTM are not fully optimized, currently running about four times slower than FlashAttention or the scan operation used in Mamba. The sLSTM blocks, due to their memory mixing, cannot be fully parallelized and are less than two times slower than the parallel mLSTM implementation. These are implementation limitations rather than architectural limitations—faster CUDA kernels following the FlashAttention approach could substantially improve wall-clock performance.

The linear computation complexity and constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length position xLSTM well for industrial applications and edge deployment, where memory constraints often limit practical deployment options. The matrix memory of mLSTM does not require parameters but involves $d \times d$ matrix operations, trading memory capacity against computational complexity. However, these computations can be parallelized on GPUs, minimizing wall-clock time impact.

# Related Work and Limitations

# Comparison with Linear Attention and State Space Models

The emergence of Transformers revolutionized sequence modeling through their self-attention mechanism, which allows each position in a sequence to attend to all other positions directly. However, this capability comes at a significant computational cost: the self-attention operation scales quadratically with the sequence length, making it prohibitively expensive for very long sequences. This limitation motivated researchers to develop alternatives that maintain the modeling power of attention while reducing computational complexity. The xLSTM architecture must be understood within this context of ongoing research into efficient sequence models.

Linear attention methods represent one major line of research aimed at addressing the quadratic complexity of standard attention. The fundamental insight behind linear attention is that the attention mechanism can be reformulated to have linear rather than quadratic complexity in the sequence length. The Synthesizer approach takes a radical departure from standard attention by learning synthetic attention weights without requiring token-to-token interactions, thereby eliminating the quadratic bottleneck entirely. The Linformer provides another perspective by recognizing that the self-attention matrix can be approximated by a low-rank matrix, enabling linear-time computation while preserving much of the modeling capacity. The Linear Transformer takes a more direct approach by reformulating the attention mechanism itself to be linear, using kernel methods to decompose the softmax attention computation. The Performer builds on this by providing a rigorous mathematical framework for approximating the attention softmax using positive orthogonal random features, offering theoretical guarantees while achieving linear complexity.

Beyond these linearization approaches, researchers have explored replacing attention entirely with alternative mechanisms. The Structured Global Convolution (SGConv) and the Hyena Hierarchy replace attention with fast long convolutions that can be computed efficiently using Fourier transforms. These convolutional approaches can capture long-range dependencies while maintaining linear complexity, though they may struggle with tasks requiring precise recall of specific past tokens. The xLSTM approach differs fundamentally from these linear attention methods in its treatment of memory. While linear attention methods maintain an implicit memory through their reformulation of the attention computation, xLSTM provides an explicit, structured memory that can be controlled through gating mechanisms. This explicit memory structure allows xLSTM to precisely control what information is stored and retrieved, rather than relying on implicit patterns in the attention weights.

State Space Models (SSMs) have emerged as another powerful paradigm for sequence modeling that achieves linear complexity. The Structured State Space sequence model (S4) was one of the pioneering works in this direction, demonstrating that carefully parameterized state space models could match or exceed the performance of Transformers on many benchmarks. The key insight of S4 is that by imposing specific structural constraints on the state matrix, the model can efficiently process long sequences while maintaining the ability to capture long-range dependencies. Following S4, several variants were developed to improve efficiency or performance. The Diagonal State Space (DSS) model simplified the state space structure to a diagonal form, making the model more computationally efficient while still capturing essential sequence dynamics. The Gated State Space (GSS) models introduced gating mechanisms similar to those in LSTMs, allowing the model to selectively remember or forget information. The S5 model further refined these ideas, achieving strong performance with efficient parallel computation. The Bidirectional Gated SSM (BiGS) extended these models to bidirectional processing, enabling the model to incorporate future context. The H3 model addressed limitations in earlier SSMs for language modeling, introducing specific architectural innovations that improved performance on language tasks. Most recently, Mamba has emerged as a leading SSM architecture, achieving competitive performance with Transformers while maintaining linear complexity through careful architectural design and efficient implementation.

The relationship between xLSTM and SSMs is multifaceted. Both approaches share the goal of achieving linear complexity sequence modeling, and both recognize the importance of gating mechanisms for controlling information flow. However, xLSTM distinguishes itself through its memory mixing capability, which enables the model to solve state tracking problems that are fundamentally difficult or impossible for SSMs. State tracking refers to the ability to maintain precise count or state information over long sequences, such as tracking the parity of parentheses in a mathematical expression or following the state of variables in a program. Research has shown that models without memory mixing, including Transformers and SSMs, cannot solve certain state tracking problems, while models with memory mixing like LSTMs and xLSTM can. This difference in computational expressiveness makes xLSTM particularly suited for tasks requiring precise state management, such as code evaluation or entity tracking in long narratives.

# Relationship to Recurrent Neural Networks and Gating Mechanisms

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) have a long and rich history in sequence modeling, predating the Transformer revolution by decades. The fundamental idea behind RNNs is to maintain a hidden state that is updated at each time step based on the current input and the previous hidden state. This recurrent structure allows the model to process sequences of arbitrary length while maintaining a fixed-size memory, naturally achieving linear complexity in the sequence length. However, traditional RNNs suffered from the vanishing and exploding gradient problems, which made it difficult to learn long-range dependencies. The LSTM was specifically designed to address these issues through the introduction of gating mechanisms and the constant error carousel.

The gating mechanism introduced in LSTM is one of its most influential contributions, representing a principled way to control information flow through the network. The input gate controls how much new information enters the cell state, the forget gate controls how much of the previous cell state is retained, and the output gate controls how much of the cell state is exposed as the hidden state. These gates are continuous-valued, learned through backpropagation, and allow the network to selectively remember or forget information over long time horizons. This gating mechanism has proven so effective that it has been rediscovered and reinterpreted in numerous modern architectures. The Hierarchically Gated Linear RNN (HGRN) and its successor HGRN2 incorporate gating into linear recurrent networks, demonstrating that gating remains beneficial even in simplified recurrent architectures. The Gated Linear Attention (GLA) applies gating to linear attention mechanisms, combining the benefits of both approaches. The Gated State Space (GSS) and Bidirectional Gated SSM (BiGS) integrate gating into state space models, improving their ability to selectively process information. The Moving Average Equipped Gated Attention (MEGA) combines gating with moving average computations for efficient long-range modeling. RWKV incorporates gating as a core component of its architecture, while Mamba uses gating extensively to achieve its impressive performance.

Recent RNN approaches have made significant strides toward closing the gap with Transformers in language modeling. RWKV has emerged as one of the most successful RNN approaches, demonstrating that carefully designed recurrent architectures can achieve performance competitive with Transformers on large-scale language modeling benchmarks. The key innovation in RWKV is the combination of linear attention-like computations with RNN-style recurrence, achieving both parallelizable training and efficient inference. RNNs with Deep Linear Recurrent Units (LRUs) have also shown promising results, demonstrating that even simplified recurrent architectures can be effective for language modeling when properly designed and trained.

The xLSTM architecture builds directly on the LSTM tradition while addressing its key limitations. The most important difference between xLSTM and other modern RNNs is the presence of memory mixing in sLSTM. Memory mixing refers to the recurrent connections between hidden states from one time step to the next, which create dependencies that must be processed sequentially. While this sequential processing limits parallelization, it enables state tracking capabilities that models without memory mixing cannot achieve. The exponential gating in xLSTM further enhances the model's ability to revise storage decisions, addressing a key limitation of standard LSTMs. The introduction of heads in sLSTM, together with exponential gating, establishes a new form of memory mixing that allows information to flow across different cell states within each head while keeping heads independent. This architectural choice balances the benefits of memory mixing for state tracking against the need for computational efficiency.

# Connections to Fast Weight Programmers and the Covariance Update Rule

The matrix memory structure in mLSTM has deep connections to classical associative memory models and the concept of fast weights. Understanding these connections illuminates both the theoretical foundations of xLSTM and its relationship to other modern architectures. The fundamental idea is to store and retrieve key-value associations using a matrix memory that can be updated and queried efficiently.

Associative memories, also known as content-addressable memories, have a rich history in neural network research. Bidirectional Associative Memories (BAMs), developed in the 1970s by researchers including Kohonen, Anderson, and Nakano, provided a mathematical framework for storing pattern associations in a matrix form. The key insight is that when storing a key-value pair $(\mathbf{k}, \mathbf{v})$, the outer product $\mathbf{v} \mathbf{k}^\top$ creates a matrix that, when multiplied by the key $\mathbf{k}$, approximately retrieves the value $\mathbf{v}$. This simple but powerful principle forms the basis of the covariance update rule used in mLSTM.

The covariance update rule, studied by Sejnowski and Dayan, provides an optimal method for storing associations in a linear matrix memory. Given key-value pairs with zero mean (achieved through layer normalization before projection), the update rule adds the outer product to the memory matrix: $\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. This rule is optimal for achieving maximal separability of retrieved binary vectors, which is equivalent to maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio in retrieval. While higher separability is possible through pairwise interactions as in standard attention, this comes at the cost of quadratic complexity.

The concept of Fast Weight Programmers, introduced by Schmidhuber and later refined by Schlag and Ba, provides another perspective on matrix memories. The idea is that instead of having fixed weights learned during training, the network can rapidly modify its weights during the forward pass based on the input sequence. This creates a form of "learning within inference" that allows the network to adapt its computation based on the specific sequence being processed. The connection to mLSTM is direct: the matrix memory $\mathbf{C}$ can be viewed as a set of fast weights that are updated according to the covariance update rule, with the forget gate providing a decay mechanism and the input gate controlling the learning rate for each new association.

Several modern architectures share these foundational concepts with mLSTM. RWKV-5 and RWKV-6 incorporate similar matrix update mechanisms, demonstrating the effectiveness of this approach for large-scale language modeling. Retention uses a related formulation for efficient sequence processing. Linear Transformer can be interpreted through the lens of matrix memories, where the attention computation is reformulated as maintaining and querying an implicit matrix. HGRN2 also builds on similar principles, combining matrix memory structures with gating mechanisms.

The key innovation of mLSTM compared to these approaches is its integration into the full LSTM framework, including exponential gating and the output gate. The forget gate in mLSTM provides a principled mechanism for controlling memory decay, allowing the network to selectively retain or forget stored associations based on the input. The input gate controls the strength of new memories, while the exponential activation ensures that the network can make sharp decisions about what to store. The output gate then controls how retrieved information is incorporated into the hidden state. This combination of matrix memory with the full LSTM gating machinery distinguishes mLSTM from other approaches that may use similar update rules but lack the sophisticated control mechanisms.

The retrieval process in mLSTM deserves special attention. Given a query $\mathbf{q}_t$, the retrieval is performed through matrix multiplication: $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ produces a retrieved value based on the similarity between the query and stored keys. The normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$ maintains a weighted sum of keys, and the normalization by $|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|$ ensures stable retrieval regardless of the number of stored associations or their magnitudes. This normalization is crucial for preventing numerical instability and ensuring that the retrieved values remain meaningful throughout the sequence.

# Current Limitations and Challenges

While xLSTM demonstrates impressive performance and addresses many limitations of traditional LSTMs, several important challenges remain. Understanding these limitations is crucial for both practical applications and future research directions. These challenges span computational efficiency, implementation optimization, and architectural considerations.

The most fundamental limitation concerns the parallelization constraints of sLSTM. The memory mixing mechanism that gives sLSTM its state tracking capabilities also enforces sequential processing. Memory mixing refers to the hidden-hidden connections where the hidden state from the previous time step influences the computation of the current time step through recurrent weight matrices. These connections create a dependency chain that cannot be parallelized across time steps, unlike mLSTM or Transformers where all positions can be processed simultaneously during training. This means that training sLSTM-based architectures requires more time than comparable parallelizable architectures, particularly for long sequences. The researchers developed a fast CUDA implementation with GPU memory optimizations at the register level to mitigate this limitation. Despite these optimizations, sLSTM remains less than two times slower than the parallel mLSTM implementation, and significantly slower than fully parallelizable architectures during training.

The CUDA kernel optimization for mLSTM presents another significant challenge. While mLSTM can be reformulated in a parallel version similar to FlashAttention or the scan operation used in Mamba, the current implementation is not fully optimized. The paper reports that the current mLSTM implementation is approximately four times slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba. This performance gap represents a significant opportunity for future optimization work. The researchers note that faster CUDA kernels could potentially be developed following the approach of FlashAttention, which carefully manages GPU memory access patterns to achieve dramatic speedups. Such optimizations would be essential for deploying xLSTM at the scale of modern Large Language Models, where even modest efficiency improvements can translate to significant cost savings and reduced training time.

The matrix memory structure of mLSTM, while providing enhanced storage capacity, introduces computational complexity considerations. Processing $d \times d$ matrices requires $O(d^2)$ computations per time step for both the memory update and retrieval operations. For large embedding dimensions, as commonly used in Large Language Models, this computational cost becomes substantial. However, the memory operations do not require additional parameters beyond the projection matrices for keys, values, and queries. Furthermore, these matrix operations can be efficiently parallelized on GPUs using standard linear algebra routines, which mitigates the wall-clock time impact. The trade-off between memory capacity and computational complexity remains an important consideration when designing xLSTM architectures for specific applications.

The initialization of forget gates represents a subtle but important practical challenge. The forget gate controls how much of the previous memory is retained, and its initialization can significantly impact training dynamics and model performance. If the forget gate is initialized to retain too much information, the memory may become overloaded, degrading retrieval quality. Conversely, if it forgets too aggressively, the model may fail to capture long-range dependencies. This initialization challenge is particularly acute for the matrix memory in mLSTM, where improper initialization can lead to numerical instability or suboptimal performance. Careful initialization strategies and hyperparameter tuning are necessary to ensure stable training and good performance.

A more subtle limitation concerns the relationship between memory capacity and sequence length. The matrix memory in mLSTM has fixed capacity regardless of sequence length, unlike the growing key-value cache in Transformers. This means that as sequence length increases, the memory must store more associations in the same fixed-size matrix, potentially overloading the memory and degrading retrieval quality. However, the experimental results in the paper demonstrate that this is not a significant limitation for contexts up to 16,384 tokens, with xLSTM maintaining low perplexities even when extrapolating to context lengths beyond those seen during training. For even longer contexts, this limitation may become more pronounced, and architectural modifications such as hierarchical memories or adaptive memory sizes may be necessary.

The computational demands of large-scale language experiments posed practical challenges for architecture optimization. The researchers note that they did not fully optimize the architecture or hyperparameters, particularly for larger xLSTM architectures. The extensive computational resources required for training large language models meant that systematic hyperparameter search was impractical. This suggests that the reported performance of xLSTM may not represent its full potential, and that careful architecture search and hyperparameter tuning could yield further improvements. Future work focusing specifically on optimizing xLSTM architectures for specific scales and tasks would likely reveal even stronger performance.

Finally, it is worth noting that the combination of sLSTM and mLSTM in practical architectures introduces design choices that are not yet fully explored. The paper uses notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$] to denote the ratio of mLSTM-based versus sLSTM-based blocks, with xLSTM[7:1] using seven mLSTM blocks for every one sLSTM block. The optimal ratio likely depends on the specific task, sequence length, and model size, and developing principled methods for selecting this ratio remains an open question. Similarly, the choice between pre up-projection and post up-projection residual block structures for different components adds another dimension to the architectural design space that requires further investigation.

# Conclusion and Future Directions

The development of Extended Long Short-Term Memory (xLSTM) represents a significant advancement in the evolution of recurrent neural network architectures for sequence modeling. This chapter synthesizes the key contributions of the xLSTM framework, evaluates its competitive standing against contemporary architectures, and explores the broader implications for future research and applications.

## Summary of xLSTM Contributions: Exponential Gating and Modified Memory Structures

The xLSTM framework introduces two fundamental modifications to the traditional LSTM architecture that address its longstanding limitations. The first major contribution is the introduction of exponential gating with appropriate normalization and stabilization techniques. Traditional LSTMs employ sigmoid activation functions for their gates, which produce values in the range $(0, 1)$. While this gating mechanism has proven effective for controlling information flow, it imposes constraints on the network's ability to revise storage decisions. The exponential gating mechanism in xLSTM replaces the sigmoid activation with exponential activation for the input gate and optionally for the forget gate, i.e., $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$.

This exponential gating is coupled with a normalizer state $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$ that tracks the cumulative product of gate values over time. The hidden state computation then becomes $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$, which normalizes the cell state by the accumulated gate strength. This normalization is crucial because exponential gates can produce arbitrarily large values that would otherwise lead to numerical instability. To further stabilize the exponential gates, xLSTM employs a stabilizer state $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$ that prevents overflow by tracking the maximum log-value and appropriately scaling the gates. The stabilized gates become $i'_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)$ and $f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)$.

The second major contribution is the modification of the LSTM memory structure, which yields two distinct variants: sLSTM and mLSTM. The sLSTM (scalar LSTM) maintains the scalar memory cell structure of the original LSTM but augments it with exponential gating. It features scalar memory, scalar updates, and new memory mixing capabilities through recurrent connections. The sLSTM can have multiple heads with memory mixing within each head but not across heads, and it can have multiple memory cells within each head with memory mixing across cells. This hierarchical structure enables a new form of memory mixing that enhances the network's expressivity.

The mLSTM (matrix LSTM) represents a more radical departure from the traditional LSTM by replacing the scalar cell state $c \in \mathbb{R}$ with a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This matrix memory is updated according to a covariance update rule: $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$, where $\mathbf{k}_t$ and $\mathbf{v}_t$ are key and value vectors derived from the input. This formulation draws inspiration from Bidirectional Associative Memories and Fast Weight Programmers, providing significantly enhanced storage capacity compared to scalar memory. The retrieval operation is performed via matrix multiplication: given a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$, the retrieved information is computed as $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$, normalized by the dot product with a normalizer state.

A critical advantage of mLSTM is its full parallelizability. Unlike sLSTM and traditional LSTMs, mLSTM has no hidden-hidden connections (memory mixing), which allows the recurrence to be reformulated in a parallel version analogous to FlashAttention or GLA. This parallelizability is essential for efficient training on modern GPU hardware and enables xLSTM to scale to billions of parameters.

## The Competitive Performance of xLSTM Against Transformers and State Space Models

The experimental validation of xLSTM demonstrates that it achieves competitive or superior performance compared to state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models across multiple benchmarks. When trained on 15B tokens from the SlimPajama dataset, xLSTM variants achieve lower validation perplexity than GPT-3, Llama, Mamba, RWKV variants, H3, and other contemporary methods. The notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$] indicates the ratio of mLSTM-based to sLSTM-based blocks, with configurations like xLSTM[1:0] (pure mLSTM) and xLSTM[7:1] (seven mLSTM blocks per sLSTM block) showing strong performance.

The scaling behavior of xLSTM is particularly noteworthy. Experiments on both 15B and 300B token datasets reveal that xLSTM follows favorable scaling laws, with performance improving consistently as model size increases from 125M to 1.3B parameters. The scaling curves indicate that xLSTM has a lower offset than Mamba and Transformers, suggesting that larger xLSTM models will continue to perform favorably compared to these architectures. This finding directly addresses the central question posed in the paper: LSTMs, when enhanced with modern techniques and scaled appropriately, can indeed compete with Transformers and State Space Models in language modeling.

xLSTM also demonstrates impressive capabilities in handling long contexts. In sequence length extrapolation experiments, models trained on context length 2048 were tested on sequences up to length 16384. Unlike other methods, xLSTM models maintain low perplexities for longer contexts, demonstrating robust extrapolation capabilities. This is attributed to the compressive nature of xLSTM memory, which has linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length.

The performance on the PALOMA language benchmark further validates xLSTM's effectiveness. Across 571 text domains ranging from nytimes.com to r/depression on Reddit, xLSTM[1:0] achieves lower perplexity than Mamba in 568 out of 571 domains (99.5%), lower than Llama in 486 domains (85.1%), and lower than RWKV-4 in 570 domains (99.8%). These results demonstrate that xLSTM's advantages are consistent across diverse text domains and are not limited to specific types of content.

On synthetic tasks testing formal language recognition and memory capacity, xLSTM shows particularly strong results. The exponential gating with memory mixing in sLSTM enables solution of state tracking problems that Transformers and State Space Models cannot solve, such as regular grammars like the parity task. This aligns with theoretical findings that RNNs with memory mixing are more expressive than Transformers and State Space Models. On the Multi-Query Associative Recall task, xLSTM[1:1] performs best among all non-Transformer models, with the sLSTM block leveraging rather than diminishing memory capacity.

## Potential Applications Beyond Language Modeling

While the experimental focus of the xLSTM paper is on language modeling, the architecture has potential applications across numerous domains where LSTMs have historically excelled. The original LSTM architecture demonstrated effectiveness across diverse sequence-related tasks including text generation, handwriting generation, sequence-to-sequence translation, computer program evaluation, image captioning, source code generation, and rainfall-runoff modeling for flooding warnings.

In reinforcement learning, LSTMs have been the best performing sequence models, powering systems like AlphaStar for StarCraft II, OpenAI Five for Dota 2, and magnetic controllers for nuclear fusion. The enhanced capabilities of xLSTM, particularly its ability to revise storage decisions through exponential gating and its increased storage capacity through matrix memory, suggest that xLSTM could improve performance in these domains. State tracking is critical in reinforcement learning for evaluating code or tracking entities in complex environments, tasks where xLSTM's memory mixing capabilities provide a distinct advantage.

Time series prediction represents another promising application domain. The constant error carousel mechanism that enables LSTMs to learn long-term dependencies is enhanced in xLSTM through exponential gating, which allows for more flexible control over memory retention. The matrix memory of mLSTM can store richer representations of temporal patterns, potentially improving forecasting accuracy for complex multivariate time series.

Physical systems modeling is identified as another area where xLSTM could have considerable impact. The ability to compress long sequences into a fixed-size memory while maintaining the capacity to retrieve relevant information makes xLSTM well-suited for modeling dynamical systems where past states influence future behavior. The linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length make xLSTM particularly attractive for industrial applications and implementations on edge devices.

## Future Outlook for Optimizing xLSTM Architectures

Despite its impressive performance, xLSTM has several limitations that point to directions for future optimization. The sLSTM variant, while offering memory mixing capabilities, is not parallelizable due to the hidden-hidden connections. The authors developed a fast CUDA implementation with GPU memory optimizations at the register level, achieving speeds less than two times slower than the parallel mLSTM implementation. Further optimization of CUDA kernels for sLSTM remains an area for future work.

The mLSTM CUDA kernels are identified as not fully optimized, currently running approximately four times slower than FlashAttention or the scan operation used in Mamba. The authors note that faster CUDA kernels could be developed following approaches similar to FlashAttention. The matrix memory of mLSTM, while providing enhanced capacity, has high computational complexity since $d \times d$ matrices must be processed. However, since the memory update and retrieval do not use additional parameters and can be parallelized using standard matrix operations, the wall clock time overhead is relatively minor.

Several architectural and hyperparameter considerations remain for future investigation. The initialization of forget gates must be chosen carefully to ensure stable training. The matrix memory's independence from sequence length raises potential concerns about memory overload for longer contexts, though experiments show this is not problematic for contexts up to 16k tokens. The extensive optimization process needed for xLSTM to reach its full potential, particularly for larger architectures, is identified as an area requiring further investigation.

The residual block architecture of xLSTM follows two patterns: post up-projection for sLSTM blocks (similar to Transformers) and pre up-projection for mLSTM blocks (similar to State Space Models). This design choice is motivated by Cover's Theorem, which states that patterns non-linearly embedded in a higher dimensional space can more likely be linearly separated. Future work could explore alternative block designs and integration strategies.

In conclusion, the xLSTM framework demonstrates that the fundamental ideas underlying LSTMs—the constant error carousel and gating—when enhanced with modern techniques including exponential gating and matrix memory, can produce architectures competitive with Transformers and State Space Models. The scaling laws suggest that larger xLSTM models will be serious competitors to current Large Language Models built with Transformer technology, potentially reshaping the landscape of sequence modeling across language and beyond.
