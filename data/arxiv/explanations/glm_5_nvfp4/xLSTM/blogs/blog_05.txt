\title{Blogs about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

# Scaling Laws Showdown: What Happens When LSTMs Grow to Billions of Parameters?

For years, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture was the undisputed king of sequence modeling. However, the rise of Transformers—powered by self-attention and massive parallelization—pushed LSTMs to the sidelines. Transformers scaled well; LSTMs, with their limitations in storage capacity and inability to revise storage decisions, did not.

But a recent paper titled "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory" asks a compelling question: what if we fix the limitations of LSTMs and scale them to the size of modern Large Language Models (LLMs)? The paper introduces **xLSTM**, an architecture that extends the classic LSTM with exponential gating and matrix memory. The results from their scaling experiments suggest that the era of LSTM dominance might not be entirely over.

## The Experimental Setup

To evaluate xLSTM's potential, the authors conducted rigorous experiments comparing it against state-of-the-art architectures. They trained models on two distinct scales:
1.  **15B Tokens:** A standard comparison using 350M parameter models.
2.  **300B Tokens:** A large-scale experiment using model sizes ranging from 125M to 1.3B parameters.

The comparisons pitted xLSTM against heavy hitters like Llama (Transformer), Mamba (State Space Model), RWKV, and others. The primary metric for comparison was validation perplexity, which measures how well a probability model predicts a sample (lower is better).

## The 15B Token Baseline

The initial experiments on 15B tokens served as a comprehensive baseline. The authors compared xLSTM against various architecture classes, including Transformers (GPT-3, Llama), State Space Models (H3, Mamba), and Recurrent Neural Networks (RWKV, HGRN).

The results were clear. xLSTM outperformed all existing methods in validation perplexity. This wasn't just a marginal improvement; it demonstrated that the architectural changes—specifically the exponential gating and the new memory structures—were effective. Ablation studies confirmed that these new components were critical, attributing the performance gains to both the enhanced gating mechanisms and the increased storage capacity of the matrix memory.

## The Scaling Laws Showdown

The most exciting results came from the large-scale experiments on 300B tokens. This is where the "scaling laws" come into play. The authors assessed the power-law scaling behavior of xLSTM against Llama, Mamba, and RWKV-4 across model sizes of 125M, 350M, 760M, and 1.3B parameters.

### Better Offsets, Better Performance
Scaling laws typically dictate that model performance improves predictably as parameters increase. The experiments revealed that while all models follow similar scaling slopes, xLSTM exhibits a significantly better offset.

In terms of validation perplexity, xLSTM variants (specifically xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1]) consistently achieved the lowest scores across all model sizes. The performance hierarchy was distinct: RWKV-4 performed worst, followed by Llama and Mamba. xLSTM sat comfortably at the top, outperforming Mamba by a margin similar to how Mamba outperforms Llama.

### Downstream Task Performance
The paper also evaluated performance on downstream tasks, specifically common sense reasoning benchmarks. Here, xLSTM continued to shine. In the vast majority of tasks and across all model sizes, xLSTM was the best method. Mamba occasionally outperformed xLSTM on specific tasks like the ARC Challenge, but xLSTM maintained a dominant overall lead.

Furthermore, when tested on the PALOMA language benchmark (covering 571 text domains), xLSTM demonstrated superior generalization. xLSTM[1:0] achieved lower perplexity than Mamba in 99.5% of the text domains and outperformed Llama in 85.1% of them.

## Beyond Perplexity: Context and Speed

Scaling isn't just about loss curves; it's about how the model handles real-world constraints like context length and inference speed.

### Extrapolating to Long Contexts
Transformers often struggle to extrapolate beyond the sequence length they were trained on. The authors tested this by training models on a context length of 2048 and evaluating them on contexts up to 16384. While other models saw their perplexity spike, xLSTM maintained low perplexities, suggesting it handles long-range dependencies effectively without the quadratic complexity of full attention.

### Inference Efficiency
Because xLSTM has linear computational complexity with respect to sequence length (unlike the quadratic complexity of standard Transformers), it offers distinct advantages at inference time. The experiments showed that xLSTM scales linearly in generation time, similar to Mamba and RWKV. More importantly, due to its constant memory footprint, xLSTM can handle much higher batch sizes than Transformer models like Llama, resulting in significantly higher throughput.

## Conclusion

So, how far do LSTMs get when scaled to billions of parameters? The paper's conclusion is optimistic: "At least as far as current technologies like Transformers or State Space Models."

The scaling laws indicate that xLSTM does not just match current state-of-the-art models; it offers a favorable offset that suggests it could outperform them as model sizes continue to grow. By addressing the fundamental limitations of the original LSTM—through exponential gating and matrix memory—the authors have successfully revitalized a classic architecture for the modern era of large-scale modeling.
