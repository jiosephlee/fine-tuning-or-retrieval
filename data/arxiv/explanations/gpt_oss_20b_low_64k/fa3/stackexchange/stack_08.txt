\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### Why is FP8 FlashAttention‐3 slower for small sequence lengths with causal masking?
Question:
The ablation study shows that for causal masking at short seq.
lengths FP8 FlashAttention‐3 underperforms cuDNN H100 kernels. Is this due to lack of persistent kernel design, or are there additional reasons such as increased register pressure or pipelining overhead when very few blocks are processed?
Answer:
**Why FP8 FlashAttention‑3 lags cuDNN on short, causally‑masked sequences**

The ablation plots in the FlashAttention‑3 paper show that, for causal masking and short sequence lengths (e.g., < 4 k tokens), the FP8 version runs slower than cuDNN’s H100 kernel. Two interlocking design choices explain this behaviour.

---

### 1. No persistent‑kernel layout for the FP8 path

The FP16 implementation of FlashAttention‑3 already uses a *persistent* kernel: a single thread‑block stays alive for the whole sequence, keeps a circular shared‑memory buffer on‑chip, and continuously streams blocks of \(K\) and \(V\) from the GPU memory while feeding them to the Tensor Cores. This keeps the SM fully occupied, eliminates per‑block launch overheads, and lets the thread‑block reuse registers that hold context between consecutive iterations.

In contrast, the FP8 version deliberately omits this persistent design. The authors note this in the discussion section: *“For our benchmarks, FP16 FlashAttention‑3 has a persistent kernel and load‑balancing strategy, while FP8 FlashAttention‑3 does not.”* Because an FP8 block has a different layout (\(k\)-major only) and requires extra register permutations (see §4.2 of the paper), reworking the whole persistent mechanism would have increased register pressure further. The choice was therefore to keep a simpler “per‑0‑block” launch model.

When sequence length is short, each launch pays a fixed overhead: AFT row or column of TMA loads, WGMMA launches, register spills, and SM syncs. On long sequences those costs are amortised over many iterations; on short sequences they dominate. cuDNN’s engineered kernels use a persistent design that can hide these per‑launch latencies even for small \(N\).

---

### 2. Register pressure from softmax – GEMM overlap

The 2‑stage pipeline in FlashAttention‑3 overlaps a softmax computation with the next block’s WGMMA (programming guide section “Intra-warpgroup overlapping”). For FP8 this overlap still exists but:

1. **Extra registers** are needed to stash a full \(S_{\text{next}}\) tile in registers while its associated softmax runs. Table 5 in section 4.2 shows that each thread must hold an additional \(B_r \times B_c\) floats per threadblock.
2. **FP8 layouts** impose extra permutations on either accumulator or operand fragments (see fig. 12–13), which use `byteperm` instructions that consume additional registers.

With fewer available registers per thread (the GPU’s limit is 256 RMEM per thread), more spills occur when `setmaxnreg` cannot reserve enough slots for all register windows simultaneously. Each spill forces data to DRAM or shared memory contiguously—precisely what attention is designed to avoid.

On short sequences this register spilling is particularly damaging: every iteration already processes only a few blocks; spilling may double or triple round‑trip latency while computation stalls for data moves that could otherwise have stayed resident on-chip.

---

### 3. Scheduling overhead when few warpgroups exist

Causal masking reduces work by roughly half because each query only attends to earlier keys. Consequently:

- The number of required *warpgroups* per CTA shrinks.
- The **ping‑pong overlapping** mechanism (warp–group A does softmax while warp–group B does WGMMA) becomes less effective because one warpgroup may be idle part of the time.
- Barriers (`bar.sync`) inside `for` loops now serialize more often as warpgroups finish their portion earlier than their partner.

The authors’ benchmarks confirm that when fewer warpgroups are active (`batch × heads × Q_block` small), even an otherwise efficient overlapping pattern loses its advantage.

---

### 4. Summary

In short, the slower FP8 FlashAttention‑3 for causal masking at small lengths is mainly due to:

1. **Absence of a persistent kernel** – launch overheads are not amortised.
2. **Higher register demand** – extra work to support FP8 layouts and WGMMA–softmax pipelining causes spills.
3. **Inefficient scheduling when warpgroups are few** – ping‑pong overlap yields diminishing returns under causal constraints.

Adding a persistent design for FP8 (as suggested by the authors’ own future‑work note) would reduce launch overhead; careful register allocation or deeper buffering could mitigate spills; and adaptive grouping based on sequence length could improve overlap ratios.

These factors together explain why cuDNN’s highly tuned H100 kernels outperform FlashAttention‑3 in this specific corner case, even though FlashAttention‑3 dominates on longer sequences or non‐causal attention where its design assumptions hold true.
