\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### "Maximising log probability ratio" – Is this equivalent to using a language model as both policy and reward?
Question:
"Your language model is secretly a reward model" claims that our policy implicitly defines a reward via $\hat r_θ(x,y)=β \log \frac{π_θ}{π_{ref}}$. Does this mean that at convergence $\,π_θ=π^*$ and thus $\,ℎ̂r$ matches ground truth? Can we practically compute $ĥr(x,y)$ during training and use it as an explicit reward for other algorithms?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
Yes – the DPO derivation shows that the *implicit* reward  

\[
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\]

is the **exact** reward function that would make the policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) the **optimal KL‑constrained maximizer** of that reward.  
If training converges to the true optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\), then \(\hat r_{\theta}\) will coincide with the *true* (latent) reward \(r^{*}\) up to an arbitrary additive function of \(x\).  
Technically, you can evaluate \(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)\) at any time during training, but using it as an explicit reward for an external RL agent is usually not necessary – DPO already solves the RL problem in a single supervised‑learning step.

---

### 1.  What the derivation really means

The classic RLHF pipeline first learns a reward model \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) from preferences and then runs reinforcement learning to maximize

\[
\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \pi}[\,r_{\phi}(x,y)-\beta \log\!\tfrac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\,] .
\]

Under a KL‑constrained objective, the **optimal policy** for any reward \(r\) has the closed‑form Gibbs form

\[
\pi_r(y|x)= \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
          \exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
\tag{1}
\]

Inverting (1) gives a bijection between rewards and policies:

\[
r(x,y)= \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta\,\log Z(x).
\tag{2}
\]

The extra term \(\beta\,\log Z(x)\) does not affect *preferences*,
because for any pair \((y_1,y_2)\)

\[
\sigma(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2))
   = \sigma(\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_r(y_1|x)}{\pi_r(y_2|x)}).
\]
Hence all rewards that differ by an arbitrary function of \(x\) (the partition‑function term) generate **identical preference distributions**. This is exactly the equivalence relation discussed in §4.2 of the paper.

The key insight of DPO is: *instead of fitting a separate \(r_{\phi}\), we fit a policy directly and treat that policy’s log‑ratio to the reference as its own reward.*  
This implicit reward satisfies (2), so when you train a policy with the DPO loss (Eq. (8)), you are implicitly learning exactly the same optimal policy that would result from RL on an explicit reward.

---

### 2.  Does convergence guarantee “true” rewards?

If training converges to the **true optimal KL‑constrained policy** \(\pi^{*}\), then by construction

\[
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=
  \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
  = r^{*}(x,y)+c_x,
\]

where \(c_x=\beta\,\log Z^{*}(x)\) depends only on the prompt \(x.\)  
Because preferences depend only on differences \(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\), this constant cancels out.  
Therefore:

- The *shape* of \(\hat r_{\theta}\) matches that of \(r^{*}\);  
- The two rewards induce identical preference models;  
- The numerical values may differ by a prompt‑specific offset.

So, at convergence, computing “ground truth” on example completions would give you precisely the same ordering as if you had written down \(r^{*}\).  However, without access to human data you cannot confirm absolute alignment—you only know that your policy is optimal with respect to whatever preference data was supplied.

---

### 3.  Computing \(\hat r_{\theta}\) during training

Yes, any time you have a sampled context–completion pair \((x,y)\), you can evaluate

```python
log_p_theta = model.log_prob(x, y)
log_p_ref   = ref_model.log_prob(x, y)
hat_r = beta * (log_p_theta - log_p_ref)
```

The cost is just one forward pass through both networks (the reference model can be cached), so it is negligible compared with standard cross‑entropy training.  

In practice DPO authors do not usually extract this quantity for downstream use because:

1. **DPO already solves RLHLF in one step.**  
   You pick a single loss (Eq. (8)) that implicitly balances reward maximization and KL control—there’s no explicit “policy → reward → policy” loop.

2. **Uncertainty and bias:**  
   Until training finishes, \(\hat r_{\theta}\) is highly noisy and may mislead another algorithm that tries to optimise it further.

3. **Design choice:**  
   Most downstream systems rely on language‑model logits directly; re‑exposing them as “rewards” adds little pragmatic value.

Nevertheless, if you wish to use an *external RL agent*, you can feed it this cached sequence‑level score as a surrogate reward – it will be mathematically justified by Eq. (2).

---

### 4.  Real‑world implications

- **Why “language model Is Secretly a Reward Model”?**  
  The paper’s title encodes this equivalence: every LM already carries a built‑in notion of desirability through its log probabilities relative to some baseline (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)).  DPO simply leverages this representation instead of learning a separate network.

- **Policy vs Reward are two views of the same underlying object**: the “policy” view focuses on sampling completions; the “reward” view focuses on estimating how good those completions are against humans.  

- **Convergence guarantees:**  
  Under standard assumptions (sufficient capacity, expressive reference model, enough preference data), DPO converges to a stationary point that satisfies necessary optimality conditions for Eq. (7). Empirically this yields policies indistinguishable from PPO‑based RLHF but with far fewer hyperparameters and no sampling during fine‑tuning.

---

### Bottom line

*Training with DPO gives you directly an implicit reward function that would make your final policy optimal under that reward.*  
If you stop at convergence your LM’s log ratio w.r.t. its reference embodies a perfect surrogate for human preference; you can compute it on demand but typically there’s no need to turn it into a separate “reward network.” The equivalence holds purely because of the KL–constrained soft‑max relationship between rewards and policies demonstrated in Section 3–4 of the paper.
