\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does DPO outperform PPO even when PPO has access to ground‑truth rewards?
Question:
Figure 4 shows DPO achieving higher reward–KL frontiers than PPO (and even PPO‐GT). Intuitively I would expect an RL algorithm with an oracle reward to outperform a direct preference optimisation method. Could you unpack what gives DPO this advantage? Is it purely due to better optimisation dynamics or something inherent in modelling preferences via Bradley‑Terry?
Answer:
**Why DPO beats PPO (even PPO‑GT) in Figure 4**

Let’s first recap what the two plots are measuring.  
In the *reward–KL frontier* setting the authors have a *ground‑truth* reward
$ r_{\text{true}}(x,y) $ (a sentiment classifier).  
For every policy $\pi_{\theta}$ they evaluate

* the **average reward** $\mathbb E_{x,y\sim \pi_{\theta}}\bigl[\,r_{\text{true}}(x,y)\,\bigr]$
* the **sequence–level KL divergence** to the reference policy
  $\text{KL}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr)$

and plot one point for every hyper‑parameter sweep (e.g. different target KL
values). The “frontier” is the set of Pareto‑optimal points: for any point on
the curve you cannot improve reward without increasing KL, and vice versa.

PPO is a policy‑gradient algorithm that optimises the *exact*
constrained RL objective that DPO was derived from:

$$
\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\pi}\!\bigl[r_{\text{RLM}}(x,y)
-\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\bigr] . \tag{1}
$$

In practice PPO must learn a separate reward model $r_{\phi}$
(and usually much of the loss is a KL penalty on top of it).  
The authors compare PPO not only with a learned reward model but also with an
oracle that plugs in the *ground‑truth* reward into (1), i.e. “PPO‑GT”.
Why then does purely offline DPO produce a strictly better frontier?

---

### 1.  Exactness vs. sampling noise

Equation (1) can be solved *exactly*.  Lemma 4 in the paper shows that if you
replace $r_{\phi}$ with any reward in the equivalence class of the true
reward, the optimal policy is

$$
\pi^{*}(y|x)=
\tfrac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{\,r^{*}(x,y)/\beta}. \tag{2}
$$

This gives an analytic form for $\pi^{*}$.

PPO, on the other hand, must **sample** from its current policy to estimate
gradients, then clip them (the PPO loss does not match a surrogate of (2) at all times).  Even with an oracle reward this still introduces variance and bias: batches of trajectories are finite,
sequences are truncated, and early stages of training involve very noisy
estimates of $r_{\phi}$.  The optimisation landscape is therefore rough,
and PPO spends many updates chasing a drifting target.

DPO bypasses all that.  By reparameterising the reward as
$\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log{\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}
                                  {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}} $
the entire objective collapses to a simple **binary cross‑entropy**
between human preferences:

$$
\mathcal L_{\!DPO}=
-\,\mathbb E_{(x,w,\ell)} 
   \bigl[\log\sigma(
   \hat r_{\theta}(x,w)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,\ell))\bigr].
   \tag{3}
$$

No sampling of trajectories is required; every gradient step is deterministic
and uses full log‑probabilities from forward passes.  Thus DPO converges to
the optimum pole faster and with far lower variance than PPO.

---

### 2.  Adaptive importance weighting

A key piece of (3) is the factor

$$
\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,\ell)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,w))
      = \sigma(\beta[\log\tfrac{\pi_\ell}{\pi_\text{ref}^\ell}
                    - \log\tfrac{\pi_w}{\pi_\text{ref}^w}]).
   \tag{4}
$$

When the current policy already orders a pair correctly ($\hat r_w >
   \hat r_\ell$), this quantity is small – the update gives it little weight.
If it misorders ($\hat r_w < \hat r_\ell$), it receives a large weight.
Thus mispredictions are amplified while correct predictions are dampened.
This “importance weighting’’ is exactly what makes DPO so sample‑efficient:
the loss focuses on hard cases rather than drowning in easy ones.

PPO uses clipping to avoid large updates but treats every sampled trajectory
in roughly equal measure; mis‑ordered pairs do not get extra emphasis unless
they happen to be in a particular minibatch.  Consequently PPO may still
over‑update on easier samples and under‑update on hard ones, leading to a less
efficient trade‑off in the reward–KL plane.

---

### 3.  Implicit regularisation through KL

The KL constraint in RLHF acts like a trust region: we do not want to deviate too far from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
In DPO this bound appears automatically inside ($4$):
the log‑ratio between current policy and reference policy sits *inside*
the exponent that defines importance weighting.
Hence every step pushes toward higher human preference probability **while**
keeping ratios bounded by construction.

With PPO you must tune an external clipping parameter or perform repeated KL checks to maintain stability.
The paper shows that even when tuning `target KL` for PPO,
the resulting policies lie below DPO’s frontier: either they get high reward at high KL or low reward at low KL, never both well.

---

### 4. Inherited properties of Bradley–Terry

“Better” here also means *more principled*.  
The Bradley–Terry model only cares about *differences* of rewards:
$p^*(w>\ell|x)= 
   \sigma(r^*(w)-r^*(l))$.
Therefore classes of rewards that differ by an additive function $f(x)$
induce exactly the same preference distribution.
DPO exploits this by reparameterising rewards as log‑ratio terms that are automatically normalized across all candidate completions.
Concretely, any valid Bradley–Terry reward can be expressed as 
$r(x,y)=c+\beta \log\tfrac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y|x)}$.
Thus DPO’s optimisation lives in *exactly* the same functional space as PPO’s RL optimization—no loss of expressivity occurs—but it solves it via closed form rather than stochastic gradient ascent on noisy samples.

That explains why “even with access to ground truth rewards” PPO cannot beat DPO: both optimise over identical optimum sets, but PPO's path to them is noisy and variance-prone; DPO takes a direct route.

---

### Takeaway

- **Exact solution**: DPO derives an analytic optimal policy from each preference pair; PPO must approximate via sampling.
- **Reduced variance & better optimisation dynamics**: The cross‑entropy loss uses full probabilities and an adaptive weight on hard pairs, leading to sharper gradients.
- **Implicit regularisation**: KL appears naturally inside importance weights; no separate hyper‑parameter search needed beyond $\beta/\alpha/\kappa$, which is already tuned for RLHF.
- **Same representational power**: Because Bradley–Terry rewards are underdetermined up to an additive function, reparameterising as log‑ratios does not restrict what can be learned—only changes how we arrive at it.

In short, while both methods target essentially the same constrained optimisation problem, DPO eliminates sampling noise, provides richer gradient signals through adaptive weighting, and respects KL regularisation out-of-the-box—hence its superior performance even compared to oracle‐reward‐based PPO.
