Chapter 7: # 7. Comparative Instability Analysis of Actor–Critic RLHF  

In this chapter we examine, from a theoretical standpoint, why the actor–critic (AC) style methods that underpin most contemporary reinforcement‑learning‑from‑human‑feedback (RLHF) pipelines suffer from high variance and sensitivity to hyper‑parameters, and how the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) scheme sidesteps these drawbacks.  We begin by re‑deriving the RLHF objective as a constrained inference problem (the *control‑as‑inference* perspective), then show how the normalizing term in the AC objective plays the role of a soft value function.  Finally, we expose the limitations of implementing this term with a learned critic in classic PPO and contrast it with the implicit reward parameterization used by DPO.

## 7.1 Control‑as‑Inference view of KL‑constrained RL  

The RLHF training loop traditionally seeks a policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) that maximizes an expected reward \(r_{\phi}\) while staying close to a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  Formally this is expressed as  

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\left[
    r_{\phi}(x,y)-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)
 \right]. \tag{7.1}
\]

Equation (7.1) is a *KL‑constrained* reward maximization objective: the first term rewards high‑return trajectories, while the second penalizes distributional drift from the reference model.  In what follows we show that this optimization is equivalent to *control as inference* (Cai et al., 2019; 
Anderson et al., 2020), providing an intuitive understanding of how each component behaves.

### 7.1.1 Likelihood of trajectories under controlled dynamics  

Consider any prefix \(x_t=(x_1,\dots,x_t)\) and a dialogue trajectory \(y=(y_1,\dots,y_T)\).  
Define a *controlled* trajectory distribution

\[
p_{\theta}(y \mid x)=
    \frac{\exp\!\bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{t=1}^T r_{\phi}(x_t,y_t)\bigr)}
         {Z(x)}\,p_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x), \tag{7.2}
\]
where \(Z(x)\) is a normalizing constant ensuring that \(p_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\) sums to one across all completions \(y\).  
Equation (7.2) states that we tilt the reference distribution \(p_{\text{ref}}\) by an exponentiated reward; the selection probability of any particular completion is proportional to its total reward under \(\pi_{\phi}\).

Taking logarithms and rearranging terms yields

\[
r'_{\theta}(x,y)
      = \log p_{\theta}(y|x)- \log p_{\text{ref}}(y|x)
      = \frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{t=1}^T r_{\phi}(x_t,y_t)+ \log Z(x).
                                      \tag{7.3}
\]

Here \(r'_{\theta}\) can be viewed as an *implicit reward*, comprising two parts:
the raw summed rewards from the model \(r_{\phi}\), and a fixed offset \(\log Z(x)\) depending only on the prompt \(x\).

### 7.1.2 Soft value function surface  

The term \(\log Z(x)\) in equation (7.3) is exactly the log–partition function of
the controlled dynamics:

\[
Z(x)=
    \sum_{y} p_{\text{ref}}(y | x)\,
           \exp\!\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{\beta}\sum_{t=1}^T r_{\phi}(x_t,y_t)\Bigr].
                                     \tag{7.4}
\]

In reinforcement learning parlance, this quantity corresponds to the **soft value function** or *potential* over states induced by the reference policy and reward function:

\[
V^{s}_{r_{\phi},\,p_{\text{ref}}}(x)
   = \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[
           \sum_{t=1}^{T} r_{\phi}(x_t,y_t)
            +   \beta\,H[p_{\text{ref}}(\,.\,|\,X)]  \right]
    = -\,\beta\,\log Z(x),
    \tag{7.5}
\]
where \(H[\cdot]\) denotes Shannon entropy.
Thus, up to constant scaling, **\( -\,V^{s}_{r_{\phi},\,p_{\text{ref}}}(x)=
   \beta\,\log Z(x)\)**.

Equation (7.5) shows that if we knew the soft value function exactly,
we could replace the implicit offset in \(r'_\theta(x,y)\) with a known constant,
removing any dependence on trajectory sampling.

## 7.2 Normalizing term as soft value function surface versus learned baseline  

Equation (7.3) suggests that an optimal policy under KL constraints can be obtained if we can evaluate or approximate 
\(V^{s}_{r_\phi,p_\text{ref}}\).  
Two practical strategies therefore emerge:

| Strategy | Description | Assumptions | Variance | Implementation burden |
|----------|-------------|------------|----------|------------------------|
| **Explicit baseline** | Train a separate critic network to approximate \(V^{s}_{r_\phi,p_\text{ref}}\). | Requires enough data to learn accurate values; assumes critic generalizes well across prompts.| High – critic errors propagate directly into policy gradients.| Complex – scheduling of learning rates, risk of overfitting |
| **Implicit normalization** | Embed normalizing factor directly into policy update via closed‐form solution.| Requires closed‐form expression for optimal policy (see §7.3). | Low – no external estimator contributes stochastic noise.| Low – single loss function |

### 7.2.1 The role of baselines in PPO  

PPO implements an actor–critic method where the **critic** estimates per‑action values
\(V_\psi(x,y)=V(y,x;\psi)\).
The surrogate loss used during PPO training typically contains a *GAE* advantage estimator:

\[
A^\mathrm{\tiny GAE}_t = G_t - V_\psi(x,y_t),          \tag{7.6}
\]
where \(G_t=\sum_{k=t}^T r_k + V_\psi(x,y_T)\).
If \(V_\psi\) deviates from its true counterpart,
the advantage becomes biased; variance inflates because small changes in 
\(V_\psi\) can lead to large swings in estimated returns.
Moreover, PPO employs importance–sampling ratios based on current and old policies,

\(r_t = {\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_\theta^{old}(a_t|s_t)}}.\)

When combined with an inaccurate baseline, overall gradient estimates are noisy,
and policy updates become erratic.
This explains why PPO often needs elaborate KL clipping, early stopping,
and careful tuning of learning rates or clipping hyper‑parameters.

# Chapter 7  
## Comparative Instability Analysis of Actor–Critic RLHF  

In this chapter we dissect why widely used actor–critic variants for reinforcement‐learning–from‐human‐feedback (RLHF)—chiefly Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO or PPO‐GT)—exhibit high variance and strong dependence on hyper–parameters such as clipping factors or baseline architectures, whereas Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) evades these pitfalls by reformulating the objective as a closed‐form supervised classification loss.

---

### 7.1 Control–as–Inference view of KL‑constrained RL  

The RLHF training objective traditionally takes the form  

> **KL‑constrained reward maximization**  
>  $$
>   J(\pi)=
>     {\mathbb E}_{x\dotsc y}
>     \!\Bigl[\, r^{*}(x,y)
>             -   \beta\: {\rm KL}\!\bigl(
>                     {\rm Pr}[y|x;\pi]\
>                     {\rm Pr}[y|x;\pi^R]
>                  )
>            \Bigr] ,
> $$

where $\pi$ denotes our target language model policy,
$\pi^R$ is a *reference* policy usually obtained from supervised fine tuning,
$r^{*}$ is an unknown ground truth human preference signal,
and $\beta>0$ controls how much $\pi$ may drift away from $\pi^R$.

#### 7.1 1 Deriving an analytical optimum

When $\beta$ is fixed and $r$ is any real–valued function on completions $y$, it is possible to show that an *optimal* solution for $\pi$ obeying Eq.(×afc...?) satisfies

> **Optimality condition**  
>  $$
>    {\rm Pr}[y|x;\,\hat{\pi}]
>    =  
>      Z^{-1}(x)\;
>      {\rm Pr}[y|x;\,\pi^R]\;
>      e^{\, r(x,y)/{\beta}}
> $$  
>  where $Z(x)=
>     {\displaystyle 
>        \sum_{y'} {\rm Pr}[y'|x;\,\pi^R] 
>           e^{\, r(x,y')/\beta}}
> $.

The derivation—slightly adapted from classical results on constrained MDPs—emerges from solving a Lagrangian with respect to $\pi$ while enforcing both normalization ($Z(x)$ acts as Lagrange multiplier for probability conservation).

Rearranging gives us

$$
    r(x,y)
    =   \beta\: 
        {\color{lightgreen}{ }
         }\!\left(
           {\large
              {\ln{\hat{\pi}(y|x)}-{\ln{\pi^R(y|x)}}}
           }
        +              
            c(x)
       	                      }
    $$
with $c(x)= -\,{\ln Z(x)}$, which depends only on prompt $x$.  
Note that $c(x)$ cancels when considering *differences* between two answer candidates:
$$
     r(x,y_1)- r(x,y_2)
     =   
       {\color{lightgreen}{ }\! }(\ln{\hat{\pi}(y_1)}-\ln{\hat{\pi}(y_2)})
       -
       (\ln{\pi^R(y_1)}-\ln{\pi^R(y_2})) .
$$

Consequently, human preferences modeled with Bradley–Terry,

$$
   P(y_1\succeq y_2|x)=
   {e^{r(x,y_1)}},
      {e^{r(x,y_2)}
      }^{-1},
$$

depend solely on how $\hat{\pi}$ distributes mass relative to $\pi^R$, **without any reference to $c(x)$**, which translates into DPO’s key insight: we don’t need an explicit reward decomposition if ultimately all we care about are pairwise preference probabilities.

#### 7.1 2 Soft value vs hard return

Expression (*optimality condition*) reveals that $\hat{\pi}$ results from exponentiating $r/\beta$ against $\pi^R$. If we denote 

$$
 V_s^\star(x):=-\,{\ln Z(x)},
$$

then $-V_s^\star$ acts as a *soft value function*: it captures how much weight must be shifted away from $\pi^R$ so that higher rewards become more probable under $\hat{\pi}$.
Because it depends only on prompt $x$, it can be viewed as an additional “potential” overlaying the probability mass initially assigned by $\π^R$.

In contrast, standard actor–critic schemes explicitly regard this potential as part of their temporal‐difference targets — and therefore their critics—leading us directly into Section 7 3.

### 7.2 Normalizing term as soft value surface versus learned baseline  

#### Learning aside – computing $Z$ directly

Suppose never had to estimate $Z$.  
Given access to samples $(X,Y)$ drawn from ${π^R}$ we could approximate

$$
 Z(X)\approx 
     E_{Y∼π^R}[e^{r(X,Y)/β}]
       = e^{-V_s^\star(X)},
$$

by straightforward Monte Carlo because both $\π^R$ and its support are known.
Plugging this exact normalizer back into Eq.(….) yields coefficient-free updates for $\hat{\π}$: there would simply be no extra term representing an evaluation function in our policy gradient estimate.
This is precisely what DPO exploits—the equality between Eq.(…) and Eq.(°), allowing us to absorb $Z(X)$ inside logits amenable to cross-entropy training:
$$
 L_{DPO}
=
- E_{(X,Y_w,Y_l)}
   [  log σ(
         β ln[πθ(Y_w)/π_R(Y_w)]
       -         β ln[πθ(Y_l)/π_R(Y_l)]
    ] .
$$

#### When building *a critic*

Actor–critic methods such as PPO avoid needing explicit sampling of many trajectories by adding a learned value head $V_\psi(X,Y)$ that approximates expected returns under some reference dynamics.
The usual critic loss looks like 

$$
 L_V(ψ)=E[(G- Vψ(X,Y))²],
```

with discounted returns $$ G=\sum_{t≥k} γ^(t-k)( r+γ Vψ…) . $$

But crucially:
- The critic must infer both immediate rewards **and** future discounted returns in closed form.
- Any mismatch between true soft values ($-β·ln Z(X)$) and its approximation inflates advantage estimates: see Eq.(13).  
When critics are imperfect—a frequent situation with high-dimensional language policies—significant variance creeps into ∇θϱ “actor” updates:
the gradient now carries contributions both from ratio clipping φ-bounds and noisy advantage estimates.
PPO addresses this empirically via clipping or adaptive learning rates but still depends acutely on:
- Properly tuned clipping ranges;
- Careful selection of entropy bonuses or KL penalties;
- Sufficiently expressive but not overfit critics;

all adding operational cost and susceptibility to failure modes.

#### Summarising variance sources

Overall:
- The *normalizer* plays exactly one role: it removes bias introduced by changing reference probabilities.
- In PPO/the red critical chain modelling these offsets leads back towers of estimation errors.

### 7.3 Why PPO’s critic incurs variance without explicit normalisation  

Re-writing the classic PPO objective yields
    
$$ 
 L_{ac}=E_{X,Y∼πθ}\biggl[ A^\circ_T · min\dfrac{
              πθ(a_T |X)}
               {π†(a_T | X)}
            , ε⟩ τ    
          +α·H[πθ]          \\           
          -β·ₙ KL[ πθ|| π_R ]  ,
$$$$ 
 
 where  
 classes `A^\circ` = `g` – `v` , g being discounted cumulative return close-to-optimal uses single-step returns `g` plus next-state estimated value `v` . 

Because `v` ≈ `$β· ln Z(X)` was never explicitly calibrated against montez-carlo estimates at training time there exists systematic bias.

When we reshape logistical losses incorporating *exact* new normaliser terms via algebraic manipulation — which DPO does — we eliminate both  
(i) inflation produced by exponentiating high reward differences (**numerical overflows/underflows**);  
(ii­or any loosely-matched auxiliary regularisers that act like added penalties or rewards but at each step internally mimic KL diverging behaviours).

Therefore DPO features “no baseline” yet achieves identical control guarantees as RL algorithms: it almost literally solves Eq.(∀?) by reparameterisation rather than optimisation in latent space through weights adjustments → convergence speed ↑ , sample complexity ↓ , robustness ↑ .

## Summary  

The study shows mathematically why actor–critic approaches commonly used in RLHF have inherent instability due solely to how they try handle normalisation—either via hard-to-train critics or lifted unclipped gradients—and why DPO sidesteps these problems through closed-form reparameterisation using only logits and cross entropy training on pairwise preferences.
By reframing human preferences directly within a probabilistic control-as-inference framework and embedding soft-value information inside language model logits themselves, DPO essentially removes one entire PAC‐policy optimization cycle: it no longer needs sampling plus bias-correcting baselines or expensive off-policy corrections.
This yields markedly more stable training dynamics, fewer hyperparams to tune while delivering performance comparable or superior to conventional RLHF pipelines across sentiment modulation, summarisation and dialogue datasets studied in our experiments._END_
