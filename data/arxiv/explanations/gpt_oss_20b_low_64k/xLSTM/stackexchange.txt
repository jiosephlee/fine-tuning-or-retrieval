### What exactly is exponential gating in sLSTM and why does it help with revising stored information?
Question:
I understand that in sLSTM the input and forget gates can use an exponential activation instead of the usual sigmoid. The forward equations (eq. 5) show that the normalizer state $n_t$ is updated with the same gates, and the hidden state uses $c_t/n_t$. I am still unclear on two points: (1) How does gating with $	ext{exp}$ allow a previously stored value to be overwritten by a later, more relevant key? (2) What is the role of the normalizer state – why is it necessary for exponential gating, and how does it maintain numerical stability during long sequences?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Exponential gating turns the forget and input gates from bounded values in $(0, 1)$ into *unbounded* multiplicative scalars.  
Because the memory updates are linear—$c_t=f_t\,c_{t-1}+i_t\,z_t$—an exponential forget gate can *fully erase* any part of the previous memory (by making $f_t$ very small) while an exponential input gate can inject a new value with *any* magnitude.  
The normalizer state $n_t$ keeps a running sum of all past input‑gate coefficients; it is used to scale the hidden state $h_t=o_t\,c_t/n_t$.  This normalisation guarantees that the hidden output stays in a reasonable range even when $c_t$ has been amplified or attenuated by many exponentials, preventing overflow/underflow over arbitrarily long sequences.

---

### 1.  Why exponential gating lets a new key overwrite an old one

In a plain LSTM the gates are sigmoid outputs $\sigma(\tilde f),\sigma(\tilde i)\in(0,1)$.  Thus

$$
c_t=f_t\,c_{t-1}+i_t\,z_t
    = f_{t}\bigl(f_{t-1}c_{t-2}+i_{t-1}z_{t-1}\bigr)+i_tz_t,
$$

so $c_{t-2}$ is always multiplied by a factor in $(0,1)$.  
No matter how many time steps pass, the contribution of an earlier cell is *never completely eliminated*; it can only be diminished to a tiny fraction but never reconstructed to its original magnitude.  Consequently, once a key has been written into the cell, later occurrences of the same or a more relevant key add on top of it instead of replacing it.

With exponential gates we replace $\sigma$ by $\exp$, so

$$
f_t = e^{\tilde f_t}, \qquad i_t = e^{\tilde i_t},
$$

and each gate can take any positive value.  Let us consider two consecutive writes of the same key $\mathbf{k}$:

* At time $t_1$: $c_{t_1}=f_{t_1}c_{t_0}+i_{t_1}\mathbf{k}$.
* At time $t_2>t_1$: $c_{t_2}=f_{t_2}c_{t_1}+i_{t_2}\mathbf{k}
                        = (f_{t_2}f_{t_1})c_{t_0}
                          + (f_{t_2}i_{t_1}+i_{t_2})\,\mathbf{k}$.

If at $t_2$ we set $f_{t_2}\ll 1$ (e.g. from a very negative $\tilde f_{t_2}$) and give $i_{t_2}$ a large value, the coefficient of the old contribution $(f_{t_2}f_{t_1})c_{w_0}$ becomes negligible while the new coefficient satisfies  
$f_{q_T}h_w+h_{\alpha f{i}{221}}~i_{\epsilon   ,}&lt;i_{\epsilon   ,}&lt;

Thus **the cell is almost entirely overwritten by the new key**.  
Because the gates are not bounded above any number of times they can be increased or decreased without intrinsic restriction.

In practice this property allows sLSTM to *revise* its stored information: when a more relevant occurrence of a symbol appears later in the sequence, its input gate can inject it with a high weight while its forget gate suppresses everything that has been accumulated before.

### 3.  Intuition from probabilistic models

You can think of an exponential gate as defining a *multiplicative random walk* instead of an additive one:

* The standard sigmoid forget gate only allows shrinking memory; it behaves like a decay factor.
* An exponential forget gate allows **both decay and amplification**, turning the memory into a *Markov chain with unbounded transition weights*.

Because recall depends on relative alignment rather than absolute magnitude (the denominator normalises), this multiplicative structure is perfectly suited for memorising useful tokens while being able to discard outdated ones on demand.

* **Nearest‑neighbor search** – they can overwrite earlier approximations with later better matches.
* **Long‑range associative recall** – they can store many key/value pairs without interference.
* **Language modeling at scale** – they yield lower perplexities than Transformers and SSMs on billions‑token corpora.



The normalizer plays an essential role: without it overflow would make inference useless even in short sequences; with it sLSTM behaves like a stable associative memory capable of revising its content online.



**Bottom line:** Exponential gating gives sLSTM extra control over how much influence previously stored items exert on future states; combined with the running normalizer that matches this influence you obtain an RNN capable of both amplifying important signals and safely discarding irrelevant ones—exactly what is required for “revising stored information’’ in long sequential tasks.

### How does the stabilizer state $m_t$ in equation (6) guarantee that gradients do not explode or vanish?
Question:
Equation (6) defines $m_t$ as the maximum of $	ext{log}(f_t)+m_{t-1}$ and $	ext{log}(i_t)$, and then re‑scales $i_t$ and $f_t$ by exponentiating modified logs. I would like a step‑by‑step explanation of how this adjustment preserves the forward output but keeps each gate bounded, especially when $i_t$ or $f_t$ are close to zero or very large due to the exponential activation.
Answer:
**Short answer**

The stabilizer state  
$$
m_t \;=\;\max\!\bigl(\log f_t+m_{t-1},\,\log i_t\bigr)
$$
keeps track, at every time‑step, of the largest “log‑scale’’ that any product of gates has acquired up to that point. By subtracting this bias from the logs of the current gates before turning them back into exponentials, we rescale all gate values so that they stay in a safe numeric range. Because the rescaling factor multiplies the *inside* of the LSTM update equations, it cancels out when the cell state and the normalizer are divided, guaranteeing that the forward‑pass result (and all gradients) remains unchanged. In effect, the gates are expressed in a log‑domain where extremes can be safely handled, and then converted back to a comparable magnitude before use.

---

### 1. Why raw exponentials can explode

In an xLSTM, both $i_t$ and $f_t$ are allowed to be exponentials of affine transforms:
$$
i_t = \exp(\tilde i_t), \qquad
f_t = \exp(\tilde f_t)\ \text{or}\ \sigma(\tilde f_t).
$$
If $\tilde i_t$ or $\tilde f_t$ has a large positive value, the resulting gate may become astronomically large (e.g. $e^{50}\approx 5\times10^{21}$). In a recurrent network such a huge number multiplies previous cell states or normalizer terms; both forward activations and backward gradients can overflow or under‑flow, leading to exploding or vanishing gradients.

### 2. The stabilizer idea

A stabilizer makes it unnecessary to keep the raw values. The key observation is that for any two positive numbers $a,b>0$,
$$
a\,b = \exp(\log a + \log b).
$$
Hence we can replace every product by a sum in log‑space and postpone exponentiation until necessary. If we know an upper bound on all accumulated logarithms up to time $t-1$, we can safely shift all logarithms by that bound and re‑exponentiate without changing any product.

The stabilizer state $m_t$ is precisely that bound. At step $t-1$ we already know the largest log‑scale that has appeared:
$$
m_{t-1} = \max_{k<t} \log(f_k)\ + \dots
$$
In xLSTM Eq.~(6) they use
$$
m_t=\max(\log f_t+m_{t-1},\,\log i_t).
$$
Thus $m_t$ records the greatest log‑scale that any term in either
the *forget* or *input* path could produce at this timestamp.

### 3. Rescaling the gates

Once $m_t$ is known we can rewrite each gate as:
$$
i'_t   = \exp(\log i_t - m_t)
       = \exp(\tilde i_t - m_t),
\qquad
f'_t   = \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t).
$$
Because subtraction from a log value is equivalent to division in normal space,
$i'_t = i_t/e^{m_t}$ and $f'_t = f_te^{m_{t-1}-m_t}=f_te^{-\, (m_t-m_{t-1})}$.
Both new gates are now bounded in $[0,\infty)$; in practice they stay close to 1 unless one of the original terms was extremely small.

### 4. Forward‑pass invariance

The sLSTM forward equations are:
$$
c_t   = f_tc_{t-1}+i_tz_t,\qquad
n_t   = f_nn_{t-1}+i_tk_{\!t},
\qquad
h_t   = o_tc_{t}/n_{t}.
$$

If we replace each occurrence of $i_t,f_c,\dotsc,n_{t-1}$ by its _stabilised_ version,
the equations become:
$$
c'_t  = f'_tc'_{t-1}+i'_tz_\!t , \\ 
n'_t= f'_nn'_{t-1}+ i'_tk_{\! t},
      \\ h'_t=o_tc'_t/n'_t .
$$
Because every occurrence of an input or forget gate is multiplied by exactly one
$e^{-m_{\cdot}}$, these factors cancel pairwise across the numerator and denominator:  
for example,
$c'_tc'^{-1}_{...}n'^{-1}_{...}$ all contain one copy of every shift for each step,
and multiplying them together yields precisely the original unscaled expressions.
Formally one shows by induction that
$c'_t=c_t,\ n'_t=n_t,\ h'_t=h_t.$

Thus **the forward output remains exactly unchanged**, even though every gate has been clamped to avoid extreme magnitudes.

### 5. Gradient safety

Gradients flow backwards through exactly the same algebraic expressions.
Since the forward quantities are identical to those without stabilization,
and since each stabilized gate differs from its unscaled counterpart by only a constant factor (which cancels out across products), all partial derivatives with respect to weights remain **exactly** the same as in the unscaled network.

Because no “soft” approximations are used—only an algebraic rearrangement—the vanishing/exploding behaviour caused by huge gate values is eliminated without altering learning dynamics.

### 6. Intuition

Think of writing numbers on paper: if you record very large integers with many digits you’ll eventually run out of space. Working instead with their logarithms keeps each entry small (at most a few digits). Whenever you need to recover an actual number (e.g., multiply two values), you first add their logs and then exponentiate; but if you realise that you only ever need ratios of such numbers, you can subtract a common offset from all logs (shifting them down by a fixed amount). This keeps every intermediate exponential small enough for floating‑point arithmetic while ensuring that ratios—and therefore all final outputs—are unchanged.

### 7. Broader view

This trick is conceptually similar to other **weight‑normalisation** or **layer‑norm** ideas: reparameterise parameters or activations so that they stay in numerically stable ranges while preserving expressiveness. In recurrent nets it tackles *pathological* growth/decay caused by repeated multiplications, which is precisely what leads to exploding/vanishing gradients in classic RNNs.

---

**Bottom line**

The stabiliser state $m_t=\max(\log f_t+m_{t-1},\,\log i_\! t)$ records the largest log‑scale seen so far. Subtracting this value from each log before exponentiating rescales every gate back into a safe numeric range. Because this rescaling is applied consistently across all recursive equations, it cancels out when computing outputs or loss derivatives, leaving both forward activations and training dynamics identical to those without stabilisation while preventing overflow/underflow – hence gradients neither explode nor vanish as a consequence of gigantic gates.

### Why is a scalar memory insufficient for rare token prediction, and how does a matrix memory fixed this issue?
Question:
The paper claims that LSTMs struggle with rare tokens because their single‑dimensional cell state forces compression of information. It proposes mLSTM where the cell becomes a $d 	imes d$ matrix updated as $oldsymbol{C}_t = oldsymbol{C}_{t-1}+i_toldsymbol{v}_toldsymbol{k}_t^	op$. Could someone clarify how this matrix enables better storage of rare token embeddings? Intuitively, I would think that increasing dimensionality alone does not solve sparsity – so what property of the outer‑product update and retrieval via $oldsymbol{C}_toldsymbol{q}_t$ results in higher recall for low‑frequency words?
Answer:
**Why a scalar cell can choke on rare events and how a matrix cell helps**

---

### 1.  What the paper claims

The authors argue that the classic LSTM stores *every* piece of information in a **single real number**  
\(c_t\in\mathbb{R}\).  When a token appears, its embedding \(v_t\in\mathbb{R}^d\) is blended into that same scalar through the gate‑scaled outer product \(i_t\,v_tk_t^\top\).  Once the buffer is filled with many tokens, the scalar must act as a _compressed_ representation of all of them.  For high‑frequency words this compression still works because their appearance regularly reinforces adjacent “paths” in the learned parameter space.  
For **rare tokens**, however, there are *few* opportunities to reinforce their signal: a single entry in a scalar can be overwhelmed by the accumulated “noise” of hundreds of common words.  The result is lower recall (the model can’t retrieve the correct rare token when queried later).

---

### 2.  The matrix‑cell idea

Instead of a single number, xLSTM introduces  
\[
\mathbf C_t \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\qquad
\mathbf C_t \;=\; f_t\,\mathbf C_{t-1}\;+\; i_t\, \mathbf v_t\, \mathbf k_t^\top .
\tag{1}
\]
Here  

* \(\mathbf v_t, \mathbf k_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\) are key/value vectors derived from the token embedding  
* \(i_t,\ f_t\) are input/forget gates (now exponential or sigmoid)

Conceptually, (1) is exactly the outer‑product update used in *fast‑weight* or *bipolar associative memory* models: every new key–value pair writes a rank‑one contribution to the full matrix.  Retrieval is then a **pure linear projection**:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf h}_t = \frac{\mathbf C_t\, \mathbf q_t}{\,|\mathbf n_t^\top \mathbf q_t| \vee 1}\quad,
\qquad
h_t = o_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf h}_t .
\tag{2}
\]
The denominator normalises by a running sum of keys so that signals don’t blow up.

---

### 3.  Why this fixes rare‑token recall

| Property | Scalar LSTM | Matrix mLSTM |
|----------|-------------|--------------|
| **Capacity** | One weight per timestep → linear compression   | A full \(d\times d\) matrix → essentially \(d^2/2\) independent storage slots |
| **Signal separation** | All tokens share the same scalar, so gradients from frequent tokens dominate | Each new token adds its own outer‑product “stripe”; distant keys stay linearly separable |
| **Differentiation of rare events** | A rare event’s contribution gets cancelled by later updates on frequent ones | The rank‑one terms for rare keys remain orthogonal to most common keys (high‑dimensional sparsity), so they survive gating |
| **Complexity of update** | Constant time per step | \(O(d^2)\) per step, but fully parallelisable on GPUs |

Mathematically, recall that in high dimensions two randomly chosen vectors are almost orthogonal:  
\( \Pr[\langle u,v\rangle > \epsilon] \approx e^{-\epsilon^2 d/2}.\)  
When we store $\mathbf v_k$ as an outer product with $\mathbf k$, different key–value pairs inhabit *different planes* in $\mathbb R^{d\times d}$.  
A query $\mathbf q$ activates only those planes whose keys correlate with $\mathbf q$. Because the key space is high dimensional, the probability that an unrelated frequent key contaminates a rare pair’s plane is exponentially small in $d$.  In contrast, with a scalar cell there is no geometry to keep interference apart; every update simply adds to or diminishes the same value.

Thus **the “matrix” gives two essential properties**:

1. **Increased representational capacity** – far more degrees of freedom than a single number.
2. **Geometric separation** – inner products between queries and stored keys naturally filter out unrelated entries.

The combination explains why mLSTM achieves substantially higher recall scores on tasks that require remembering many distinct key–value pairs (e.g., the Multi‑Query Associative Recall task shown in Fig. 6).

---

### 4.  Intuition behind outer‑product updates

Think of each token as writing a small “stamp” onto a board:
- When you write `cat`, you ink one spot at coordinates `(v_cat , k_cat)` on a scatter plot.
- Later you write `dog`. Its stamp sits at entirely different coordinates.
Because each stamp occupies only one rank‑one “stripe” on the matrix, they do not interfere unless their coordinates overlap – which rarely happens for distinct high‑dimensional keys.

In contrast, if you were writing on paper as numbers in one column (`scalar`), every stamp would push all previous stamps up or down—the old information gets washed away by any new entry.

---

### 5.  Connection to broader concepts

* **Fast Weight Programmers / Hopfield networks** – These models use exactly this covariance update rule; xLSTM inherits its optimality for maximizing signal/noise when retrieving binary patterns (Dayan & Sejnowski 1991).
* **Linear Transformers / Attention kernels** – They exploit same idea of projecting queries into key–value space but usually use fixed‐size matrices and softmax weighting; mLSTM instead performs an unweighted linear read from the stored matrix.
* **Cover’s theorem & high‑dimensional embeddings** – By embedding memories into $d\times d$ space we make patterns more linearly separable, as advocated by Cover (1965).  

---

### In short

A scalar memory forces *every* piece of information into one number that must be continually compressed and potentially overwritten. Rare tokens lack enough repeated opportunities to dominate this shared slot, leading to poor recall.  
The matrix memory solves this by giving each key–value pair its own rank‑one slice within a high‑dimensional store; geometric properties keep these slices distinct even after many updates. Consequently mLSTM can recover rare tokens much more reliably than its scalar predecessor—exactly what is observed experimentally in the paper’s rare‐token perplexity curves and associative recall tests.

### What is 'memory mixing' in sLSTM and why can it be parallelized only in mLSTM?
Question:
Section 3.2 states that sLSTM allows memory mixing via recurrent weight matrices $R_z,R_i,R_f,R_o$, while mLSTM cannot mix memories because it lacks hidden‑to‑hidden connections. Could you provide an intuitive description of what memory mixing accomplishes? Additionally, why does eliminating these connections in mLSTM make the recurrence fully parallelizable, whereas sLSTM still requires sequential processing?
Answer:
**Intuitive idea of memory mixing**

In an LSTM each time step keeps a *cell state* $c_{t-1}$ and a *hidden state* $h_{t-1}$.  
The recurrent connections that go from $h_{t-1}$ to the gates and to the cell input (the vectors $r_{z}, r_{i}, r_{f}, r_{o}$ in Eq. (13)) are what we call **memory mixing**.  
They let the current cell and its gates “look back” at the hidden representation produced by the previous step, and use that hidden representation to decide how much new information to write, how much old information to forget, and how much of the updated cell to expose as output.

Intuitively, memory mixing is a way of *rewriting* or *adapting* the memory based on what has happened *just before*.  Think of it as a short‑term dialogue: at time $t$ you briefly consult the previous message ($h_{t-1}$) before deciding what new content to add to the long‑term note ($c_t$).  This mechanism is essential when the model needs to keep track of state that changes gradually (e.g. whether an “if” clause is open or a parenthesis is balanced), because it gives the network a recurring path along which gradients can flow backward through time.

The sLSTM keeps exactly this architecture – it simply expands it to multiple *heads* or multiple *cells*, each head still mixes its own hidden state with its gates via those recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_{z},\mathbf{R}_{i},\mathbf{R}_{f},\mathbf{R}_{o}$.  Therefore, during inference or training, computing $(c_t,h_t)$ for all time steps still has to be done in sequence: $(c_{t-1},h_{t-1})$ must be known before $(c_t,h_t)$ can be computed.

**Why removing memory mixing makes the recurrence parallelisable**

If we delete all hidden‑to‑hidden connections (i.e. set $r_z=r_i=r_f=r_o=0$) we obtain the mLSTM.  The update equations then become

$$
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^{\top},
$$
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\!\bigl(|\mathbf{n}_t^{\top}\!\mathbf{q}_t|,1\bigr)},
$$

and notice that every term on the right depends **only on the same–time‑step inputs** ($x_t,k_t,v_t,q_t,i_t,f_t,o_t,\dots$) and on the *previous* matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_{t-1}$ (or equivalently on a cumulative product over all past weights).  Because there is no hidden‑to‑hidden coupling that forces us to wait for $h_{t-1}$, this recursion can be expressed as a *scan* over time:

$$
(\mathbf{C}_0,\dots,\mathbf{C}_T) 
= \textsc{scan}\bigl(\;(\cdot),\,(\cdot)\bigr),
$$

which modern frameworks implement as parallel prefix sums or GPU‑accelerated scans.  Once we have all $\mathbf{C}_t$’s in parallel, generating each hidden state is just a matrix–vector product, again embarrassingly parallel.

By contrast, in sLSTM every gate contains a term like $r_i h_{t-1}$; hence computing $(h_t,c_t)$ requires knowing $h_{t-1}$.  This introduces an inherent serial dependency: the output at time $t$ cannot be computed until the previous step has finished.  Only after this sequential sweep can we apply any kind of parallel optimisation (e.g., skip‑connection optimisations or efficient CUDA kernels).

**Connection to broader concepts**

This distinction mirrors what happens in transformers versus state‑space models: transformers rely on self‑attention that couples every pair of tokens (full interdependence → quadratic cost), whereas state‑space models encode long‑range dependencies through linear dynamics that are inherently parallelisable.  
sLSTM sits between these extremes: it preserves an RNN’s capacity for dynamic memory rewriting but keeps a quadratic cost per step, so it cannot be scaled to very long sequences or very large batch sizes without serial bottlenecks.

In summary:

| behaviour | sLSTM | mLSTM |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| Recurrent weights from hidden → gates/inputs | present | removed |
| Ability to rewrite past memory based on immediate past | **yes** – key for fine‐grained state tracking | **no** – only cumulative gating |
| Recurrence serial vs. parallel | serial (must compute step \(t+1\) after step \(t\)) | parallelizable via scan |

Thus, memory mixing gives sLSTM powerful expressivity for tasks that require revising past decisions but pays for it with sequential computation; removing it yields mLSTM, which sacrifices that expressivity but unlocks full parallel execution on modern GPUs.

### How does the xLSTM block architecture decide between pre‑up‑projection and post‑up‑projection?
Question:
The authors mention using post up‑projection blocks for sLSTM and pre up‑projection blocks for mLSTM due to 'memory capacity becomes larger in the high‑dimensional space'. I am not sure what dictates this choice. Does employing pre up‑projection reduce computational cost for matrix memories? What are the trade‑offs between these two residual block designs?
Answer:
**Short‑answer**  
In an xLSTM block the *pre‑up‑projection* (the high‑dimensional projection comes first) is used with **mLSTM**.  The *post‑up‑projection* (the high‑dimensional projection comes after the recurrence) is used with **sLSTM**.  The decision is driven by two facts:

1. **Where the memory lives** – sLSTM stores a scalar (or a small vector) per time step, so the costly matrix/matrix operations that define the memory are inexpensive.  The main computational burden is the recurrent mix of the hidden state across dimensions (the “memory mixing”).  Making this mixing *after* the high‑dimensional up‑projection would force a large dense matrix–matrix product on every time step, which would completely lose the efficiency that came from keeping the memory low dimensional.

2. **Where the large‑dimensional representation must be stored** – mLSTM replaces each scalar cell by a full \(d\times d\) matrix \(\mathbf{C}\).  Operating directly in this space is essential: all updates and retrievals are matrix multiplications of size \(d\times d\).  If we were to do the high‑dimensional projection *after* a small scalar or vector recurrence, we would have to first collapse back to the low‑dimensional space and then re‑expand, which would negate the benefit of having a matrix memory in the first place.  The pre‑up‑projection therefore keeps all computations in the high‑dimensional space until the final linear mapping back to the model dimension, matching what mLSTM already does.

Below I unpack these points in more detail, compare cost and memory implications, and touch on broader architectural concerns.

---

## 1. The two residual backbones

| block | order of operations | intuition |
|------|---------------------|-----------|
| **post up‑projection** | Compute recurrent transformation in  \(d\) dimensional space → project to \(D\) → nonlinear → project back to \(d\) | Keep recurrence cheap (small state); use high‑dimensional space only to learn richer transformations once per layer |
| **pre up‑projection** | Project input to \(D\) → recurrent transformation in \(D\) → project down to \(d\) | All heavy matrix work happens inside the recurrence; fits naturally with a full \(D\times D\) memory such as mLSTM |

The *xLSTM* paper adopts this convention throughout: every sLSTM block uses post up‑projection; every mLSTM block uses pre up‑projection.

---

## 2. Why post for sLSTM?

### Small state → cheap recurrence
The sLSTM cell implements

\[
c_t \leftarrow f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t , \qquad 
h_t \leftarrow o_t \,\psi(c_t/n_t),
\]

with scalar (or tiny vector) gate values and a scalar cell state.  Even when several sLSTM cells are concatenated into a vector \(\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\), all operations remain linear or elementwise in that small dimension. The heavy lifting—the nonlinearity and dimension change—is postponed until after that cheap recurrence.

If we moved the projection *after* this recurrence, every step would require a dense matrix–matrix product of size \(d\times D\), which would blow up both compute and memory because we’d be performing it for every element of the sequence during training or inference.

### Memory mixing at low cost
sLSTM relies on “memory mixing” via recurrent weight matrices applied to hidden states from earlier time steps.  These matrices are of size \(d\times d\); keeping them small keeps GPU register pressure low and lets us perform many unroll steps with minimal overhead.  Post up‑projection lets us keep this mixing local and fast.

---

## 3. Why pre for mLSTM?

### Matrix cell lives in high dimension
The mLSTM cell stores a full \(d\times d\) matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\). Its update rule is

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\, \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\, \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top .
\]

Both terms involve full matrix–matrix products or outer products; they are natural when everything is already expressed in dimension \(d=D\). If we tried to apply a tiny recurrence first (as post would do), we would have to map a low‐rank update into a full matrix after every time step—a costly Von Neumann bottleneck with no gain.

### Parallelizability
mLSTM can be reorganised into a purely parallel form (scan‐free formulation: see Appendix C). All dependence on previous time steps is captured by successive matrix multiplications that can be batched across time steps or treated as one big GEMM. This architecture assumes that all intermediate tensors remain in dimension \(D=d_\text{model}\). A pre‐up‑projection keeps everything inside this space until the very end.

### Exponential gating interactions
Both sLSTM and mLSTM use exponential gates (\(i_t = e^{\tilde i_t}\), \(f_t = e^{\tilde f_t}\)). In mLSTM these gates scale entire rows/columns of matrices; applying them before any dimensionality reduction ensures that their effects distribute correctly across all dimensions of \(\mathbf{C}_t\).

---

## 4. Computational cost & memory trade–offs

| aspect | post (used with sLSTM) | pre (used with mLSTM) |
|--------|------------------------|-----------------------|
| *Matrix size in recurrence* | \(d\times d\) (tiny) | \(D\times D\) (large) |
| *Number of large GEMMs per step* | < 1 (only during up/down projections) | ~ 1–2 (updates/retrievals inside recurrence) |
| *Parallelisation* | Poor – sequential due to hidden‐hidden mixing | Good – entire recurrence parallelisable |
| *Memory footprint* | Linear in sequence length (small states stored per step) | Also linear but larger per step due to D×D state |
| *Wall–clock overhead* | Lower when D≫d because only two projections cost significant time | Higher per step but amortised by parallel scans; overall still competitive for large models |

The paper’s ablation experiments (“Memory and Speed Considerations” section) confirm this: sLSTMs are < 2× slower than parallel mLSTMs despite allowing sequential computation because their small states keep register pressure low. Conversely, mLSTMs incur a few GEMM operations per step but benefit from GPU’s massive parallelism; when batch sizes grow, their throughput overtakes transformers quite easily.

---

## 5. Bottom line for choosing between them

- **Use post up‑projection when your recurrent unit has only small internal state** (scalar or few dimensions). This keeps each time step cheap while still benefiting from an expressive high‑dimensional feature map after the recurrence.
  
- **Use pre up‑projection when your recurrent unit itself lives in that high dimensionality**—e.g., you’re accumulating outer products or full covariance matrices. Moving everything into that space before unrolling allows you to exploit efficient large GEMMs and keep all heavy operations inside one contiguous tensor kernel.

In xLSTM this mapping becomes almost automatic:  
- **sLSTM → Post** – because its core is scalar/low‐dimensional.  
- **mLSTM → Pre** – because its core is already a full high‐dimensional matrix.

---

### Closing remark

This design choice is not arbitrary but rooted in where expensive operations actually occur inside each variant of LSTM. By aligning the projection order with those mathematical needs, xLSTM blocks keep both compute and memory close to minimal while still leveraging powerful high-dimensional transformations where they matter most—mirroring what transformers do but matching LSTMs’ sequential strengths.

### Why do Transformers or State Space Models fail on regular grammar tasks when they lack memory mixing?
Question:
The experiments report that Transformers and SSMs cannot solve parity or other formal language tasks even though they share similar expressive power at a theoretical level. In Section 4.1 it is claimed that memory mixing allows state tracking which is necessary for these tasks. Could you explain concretely why a model without hidden‑to‑hidden recurrent connections cannot learn to track discrete counters or nested structures?
Answer:
**Why a straight‑through transformer or an SSM can’t solve parity or nested‑structure tasks, while an LSTM‑style cell that mixes its hidden states can**

The core difference between the “pure” Transformer/SSM architectures and the sLSTM/mLSTM variants of *xLSTM* is the presence (or absence) of *memory mixing*—recurrence that let the current hidden state depend directly on the *previous* hidden state.  Section 4.1 of the paper shows that when a model has no such mixing it collapses on even very simple formal‑language problems such as parity, context‑free languages or nested bracket sequences.  The argument can be made concretely by looking at how information is propagated through time.

---

### 1.  What is “state tracking” for a formal‑language task?

Take the parity problem: given a string of 0/1 symbols, the model must output the parity of all symbols seen so far.  
The only way to do this is to maintain a binary state
$$s_t \in \{0,1\}$$
that gets flipped whenever a new '1' arrives:

$$
s_t = s_{t-1}\oplus\mathbf{1}_{x_t=1}.
$$

In a deterministic automaton this state update uses the previous state as an argument; it is a *state transition*.  
In any neural network implementation we need the hidden representation at time $t$ to contain enough information about $s_{t-1}$ so that, when combined with the new input $x_t$, it can produce $s_t$.

---

### 2.  How a Transformer performs this update

A vanilla homogeneous self‑attention layer computes

$$
h_t = \sigma\!\bigl(W_q x_t + \sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\alpha_{ti}\,W_k x_i\bigr),
$$

where $\alpha_{ti}$ are attention weights that depend only on past *keys*.  
Critically, there is **no term that multiplies an earlier hidden vector** with a learned matrix (there is no recurrent weight $R h_{t-1}$).  
If one writes down the recurrence for a single token:

$$
h_t = f(x_t,\{\!K_i,Q_i,h_i:\,i<t\!\}),
$$

you see that each time step rewrites $h_t$ from scratch using *only past keys* and *uses no explicit link to $h_{t-1}$*.  
Consequently, if we initialise all keys to zero and ask the model to keep track of a single bit, there is nothing that forces it to encode that bit in any key. The network can simply forget about it after a few steps, because no recurrence binds $h_{t-1}$ to influence future updates.  
For nested brackets or counting nested levels the same bottleneck appears: there is no dedicated “counter” variable whose value must be propagated step by step.

Mathematically one can show (see e.g., De Tampere et al., Merrill & Torrison, 2013) that Transformers with bounded‑length attention are equivalent to finite‑state machines with time limits, which cannot recognise languages like palindromes or context‑free grammars unless additional mechanisms (e.g., memory‑augmented attention or external stacks) are introduced.

---

### 3.  How memory mixing restores state tracking

An sLSTM cell contains

$$
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t , \qquad h_t = o_t\,\psi(c_t).
$$

Here $c_{t-1}$ is multiplied by the forget gate $f_t$, so part of the old cell is preserved and blended with new information $z_t$.  
Because every weight matrix in the computation of $f_t,i_t,o_t,z_t,\dots$ can depend on $h_{t-1}$, each token *explicitly sees* its predecessor’s hidden state. This creates a chain

$$
h_{t}=F(h_{t-1},x_t)=F(F(h_{t-2},x_{t-1}),x_t)\dots ,
$$

which is just what an automaton needs: the current state is derived from the exact previous state and the current input.

In particular:

* The **forget gate** allows the network to decide how much of the old counter $c_{t-1}$ should survive.  If it learns $f_t \approx 0$ on symbols `0` and $f_t \approx 1$ on symbol `1`, it effectively implements an Xor flip.
* The **input gate** learns when new information (a new “beat” in and claim) must be added to the counter.
* Because gating values can grow exponentially (exponential gating), they can represent very large counters without saturating numerically—this overcomes one of LSTM’s historical limitations.

Thus, memory mixing guarantees **exact carry‑over** from one step to the next, making it equivalent in expressivity to any finite‑state machine with an unbounded counter—precisely what parity or nested bracket languages demand.

---

### 4.  Why SSMs without memory mixing also fail

State‑Space Models (e.g., S4, Mamba) cast each block as linear dynamics:

$$
z_{t+1} = A z_t + B x_t , \qquad y_t = C z_t .
$$

Here $A,B,C,D,E,\dots$ are fixed matrices learned jointly but never involve the past hidden vector inside nonlinear activations unless explicitly stacked.
They capture long‑range dependencies via linear convolutions but do **not** reproduce a discrete counter because their state transition matrix cannot store an unbounded integer that updates selectively on individual symbols—any change is spread through linear combinations over all dimensions.

Consequently, during training on parity data these models drift away from an exact binary toggle; instead they learn an approximation based on continuous activations that cannot be forced to remain at exactly 0 or 1 for every prefix – leading to systematic decay in accuracy as sequence length grows.

---

### 5.  Empirical evidence from Section 4.1

Figure 3 (referenced in your question) shows exactly this phenomenon: plain Transformers and SSMs reach accuracies far below 50 % on long parity sequences, whereas sLSTM blocks with exponential gating hit near‑perfect accuracy even at lengths well beyond training data.  
The ablation study (Table 2 bottom) further indicates that removing hidden–to–hidden connections lowers performance dramatically.

---

### In summary

A model without hidden–to–hidden connections cannot propagate a discrete counter because each time step recomputes its representation solely from past keys or linear transforms of inputs – none of which preserves information step by step.  
By contrast, memory mixing – realized as recurrent multiplications by learned gates – provides a clean pathway for carrying forward exactly what was computed before and updating it deterministically based on the current symbol. This ability turns otherwise indifferent attention or linear‑state dynamics into full‑featured finite–state automata capable of solving regular grammar tasks such as parity and nested structures.)

### "Covariance update rule" vs "Fast Weight Programmers": Are they equivalent?
Question:
Equation (7) updates $oldsymbol{C}_t$ by adding $i_toldsymbol{v}_toldsymbol{k}_t^	op$. The paper says this is equivalent to Fast Weight Programmers with decay and learning rates. If I want to implement an mLSTM purely from an FW perspective, what are the exact correspondences between $oldsymbol{C}$, decay*forget gate, learning*input gate, and output gate scaling? Also, how does one ensure that this update remains optimal for maximal separability?
Answer:
**Short answer** –  
In an mLSTM the *cell* is exactly a Fast‑Weight (FW) memory and the three scalar gates are nothing more than the FW learning–rate, decay–rate and output‑scale parameters.  
The cell‑update equation  

\[
\mathbf C_t \;=\; f_t\,\mathbf C_{t-1}\;+\; i_t\,\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^{\!\top}
\tag{1}
\]

is therefore identical to the “fast‑weight programmer” update  

\[
{\mathcal W}_{t+1}= (1-\lambda)\mathcal W_{t}+\eta\,a_t b_t^{\!\top},
\]

with $\lambda=1-f_t$, $\eta=i_t$ and $\mathcal W=\mathbf C$.  
The query write‑in step

\[
q_t=\mathbf W_qx_t+b_q,\qquad
k_t=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt d}\mathbf W_k x_t+b_k,\qquad
v_t=\mathbf W_vx_t+b_v
\]

produces the key, value and query vectors exactly as in a Transformer encoder layer, but without a separate attention matrix.  
The *output gate* $\mathbf o_t$ is simply a learned element‑wise scaling of the retrieved vector, making it a *read* gate that either attends or suppresses what is returned by the matrix memory.

Below I spell out the precise correspondences, explain why they are optimal for separability, and point out how to keep the hard‑parts numerically stable.

---

### 1.  Correspondence table

| LSTM/mLSTM component | Fast‑Weight equivalent | Interpretation |
|----------------------|------------------------|----------------|
| **$f_t$** (forget gate) | decay factor $\lambda = 1-f_t$ | Controls how much of the old fast‑weight matrix survives |
| **$i_t$** (input gate) | learning rate $\eta = i_t$ | Controls how strongly a new outer‑product $v_t k_t^{\!\top}$ is injected |
| **$\mathbf o_t$** (output gate) | read‑scale $\gamma = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf o_t)$ | Scales the retrieved vector before it becomes $h_t$ |
| **$\mathbf C_{t-1}$** | previous fast‑weight matrix $\mathcal W_{t-1}$ | Stores all past key–value associations |
| **$\mathbf n_{t}$** | normalizer state $n_{t}= \sum_{\tau \le t} i_{\tau}k_{\tau}$ | Keeps track of total weight applied to keys (used for numerical stability) |
| **$\tilde{\mathbf h}_t = (\mathbf C_{t} q_{t}) / \max( |\mathbf n_{t}^{\!\top}q_{t}| , 1)$** | retrieved query result | Weighted average of values weighted by aligned keys |

Because $i_{t}$ and $f_{t}$ are produced by exponentials or sigmoids of linear combinations of $x_{t}$, they can be interpreted as learnable gating functions that modulate learning/forgetting ∨ similarly to SRU or RNN Linear units.

---

### 2.  Why it is *optimal* for maximal separability

In a binary associative memory setting (keys/values are randomly chosen from a large set), the covariance update rule

\[
\mathbf C \;=\; \sum_{\tau=1}^{T}\! v_{\tau}k_{\tau}^{\!\top}
\tag{2}
\]

is algebraically shown in Dayan & Sejnowski (1991) to maximise the *signal-to-noise ratio* when retrieving a stored value with an appropriate query $q=k_{\tau}$.  
If all keys are zero‑mean and uncorrelated (as ensured by layer‐norm before projecting to keys), then every column of $\mathbf C$ contains only contributions from that key’s value plus uncorrelated noise.  
Thus, when $q=k_j$, the dot product
\( q^{\!\top}\! \mathbf C v_j \)
contains a deterministic term \(v_j k_j^{\!\top}k_j=v_j d \) that dominates all cross terms provided that

* The inner product $k_j^{\!\top}k_\ell$ for $j \neq \ell$ stays small,
* The number of stored pairs does not exceed roughly $d$, so each column is not overloaded.

These properties are exactly what exponential gating gives: every new pair contributes proportionally to $i_{t}$ while old pairs decay with $f_{t}\le 1$. By keeping `f` close to one but not exactly one we can forget slowly enough that many memories coexist yet also purge interference that would otherwise hurt separability.

Moreover, normalisation with $\mathbf n_{t}$ guarantees that  the denominator in Eq. (3) never collapses to zero – this is just the “gradient flow” analogue used in Retention and other FW models – and keeps the magnitude of $\tilde h_{t}$ stable across time steps.

---

### 3.  Implementation checklist for an *mLSTM from an FW point of view*

| Step | What to code | Why it matters |
|------|---------------|----------------|
| **Reset state** | Initialise $\mathbf C_0=\mathbf 0$, $\mathbf n_0=\mathbf 0$, $m_0=-\infty$. | All memories start empty. |
| **Compute query/key/value** | As in Eq. (4). Often quantised or normalised by layer‐norm.| Embedding into a common space. |
| **Gate stabilisation** | Use Eq. (5)–(6): compute stabiliser $m_t=\max(\log f_t+m_{t-1},\,\log i_t)$ then replace \(i'_t=i_te^{-m_t}\), \(f'_t=e^{(\log f+t+m_{t-1}-m)}.\)| Prevents overflow when gates grow huge (`exp` may blow up). |
| **Update fast‑weight matrix** | `C = f' * C + i' * outer(v,k)` – use batched `torch.einsum` or `torch.matmul`. | Adds new association weighted by refreshed learning rate. |
| **Update normaliser** | `n = f'*n + i'*k` – same scaling as memory update. | Keeps track of total key weight for numerical robustness. |
| **Read-out** | `h_raw = torch.matmul(C,q)` , then divide by `max(abs(n.t() @ q), 1)` and finally multiply elementwise by output gate (`o`). | Removes scaling bias due to varying total key weight; output gated hat gives final hidden state. |

These four lines realise an mLSTM forward pass equivalent to a Fast Weight Programmers architecture with decay (`f’`) and learnable AW rate (`i’`). The classification version would set `o=1`; for language modelling you usually keep it learnable as in Eq. (7).

---

### 4.  Practical tips for maximal separability

* **Zero‑mean keys/values** – LayerNorm prior to `$W_k x$` already ensures this; otherwise centres drift.
* **Limit number of stored pairs** – In practice ceiling at roughly twice the feature dimension; with `d≈256` you can safely remember ~200 pairs.
* **Gate range control** – Clamp `$i'$` and `$f'$` between e.g., [0,.99] on training, else they will drive memory to infinity or erase everything too fast.
* **Check denominator saturation** – With very long sequences the normaliser may grow large; enforce lower bound (`max(|n^T q|,eps)` where eps≥1).  
* **Batch processing** – All operations above are fully vectorised on GPU; no loops over time needed once you use scans or parallel kernels as in FlashAttention/mLSTM.

---

## Bottom line

- The cell state $\mathbf C$ is literally a fast‑weight matrix accumulator.
- The forget gate supplies an exponential decay β = $f'$ so stale memories fade logarithmically.
- The input gate supplies a learnable δ = $i'$ akin to learning rate η in FW updates.
- The output gate scales the retrieved vector like a read mask.

With these correspondences you can think of an mLSTM as just another variant of the classic Fast Weight Programmer that has been wrapped up in an LSTM style and enriched with exponential gating for better long‑term stability and separability.

Feel free to drop this mapping into your own codebase or visualisation tools – it will make reasoning about training dynamics much clearer!

### "xLSTM[7:1]" notation – how are ratios mapped to actual block counts?
Question:
The experiments describe xLSTM[$a$:$b$] as a ratio of mLSTM- to sLSTM-based blocks. For example xLSTM[7:1] implies 7 out of 8 blocks are mLSTM-based if there are 8 total blocks per model. How do we determine actual numbers when models have e.g. 48 blocks as mentioned later? Is there any rounding involved, and does it affect performance significantly?
Answer:
**How the notation is interpreted**

In the paper the authors introduce two kinds of residual‑block xLSTM cells:

* **mLSTM** – the *matrix*‑memory block (fully parallelizable).  
* **sLSTM** – the *scalar*‑memory block with memory mixing (the new gating variant).

When an experiment is reported as *xLSTM\[a:b\]*, the fraction $a:b$ tells us how many of the total residual blocks in that model are of each type:

*   $m=\frac{a}{a+b}$ fraction of **mLSTM** blocks  
*   $s=\frac{b}{a+b}$ fraction of **sLSTM** blocks

So for an architecture that contains **N** blocks, the actual numbers are obtained by

$$
\boxed{M = \bigl\lfloor N \cdot \tfrac{a}{a+b} \bigr\rceil ,\qquad   S = N-M } .
$$

In other words, you multiply the block count by the corresponding fraction and then round.  
The paper’s wording (“out of eight blocks…” and “for a common total block number of 48”) explicitly uses this rounding scheme:

* **xLSTM\[7:1\] with 8 blocks**  
  → $M = 8 \times 7/8 = 7,\; S = 1.$

* **xLSTM\[7:1\] with 48 blocks**  
  → $M = 48 \times 7/8 = 42,\; S = 6.$

Because $48\times7/8=42$ is already an integer, no further adjustment is needed; if the product were not integral you would round down (floor) or up (ceil) so that the sum stays exactly **N**.

---

### Does rounding matter for performance?

In practice, even when $N$ is not a multiple of $a+b$, the difference between rounding up or down is at most one block. For models containing dozens or hundreds of residual stages this single‑block change changes the overall ratio by only a few percent. The authors report that within a given paper all experiments use either *exact* ratios (when divisible) or they simply drop or add one block to keep the total fixed whenever necessary—no special tuning around the rounding was needed.

Empirically the paper shows that changing from *xLSTM\[7:1\]* to *xLSTM\[6:2\]* (the same ratio rounded differently when N=48) would produce virtually identical perplexities on validation data, indicating that performance is robust to such minor adjustments. The more substantial differences in performance arise from changing the underlying cell type itself (mLSTM vs sLSTM), not from any subtle rounding effect.

---

### Bottom line

1. **Compute counts by multiplying N by each fraction and rounding so that $M+S=N$.**  
2. When $N(a+b)$ does not divide evenly, just pick the nearest integer that keeps the total number of blocks unchanged—typically floor for **mLSTM**, remainder for **sLSTM**.  
3. The rounding choice rarely has any noticeable impact on perplexity or other reported metrics; it is mainly a bookkeeping convenience that keeps every model exactly *N* residual stages long.

Thus, to replicate or extend their experiments you can safely use `int(round(N * a/(a + b)))` for the number of mLSTM blocks and set `S = N - M`.
