\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What is the role of the monotonicity constraint λ_i ≤ λ_{i+1} in the evolutionary search?
Question:
Algorithm 1 enforces that λ values increase monotonically with RoPE dimension, citing NTK theory. Could someone explain why higher frequency dimensions (smaller indices) should have smaller λ? What would happen if we violated this constraint—would we also see increased perplexity or just slower convergence?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$ enforces that higher‑frequency RoPE dimensions (small $i$) receive *less* scaling (i.e. a smaller $\lambda_i$) than lower‑frequency ones.  
NTK theory predicts that, for a Fourier‑like positional embedding, the higher‑frequency components are most sensitive to small changes in the accumulated phase, so they must be preserved as much as possible when we interpolate or extrapolate to new positions.  If the constraint is violated, low‑frequency dimensions would be over‑scaled while high‑frequency ones would be compressed, which degrades the model’s ability to distinguish nearby token positions and typically results in higher perplexity and slower convergence on very long contexts.

---

### Why higher‑frequency dimensions should have smaller $\lambda$

RoPE encodes a token at position $n$ in each dimensional pair as  
\[
\bigl(\cos(n\theta_i),\,\sin(n\theta_i)\bigr), \qquad
\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}.
\]
Thus $i=0$ corresponds to the highest frequency $\theta_0$, and $i=(d/2-1)$ to the lowest frequency.  
When we extend the context window from $L$ to $L'=sL$, we need to map the *new* positions $n'$ ($>L$) onto the *old* embedding space.  In the unified form (Eq.\,5) this is done by rescaling every dimension by a factor $\lambda_i$:
\[
n'\;\to\;\frac{n'}{\lambda_i}\quad \text{in dimension }i .
\]

If we set all $\lambda_i$ equal (plain PI with $\lambda=s$), every frequency component is compressed by the same amount.  The high‑frequency part then forces many adjacent tokens into an almost indistinguishable phase range, causing attention heads to “crowd” together and reducing expressiveness—a phenomenon already observed in PI experiments.

NTK and its extensions think of RoPE as a set of complex exponentials forming an over‑complete Fourier dictionary.  In a random infinite‑width limit, the kernel that describes how two tokens influence one another depends linearly on the inner product of these exponentials.  If we rescale all frequencies by different amounts, the relative contribution of high‑ vs. low‑frequency terms changes; keeping high frequencies relatively *larger* (smaller scaling) preserves the fine‑grained positional differences that are crucial for modeling local structure at short distances, while letting low frequencies absorb more extrapolation to carry long‑range information.

Empirically, LongRoPE’s evolutionary search consistently finds solutions where  
$\lambda_0< \lambda_1<\dots< \lambda_{d/2-1}$, exactly as NTK suggests.  Any violation—e.g., a smaller $\lambda$ on a lower frequency dimension than on a higher one—corresponds to over‑compressing detail that should remain sharp, leading to rapidly increasing perplexity as soon as the context exceeds the original window.

---

### What happens if we drop the monotonicity constraint?

1. **Increased perplexity**  
   The search would explore many candidates where some high‑frequency dimensions are over‑scaled.  These candidates tend to crowd nearby token positions together because their phases change very slowly with $n$.  Attention masks can no longer distinguish between “near” and “far” tokens consistently, which diminishes language modelling performance almost immediately beyond a modest extension (often after just \(2\times\) or \(4\times\)).

2. **Slower convergence**  
   When fine‑tuning an already pre‑trained LLM with such misaligned scaling factors, gradients must first re‑learn how to use a drastically reshaped positional spectrum.  This additional plasticity slows down learning and consumes more GPU steps (in practice tens of thousands of extra steps were seen in Ablation 5).  

3. **Potential numerical instability**  
   High frequencies compressed too much can cause large cosine/sine values that are numerically close to each other across many tokens; conversely, low frequencies stretched too far can produce very slowly varying terms that become effectively constant for most of the sequence.  Both effects aggravate vanishing/exploding gradients during back–propagation.

In short, abandoning monotonicity does not simply make training marginally slower—it usually yields models that are *harder* to train and systematically report much higher perplexities on long documents.

---

### Broader connection

The monotonicity aligns with what happens in other Fourier-based positional systems:

- In *Fourier kernels* used for efficient attention (e.g., Performer), higher harmonics are kept intact while lower ones are swept out.
- In *neural tangent kernel* analysis of Transformers, more weight is given to low-frequency components for global dependence but high-frequency terms must remain largely unchanged for local accuracy.
- In *rotary embeddings* used by many recent LLMs (e.g., LLaMA2), pretraining was done with a base frequency $\theta=10\,000$.  Changing only $\theta$ globally would violate these relative scales.

Therefore the evolutionary search’s implicit prior—“keep fast oscillations less altered”—is not arbitrary; it is rooted both in theory (NTK) and practice (observed perplexities).  

---

**Conclusion**

The constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ enforces an ordering that preserves high-frequency positional detail while allowing low-frequency components to absorb more of the necessary extrapolation when extending context windows. Loosening this ordering leads to over-compression of fine-grained positions, causing significant increases in perplexity and making fine-tuning slower or unstable—exactly what was observed when violating the constraint in ablation studies of LongRoPE.
