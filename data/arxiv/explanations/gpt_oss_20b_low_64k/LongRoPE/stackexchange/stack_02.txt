\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### How does the token position threshold ℛ affect attention distribution?
Question:
Finding 2 suggests that the first ℛ tokens should be exempted from interpolation. What is the intuition behind keeping initial tokens unscaled? Does this have to do with the causal attention mask focusing more on earlier positions, or is it related to streaming LLM architectures? Could you provide a concrete example of how λ=1 for the first 128 tokens changes the rotary encoding compared to applying a global λ?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The token‑position threshold \( \hat n \) in LongRoPE is a way to keep the *first* few tokens at their original RoPE angles (i.e., \( \lambda = 1 \)) and only start rescaling the rotary angles after that point. The intuition is that the most important information for a transformer often sits at the left‑most part of a sequence – because (1) causal self‑attention can only see earlier positions, (2) early tokens dominate the attention scores, and (3) streaming or “infinite‑sequence” LLMs explicitly use a fixed‑window still retain strong weight on the beginning of the input. By protecting those positions from being “compressed” by interpolation, LongRoPE preserves the high‑frequency encoding that typically contains fine‑grained positional detail, thus reducing the information loss that would otherwise occur in a long context.

Below is a concrete walk‑through of how setting \( \lambda = 1 \) for the first 128 tokens changes the rotary encoding compared to using a global \( \lambda=s\,\)(the extension ratio).

---

### What RoPE looks like before and after interpolation

In vanilla RoPE every dimension \( i \) uses an angle

$$
\theta_{i}(n)=\frac{n}{\beta^i}\,,\qquad 
\beta = \theta^{2/d}\; .
$$

For a token at absolute position \( n \), its two‑dimensional embedding for dimension \( i \) is  

$$
\left[\,\cos(\theta_i(n)),\,\sin(\theta_i(n))\,\right].
$$

If we want to extend the context from an original length \( L \) to a new length \( L' = sL\), Positional Interpolation (PI) would replace every angle by

$$
\theta^{PI}_i(n)=\frac{n}{s\,\beta^i}.
$$

Everything is uniformly “shrunk” by a factor of \( s \).  
For a huge extension ratio (say \( s=512 \)) this means that even relatively near positions like  
\( n=5000 \) and \( n=5010 \) will have angles that differ by only about

$$
\Delta \theta^{PI}\approx
\frac{10}{s\,\beta^i},
$$

which becomes tiny for low‑frequency dimensions, causing many positions to map to almost the same angle – **the crowding problem**.

---

### How LongRoPE handles the first 128 positions

LongRoPE introduces a *token‑position threshold* \( \hat n \). The associated indicator function in Eq. (4) of the paper is

$$
I(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)(n)=
  1          ,  \text{if } n <  \hat n   \;(\text{no scaling})\\
  1/\lambda_i      ,  \text{if } n ≥  \hat n   (\text{apply scaling})
$$

So for each dimension we keep **exactly** the original angles for all tokens with index strictly smaller than \( \hat n \).  
Assuming we use the typical “no‑scaling” value from PI (\( \lambda_i = s=512 \)), but override it for the first 128 tokens, we get

$$
θ'_i(n)=
    θ_i(n)           ,   n < 128\\
    θ_i(n)/512       ,   n ≥ 128.
$$

That means:

| Position | Original angle | PI‑scaled angle | LongRoPE‑scaled angle |
|----------|----------------|-----------------|-----------------------|
|   50     | \(50/\beta^i\)| \(50/(512\,β^i) ≈0.097β^{-i}\)| \(50/β^i ≈β^{-i}·50\) |
|   200    | \(200/β^i\)| \(200/(512\,β^i) ≈0.39β^{-i}\)| \(200/(512\,β^i) ≈0.39β^{-i}\) |
|   1000   | \(1000/β^i\)| \(1000/(512\,β^i)≈1.95β^{-i}\)|≈\(1.95β^{-i}\)

Graphically, in an angle–vs–position plot:

* For positions < 128 you trace the *unscaled* straight line.
* At position 128 you snap down to an order of magnitude **smaller** slope (by factor 512).
* Beyond that, you follow exactly the PI line.

The effect is that **the first block of tokens has much more angular resolution** than any later block. For low‑frequency dimensions this difference can be several orders of magnitude: the unscaled angles distinguish neighbouring positions with very fine granularity, while later positions are crammed together.

---

### Why this matters for attention

Attention in causal transformers takes pairwise dot products between queries and keys constructed from these rotated embeddings. The dot product depends on how *different* two positions are in every dimension.

* If two consecutive early tokens have almost identical raw angles (because they were *interpolated*), their dot product will be very high – their representations are essentially indistinguishable.
* Keeping early tokens unscaled keeps their angular differences large enough, so attention weights can still discriminate between them.
* For far‑ahead tokens (>128), interpolation reduces angular variance but those tokens are rarely compared to each other and are always “in front” of earlier tokens; they do not suffer as much from crowded angles because everything ahead of them will be interpolated equally.

Empirically LongRoPE shows exactly this benefit: when measuring perplexity or Passkey retrieval accuracy at lengths such as 8k or 16k, running with a threshold of \( \hat n=128 \) outperforms both pure PI and NTK variants by several percentage points on LLaMA‑2​-7B and Mistral​-7B.

---

### Connection to streaming / infinite sequence models

Streaming / infinite‐sequence LLMs (e.g., Streaming‑LLM or LM‑Infinite) rely on a **fixed memory window**: every new token sees only a limited set of previous contexts. In those works it was observed that *earlier windows matter more*, because they can be cached more efficiently or get higher entropy in training data. 

LongRoPE implicitly follows this philosophy by giving *special treatment* to early symbols: rescaling starts later so their relative geometry better reflects their true positional importance. In practice this creates a positional embedding that behaves like:

```
[unscaled | scaled]
 ^<-- short prefix with fine detail
``` 

which matches what streaming models already understand – they need precise relative positioning among early tokens but can tolerate coarser granularity farther back.

---

### Bottom line

Setting \( λ=1 \) for token indices below some threshold keeps those positions at their original RoPE angles, which:

1. **Preserves high‑frequency angular information**—makes early tokens distinguishable even after huge extensions.
2. **Mitigates crowding**—early parts do not compete with later parts for angular space.
3. **Matches causal attention dynamics**—early symbols dominate because attention masks only look backwards; keeping them sharp aligns with attention focus.
4. **Is inexpensive**—implemented as an indicator function without extra parameters.

That’s why LongRoPE proposes keeping initial tokens exempted from interpolation: it gives you less degradation at long context lengths while still allowing almost arbitrary extension ratios when combined with its other non‑uniform search over dimensions.
