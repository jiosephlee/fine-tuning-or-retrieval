\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Could LongRoPE be applied to other positional embeddings beyond RoPE?
Question:
The paper focuses exclusively on rotary embeddings. Can the same idea—dimension‑wise and token‑wise rescaling searched by evolution—be adapted to sinusoidal positional encodings or learned absolute positions? Would we expect similar gains or are there theoretical reasons why rotary embeddings benefit more from non‑uniform interpolation?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
LongRoPE’s key idea—searching for *per‑dimension* and *per‑token* rescaling factors for the rotary angles—is tightly coupled to the mathematical form of RoPE (a sinusoidal rotation in a 2‑D plane per dimension).  The same evolutionary‑search trick can be applied to any positional encoding that can be expressed as a product of a scalar “frequency” with the token index, but for truly different schemes (e.g., absolute learned embeddings or sine–cosine globals) the benefits and the search space change dramatically.  In practice, non‑uniform interpolation tends to give the biggest gains for RoPE because its rotation operators already encode relative position in a way that is sensitive to angle scaling; other encodings either do not provide that angular structure or already use learned parameters that absorb small changes.

---

## Why rotary embeddings benefit more from non‑uniform interpolation

RoPE defines for each even‑indexed dimension $i$ a rotation angle $\theta_i=n/\beta^i$, where $n$ is the token index and $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$.  This angle is then used in a pairwise $(\cos,\sin)$ embedding (Eq.~(1)).  The *relative* position between two tokens $n_1,n_2$ manifests as a phase difference
\[
n_1\theta_i - n_2\theta_i = (n_1-n_2)\frac{1}{\beta^i},
\]
so that same relative offsets are represented consistently regardless of absolute position.

When the context window is extended to a longer sequence length $L'$, positions beyond the pre‑trained maximum are effectively “stretched” along the index axis.  A naive linear downscale (PI) simply multiplies all $\theta_i$ by a constant $\lambda=s^{-1}$, which preserves relative differences but also compresses all angles into a smaller angular band.  High‑frequency dimensions (small $i$) become very crowded: two nearby tokens that previously had distinct phase differences now lie almost on top of each other, causing attention heads to lose discriminative power.  Low‑frequency dimensions are less affected because their angular changes per step are already tiny.

The NTK/YaRN intuition says: **use more interpolation on low‑frequency dimensions and less on high‑frequency ones**.  That is exactly what LongRoPE’s evolutionary search discovers: for dimension $i$, the optimal rescale factor $\lambda_i$ follows an increasing trend with $i$ (monotonicity constraint), while early‐position indices can even be left unchanged ($\hat n>0$).  This preserves the fine angular resolution where it matters most, without forcing all dimensions to compress uniformly.

With *absolute* sinusoidal positional encodings (the classic “sin (k·pos)” in transformers with base 10000), every token gets a fixed embedding that depends only on its absolute index, not on angle packing across dimensions.  Stretched indices still produce larger arguments to sin/cos, but since these embeddings are *fixed*, there is no notion of relative phase being preserved across dimensions; consequently, “crowding” manifests as far–repeated patterns rather than loss of relative ability.  Moreover, absolute encodings are typically added once (as in GPT and BERT), whereas rotary embeddings multiply or rotate key/value vectors before attention.  Hence an angle rescoring gives Direct control over the transformer’s geometric operations, whereas rescaling an absolute vector would only blur its projection onto random directions in hidden space without regard to attention geometry.

Finally, low‑dimensional *learned* absolute embeddings introduce free parameters that can absorb small perturbations: if you change token indices slightly, the model can adapt during fine‑tuning because each dimension has its own weights.  Using a fixed non‑uniform interpolation on top of them would either hurt or offer negligible benefit unless you also re‑fine‑tune those weights, which defeats one of LongRoPE’s appeal (fast extension without heavy fine‑tuning).

## Can LongRoPE ideas be transplanted?

| Encoding type | What’s analogous to “rescale factor”? | Practical feasibility? | Expected performance gain |
|---------------|----------------------------------------|------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Rotary (RoPE)** | Per‑dimension $\lambda_i$; per‑token prefix $\hat n$. | Very natural; identical math as in paper. | Largest gains (< 0.5 ppl). |
| **Sine–cosine global** | Scale the frequency base ($10000^{k/(d/2-1)}$) per embedding dimension or per positional segment. | You can reparameterize but need to ensure angular underflow/overflow; search space volatile; learning may mask effects. | Modest improvements if you recompute from scratch; otherwise limited. |
| **Learned absolute** | Replace raw embedding matrix with a frequency–scaled version or apply an extra learned affine layer before adding positions. | Requires extra training; evolution search would have to evaluate many parameter sets—heavy cost. | Potential but small; existing fine‑tuning likely dominates. |

To adapt LongRoPE to *sine/cosine global* encodings you would have to rewrite Eq.(3) so that instead of rotating pairs you scale the argument of sine/cosine differently per dimension:
\[
x_i = \left[\cos(n\,f_i),\, \sin(n\,f_i)\right], 
\]
with $f_i=\frac{1}{\beta^i}$ replaced by learnable $f'_i=\lambda_i f_i$.  The evolutionary procedure can still be run: each candidate is an array of $\lambda_i$ values, evaluated by feeding forward short synthetic sequences and measuring perplexity or gradient norms.  However:
- The *periodicity* of sin/cos makes large scaling risky: local minima repeat every $2\pi/f'_i$.
- Since absolute encodings do not interact with attention multiplicatively like RoPE, any change mainly changes how position vectors project onto hidden subspaces; this may not directly remedy crowded attention.

For **learned absolute positions**, you could conceptually turn them into pseudo–rotations by representing them as complex numbers $e^{i n f}$.  But unless you restructure the transformer to treat them as rotations (which would effectively bring it back to RoPE), changing only the scale would not alter attention in any meaningful way.

Thus, while a similar *search* can be performed for any positional scheme, **the structural reasons behind LongRoPE’s effectiveness lie in how rotatory transformations interact with self‑attention**—a property missing from vanilla sinusoidal or learned bases.

---

## Bottom line

- **Rotary embeddings are uniquely positioned**: their geometry makes linear downscaling destructive at high frequencies; non-uniform interpolation restores perceptual separability without retraining weights.
- **Absolute sin/cosine or learned embeddings lack this geometric sensitivity**, so applying non-uniform rescaling yields marginal gains at best.
- If one still wants to experiment with other encodings, start by reparameterizing their frequency bases and running a lightweight evolutionary search on short validation traces; expect results comparable to plain linear interpolation unless you redesign the transformer architecture itself.

So LongRoPE’s core innovation—dimension‑wise and token‑wise rescaling found via evolution—is most naturally—and most fruitfully—applied to rotary embeddings.
