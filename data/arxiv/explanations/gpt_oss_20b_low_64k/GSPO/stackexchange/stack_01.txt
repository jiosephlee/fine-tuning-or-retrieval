\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(	heta)$ in GSPO avoid the high-variance problem that plagues token-level ratios in GRPO?
Question:
In GRPO each token gets an importance weight $w_{i,t}(	heta)$ based on a single sample from the old policy. I understand that this leads to large variance especially for long responses, but I still don’t see why replacing the per‑token weight with a single sequence‑level weight normalized by length eliminates this variance. Can you explain the intuition behind length normalization and how it stabilizes the gradient?
Answer:
**Short answer**

GSPO takes the *average log‐ratio* of all tokens in a response, i.e.  
\[
s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \log
        \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
             {\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
      \Bigr).
\]
The division by the sequence length turns a product of many
token‑wise ratios into a **geometric mean** that lies in a much smaller
numeric range.  
This has two practical consequences:

1. **Variance reduction** – each token contributes
   $\log w_{i,t}(\theta)$.  Because the log ratio is summed over
   $|y_i|$ tokens, the denominator normalises by the same length:
   \[
   {\rm Var}\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
     \sum_t\log w_{i,t}\Bigr)
     =\tfrac{1}{|y_i|^{2}}\operatorname{Var}
      (\sum_t\log w_{i,t}).
   \]
   Without the division, highly variable long responses would push
   $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ far outside the clipping window, creating a
   high‑variance, biased gradient.  The normalisation keeps most values
   around $[0,1]$ regardless of the response length.

2. **Unified clipping window** – GRPO clips *token‑wise* ratios that can
   differ wildly across tokens and across responses.  In contrast,
   $s_i(\theta)$ is a single scalar per response; with length
   normalisation all responses share roughly the same range.
   Consequently, the clipping bounds in GSPO are calibrated once for
   all sequences, while in GRPO every token may need a different bound,
   leading to an inconsistent and noisy clipping effect.

Below we go through these points in more detail, starting from the
basic principles of importance sampling and ending with an intuitive
picture of how GSPO’s length normalisation stabilises learning.

---

### 1. Importance sampling and why we need “many’’ samples

The core idea behind off‑policy policy gradient is

\[
\E_{\pi_{\theta}}\!\bigl[f(z)\bigr]
  = \E_{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}\!\bigl[\,w(z)\,f(z)\bigr],
  \qquad
  w(z)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(z)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(z)} .
\]

This identity is exact only when we average over *many* samples from the
behaviour distribution $\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}$; otherwise the weight
$w(z)$ may be huge or tiny for an isolated sample and will dominate
the expectation.  In language generation we naturally pool many tokens
into one *sample*, namely a full response $y$.  By focusing on the
sequence likelihood

\[
w(y)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y)}
     = \prod_t w_{i,t},
\]

we explicitly utilise that a long response contains many tokens,
each of which contributes multiplicatively to correct for policy shift.
GRPO applied this idea at a single token level, breaking it up too fine;
the resulting weight $w_{i,t}$ comes from only one draw of the next‑token
distribution and therefore does not provide enough samples to ensure a
small variance estimator.

---

### 2. What happens without length normalisation?

Take two responses:

- $y^{(1)}$: length $5$, token ratios $w=2,\;2,\;2,\;2,\;2$
  → product $\;=32$.

- $y^{(2)}$: length $20$, token ratios identical.
  → product $\;=2^{20}=1048576$.

The raw importance ratio for $y^{(2)}$ is vastly larger than for
$y^{(1)}$; both will fall outside any reasonable clipping window.
Moreover, their variances are magnified because each new token multiplies
the ratio rather than adds it.  

If we simply divide by $ |y|$, we are effectively taking the **geometric mean**:

- For both sequences:
  \[
    s=\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{|y|}
      \sum_t \log w_t\bigr)
      = \exp(\log 2)=2 .
  \]
So all lengths collapse to comparable values irrespective of how many tokens were drawn.

Mathematically,

```
sum(log w_t)     ~ O(|y|)
----------- ≈ O(1)      after division by |y|
```

Hence the noise contributed by each token gets averaged out,
leading to a coefficient that changes smoothly as you change policies.
Contrast this with un‑normalised products where small fluctuations grow exponentially with length.

---

### 3. Intuition from variance analysis

Let us look at Eq.(5) (the GSPO gradient) and Eq.(7) (GRPO gradient).

- **GSPO**: every token’s log‑gradient is multiplied by *the same*
      scalar \( s_i^{\,1/|y_i|} \).  
      The weight does **not** fluctuate across tokens: it depends only on the whole sequence.

- **GRPO**: token‑wise weights \(w_{i,t}\) can be sampled close to zero or very large.
      Their product over tokens does not appear; instead each token receives its own scale.
      If one token flips from right to left wrist (e.g., due to random chance),
      its weight could be `$0.01$` while neighbours are `$100$`.  
      Such imbalanced scaling drives up variance dramatically.

A simple variance calculation confirms this intuition.  
Suppose each token log‑ratio has variance $\sigma^2$.  
For GSPO,

```
Var(s^(1/|y|)) ≈ σ^2 / |y|.
```

For GRPO,

```
Var(product over t w_t ) ≈ exp(|y|\sigma^2) – huge.
```

So even modest per‑token variance explodes across long sequences in GRPO,
whereas it is suppressed linearly in GSPO thanks to averaging over length.

---

### 4. How length normalisation interacts with clipping

In a clipped objective we require that all importance ratios lie roughly within a compact interval around 1 otherwise they are discarded or saturated.
When you clip *token–wise* weights you implicitly assume that each ratio is already stable; but because an unnormalised sequence ratio can be several orders of magnitude larger or smaller for longer sequences (see example above), you need very different clipping windows for different tokens or responses – impractical and introducing bias.
With sequence‑wise normalisation all responses – no matter if they comprise 10 or 200 tokens – have weights concentrated around \(e^{0}=1.\)
Hence one global clipping range suffices (e.g., [0.9, 1.1]).
This makes clipped gradients consistent across batch elements and eliminates pathological cases where long texts get clipped out automatically while short ones never get clipped at all.

---

### 5. Summary & Takeaway

- **Token‑wise GRPO** uses an off–policy correction factor that depends on only one random draw per token; its variance grows exponentially with response length because each weight risks being anywhere between zero and infinity.

- **Sequence‑wise GSPO** rewrites the correction factor as an averaged log‑ratio (`length normalization`) → geometric mean of token ratios.
  This averaging *on logs* reduces variance linearly in sequence length and keeps values bounded near 1.

- The bounded importance ratio aligns perfectly with both reward signals (assigned per whole response) and with PPO’s clipping scheme; it also removes sensitivity to expert activation changes in MoE models because only overall likelihood matters.

- Practically this means *fewer clipped samples*, *smaller gradients*, and *stable learning dynamics* even for very large MoE transformers or very long generations—exactly what was observed empirically in the paper.

So, length normalization turns a multiplicative mistake into an additive correction that scales gracefully with sequence size—thereby eliminating the high‑variance problem that plagued token‑level weights in GRPO.
