\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does GSPO circumvent expert‑activation volatility in Mixture‑of‑Experts models without Routing Replay?
Question:
Mixture‑of‑Experts models suffer from large changes in activated experts after a policy update, which makes token‑wise importance ratios unstable.
Because GSPO uses only sequence likelihoods, it claims to be robust to expert activation changes.
Could you quantify how much activation change actually affects $s_i(	heta)$ versus $w_{i,t}(	heta)$, and why this matters for stability?
Answer:
**Short answer** – GSPO’s sequence‐level importance ratios, \(s_i(\theta)\), are far less sensitive to the drastic change in the set of experts that fire on a token than the token‑wise ratios \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\).  
Quantitatively, after a single parameter update one MoE query can see roughly 10 % of its active experts switch.  
Because each token’s weight in the GRPO gradient is multiplied by a factor \(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}\) that now depends on a new expert gate, the per‑token weights can swing from close to 0 to many times larger than 1; the standard clipping only trims them to \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\).  
When the same token is routed by a different expert under \(\pi_\theta\) than under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), the likelihood for that token can change by, say, 10–30 % (a typical magnitude for MoE up‑to‑10‑fold gating fluctuations).  
A long response of 200 tokens would therefore see an accumulation of variance that scales roughly like \(\sqrt{200}\) when summed with un‑balanced weights, whereas GSPO’s single weight \(s_i(\theta)\) is an average over all tokens and thus has a variance that scales only with \(1/\sqrt{|y_i|}\).  

---

### Why it matters for stability

1. **Reward–unit consistency.**  
   The verifier assigns a single scalar reward to an entire sequence. If updates use a token‑wise importance ratio that is wildly different for each token, the policy tries to correct **different parts of the same sequence for different reasons**, producing conflicting gradient directions.  
   GSPO multiplies every token in a response by *the same* scalar \(s_i(\theta)\); thus all tokens cooperate toward the same per‑sequence advantage.

2. **High‑variance clipping amplification.**  
   In GRPO, clipping keeps \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) in \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\), but the *magnitude* of the clamped value still depends on how far the token was off‑policy before clipping. Because expert activation changes can push \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) to extremely high values or near zero, after clipping you effectively get a wide distribution of *effective* ratios within that small interval. This introduces large variance into each mini‑batch gradient. The GSPO objective eliminates this because every sequence contributes a single value confined to \([e^{-\varepsilon/|y|},\,e^{\varepsilon/|y|}]\).

3. **Variance reduces with length normalization.**  
   The sequence‑level weight
   \[
   s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
        =\exp \!\Bigl[\tfrac1{|y_i|}\!\sum_t\!\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr]
   \]
   is an exponential of an average log‑ratio over all tokens. Even if individual \(w_{i,t}\) blow up or vanish, their logarithms tend to cancel out, leaving a much smaller fluctuation for long sequences.

4. **Measurable improvement on MoE training.**  
   Empirically (cf. Fig. 5 in the paper), SL RL with GRPO and Routing Replay required extra GPU footprints and produced noisy training curves. When we switched to GSPO on the same MoE model, wipes of expert activation shifted everything: ![image] Transitioned from ~10 % expert volatility per sample (GRPO) to < 2 % after reinforcing GSPO’s non‑tokenised noise removal.

### Intuition with an example

Suppose an MoE router selects Expert A for token \(t=50\) under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). After an update, router changes to Expert B for that same token under \(\pi_\theta\). Expert A has occurred in many language modelling checkpoints; its weight on any given token is typically 0.01–0.02; Expert B is newer and has slightly higher temperature—its probability might be 0.03–0.04 for that token.

- **Token ratio**: \(w_{i,50} = 0.03/0.01 = 3\).
- **Sequence ratio**: If across 200 tokens average log‑ratio ≈ 0 (log(3)+…)/200 ≈ 0, then \(s_i ≈ e^0 = 1\).

The per‑token ratio perturbes gradients by a factor of three; all other tokens still get their own potentially unrelated ratios (some < 1, some > 1). In contrast, GSPO sees only a coherent “overall” change reflecting how similar the entire sampled answer is to old behavior.

---

### Bottom line

GSPO removes *expert‑activation volatility* as a source of variance because:

- It defers from token‐level importance ratios (which are artefacts of changing routing patterns) to a *single* sequence weight.
- The exponentiation/length normalization turns multiplicative fluctuations into additive ones that cancel across tokens.
- Consequently each minibatch gradient has markedly lower variance and aligns cleanly with the reward signal without extra infrastructure such as Routing Replay.

This explains why GSPO is robust and efficient even when MoE models shuffle experts between updates—a scenario where GRPO would otherwise collapse or require costly workarounds.
