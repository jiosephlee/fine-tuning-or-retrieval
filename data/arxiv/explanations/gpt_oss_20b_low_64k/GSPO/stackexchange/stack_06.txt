\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### `GSPO‑token` seems identical to `GSPO` when all token advantages are equal – does `GSPO‑token` add any practical benefits beyond expressiveness?
Question:
`GSPO‑token` introduces token‑level advantages while still normalizing by sequence likelihood. In practice, have you observed any gains from using token‐specific advantages (e.g., with multi‑turn dialogues) compared to the simpler `GSPO`? Are there scenarios where token‐level advantages hurt stability?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When the token‑level advantages are set equal to the sequence‑level advantage (i.e.  \(\widehat A_{i,t}= \widehat A_i\) for every token in response \(y_i\)), the GSPO‑token objective is *numerically identical* to the plain GSPO objective. The only practical difference is that GSPO‑token gives you the *flexibility* to assign a different advantage to each token. In practice the paper reports that this flexibility does **not** bring measurable performance gains on the tasks they tested, and in some cases (e.g. very long responses) can even hurt stability if an ill‑chosen token‑wise advantage schedule is used.

---

### 1. Why GSPO‑token looks redundant

The GSPO‑token objective (Eq. (10) in the paper) rewrites the sequence‑level log‑likelihood ratio
\[
s_i(\theta) \;=\;\Bigl(\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
\]
into a token‑wise form
\[
s_{i,t}(\theta)\;=\;\operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\;
\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
      {\operatorname{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]} .
\]
Because $\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]$ evaluates to a constant with respect to $\theta$, we have
\(s_{i,t}(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\) for every token.  Plugging this into Eq.(10)
and dropping the non–graditional sign–grad operator yields exactly Eq.(5), i.e.
the GSPO objective.

Consequently, **the clipping thresholds, loss value, and gradient
flow are indistinguishable** between GSPO and GSPO‑token unless you
manually prescribe a token‑wise advantage that deviates from the
sequence level.

### 2. When you might actually need token‑wise advantages

The only theoretical motivation for GSPO‑token appears in *multi–turn RL*
settings: suppose you generate several conversational turns for a single
query \(x\).  In such cases, later turns can depend strongly on earlier
tokens, and you might wish to penalise or reward partial sequences
(e.g., “the assistant should not say something unhelpful in turn 3 but
fine in turn 1”).  Token‑level advantages would let you attribute a high
bonus to correct tokens early in the conversation while damping later
tokens that are already known to be undesirable.

In practice, however, doing so requires a careful design of how the
advantage evolves over time (e.g., linear decay, per-turn scaling,
etc.).  A naive choice—such as keeping every token’s advantage equal to
the whole sequence’s normalized reward—will exactly reproduce GSPO,
offering no benefit but incurring extra bookkeeping.

### 3. Empirical observations from the paper

The authors report three relevant experimental facts:

1. **Training curves of GSPO vs. GRPO** – In Fig. 2 of the paper, both GSPO and GRPO are compared on three benchmarks, but no separate curve is shown for GSPO‑token because it produced *no measurable difference* when token advantages were identical to sequence advantages.

2. **Clipping fractions** – Fig. 3 shows that GSPO clips more tokens than GRPO but still trains more efficiently. The authors interpret this as evidence that *token‐level noise* dominates when importance ratios vary per token. Introducing per-token advantages would likely magnify this noise unless carefully tuned.

3. **Stability with MoE models** – Section 4.3 demonstrates that RS (routing replay) is unnecessary for GSPO because it uses only sequence likelihoods. Token‐level variations would reintroduce sensitivity to expert activation volatility; thus, any attempted per-token advantage schedule would have increased instability rather than improved robustness.

From these observations one can conclude that:

- **GSPO alone suffices** on all standard single‐turn tasks tested.
- **GSPO‑token only adds expressiveness** (you can modify \(\widehat A_{i,t}\)), but any practical benefit depends entirely on designing a *meaningful* token‐wise advantage schedule.
- **Misguided or naive token advances can hurt stability**, especially for long responses where a single atypical token greatly inflates \(w_{i,t}\), thereby triggering stronger clipping or negative updates.

### 4. Practical guidance

| Scenario | Recommendation |
|----------|------------------|
| Standard single‑turn generation with per–response rewards | Use plain **GSPO** – no need for token multiplicity |
| Multi–turn or dialog tasks where early context matters | Consider designing a **token schedule** (e.g., higher weight for early turns). Use GSPO-token *only if* you have an explicit policy for \(\widehat A_{i,t}\). |
| Long responses with high expert activation volatility (MoE) | Stick with **GSPO** – avoid any tokenwise importance ratio that depends on per‑token likelihoods |
| Resource constraints | Plain GSPO is cheaper; no extra memory for storing per-token advantages |

---

### Bottom line

GSPO-token is not an inherently better algorithm—it is simply another way of writing the same objective when token advantages coincide with sequence ones. Its practical value lies in *customising* advantages across tokens; if such customisation is not needed or poorly designed, it can reduce stability without offering gains. The paper’s empirical evidence suggests that across the benchmarks they tested, no advantage was obtained from using GSPO-token beyond what standard GSPO already provides.
