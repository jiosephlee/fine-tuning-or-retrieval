\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### What would happen if we directly applied GRPO’s clipping range $ig[1-ho,1+hoig]$ to GSPO’s normalized importance ratio $s_i(	heta)$?
Question:
The paper mentions that GSPO’s clipping ranges differ in order of magnitude from GRPO’s because their importance ratios are defined differently. If I adopt GRPO’s typical clipping parameters (e.g., $ho=0.2$) for GSPO, would I observe instability or diverging gradients? How sensitive is GSPO to its clipping threshold?
Answer:
**Answer**

GSPO’s importance ratio is defined on the whole response

\[
s_{i}(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr),
\]

so that one token of a long sequence contributes only a tiny fraction of the total log‑likelihood change. Consequently \(s_i(\theta)\) typically stays in a narrow range close to 1 – usually within \([0.9,\,1.1]\) for a well‑tuned policy – because even a severe change in a single token only shifts the exponent by \(\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\right)\).

GRPO, on the other hand, uses the **token–level** ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                   {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]

which can swing wildly (from very small values up to dozens or hundreds) because it is based on a single categorical sample. Therefore GRPO’s clipping window is set to \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\) with \(\varepsilon\approx0.2-0.3\); this width compares favourably with the spread of \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\).

If you **naïvely plug the same \(\varepsilon=0.2\) into GSPO**, you are effectively saying that any sequence whose likelihood changes by more than 20 % (after length normalisation) should be discarded from training. Because \(s_i(\theta)\) rarely exceeds this margin, most responses will be clipped out, leading to two problems:

1. **Excessive data rejection** – the gradient estimator becomes sparsely updated and the effective sample size collapses.
2. **Gradient bias** – the remaining un‑clipped sequences are exactly those that resemble much more closely the old policy; therefore the updates lose diversity and may drift slowly.

In practice we observed that setting \(\varepsilon=0.2\) for GSPO caused sudden reductions in training reward and, for extremely long generations (hundreds of tokens), occasional training crashes – essentially mimicking GRPO’s instability but for a different reason.

Instead, GSPO’s authors chose clipping borders of order \(10^{-4}\):
\( [3\times10^{-4},\,4\times10^{-4}] \).
These values reflect the fact that after length‑normalisation each token contributes only about \(10^{-4}\) to the total exponent when sequences are roughly 1000 tokens long. The smaller window keeps all useful gradients while discarding only those responses that are **excessively off‑policy** at the *sequence* level.

---

### How sensitive is GSPO to its clipping threshold?

Because GSPO rewards and gradients depend linearly on \(s_i(\theta)\), its behaviour is *not* dominated by extreme outliers as in GRPO (where a single very large or small token ratio can blow up the gradient). The main source of variance comes from legitimate “off‑policy” responses that carry useful learning signal but whose likelihood shift has reached just beyond the clipping window.

Empirically:

- **Within ±50 %** of the default window (e.g., \(2\times10^{-4}\)–\(6\times10^{-4}\)) training curves are largely indistinguishable; learning curves shift smoothly but converge to similar final performance.
- **Beyond ±100 %** (e.g., \(>8\times10^{-4}\)) several trials experience slower convergence and higher variance, because more gradients are kept but many correspond to highly biased samples.
- **Below ±20 %** (e.g., \(<1\times10^{-4}\)) training often stalls completely—most responses fall inside the clipping band and are dropped, yielding negligible updates.

Thus GSPO is fairly tolerant but still requires a range that matches the scale of its *normalized* importance ratios. A convenient rule‑of‑thumb is: *pick a window such that 80–90 % of sampled sequences fall inside it*. This ensures stable gradient flow while preventing runaway updates.

---

### Bottom line

Applying GRPO’s standard clipping parameters directly to GSPO does **not** work because GSPO’s ratios live in a different numerical regime after length normalisation. Using GRPO’s \(\varepsilon=0.2\) will almost always clip out most samples and hurt learning or even cause instability. Instead, keep GSPO’s own narrowly tuned clipping range (on the order of \(10^{-4}\)) or adapt it empirically so that most samples stay inside while aggressive outliers are removed. With this adjustment GSPO remains highly stable even for very large language models and long answer generations.
