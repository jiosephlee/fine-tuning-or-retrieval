# Chapter Title  
**Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models**

## Introduction to RL for Language Models

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged over the past decade as a powerful paradigm for shaping the behavior of language models beyond what supervised fine‑tuning alone can achieve. While supervised objectives teach a model to predict the next token from a human‑annotated corpus, RL endows the model with a mechanism to optimize arbitrary, often opaque, signal functions such as human‑feedback scores, task‑specific metrics, or composite reward schemes that capture logical consistency, factuality, or fairness. Crucially, a language model trained solely on next‑token prediction cannot “reason” about multi‑step consequences of its own output; RL provides a framework in which longer sequences are evaluated as whole entities and updates are driven by their quality as judged by a verifier.

The ability of RL to generate high‑quality reasoning traces has been demonstrated in numerous benchmarks: solving algebraic problems that require nested deduction chains, completing programming tasks that span dozens of lines, or guiding dialogue systems toward coherent goal achievement. These achievements have been largely made possible by large, autoregressive language models (LLMs) with billions of parameters that can internally represent complex algorithmic reasoning patterns.

Yet the same scalability that unlocks these capabilities also induces fragile training dynamics. When scaling model width or depth and pushing generation lengths beyond several hundred tokens, even modest methodological differences can result in catastrophic training collapse—where the model abruptly loses its language modeling ability and cannot recover even after reverting to earlier checkpoints. This phenomenon has been witnessed repeatedly when training Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) variants of LLMs, where sparsity introduces volatile routing patterns across updates. The RL training loop typically injects off‑policy samples into gradient computation via importance sampling and clipping; if these mechanisms are imperfectly designed, the variance propagated through many tokens can snowball into irreversible divergence.

This chapter surveys the historical trajectory of RL in natural language processing (NLP), identifies the specific obstacles encountered when extending RL to massive LLMs, and examines why earlier algorithms such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) fail to maintain stability at scale. We end by setting the stage for an algorithmic refinement that restores stable learning while retaining strong performance: Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).

---

### Historical Evolution of RL in NLP

| Era | Representative Methods | Key Contributions |
|-----|------------------------|-------------------|
| 2014–2017 | *Adversarial Learning* (seqGAN), *Policy Gradient* from 2016 onward | Introduced the use of generative adversarial networks and actor–critic methods for text generation; laid groundwork for treating text as a sequence of actions in an MDP |
| 2018–2020 | *PPO for Dialogue* (RLHF), *CPO*, *CoRe* | Applied proximal policy optimization to human feedback tuned systems; pioneered trust‑region ideas in NLP |
| 2021–2023 | *GRPO*, *RLHF with large MoE models* | Demonstrated that large LLMs could be rewarded on long responses; exposed severe collapse issues when using token‑level importance ratios |
| 2024 | *GSPO* | Reoriented importance sampling to sequence likelihood, achieving stable training even for MoE LLMs |

This progression shows an increasing push toward massive LLMs: from few‑million parameter recurrent models up to thousands of billions of parameters in MoE configurations. Each step required revisiting algorithmic foundations because scaling changes not only sample efficiency but also statistical properties such as gradient variance and distribution mismatch between old and new policies.

---

### Scale‑up Challenges for LLMs

1. **Off‑Policy Drift**  
   Training data is generated from an older policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ while gradients are computed against a newer policy $\pi_\theta$. As $\pi_\theta$ diverges from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$—a natural consequence of large batch sizes required for hardware utilization—the raw samples become increasingly off‑policy. In conventional actor–critic setups this mismatch is corrected through importance sampling ratios and trust‑region clipping.

2. **Gradient Variance Accumulation**  
   An autoregressive token generation produces a chain $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{|y|}$ whose joint likelihood is $\pi_\theta(y|x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})$. Even when individual importance ratios $w_{t}=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t)}$ are bounded around $1$, their product over dozens or hundreds of steps can explode or vanish due to numerical underflow/overflow or homogeneity assumptions breaking down.

3. **MoE Routing Volatility**  
   In MoE architectures each token’s computation path depends on router logits that change sharply with each update because sparsely activated experts receive disproportionate gradient signals. This instability manifests as large changes in which experts participate in forward passes after one gradient step—leading token‑level importance ratios to vary wildly.

4. **Computation Bottlenecks**  
   To compute per‑token advantage estimates one typically requires an auxiliary value network matching policy size, which is both memory intensive and adds compute overhead not negligible at scale.

5. **Precision Mismatch Between Training and Inference Engines**  
   Training frameworks (e.g., Megatron) often use mixed precision FP32/FP16 while inference engines (e.g., vLLM) resort to lower precision or quantization for speed. Calculating token‐level likelihoods across these engines introduces additional errors not found when using sequence‐level likelihoods.

---

### Stability vs. Performance Trade‐offs

Stability guarantees usually demand conservative update rules: small clipping ranges ($\varepsilon$), high sample efficiency demands many rollouts per batch, and frequent re-generation avoid catastrophic drift. However conservative settings hurt performance because reward signals are suppressed: good policies may be penalized if their likelihood changes exceed clipping thresholds too often. Conversely, enlarging clipping ranges enhances exploration but risks runaway variance—especially detrimental when rewards are only available at sequence level but gradients accrue at token level.

The ideal algorithm simultaneously respects the distributional nature of the problem (importance sampling must be applied correctly), leverages sequence‑level feedback naturally aligned with rewards, yet remains computationally tractable on gigantic LLMs.

---

### Limitations of Existing Algorithms (e.g., GRPO)

GRPO was conceived to sidestep the need for costly value networks by exploiting relative advantages within groups of responses to a single query:
\[
\widehat A_i
=
\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\!\left(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\right)}
       {\operatorname{std}\!\left(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\right)} .
\]
Its objective sums over tokens
\[
s_i
=\min \bigl( w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i ,\, \mathrm{clip}\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta),\, 1-\varepsilon ,\, 1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_i\bigr),
\]
where $w_{i,t}$ is computed at every token location from a single sample drawn from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.

This construction breaks two essential properties:

1. **Misapplication of Importance Sampling**  
   The classic identity
   \[
   \mathbb E_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
   = 
   \mathbb E_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\Bigl[\, \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} f(z)\Bigr]
   \]
   presumes many independent draws $z$ from $\pi_{\text{beh}}$ so that averaging over them cancels variance introduced by reweighting.
   GRPO applies the ratio $w_{i,t}$ derived from only one sample at each time step; thus no averaging occurs to mitigate variance.
   
2. **Token vs Sequence Mismatch**  
   Rewards $r(x,y)$ are attributed to whole sequences $y$, yet GRPO multiplies per-token importance ratios with sequence-level advantages. For long responses this coherence disappears—the policy update is effectively guided by contradictory signals at two different temporal resolutions leading to highly noisy gradients whose variance scales linearly with response length.

Empirically GRPO becomes unstable on modern MoE LLMs whenever expert routing shifts sufficiently between successive updates—a phenomenon known as expert‐activation volatility—which magnifies token‐level linkages between probability ratios and routing decisions.

In summary, GRPO’s design forces an ill‑posed optimization where importance weights do not act as correction factors but rather as amplification factors for high-variance noise, causing trajectory collapse early during training on massive models or MoE configurations.

# 2. Preliminaries and Notation

In order to study reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models, we must first establish a clean mathematical playground.  This chapter defines the objects that appear in every equation that follows: a language model viewed as a stochastic policy, the data that drives training, how we quantify the quality of a generated sequence, and finally the two main baselines – Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).  The notation introduced here is reused verbatim throughout the rest of the book, so it is worth taking the time to internalize it.

---

## Autoregressive Policy Definition \( \theta \)

A generative language model produces tokens one after another, conditioning on the prompt (or *query*) \( x \) and on the history already generated.  Formally, for any token time step \( t\in \{1,\dots ,|y|\} \), a batch of queries \( x\in \mathcal{D} \) and a response sequence \( y = (y_1,\dots ,y_{|y|})\) are given.  The model assigns a conditional probability
\[
\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_t \mid x,\; y_{<t}\bigr)\quad ,
\]
where \( y_{<t}=(y_1,\dots ,y_{t-1}) \) denotes all previously sampled tokens.  By multiplying these one‑step probabilities we obtain the *sequence likelihood*
\[
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_t \mid x,y_{<t}\bigr).
\]
The vector of learnable parameters \( \theta \) fully determines this probability distribution; hence we may regard \( \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x) \) as an **Autoregressive Policy**.

*Intuition.*  
The policy view brings RL machinery—policy gradients, importance sampling, clipping—directly into sequence modeling.  Every token emitted by an autoregressive model is just an action taken by the policy at a particular step of an MDP whose state comprises the query together with generated history.

---

## Query–Response Representation

A training example consists of two parts:

1. **Query**: A text prompt \(x\).  We collect a dataset
   \[
   \mathcal{D}=\{x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\dots ,x^{(N)}\},
   \]
   where each element is independently sampled from some distribution over prompts relevant to the downstream task.

2. **Response**: A sequence of tokens \( y^i = (y^i_1,\dots ,y^i_{|y^i|})\) generated from the current policy given a fixed query \(x^i\).  
   During training we produce multiple responses per query:
   \[
   y_i^{(k)} = g_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(x^i)\quad k=1,\dots ,G,
   \]
   where \(g_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) denotes stochastic decoding (e.g., sampling or top‑\(k\)).  

The pair \( (x^i,y_i^{(k)}) \) is then fed to an external verifier or reward function to compute how useful or correct that response was.

---

## Reward Modeling

After decoding, each pair receives a scalar **reward**
\[
r(x,y)\in[0,1].
\]
In practice this could be an oracle evaluation reward such as pass‑rate on CodeForces or AIME pass‑score, or any environment‑specific scalar score produced by a verifier neural network.

Because RL typically optimizes *relative* outcomes rather than absolute scores, we often normalize rewards within a *group*—the set of responses generated for one particular query—in subsequent algorithms.

---

## Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

PPO is perhaps the most widely used policy‑gradient method in modern RL.  Its core idea is to update the policy parameters by maximizing an objective that includes **clipped importance ratios** so that each update stays close to the old policy.

Let us denote by
\[
w_t(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_t \mid x,y_{<t}\bigr)}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\!\bigl(y_t \mid x,y_{<t}\bigr)}
\]
the **token‑level importance ratio**—the likelihood ratio under current versus old policies for token \(t\).  
The advantage estimate for token \(t\) is denoted by
\( A_t=\widehat{A}_t\).  In standard PPO an auxiliary value network predicts \(A_t\), but this part is omitted here for brevity.

The PPO objective then reads:
\[
J_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)
=
\E_{\,x\sim{\cal D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
    \Biggl[
      \frac{1}{|y|}
      \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
          {\rm clip}\!\Bigl(
              w_t(\theta),\, 1-\varepsilon,\,
                              1+\varepsilon
          )
          A_t
    \Biggr].
    \tag{2.1}
\]
The clipping operator ${\rm clip}(z,a,b)$ returns $a$ if $z<a$, $b$ if $z>b$, otherwise $z$.  
Intuitively, if an update would push too far away from data collected by $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ (issueing large $w_t$), it is capped at $\pm\,{\varepsilon}$ so that learning remains conservative.

Note that in PPO every token receives its own importance ratio; negative advantages are also clipped separately in order to avoid overly aggressive gradient steps in discouraging sequences.

---

## Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

GRPO introduces two innovations aimed at making RL training more scalable when we have *lots of parameters* but *limited compute per step*:

1. **Remove Value Network**: Instead of learning token‑wise value estimates from scratch, GRPO re‑scales each *entire response* against its siblings in the same query group.
2. **Token‑Level Importance Ratio**: GRPO still uses $w_{i,t}$ per token but couples it with group‑based advantage estimates.

Concretely, given one query $x$ and $G$ sampled responses $\{ y_i : i=1,\ldots,G \}$ from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$,
the GRPO objective is:
\[
J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
=
\E_{\,x,\{y_i\}}
    \Biggl[
      \frac{1}{G}
      \sum_{i=1}^{G}
            {\rm clip}\!\Bigl(
                w_{i,t}(\theta),\, 1-\varepsilon,\,
                                1+\varepsilon
            )
            A_i
    \Biggr],
    % with tokens inside each response summed:
    % Actually
    % (...)
    =(This expression omitted for brevity)
    .
    ~~~(2.2)
\]
where

- Token‑level ratio:
  \[
  w_{i,t}(\theta)=
      \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
           {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
  \]
- Token‑level advantage:
  Since all tokens share the same advantage for response $i$,
  define
  \[
  A_i=
      r(x,y_i)
          -
      {\rm mean}_{j=1}^G r(x,y_j),
  \quad 
        {\rm std}_{j=1}^G r(x,y_j),
        %
        \
       A_i=\frac {A_i}{ {\rm std}_{j=1}^G r(x,y_j)} .
        %
        (\text{i.e., normalized by group statistics})
    .
    (
      %
       )
    .
    ~~~(2.3)
  ```

Each weight $w_{i,t}$ applies a different “importance” factor to each token in every generated response.

*Why this matters.*  
PPO uses only samples drawn from $\pi_{\theta}$ itself for both estimating values and computing rewards; BE most efficient but expensive because a separate value network must be trained and maintained.
GRPO cuts through this overhead by using relative rewards inside groups – cheaper but still reliance on token‑wise ratios gives rise to high variance when sequences are long or models have sparse activation patterns like Mixture‑of‑Experts models.

--- 

With these preliminaries established—the policy form, data representation, reward computation, and operationally critical PPO/GRPO objectives—we now possess the foundational vocabulary necessary for introducing novel algorithmic ideas that follow in later chapters.

# 3. Why Token‑Level Importance Ratios Fail

Reinforcement‐learning (RL) training of large language models often relies on a proxy objective that mirrors the expected reward of a generated sequence. One popular approach is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which augments the standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) objective with a relative advantage estimation over a small cohort of responses to the same prompt. At first glance, GRPO looks like a natural extension of PPO: it replaces the per‑token advantage with a group‑based relative advantage and keeps the clipping mechanism to keep off‑policy updates in check. However, when we probe deeper into the statistical underpinnings of GRPO, we uncover that its token‑level importance ratios violate the core premise of importance sampling. This misalignment leads to a cascade of high‑variance gradient estimates that become catastrophic as response lengths grow.

In this chapter we unpack the reasoning behind this phenomenon in four steps:

1. Recall the classic *importance sampling* principle that justifies re‑weighting of samples.
2. Examine how GRPO defines its per‑token importance ratios.
3. Show how these ratios break the natural alignment between *reward units* (sequence‑level) and *gradient units* (token‑level).
4. Quantify how variance accumulates across long sequences and why this manifests in empirical collapse.

Let us proceed systematically.

## 3.1 Importance Sampling Principle

Suppose we want to estimate an expectation under a target distribution \(\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}\) for some measurable function \(f(z)\),

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\mathrm{tar}}}\!\bigl[f(z)\bigr]
:= \int f(z)\,\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z .
\]

Direct sampling from \(\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}\) might be expensive or impossible; instead we can sample from a *behavior* distribution \(\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}\), provided it covers the support of \(\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}\). The classical trick is to rewrite the expectation as

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\mathrm{tar}}}\!\bigl[f(z)\bigr]
=
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\mathrm{beh}}}\!\left[\, w(z)\,f(z)\right],
\qquad
w(z)
:=
\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}(z)} .
\tag{3.1}
\]

The factor \(w(z)\) is called the *importance weight*. Its purpose is twofold:

1. **Distribution Correction** – It re‑weights each behavior sample so that, in expectation, the weighted samples mimic draws from the target.
2. **Variance Control** – When many behavior samples are averaged, extreme values of \(w(z)\) tend to cancel out, provided \(w(z)\) is not too heavy‐tailed.

Crucially, learning updates that rely on importance sampling rely on averaging over *many* independent samples from \(\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}\). Only then does \(w(z)\) serve its intended purpose; if you apply \(w(z)\) to a single draw, you are not correcting any distributional bias but simply injecting an extra scaling factor that can dramatically alter gradients.

In language modelling, each generated response \(y = (y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})\) can be seen as being drawn from an autoregressive policy \(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\). The natural target policy for RL update is the current policy \(\pi_\theta\), while the old policy used for sample collection is \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\). Accordingly, a precise importance weight for an entire response should be

\[
s(y;\theta)
:= \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)} .
\tag{3.2}
\]

Because sequence likelihood factorises over tokens, we can compute this ratio incrementally:

\[
s(y;\theta)
= \prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})} .
\]

Since RL rewards are assigned to full sequences by a verifier \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\), it is intuitively correct to compare rewards against this *sequence‑level* importance weight.

## 3.2 GRPO’s Per‑Token Importance Ratio Definition

GRPO follows PPO’s clipping philosophy but replaces the token‐wise advantage estimation with a group relative advantage computed over multiple responses to the same prompt \(x\). For a grouped batch of \(G\) responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\), GRPO’s objective contains terms that look like

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
:= 
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})} ,
\quad
\widehat A_{i,t}
:= 
\widehat A_i
:= 
\dfrac{
  r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(r(x,y_1),\dots,r(x,y_G)\bigr)
}{
  \operatorname{std}\!\bigl(r(x,y_1),\dots,r(x,y_G)\bigr)
}.
\tag{3.3}
\]

That is, each token receives its own *independent* importance weight equally derived from its current versus old local next‑token probability. The total update for response \(y_i\) then aggregates weighted advantages over every token in turn:

\[
J_{\text{GRPO}}
=
\E_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\left[
   \frac1G
   \sum_{i=1}^G
      \frac1{|y_i|}
      \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \min(
           w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,
           ~
           \clip(w_{i,t}(\theta), 1-\varepsilon , 1+\varepsilon )\, 
           \widehat A_i
        )
   \right].
   \tag{3.4}
\]

Observe that GRPO’s per-token weight is defined *once per token*, even though it is used multiplicatively across all tokens in that same response when forming gradients through terms like  

\(w_{i,t}(\theta)\nabla_\theta \log\tau_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t}).\)

This contrasts directly with equation (3.2): GRPO omits the product over tokens and treats each token separately.

## 3.3 Misalignment Between Reward and Gradient Units

The reward supplied by verifier \(r(x,y_i)\) accounts for an entire sequence; its natural unit is “score per response”. Yet GRPO assigns a *token*-wise adjustment factor based on an individual next‑token probability ratio:

- **Reward unit** – Sequence level (\([0,1]\)).
- **Gradient weighting unit** – Token level (\([0,\infty)\)).

Because every token adopts its own weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), gradients are effectively weighted heterogeneously within one response’s contribution to loss, which should not happen when all tokens jointly contribute to the same reward signal.

Moreover, note that if one user query yields a short sequence versus another query yielding a longer sequence, their weights will be compared against different magnitudes since Rotten Hypothesis: The multiplier per token can vary dramatically because many tokens may have low or high probabilities under either policy. Consequently, two sequences of different lengths may experience very different levels of weight amplification or reduction erroneously.

In effect:

- **Token-level weighting violates symmetry**: all tokens participate in generating one reward; treating them separately distributes unjustified emphasis.
- **Clipping range mismatch**: Tokens with large weights are clipped only within a narrow interval defined by ego hyperparameters suited for scalar weights (close to 0 or 1), yet their actual magnitudes might be far larger due to cumulative effect across many tokens.

Thus there exists an intrinsic mismatch between what is being optimized (the expected reward over sequences) and how gradients influence parameters (per-token weighting). This misalignment breeds high variance.

## 3.4 Variance Accumulation Over Long Responses

Let us quantify how this mismatch expands variance in practice. Consider a simple illustration where we ignore clipping for clarity:

For response \(y_i=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)\),

- The true sequence likelihood ratio equals  
\(s(y;\theta)= \prod_{t=1}^{n} w_t(\theta).\)

- GRPO incorrectly weights each token by its own single‐step ratio:
  
\(g^{(GR)} 
:= 
\E_{
   x,\{y_i,(caret):+?\}
}\!
    [\, w_t(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta
        \log\tau_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})\,].  
   ` 

When we index overall πθ changes and take expectation over all tokens across responses,
the variance term associated with each gradient update contains moments of all individual ratios—particularly because they multiply heterogeneously across tokens inside one sum—and these terms do not cancel out across tokens.

Formally,
let δ_t denote log-KL divergence increments:
\(δ_t = 
   -\,{\rm KL}\Bigl(
      τ_{\theta}(·|x,y_{<t})
      || τ_{\tildeθ}(·|x,y_{<t})
   \Bigr),
   ,
    +??,
```

where θ denotes current policy parameters and tildeθ denotes parameter snapshots at previous epochs or alternative local resets typical in RL training pipelines.

The product form in equation (3.2) implies that if each δ_t has mean zero under θ ≈ θ_old then their sum’s variance grows linearly with n: each step adds independent noise terms leading to cumulative variance ≈ n σ² . By contrast, multiplying all δ_t together yields noise whose relative scale decreases as more terms are added because exponentiation tends toward stabilisation under bounded δ(t).

Empirically,
long responses (≥ 200 words common in instruction–follow tasks or evaluation scenarios such as code generation problems) amplify these differences dramatically:
the product form yields sensible discounting because more probable long sequences tend to have small ratios close to unity; whereas tokens’ individual ratios range widely and their average does not converge toward unity unless averaged over many independent draws—contrary to our actual practice where we average only among ~ 4 mini‐batches comprising ~ 10^5 samples total per iteration.

Hence variance grows super linearly with length when using token-level Δ_t weighting and cannot be counteracted by clipping because each token may lie individually outside clipping bounds while still contributing proportionally large gradient components due to overall log-likelihood drift.

## 3.5 Empirical Evidence of Catastrophic Collapse

The theoretical mismatch crystallises in robust empirical failure modes observed on large models:

- During RL fine-tuning with GRPO on Qwen series MoE models (48 layers × 107B parameters), intermediated gradient updates cause “expert‐activation volatility” where many sub‑networks become enabled or disabled between consecutive policy snapshots.
  
- This volatility produces wildly fluctuating per-token ratio values; some ratios explode beyond pre-set clipping thresholds even though global sequence likelihood stays roughly stable.
  
- When these outlier ratios drive updates via equation (3.4), they propel abrupt policy shifts that undermine future rollouts; once rollout policies become misaligned with training data distribution, PPO/GRPO’s minimisation principle cannot rescue them—training collapses irreversibly into degenerate “generation freeze” behaviors where no useful content is produced.
  
- Rebooting from earlier checkpoints fails because the underlying optimiser has amassed pathological curvature: gradient ascent directions suddenly change sign due to extreme off-policy corrections hidden inside misplaced token-wise weights.
  
These failure traceable symptoms become increasingly severe as model size grows: more experts → higher chance that different routing modes activate arbitrarily between rolls → higher variance in w_ts → catastrophic gradient spikes → model collapse within only five or ten RL iterations while still operating on unchanged test queries.

In contrast, PG experiments using GSPO—which applies sequence-level correction as defined in equation (3.2)—show no such collapse: each gradient step incorporates a perfectly balanced influence from all tokens since it uses s(y;θ)=∏_t w_t(θ). Even when truncating length or narrowing clipping ranges by orders of magnitude compared to GRPO values (≈10⁻⁴ vs ≈10⁻¹ proximity around unity), performance remains stable thanks to lower effective variance after normalization across entire sequences.

---

In summary, although at first glance GRPO seems poised as an elegant extension of PPO for language models—leveraging advances such as grouping—but its core reliance on token-level importance weights directly violates agreement between reward granularity and optimisation granularity mandated by proper importance sampling theory. This oversight magnifies variance especially along longer generations and ultimately leads to irreversible training collapse in state-of-the-art large language models and Mixture-of-Experts architectures. Recognising this fundamental flaw motivates group sequence policy optimisation that operates naturally at sequence scale and restores both statistical soundness and practical robustness._

# 4. The Core Idea of GSPO – Sequence‑Level Clipping

Reinforcement learning for language models is a paradigmatic example of the mismatch that can arise when we impose per‑token objectives on what is, in practice, a reward that is assigned to an entire generated sequence. The Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm resolves this mismatch by redefining the importance weight, the clipping target, and ultimately the entire learning dynamics in terms of **sequence likelihood** rather than token likelihood. In this chapter we unpack the intuition and mathematics underlying this insight, explain why length‑normalization is essential to keep the algorithm numerically stable, and revisit the basic principle of importance sampling to rigorously justify the proposed design.

---

## Sequence Likelihood as Importance Weight

Recall that an autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$ defines a policy
\[
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x , y_{<t}),
\]
which is simply the product of next‑token probabilities for each position $t$ in response $y$ given query $x$. In classic on‑policy training each sample is drawn from $\pi_\theta$ itself; but during large‑scale RL we typically have a *behavior* policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ from which we sample responses and then condition updates on them. To correct for this off‑policy discrepancy we need an **importance ratio** that reweights expectations under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ to match those under $\pi_\theta$. 

The natural choice is therefore
\[
s_i(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)},
\qquad \forall\, i\in\{1,\dots,G\}.
\]
Unlike token‑level ratios such as
$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$,
which are defined on a single token, the sequence‑level weight aggregates over the entire sequence and, crucially, obeys the same probabilistic interpretation as in *importance sampling*: it measures how much more (or less) likely the sampled response $y_i$ would be under the current policy compared with its likelihood under the old policy.

This formulation brings two immediate benefits:

1. **Alignment with rewards** – The verifier $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$ assigns a scalar score to the whole response. Using a sequence-level weight guarantees that our gradient updates are modulated by an importance factor that pertains to exactly this unit of reward, avoiding any mismatch that arises from attempting to adjust tokens individually.

2. **Simplicity of clipping** – In proximal policy optimization (PPO), clipping acts as a safeguard against too large updates by bounding how far we may move away from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. With token‑level ratios these bounds are applied to each token separately; with sequence weights we instead bound a single quantity $\forall i$, simplifying both implementation and tuning.

Mathematically GSPO’s core objective can now be written compactly as
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\!\left[
 \frac{1}{G}\!\sum_{i=1}^G
   \min \bigl(
      s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,
     \, \operatorname{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat A_i
   \bigr)
 \right],
\]
where each advantage $\widehat A_i$ is normalized over responses belonging to one query. This objective mirrors exactly how PPO handles per‑step returns but lifted entirely to the sequence level.

---

## Length Normalization Technique

A practical obstacle arises when computing sequence likelihood ratios: longer sequences tend to accrue larger exponentials or products that may overflow or underflow numeric ranges. Moreover, unless properly scaled, very long sequences can produce importance ratios that vary wildly—one token flip could drastically change $\pi_\theta(y)$ relative to $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)$. To allay these issues GSPO introduces **length normalization**:

\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\left(
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}
\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=
\exp
   \left(
     \frac{1}{|y_i|}
       \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log
           \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x , y_{i,<t})}
                {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x , y_{i,<t})}
   \right).
\]

Because it is an exponentiated *average* over log‐ratios per token, each $s_i(\theta)$ lies close to unity for moderate deviation between policies regardless of sequence length. In other words:

- If every token’s forward probability changes only slightly when moving from $\theta_{\text{old}}$ to $\theta$, then each log-ratio stays small and $s_i(\theta)\approx 1$, even for sequences of several hundred tokens.
- Conversely, if many tokens change dramatically, $s_i(\theta)$ will deviate from one accordingly; yet because it is averaged, it will not explode or vanish.

Thus length normalization shields GSPO from two intertwined problems: exploding gradients due to uncontrolled importance ratios and sensitivity to slight changes in longer sequences—both essential for stable learning at billion‑parameter scales.

---

## Theoretical Justification via Importance Sampling

Let us revisit the core premise of importance sampling in probability theory. Suppose we want to compute expectation of some function $f(z)$ under a target distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)$. If we can only draw samples from an easier behaviour distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)$, then

\[
E_{\pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
=
E_{\pi_{\text{beh}}}
   \!\!
      \left[
        \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}
        f(z)
      \right].
\]

The fraction $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ is precisely the *importance weight*. It guarantees unbiasedness if many independent samples are used; its variance decreases with larger sample sizes because fluctuations in any single sample are averaged out across many draws.

In RL language generation we identify:

- Behaviour distribution: $\pi_{\θold}(\cdot|x)$ – policy used for rollout.
- Target distribution:  $\πθ(\cdot|x)$ – policy being updated.
- Function:   $f(y)=r(x,y)-b(x)$ where $b(x)$ is a baseline (here represented implicitly via advantage normalization).

The natural applicability rule says **the importance weight must be defined over whole sampled outcomes** (the generated responses). Therefore, assigning a weight per *token* violates this rule because each token is not an independent draw from any distribution—tokens are sequentially dependent via their conditioning context. Even though each step uses a conditional distribution, applying an importance ratio at one step but weighting another step independently disrupts the overall expectation formula.

By defining $s_i(\θ)=πθ(y_i|x)/πθold(y_i|x)$ we restore exactness:

- We reweight every entire sample correctly because we multiply the ratios across all tokens together.
- Summing over many samples ensures high‑variance cancellation through Monte Carlo estimation.
- Our objective becomes
  \(E [ s\,A ] = E[ πθ/πθold × A ] = E[ A' ]\)
  where \(A' = r(x,y)-b(x)\) transformed appropriately.
  
Moreover, clipping at the *sequence level* corresponds directly to truncating extreme importance weights that would otherwise lead us too far away from our old policy—a concept analogous to PPO’s *trust region*. Since GSPO’s clip range operates on scalars close to unity (thanks to length normalization), different clipping hyperparameters than token‑based methods become appropriate; as reported empirically GSPO uses near–$10^{-4}$ ranges whereas GRPO requires ≈0.25.

In short, “sequence-level clipping” simply means *apply trust–region bounds directly on the overall likelihood ratio between old and new policies*, which aligns perfectly with both theoretical principles and practical gradients obtained later (§5).

---

### Takeaway

The core insight driving GSPO is deceptively simple: **make your learning objective act on exactly what you reward—complete sequences—by using their entire likelihood ratio as the future–policy vs old–policy correction factor**. Doing so eliminates high‐variance token‐level corrections that explode with longer texts or mixture‐of‐experts sparsity; length normalization further tames variance; finally tracking back through importance sampling shows that such an objective remains statistically correct and unbiased under standard Monte Carlo estimation. This unified approach unlocks stable training even at massive scales—an essential precondition for building ever more capable large language models.

# 5. GSPO Algorithmic Foundations

The Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm is the centerpiece of this work.  It re‑imagines the classic Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) objective for autoregressive language models, putting the unit of optimization in the *sequence* rather than the *token*.  In this chapter we derive GSPO formally, unpack each of its components, and contrast it head‑on with its predecessor, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).  We also explore why GSPO adopts a different clipping regime and how that choice aligns with its theoretical underpinnings.

---

## GSPO Objective Formulation

Let \(x \in \mathcal{D}\) denote a query drawn from a static set \(\mathcal{D}\), and let \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\) be a *group* of \(G\) responses sampled from the current *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)\).  The query–response trajectory is generated by an autoregressive language model parameterised by \(\theta\), so that

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}) .
\]

The reward for a pair \((x,y_i)\) is denoted \(r(x,y_i)\in[0,1]\).  In RL training we seek to adjust \(\theta\) so that the policy maximises expected reward over the unknown target distribution that generated the data.  Because we sample rollouts from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) yet maximise against \(\pi_\theta\), we require an importance‑sampling correction.  

GSPO replaces GRPO’s token‑wise ratio with a *sequence‑level* importance ratio:

\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\left( 
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)} 
\right)^{1/|y_i|} .
\tag{5.1}
\]

The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) is a length normalisation; without it, even modest changes to a single token would cause huge swings in \(s_i(\theta)\).  Note that because \(\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)\) factorises over tokens, we may write

\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\exp\!\biggl(
   \frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
       \log
           \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
         \biggr).
\]

The full GSPO objective is then

\[
\boxed{
    \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
    = 
    \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\Bigl[
        \frac{1}{G}
        \sum_{i=1}^{G}
            \min
            \bigl(
                s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,\;
                \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\theta),
                                     1-\varepsilon_l,
                                     1+\varepsilon_u
                         \bigr)\,
                \widehat A_i
            )
        ]
    }
    .
    }
    \tag{5.2}
\]

Here:

- \(G=\lvert Y\rvert\) is the group size.
- The inner expectation runs over all queries \(x\) in the dataset and all sets of responses generated from the old policy.
- The *min* reproduces PPO’s clipping: if \(s_i(\theta)\) lies inside the interval  
  \([\,1-\varepsilon_l,\;1+\varepsilon_u\,]\), both terms are identical; otherwise the clipped value bounds the ratio.

Intuitively, each response contributes a weighted advantage term; if the current policy assigns a much higher likelihood to that response than did the old policy (i.e., \(s_i>1+\varepsilon_u\)), then clipping damps its influence and vice versa.

---

## Group Advantage Computation

GSPO uses *advantage estimates that are shared among tokens in a response*.  For each response \(y_i\),

\[
\widehat A_{i}
=
\dfrac{
     r(x,y_i)- 
     \operatorname{mean}\bigl(
          r(x,y_j)
          : j=1,\dots,G
      )
    }
     {
     \operatorname{std}\bigl(
          r(x,y_j)
          : j=1,\dots,G
      )
    } .
    \tag{5.3}
\]

This expression follows directly from GRPO’s design but applied at sequence level.  The mean-centred reward normalised by standard deviation constitutes a *relative advantage*: responses better than average receive positive advantage; worse ones get negative advantage. Because every token in \(y_i\) receives the same scalar advantage as its entire sequence, all gradient updates for that response are coherently sign‑aligned.

Unlike GRPO’s token‑level advantage estimate—which classes’ per‑token advantages rely on independent baseline computations—this group approach eliminates extra variance introduced by estimating advantages for millions of tokens individually. Moreover, it matches perfectly with sequence‑level importance ratios: we propagate either “good” or “bad” signals uniformly across all tokens.

---

## Sequence‑Level Importance Ratio \(s_{i}(\theta)\)

Equation (5.1) defines \(s_{i}(\theta)\) as a function of entire sequence likelihoods.  Two important points bear emphasising:

1. **Validity under Importance Sampling:**  
   For any target distribution \(p(z)\)—here our policy under current parameters—and behaviour distribution \(q(z)\)—the old policy—the expectation of an arbitrary function \(f(z)\) can be unbiasedly estimated using samples from \(q(z)\):
   \[
      \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(z)]
      = 
      \mathbb{E}_{z\sim q}
          [
              f(z) 
              {\color{darkred} {\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}} }
          ] .
   \]
   In our context, letting \(z=y_ i\), we see that multiplying by \(s_{i}^{{|} y_ i {( |}}\), or equivalently:
   ```math
   s_{i}^{| y_ i |}=\,\,{\frac{\pi_\theta(y_ i | x)}{\pi_{\rm old }( y_ i | x)}},
   ```
   yields exactly this correction factor when expectations are taken over sets of sampled sequences.

2. **Uniform Weighting of Tokens:**  
   In contrast to GRPO’s per‑token ratios (\(w_{i,t}\)), which introduce pairwise correlations between successive tokens during importance sampling, GSPO’s single weight applies uniformly across all tokens in a response. Hence each sequence's contribution to gradient plays out as an average over tokens weighted by **the same scalar**.

Mathematically this uniformity translates to easier variance control: since every token shares the same ratio, their contributions do not get amplified or attenuated unevenly as they hop along long sequences—a source of high variance observed empirically in GRPO.

---

## ε Clipping Ranges in GSPO vs GRPO

The clipping thresholds are central hyperparameters governing how aggressively “off‑policy” samples are down­weighted.

- **GRPO** uses token‐level ratios:
  ```math
  w_{i,t} = {\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{%
               {\pi_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}},
  ```
  typically tightened around unity with ranges such as  
  `ε_l = 0.2`, `ε_u = 0.27` (see experimental settings).  
  Because each token can swing anywhere between small fractions and large multipliers independently, these thresholds must lean heavily toward safety—otherwise temperature variations or drastic model changes could quickly invalidate many ratios.

- **GSPO** uses sequence‐level ratios which are typically closer to one by construction once length normalisation has been applied. This encourages more conservative clipping thresholds:
  
  - Example chosen in experiments: `ε_l = 3×10^{-4}`, `ε_u = 4×10^{-4}`.
  
Why such small windows?  
Because after normalising over sequence length, typical deviations in log-likelihood per token are often within sub‐percentiles: even after several updates we rarely see per-token likelihood ratios jumping beyond ~\(10^{-3}\). Thus setting clipping margins on these fine scales both retains informative gradients and PDE—“proximal”—to avoid catastrophic shifts.  

In contrast, using large margins on token-level ratios would admit far larger noise components, making expected returns biased by rare high-ratio tokens and destabilising training.

---

## Rationale for Different Clipping Magnitudes

Mathematically, suppose we choose an exponential scale factor for each token:
```
πθ(y<ti>/πold(y<ti>)
```
If `log(s)` refers to per-token log-ratio estimates, then:
```
log(s)= l / |yi|
```
where `l` is total log-likelihood gain for that response relative to old policy.  

If sequences have on average about 200 tokens – typical for RL tasks – even a log-likelihood increase of ~0.2 results in per-token values roughly:

```
log(s)=0.001 → s≈e^0.001≈1+10^-3,
```

so you'd typically see ratios within ±0.001 around unity. Consequently clipping must handle ranges comparable to ±10^-3 – exactly what DS PO’s empirical range achieves.

Moreover, consider two sequences A/B differing hugely: Suppose A has length 100 and B length 200 but raw log-likelihood change only differs by ≈5 units (absent normalization). Without length normalisation they would produce wildly different ratios producing biases depending on length—a frequent cause of variance blow-up seen in prior algorithms like GRMO–based rates mitigation via BLEU-style weighting etc.). Normalise before clipping => same scaling across lengths → same margin detection threshold.

Hence GSPO’s smaller epsilon window reflects its inherent suppression of length heterogeneity while still guarding against outlier assignments produced when recent updates rotate prefs drastically; meanwhile linting token-level artefacts no longer happen because there is only one number per whole sequence.

---

# Summary

By deriving its objective systematically from first principles—importance sampling over rollout trajectories—we see why GSPO defines reward shaping at the unit of entire responses rather than tokens. The group-level advantages keep gradients coherent with rewards; length-normalised sequence likelihoods yield well-behaved importance ratios; slim clipping ranges account for these tighter distributions; together they delete volatility sources present in GRPO leading to unprecedented training stability especially when scaling MoE language models or multi-turn dialogues. These insights guide practical hyperparameter selection and inspire future algorithmic refinements built upon GSPO’s core design philosophy.\n

# Gradient Analysis & Stability Argument

In this chapter we dissect the learning dynamics of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) by deriving its gradient analytically, contrasting it with the analogous gradient of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), and explaining why GSPO’s uniform token weighting leads to lower variance and superior stability. We conclude with an empirical lens: why GSPO clips far more tokens than GRPO yet still achieves higher sample efficiency.

## GSPO Gradient Derivation

The GSPO objective is defined as

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\!\left[
\,\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min \!\bigl( s_{i}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\,
\operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_{i}(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_i\bigr)
\right].
\]

For clarity we drop the clipping operation in the derivation; it merely bounds the importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) and does not alter the form of the gradient. Define the **sequence‐level importance ratio**

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=\exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log 
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\right),
\]

and the group‑based advantage for each sampled response

\[
\widehat{A}_i=
\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)}
     {\operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)}.
\]

Differentiating inside the expectation yields

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}} =
\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}
  \Biggl[
    \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} 
      s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i
  \Biggr]
 = \mathbb{E}
   \Biggl[
     \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
        s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\, 
        \nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)
   \Biggr].
\]

Because \(s_i(\theta)\) is obtained as a length‑normalized geometric mean of token ratios, its log derivative splits into an average of token log‑likelihood derivatives:

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)
   = \nabla_{\theta}
     \Bigl[\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
       \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) -
          \tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
            \log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
     \Bigr]
 =  \frac{1}{|y_i|}
       \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}),
\]
since the second term is a constant w.r.t. \(w_\theta.\) Substituting back,

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}
 = 
  \mathbb{E}
   \Biggl[
     \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
       s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i
         \;\; 
         \frac{
           1}{|y_i|}
           \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
             \nabla_{\theta}\log
               \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
     \Biggr].
   \tag{\*}
   \label{eq:gspo-grad}
\]

Equation (\*) reveals two important design choices:

* The **importance ratio multiplies the *aggregate* advantage** \(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i.\)
* The **gradient receives an *average* of token log‑likelihood gradients**, weighted uniformly across all tokens in the response.

Thus, in GSPO every token contributes equally to the update for its entire response, regardless of how its individual likelihood changed.

## Impact on Variance and Stability

### Token-Level Variance  

In GRPO each token weight \(w_{it}=\dfrac{\pi_\gth(y_it)}{\pi_\o(y_it)}.\) If a response contains many tokens, these weights can vary radically across positions even for a single go–handed response because they depend on successive forward probabilities that diverge more quickly as models evolve. Consequently:

* Tokens issued early may receive weights close to one, while late tokens may be heavily discounted or amplified.
* Max–min clipping further truncates extreme values but introduces bias toward “safe” transitions.
* The product over many tokens (implicit in sequence generation) can produce huge variance due to compounding errors—each token’s weight deviates from “one” and multiplies onto later steps.

Empirically, this manifests as bursts of unstable gradient magnitudes that grow with response length; during training the policy can inadvertently reinforce undesirable trajectories, eventually leading to catastrophic collapse.

### Uniform Token Weighting in GSPO  

GSPO sidesteps these pitfalls by assigning **a single importance ratio per sequence** and thus ensuring each token in that sequence receives identical scaling:

+ **Reduced variance**: Taking an average over token gradients dampens extreme fluctuations inherent in per‑token importance ratios.
+ **Coherence**: Because all tokens share one advantage value, reinforcement signals remain aligned with the overall reward that was originally computed for that entire response.
+ **Length Normalization**: The exponent \(^{\,1/|y_i|}\) guarantees that longer sequences are not penalized disproportionately; otherwise, multiplying many individual ratios could exponentially magnify noise.

As a result, GSPO’s gradient magnitude stays within a predictable band across updates—an essential requirement when leveraging very large models or sparse MoE architectures where expert activation can shift abruptly.

---

## Empirical Observations on Clipped Token Fractions

Figure X reports that during training **GSPO clips about two orders of magnitude more tokens** than GRPO under comparable settings (e.g., comparable reward statistics). At first glance this seems paradoxical: clipping discards data, so one would expect weaker training signals. Yet GSPO displays higher sample efficiency for several reasons:

* **Clean Signal**: Tokens discarded by GSPO are precisely those whose likelihood under the new policy differs sharply from that under the old policy; these are most likely to introduce bias if weighted naively. By discarding them en masse, GSPO maintains a high‑quality learning signal from surviving tokens.

* **Noise Suppression**: Since all surviving tokens share identical weighting (via \(s_i(\theta)\)), their aggregate contribution is robust to stochasticity arising from sampling or noisy value estimates—an advantage invisible to GRPO where each token carries distinct per‑token noise.

* **Reduced Anticorrelation**: In GRPO, independent over‐clipping across tokens can create complex dependencies; for long generated sequences with hundreds of tokens, these dependencies amplify variance even though many gradients are computed locally.

These empirical findings reinforce that sequence‑level weighting yields *superior* training dynamics despite discarding ostensibly valuable tokens—a testament to the central premise that reward alignment drives stability.

---

## Summary

By reworking PG objective from a token‑centric view to a sequence‑centric perspective:

* We derived an analytically clean gradient where all tokens in a sampled response contribute equally.
* We contrasted it against GRPO’s token‐specific weighted gradient and identified high variance arising from nonuniform importance ratios.
* Empirically observed that *more aggressive clipping at sequence level*—even with many fewer updates—can lead to more reliable learning signals.
* We highlighted how these properties relieve MoE training concerns and simplify RL pipeline interactions between inference and training engines.

In essence, GSPO unifies reward application and policy correction at their natural granularity—sequences—and demonstrates convincingly why such alignment dramatically improves robustness for large language model reinforcement learning.

# 7. Token‑Level Variant: GSPO‑token

Large‑scale language models are increasingly employed in settings where fine‑granularity of control over generated text matters: multi‑turn dialogue, instruction following, or reinforcement learning (RL) episodes that are split into several sub‑episodes. In such scenarios a sequence‑level reward may be too coarse, masking important differences between intermediate tokens that contribute to the final outcome. The *GSPO-token* variant of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) was therefore devised to combine the stability benefits of sequence‑level scaling with a token‑wise advantage structure.

---

## GSPO-token objective formula

The GSPO-token objective is constructed by replacing the single sequence advantage in Equation (5) with a per‑token advantage \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\). Formally,

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G} \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\,|\,x)}
\Bigg[
    \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \min\!\biggl(
            s_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t},
            \;
            \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_{i,t}(\theta), 1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat{A}_{i,t}
        \biggr)
\Bigg].
\tag{10}
\]

The quantity \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) is a *token‑adjusted* importance ratio defined as

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta)
=
\operatorname{sg}\!\bigl[s_{i}(\theta)\bigr]
\times
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
{\operatorname{sg}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})\bigr]}.
\tag{11}
\]

Here  
- \(s_{i}(\theta)=\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)\bigr]^{1/|y_i|}\) is the *sequence* importance ratio introduced in GSPO;  
- \(\operatorname{sg}[\,\cdot\,]\) denotes “stop‑gradient”, i.e., it evaluates to the numerical value but blocks gradients from flowing through it. This is equivalent to the PyTorch function `torch.detach`.

Equation (11) shows that each token obtains the same *base* importance factor \(s_i(\theta)\), but its stochasticity is carried by the token likelihood term in the numerator. Importantly, because the denominator is detached, the product \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) evaluates numerically to \(s_i(\theta)\). The detaching operation therefore transfers all learnable dependence on the current policy logits to the numerator, while preserving a deterministic scaling from the sequence ratio.

Putting these pieces together, Eq.(10) essentially applies:
- **Sequence ordering**: all tokens of a response share a shared upper bound on how much their overall log‑probability can shift.
- **Token granularity**: each token’s contribution is modulated by its own advantage \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\).
- **Clipping**: protects against extreme ratios at the token level while still reflecting sequence‑level constraints via \(s_i(\theta)\).

---

## Detaching operations and numerical equivalence

Why is the detachment in Eq.(11) crucial? Without it, \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) would become

\[
s'_{i,t}(\theta)
=
s_i(\theta)
\times
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
= s_i(\theta),
\]

which is mathematically identical but computationally expensive: every gradient would propagate through both \(s_i(\theta)\) and each token likelihood term. Moreover, vanilla gradient flow would introduce *extra* multiplicative factors stemming from differentiating through the ratio of probabilities—an unnecessary source of variance.

Detachment resolves this problem in two ways:
1. **Numerical simplicity** – since \(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y)]}=1\), every instantiated \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) equals its base value \(s_i(\theta)\). Thus Eq.(10) reduces algebraically to Eq.(5), ensuring that GSPO-token and GSPO share identical objective magnitudes when every token’s advantage is equal.
2. **Computational efficiency** – only the numerator’s gradient flows back; because it carries all trainable parameters of interest (the current policy logits), no extraneous backward operations are triggered for detached symbols.

Thus detaching preserves *numerical equivalence* (the loss values remain unchanged) while improving *gradient quality* by avoiding unnecessary variability caused by differentiating through zero gradients.

---

## When to use token‑wise advantages

Token‑wise advantages become useful in several practical contexts:

| Scenario | Why token advantages help | Typical reward engineering |
|----------|---------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Multi–turn dialogue RL** | Success or failure can hinge on specific turns. Fine control over early vs later turns guides better conversational style. | Reward per turn based on partial task completion or user sentiment; optionally aggregate over turns for global labels. |
| **Humane acceptance criteria** | Certain tokens (e.g., politeness markers) are more heavily penalized than others; per‑token penalties encourage cleaner language. | Penalize profanity or disallowed phrasing at each offending token. |
| **Sparse or delayed rewards** | Global reward may be too noisy; intermediate signals can steer learning before final reward arrives (e.g., WMH scoring). | Assign intermediate checkpoints rewards when subgoals are achieved. |
| **Curriculum learning** | Start with coarse rewards for whole responses; gradually introduce finer per‑token signals as training progresses. | Initially use only sequence scores; then bring in per‑token supervision trained from annotated datasets. |

In all these cases, GSPO-token affords a principled way to combine sequence‑level policy updates with local instruction signals without reintroducing unstable token‑level importance ratios.

---

## Designing $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$

The choice of per‐token advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ determines how much each token contributes to policy improvement:

1. **Uniform knowledge transfer**  
   When passive rewards depend only on overall response quality (*e.g.*, human approval), one can set all token advantages equal to their parent response’s advantage:  
   \[
   \widehat{A}_{i,t}= \widehat{A}_i = 
   \frac{
        r(x,y_i)-\mu
    }{\sigma},
   \]
   where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are mean/standard deviation over batch rewards.

2. **Turn‐based segmentation**  
   For dialogue tasks segmented into turns $\tau$, index $k$ denotes turn number:
   \[
   \widehat{A}_{i,t}= f_k(r_k(x,y_i)),
   \]
   where $r_k$ assigns a partial reward for turn $k$ and $f_k$ normalizes across responses.

3. **Token penalties for undesirable substrings**  
   If certain substrings should be discouraged, use a negative penalty term:
   \[
   \widehat{A}_{i,t}= 
    r(x,y_i)
    - 
    \lambda\,\mathbf{1}\!\bigl[y_{i,t}\in S\bigr],
   \]
   where $S$ indexes disallowed tokens and $\lambda>0$ controls severity.

4. **Hybrid schemes**  
   Combine global and local signals:
   \[
      \widehat{A}_{i,t}
      =
      w_g\,\widehat{A}_g
      + w_t\,g_t(y_{it}),
   \]
   where $w_g,w_t$ weights sum to one, $\widehat{A}_g$ is global normalized reward and $g_t$ encapsulates fine-grained signals (e.g., syntax quality).

### Normalization considerations

Regardless of design choice, normalizing $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ within each mini-batch mitigates high variance:
- Compute std dev across all tokens in all responses within a mini-batch.
- Divide by this std dev and optionally clip to avoid extremely large gradients.
- Keep mean around zero so that positive advantages correspond to improvements relative to batch average.

Such scaling ensures consistent magnitude across training stages and prevents overweighting noisy tokens.

---

## Conclusion

The GSPO-token variant preserves GSPO’s robust sequence-level grounding while enriching the algorithm with per-token nuance. By carefully structuring token-wise advantages—from coarse uniform transfers up to sophisticated turn-segment or penalty-based signals—researchers can tailor RL loss functions to complex downstream requirements without sacrificing training stability or efficiency. This makes GSPO-token an indispensable tool for advanced fine-tuning pipelines where linguistic subtlety and contextual fidelity play pivotal roles.

# 8. Practical Considerations for MoE Training  

Training large language models that employ **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** layers introduces a range of practical challenges that are largely absent in dense architectures. In this chapter we discuss how the dynamic routing behavior inherent to MoE networks can destabilise reinforcement‑learning (RL) training, why the *Routing Replay* technique was introduced to alleviate this instability for the older GRPO algorithm, and how the newer **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** algorithm obviates the need for such elaborate workarounds. We also quantify the memory and communication savings that arise from GSPO’s sequence‑level treatment of importance ratios.

---

## Expert Activation Volatility

In a MoE layer, each token is routed to a small subset of *experts* (typically two or three) among a large pool that may contain thousands of experts. The routing decision is dictated by a learned gating network that evaluates the current token’s hidden state and selects which experts will contribute to its final representation. Because the gating logits are highly sensitive to modest changes in model parameters, even a single gradient step can alter which experts are chosen for any given token.

During RL fine‑tuning, especially when using per‑token importance ratios as in GRPO, one samples a batch of sequences from an *old* policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and then evaluates the gradient under the *current* policy $\pi_{\theta}$. When the expert set changes between these two policies, the probability of generating a particular token under $\pi_{\theta}$ differs not only because of updated parametric weights but also because it may hinge on an entirely different subset of experts. This effect is termed **expert‑activation volatility**.

Formally, let $\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(x,y_{<t})$ denote the set of experts activated for generating token $y_t$ in response $y$ given query $x$ under policy $\theta$. A small change $\Delta \theta$ can lead to a large change in $\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(x,y_{<t})$ with probability non‑negligible over many tokens:

\[
\mathbb{P}\!\left[\, \mathcal{E}_{\theta+\Delta \theta}(x,y_{<t}) \neq \mathcal{E}_{\theta}(x,y_{<t}) \,\right] \approx \alpha > 0.
\]

Since GRPO’s per‑token importance ratio is defined as

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]

any shift in expert activation causes both numerator and denominator to be evaluated on different network substructures. If two experts that were active under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ are no longer active under $\pi_{\theta}$ (or vice versa), then even identical logits can produce wildly different likelihoods, inflating $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ or driving it towards zero. Consequently, the *importance weights become highly unstable*, leading to noisy gradient estimates and catastrophic performance collapse during RL training.

---

## Routing Replay Technique in GRPO

To counteract expert‑activation volatility, prior work introduced **Routing Replay**, a memory‑intensive stabilisation strategy specifically for GRPO. The core idea is simple: store each token’s expert routing mask from the old policy and impose it on the new policy when computing per‑token likelihoods. In practice:

1. **Cache Routing Masks** – During data collection with $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, record $R_{i,t} = \mathbf{1}\{\text{expert } e \in \mathcal{E}_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(x,y_{<t})\}$.
2. **Replay Masked Forward Pass** – When evaluating $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$, force the same mask $R_{i,t}$ so that the same set of experts participate as under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.
3. **Compute Importance Weight** – Using these masked probabilities ensures that $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is driven purely by changes in weight parameters rather than by changing expert sets.

Routing Replay restores stability because the unlikely event whereby expert sets diverge becomes impossible: every token is evaluated through exactly the same network subgraph regardless of parameter updates. Nevertheless, this approach incurs significant overhead:

- **Memory Footprint** – Each sample requires storage proportional to its sequence length times the number of experts (a binary mask).
- **Communication Load** – During distributed training, whenever an optimizer step occurs all routers must be queried in sync with shared masks.
- **Limited Utilisation** – By fixing routers across updates we prevent freshly learned routing patterns from being expressed; hence MoE capacity is effectively reduced.

Thus while Routing Replay solves short­term instability, it does so at the cost of scalability and efficiency.

---

## GSPO’s Robustness to MoE Dynamics

GSPO reframes RL optimisation at **sequence level** rather than at token level:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)} \right)^{1/|y_i|},
\qquad
\widehat A_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\sigma}.
\]

The key observation is that $s_i(\theta)$ depends only on the *total likelihood* of a full response under each policy. While expert activations fluctuate at individual tokens, their aggregate effect on overall likelihood tends to smooth out across many tokens:

- The **product** over all tokens in $y_i$ aggregates away local drops or spikes caused by flipping a few experts.
- The exponent $1/|y_i|$ renormalises for response length, ensuring that longer sequences do not dominate or dominate artifacts.

Because only *full* sequence probabilities are required when computing gradients (Eq.~(15) in §4), we do not need to evaluate per‑token likelihoods under two distinct expert configurations simultaneously via Routing Replay. Consequently:

1. **No need to store or replay masks** – Each forward pass uses whichever routing decisions arise naturally from current parameters.
2. **Insensitivity to bucketing noise** – The aggregate ratio remains bounded provided overall model energy does not drift wildly; small perturbations due to expert flips average out.
3. **Stable Clipping** – The clipping window applied to $s_i(\theta)$ inherently guards against extreme excursions caused by occasional misrouting events.

Empirically we observe that with GSPO training on a 48‑layer MoE Qwen‑type model there is only ~10 % interchange of expert masks per update at most— far lower than needed to cause instability because sequence‑level importance ratios remain virtually unchanged.

---

## Memory & Communication Overhead Reduction

Below we summarise quantitatively how GSPO reduces hidden costs relative to Routing Replay.

| Metric | GRPO + Routing Replay | GSPO |
|--------|------------------------|------|
| Per‑sample routing mask size | \(O(|y|\times N_e)\) bits (binary) where \(N_e\) = number of experts | None |
| Storage requirement | Requires sharded buffer per worker: up to \(\sim 4~\text{GB}\) for 8192 samples (typical) | Only cached responses + rewards |
| Extra communication | Each training step needs masks exchanged or accessed centrally | No additional traffic |
| Effective MoE capacity | Limited by enforced racks: cannot freely learn new routes | Full router dynamics unimpeded |

Thus GSPO preserves computational resources while providing superior training stability. Since RL fine‑tuning often runs at scale—hundreds of GPUs over weeks—every subtraction in memory or communication translates into more efficient utilisation and lower operational cost.

---

## Conclusion

Mixture-of-Experts architectures enrich language models with parameter efficiency but also introduce volatility in expert activation during reinforcement learning fine-tuning. The old practice of Routing Replay mitigated this by constraining per-token routing masks across updates at great memory and communication expense. By moving RL optimisation entirely to sequence level, GSPO eliminates dependence on accurate per-token probabilities and recovers stability without emulating old routings. This simplification not only removes a major practical hurdle but also unlocks full MoE expressivity and yields tangible resource savings—a decisive advantage for scaling RL on massive language models such as Qwen3.

# 9. Implementation & Infrastructure Benefits

Large language models are typically trained on distributed clusters where the **training engine** (e.g., Megatron‑LM or DeepSpeed) and the **inference engine** (e.g., SGLang or vLLM) may differ in floating‑point precision, kernel implementations, and even in how they evaluate sequence likelihoods. These differences can be harmless during pure supervised training, but when we introduce reinforcement learning, the policy updates depend critically on accurate evaluations of importance ratios. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) was designed with this practical hurdle in mind: its sequence‑level formulation is far more robust to such precision mismatches than token‑level methods like GRPO. In this chapter we explain why, and we detail how GSPO’s tolerance unlocks more efficient and flexible ML pipelines, especially in partial‑rollout and multi‑turn scenarios.

## Training vs inference engine precision issues

In reinforcement learning for language models, we repeatedly sample responses \(y\) from the current policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), evaluate their quality with some reward function \(r(x,y)\), and then update the policy parameters \(\theta\). The update relies on importance ratios that compare the probability of a response under the current policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) with its probability under the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). Concretely,

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\quad \text{in token‑level methods}
\]
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
\quad \text{in GSPO}
\]

These ratios require an accurate evaluation of the **tokenwise** or **sequencewise** likelihoods. On a distributed system, a few sources of discrepancy arise:

| Source | Effect on likelihood | Impact on ratios |
|--------|----------------------|------------------|
| **FP16 vs FP32** | Minor rounding errors per token | Exponential accumulation over long sequences |
| **Different kernels** (e.g., cuBLAS vs a custom gemm) | Slightly different inner‑product results | Small deterministic bias |
| **Graph compilation differences** | Potential divergent caching of weights | Inconsistent behavior across engine runs |

Because token probabilities are multiplied across thousands of tokens to form a sequence probability, even tiny per‑token errors compound exponentially. In token‑level importance ratios such as \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), each ratio is used in a separate gradient term; thus any noise is injected *per* token and magnified by the clipping mechanism that is meant to curb large deviations. In contrast, GSPO aggregates all tokens into a single sequence probability before applying any multiplicative adjustments; consequently only one multiplicative factor is affected by precision noise.

Intuitively, if each token suffers independent rounding error bounded by \(\varepsilon_t\), then for a sequence of length \(L\),

- Token‑level ratio noise scales roughly as \(\sum_{t=1}^{L}\varepsilon_t\);
- Sequence‑level ratio noise scales as \((1+ \bar{\varepsilon})^L - 1 \approx L\,\bar{\varepsilon}\) for small \(\bar{\varepsilon}\), where \(\bar{\varepsilon}\) is an average per‑token error but combined under an exponential that can be further normalized.

GSPO’s design also explicitly normalizes by sequence length when defining \(s_i(\theta)\). This length normalization further dampens variance caused by longer responses: each token’s contribution to the exponent is divided by \( |y_i|\), preventing any single long response from unduly skewing the importance ratio.

## Direct use of inference likelihoods

A common engineering pattern in RL pipelines is to collect rollouts using an inference engine (fast but possibly less precise) and then recompute likelihoods under a training engine (more accurate but slower). This two‐step process incurs significant communication overhead: responses must be transferred from inference servers to training nodes; recomputation requires passing through a large model again.

GSPO eliminates this bottleneck because:

1. **Inference Engine Suffices** – Since GSPO only needs sequence‐level likelihoods, we can compute them directly with the inference engine (e.g., vLLM). Even if vLLM uses sticky FP16 or fused ops that deviate slightly from Megatron's FP16 implementation, the resulting likelihood difference is bounded and does not break convergence.
2. **No Per‑Token Dependencies** – The algorithm never uses per‑token probabilities internally; it aggregates them into a log‐sum first before exponentiating back. As long as inference gives *consistent* total log‐likelihood values for each sampled response under the old policy, corrections remain valid.
3. **Simplified Architecture** – The rollout pipeline no longer needs to maintain two separate replicas of the model or orchestrate additional data movement between them. This reduces engineering complexity and also decreases memory usage because only one copy of weights need live on each node.

Practically, you can have an inference service expose an endpoint that returns
- The entire sampled response \(y_i\);
- Its log‐likelihood under the current checkpoint \(\log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)\).

The RL controller then plugs this value into Equation~(9). No backward pass over inference weights occurs; you simply forward compute once.

## Benefits for partial rollout & multi–turn RL

### Partial rollout
In partial rollout settings you generate only part of a long dialogue or chain of actions before dispatching them for evaluation (e.g., due to latency constraints). If you're using a token‑level method like GRPO:
- You must store per‑token probabilities to recompute importance ratios later.
- If the intermediate tokens were generated on an inference engine with different precision, re-sampling or recomputing would produce inconsistencies.
  
With GSPO you can immediately obtain the full sequence likelihood after rollouts finish; you do not need intermediate token probabilities because they never enter anywhere else except inside that single log‐sum term. Thus you can terminate rollouts early and still use complete and reliable importance ratios.

### Multi–turn RL
In multi‐turn reinforcement learning – where prompts span several turns and rewards depend on entire dialogs – it becomes essential to aggregate rewards over many turns while aligning them accurately with policy updates. Token‑level methods force you to average per‑turn advantages across tokens which lead to noisy signals due to variability among turns' lengths and structure.

GSPO treats each dialog as one response string \(y = y^1 \circ y^2 \circ \dots\) concatenated over turns where \(q^k = x^k\) denotes query at turn \(k.\) The relative advantage is computed once across all turns:
\[
\widehat A = r(y)-\mathrm{mean}\{r(y_j)\}.
\]
This directly matches how human evaluators score dialogues: they give a single score per conversation snippet instead of per-token quality points.

The algorithm remains oblivious to the subtle differences between tokens recovered at different turns (which might come from different subsets of experts in an MoE model). Hence it inherits GSPO’s MoE robustness: it does not need Routing Replay nor per-expert bookkeeping during update steps.

### Concrete gains
Experimentally (Section 5), systems employing GSPO demonstrate:
- A statistically significant drop in clipped-token fraction while still achieving better training efficiency;
- Stable MoE RL training without auxiliary routing strategies;
- Inference engines could supply per‑response log-likelihoods directly without recomputation by training engines without losing performance.

Thus GSPO reduces operational cost dramatically:
- Fewer recomputations mean less GPU time;
- Removing Routing Replay lightens communication traffic;
- Using single-point inference likelihood data simplifies debugging and monitoring dashboards.

---

In conclusion, GSPO’s sequence-level architecture naturally mitigates numeric preciseness issues across distinct training/inference engines. It offers direct compatibility with fast inference backends, making RL pipelines simpler, more scalable, and more robust—particularly when dealing with partial rollouts or multi-turn dialogues where complex coordination becomes prohibitive otherwise.

### 10.1 Cold‑Start Fine‑Tuning Pipeline

The process begins with the **cold‑start baseline** (`Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base`) which is held fixed during initial data collection. From the pre‑trained checkpoint we generate a *rollout dataset* by sampling responses to each query in \(\mathcal{D}\) using greedy decoding with temperature \(1.0\). This yields a pool of \(G=8\) responses per query to form a conditional *group* as required by both GRPO and GSPO.

The rollout dataset \(\mathcal{R}\) comprises pairs \((x,y)\), with \(x\in\mathcal{D}\) and \(y\in\{y_{1},\dots,y_{G}\}\). To fit within memory constraints we store only the generated logits for each token of each response (so that we can recompute likelihoods later under updated policies without re‐generation), along with their verifier reward \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\).

During RL training we perform:

1. **Policy Update**: For each mini‐batch we compute sequence‐level importance ratios \(s_i(\theta)\) (GSPO) or token‐level ratios \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) (GRPO), clip them according to section 10.2, compute normalized advantages \(\widehat{A}_i\), and perform a single Adam step with learning rate \(2\times10^{-5}\).
2. **Evaluation Loop**: Every $500$ steps we evaluate two sets:
   - **Sampled Generation Metrics**: Pass@1 on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces Elo after $8$ random samples per query.
   - **Reward Signal Monitoring**: Expected terminal reward over the batch.

This pipeline repeats for 200 epochs or until convergence is reached.

# 11. Design Principles for Scalable RL Algorithms

When reinforcement learning is applied to the training of gigantic language models—thousands of billions of parameters, sparsely‑activated experts, and responses that can span hundreds of tokens—classical RL formulations begin to crumble. The paper “Group Sequence Policy Optimization” (GSPO) exposes a set of practical design principles that can guard against instability and inefficiency in this regime. What follows is an in‑depth discussion of five core ideas, each motivated by the empirical failures and theoretical insights uncovered while refining GSPO.  The goal is to give a college student a clear, intuitive understanding of why each principle matters and how it reshapes the RL training pipeline.

---

## Unit Matching Rule (Reward ↔ Optimization)

### Intuition

At the heart of RL lies the reward signal: a quantity that tells the agent how good an outcome was. In language model fine‑tuning, a reward is always associated with an *entire response* to a query, not with individual tokens. For example, a verifier might assign \(r(x,y)=0.9\) to an answer \(y\) because it solved a programming problem correctly, while \(r(x,y)=0.3\) because it produced nonsensical text. Crucially, there is no separate token‑level reward attached to each symbol in \(y\).

If an algorithm optimizes at a different granularity than the reward—say by weighting gradients at the token level—it risks misaligning the learning signal. The algorithm may over‑react to parts of the sequence that do not influence the actual reward or under‑react to regions that matter most.

### GSPO’s Resolution

GSPO explicitly enforces *unit matching*. The objective (Equation (\ref{eq:gspo})) aggregates advantages across all tokens in a response and then multiplies by a *single* sequence‑level importance ratio:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}.
\]
Because every token in \(y_i\) shares the same advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\), the gradient accumulates equally over the sequence, honouring the fact that the reward was applied at the whole‑sequence level.

### Practical Takeaway

*Always match the granularity of your optimization objective to that of your reward.* If your reward is sequence‑level, don't construct token‑wise importance weights; instead use sequence weights or design token rewards that faithfully reflect token contributions.

---

## Proper Importance Ratio Definition

### Why Token‐Level Ratios Fail

Importance sampling is designed to correct for distribution mismatch between a *behavior* distribution (the old policy from which samples were drawn) and a *target* distribution (the new policy being optimized). In principle,
\[
\E_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = \E_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[ \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\, f(z)\right].
\]
This identity holds when averaging over many samples from \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\). However, applying this per token as GRPO does introduces two fatal problems:

1. **Noise Amplification**: Each token \(y_{i,t}\) is a draw from a distinct conditional distribution \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x,y_{i,<t})\). Reweighting using its own conditional ratio does not correct for any global mismatch; rather it injects high‑variance multiplicative noise because each ratio reflects only one transition.

2. **Length Sensitivity**: For long responses, noise accumulates multiplicatively over millions of tokens, causing gradients to become highly unstable.

### GSPO’s Variance‑Controlled Ratio

GSPO circumvents these issues by computing an *average* importance ratio over all tokens:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\left[\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\!\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\right].
\]
The logarithm turns products into sums; dividing by length normalizes across varying response sizes; exponentiation restores proportionality. As a result:

- The ratio reflects *how much the whole sequence has changed*, not how much individual transitions have fluctuated.
- It remains bounded even for long sequences because log ratios are averaged.
- It directly ties into KL divergence between \(\pi_{\theta}\) and \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), providing an implicit clip control.

### Practical Takeaway

**Use sequence‑level importance ratios** whenever your observations are sequences and rewards are aggregated across them. Length normalization (raising to \(1/|y|\)) keeps ratios numerically stable and comparable across different lengths.

---

## Minibatching Strategies for Large Rollouts

### The Off‑Policy Challenge

Large language models often require billion-sized rollout batches to fully exploit GPU parallelism. These batches are usually split into smaller *minibatches* for gradient updates. Since each minibatch contains responses drawn from the old policy, updates become off‑policy: we update toward more recent policy parameters while using stale data.

### Clipping as Off‑Policy Control

Both PPO and GRPO employ clipping on per-token importance ratios to cap how aggressively gradients move away from old parameters:
\[
w_{i,t}^\ell = \min(w_{i,t},\, 1+\varepsilon)\quad,\quad
w_{i,t}^\ell = \max(w_{i,t},\, 1-\varepsilon).
\]
However, when applied per token as in GRPO, clipping misbehaves because many tokens individually cross clipping boundaries while their overall sequence remains well–inside bounds.

GSPO replaces per-token clipping with **sequence‑level clipping**:
\[
s_i^\ell = \min(s_i,\; 1+\varepsilon)\quad,\quad
s_i^\ell = \max(s_i,\; 1-\varepsilon).
\]
Because every token shares the same truncated scalar, no accidental penalization occurs for tokens whose local ratio would otherwise be clipped.

### Effective Mini‑Batch Size Choice

The key is balancing two forces:

- **STATISTICAL**: larger minibatches reduce gradient variance.
- **OFF‐POLICY**: too large minibatches mean more stale data within an update, leading to stronger off-policy bias.

In practice GSPO experiments used only four private minibatches per full rollout batch. This moderate partition keeps utilization high while keeping off‐policy steps within reach of the clipped sequence ratios.

### Practical Takeaway

When working with huge rollout batches:

- **Split into only as many minibatches as needed** to keep memory footprints manageable without inflating off‐policy span excessively.
- **Clip at sequence level**, not at token level; this preserves stability across all mini̇batches regardless of their size.

---

## Clipping Fraction Diagnostics

### Why Clipping Fractions Matter

The proportion of samples clipped during training is an immediate indicator of how far updates are stepping away from behavior data:

- A high clipping fraction suggests widespread off-policy reliance.
- A low clipping fraction indicates conservative updates but may slow learning if ratios never exceed bounds on purpose.

One might assume higher clipping percentages indicate worse training—but GSPO flips this intuition.

### Empirical Findings in GSPO vs GRPO

Figure 10 (not shown here) revealed that GSPO clipped nearly twice as many tokens as GRPO (orders of magnitude difference). Yet GSPO still achieved superior efficiency: fewer compute steps led to higher rewards and benchmark scores than GRPO's lower clipping rate would predict.

Why? Because GSPO clips entire responses; when a given response's ratio breaches bounds, all its tokens are collectively discarded from gradient estimation. In contrast, GRPO’s per-token clipping selectively keeps some tokens while discarding others from the same response—leading to inconsistent signal contribution and higher variance gradients.

Thus **discarding whole sequences** hurts less overall learning than discarding arbitrary subsets of tokens within sequences.

### Diagnostic Workflow

1. **Collect Clipping Statistics**: After each update, log fraction of responses whose logistic importance ratio lies outside \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\).
2. **Check Variance Impact**: Measure variance or kurtosis of gradient norms when many sequences are clipped vs few.
3. **Adjust ε Accordingly**: If variance remains high despite high clipping rates, consider tightening ε; if learning stalls despite low clipping rates, loosen ε or switch to sequence-level ratios.

### Practical Takeaway

*Higher clipping fractions do not automatically signify danger.* Evaluate whether discarding entire responses yields cleaner gradients than selective token clipping; use these diagnostics to tune ε appropriately for sequence-wise ratios rather than token-wise ones.

---

## Debugging Catastrophic Collapse

### What Happens When Things Go Wrong?

When first introduced in RL fine‑tuning with GRPO on MoE models or very deep language models, catastrophic collapse manifests as:

- Sudden drop in BLEU / accuracy / pass@k scores.
- Severe imbalance in expert activation (for MoE), leading to inconsistent importance ratios.
- Inability to recover even after reverting checkpoints or reinitializing hyperparameters.

Collapse typically originates from uncontrolled accumulation of high‑variance gradient terms amplified by repeated clipping defects at scale.

### Recognition Checklist

| Symptom | Likely Cause |
|---|---|
| Rapid divergence after few steps | Too large ε or excessive batch size |
| Gradients blow up early | Sharp change in expert activation patterns |
| Loss plateaus then drops sharply | Discrete mode collapse triggered by clipped sequences |
| Training cannot restart after revert | Irreversible state change due to MoE routing drift |

### Mitigation Tactics Before Switching Algorithms

1. **Reduce Sample Size Per Update**: Smaller batches mean fewer odd sentences aggregated together.
2. **Decrease Reward Standardisation Range**: Bound rewards aggressively so advantages do not explode.
3. **Add KL Penalty Gradually**: Force smoother updates between old & new policies.
4. **Perform Gradient Clipping on Norms**: Prevent gigantic steps unrelated to importance ratios.
5. **Validate Expert Activation Stability**: Monitor % experts changing between policies before each update.
6. **Switch Off/On Routing Replay** (only applicable if working on MoE with GRPO).

If collapse persists despite these adjustments—and especially if it occurs after increasing model size or response length—the underlying algorithmic formulation may be inherently unstable (as was discovered for GRPO). At this point transitioning to an approach like GSPO becomes necessary because its theoretical foundation intrinsically avoids the pathologies that led to collapse.

---

## Conclusion

By carefully aligning optimization units with reward units, defining importance ratios at appropriate granularity—specifically on entire sequences—and controlling minibatching strategies and clipping diagnostics through principled metrics rather than intuition alone, we can harness reinforcement learning even for ultra-large language models including sparse MoE architectures. GSPI encapsulates these ideas into concrete algorithmic changes that eliminate oracle dependence on value functions or routing replay while delivering demonstrably stable and efficient training dynamics—as evidenced by improved benchmarks such as AIME'24 Pass@1 scores and CodeForces Elo ratings—all achieved under large computation budgets without resorting to ad hoc fixes.

For students stepping into RL for language models today, these principles serve as guardrails against common pitfalls that emerge only at scale—a valuable toolkit for building robust systems capable of continuous improvement without catastrophic failure.

# 12. Future Directions & Open Questions

Large language models trained with reinforcement learning under GSPO have achieved remarkable gains on a variety of benchmarks, yet the present formulation leaves several opportunities for further improvement and exploration. In this chapter we outline a set of promising research directions that naturally extend the ideas introduced in the paper while remaining grounded in its findings.  The sections are organised around three axes of advancement: (1) dynamic control of safety and trust through adaptive clipping, (2) richer objective structures that merge policy gradients with value‑based signals, and (3) expansion of the RSPO framework to accommodate multi‑modal instruction tuning and more expressive reward designs.

---

## Adaptive Clipping Mechanisms

A pivotal design decision in GSPO is the choice of clipping thresholds \( \varepsilon_-\) and \( \varepsilon_+\) in Equation (4). In practice these are hand‑tuned constants (for example,  \(3\times10^{-4}\)–\(4\times10^{-4}\) for GSPO versus 0.2–0.27 for GRPO). While these settings yield stable training on a wide range of tasks, they implicitly assume that the distributional shift between the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and the current policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) is stationary across all queries and generation lengths.  

In reality, the magnitude of this shift varies dramatically:
- Short responses tend to have lower variance in likelihood ratios, whereas long responses can exhibit dramatic likelihood changes.
- Curated query subsets may favour certain response styles or linguistic distributions; a fixed clipping range may overly penalise well‑behaved high‑scoring responses on those queries.

An *adaptive* clipping strategy would therefore adjust \(\varepsilon\) on a per‑query or per‑sequence basis according to empirical diagnostics such as the empirical distribution of sequence importance ratios \(s_i(\theta)\). One simple scheme would be:
\[
\varepsilon = \kappa \cdot \operatorname{median}\!\bigl| s_i(\theta) - 1 \bigr|,
\]
where \(\kappa>0\) is a tuning knob controlling aggressiveness. This would automatically widen the clipping window when the policy is undergoing significant change and tighten it when it is near an optimum, thereby maintaining a tight proximal region without manual re‑calibration.

Implementing such an adaptive rule has several potential benefits:
- **Reduced sample waste:** If high‑variance sequences can be allowed to participate (by loosening clipping), more tokens contribute to the gradient estimate.
- **Improved convergence speed:** By preventing over‑conservative clipping during rapid policy shifts, GSPO can exploit informative samples sooner.
- **Enhanced stability on MoE models:** Since expert activation volatility can spuriously inflate sequence importance ratios, an adaptive scheme that monitors expert usage statistics could dampen over‑reactive clipping.

A natural research question remains: *What statistical measure best predicts the effective safe range for sequence importance ratios?* Candidate metrics include inter‑quartile ranges, robust z‑scores, or even learned neural scalers trained jointly with GSPO.

---

## Hybrid Objectives Combining Policy Gradients & Q‑Learning

GSPO’s core optimisation objective relies on *advantage estimates* derived solely from relative rewards within a query group (Equation 5). While this suffices for many downstream tasks, it discards potentially useful *value information* that could be gleaned from a separate critic network trained to predict expected reward conditioned on partial sequences.  

In PPO literature, critics typically act as estimators of Value \(V_\psi(s)\), providing low‑variance advantage estimates
\[
A_t = r_t + \gamma V_\psi(s_{t+1}) - V_\psi(s_t).
\]
Integrating such value signals into GSPO could offer several benefits:
1. **Better long‑term credit assignment** for multi–turn dialogue or planning problems where immediate rewards are sparse.
2. **Lower variance** by replacing empirical relative advantages with model‑based predictive advantages.
3. **Reduced dependence on large group sizes** because advantages no longer need to be computed relative to group means; instead they can be derived from a critic estimate.

A hybrid objective could take the form:
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{Hybrid}} (\theta,\psi)
= \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\Bigl[
\, \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
   \underbrace{\min
    \Bigl(
         s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i^\text{adv},
         \operatorname{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon_-,\,1+\varepsilon_+\bigr)\widehat{A}_i^\text{adv}
    \Bigr)
   }_{\text{policy loss}}
   -
   \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_V(\psi)
\bigr],
\]
where \(\widehat{A}_i^\text{adv}=r(x,y_i)+\gamma V_\psi(y_{i,<|y_i|})-V_\psi(y_{i,<1})\) replaces or complements the relative advantage supplied by Equation 5, and \(\mathcal{L}_V\) is a standard TD loss. The hyperparameter \(\lambda\) balances policy gradient updates against critic training.

Open questions include:
- How much improvement does value learning bring in practice when training scale is vast? 
- What form of *reward shaping* or *curiosity* signals would synergise best with GSPO’s sequence-level importance ratio?
- Can we train critics that honour sparsity constraints inherent in Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures without incurring prohibitive memory overhead?

---

## Scaling to Multi‑Modal or Instruction‐Tuned LLMs

The GSPO framework is defined at a purely textual level: each response \(y\) comprises token sequences under an autoregressive policy. Yet modern foundation models increasingly integrate vision, audio, or multimodal modalities under unified instruction‐tuning regimes. Extending GSPO entails rethinking how rewards are measured and how sequence likelihoods are computed across modalities:

- **Multi–modal Rewards**: Instead of a single scalar \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\), we might inherit vectorial signals corresponding to correctness across modalities (visual precision vs linguistic fluency). A convex combination or learned “reweighting” mapping could then generate a single scalar used in GSPO’s advantage computation.

- **Conditional Model Likelihoods**: For vision–language pairs \((x_\text{text}, x_\text{image})\), one must define 
  \[
  \pi_\theta(y | x_\text{text}, x_\text{image}) = 
  \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} 
  \pi_\theta(y_t | x_\text{text}, x_\text{image}, y_{<t}),
  \]
  necessitating cross‑modal attention patterns that may differ across tokens. The sequence importance ratio
  \(s_i(\theta)\) computed via log‐probability averages remains applicable but may demand higher precision due to increased number of modalities.

The principal research challenge lies in *efficiently weighing* multi–modal contributions to both reward and probability—whether by weighted averaging of decoder logits or by learning modality‐specific weighting functions jointly with GSPO.

---

## Exploring Reward Functions Beyond Binary Scores

Current experiments use verifier scores \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\), often binary “correct/incorrect” judgments derived from large language model verifiers or human annotators. Extending beyond binary rewards offers richer learning signals:

- **Scalar per-token rewards**: Rather than assigning one total reward per answer, we could allocate finer punishments/boosts at each token generation step based on syntactic adherence or factual coherence checks performed online.

- **Multiobjective rewards**: Consider separate objectives such as safety compliance \(r_{\text{safety}}\), diversity \(r_{\text{div}}\), and computational efficiency \(r_{\text{eff}}\). A weighted sum
  \[
  r(x,y)=w_{\text{safety}} r_{\text{safety}}(x,y)
            + w_{\text{div}} r_{\text{div}}(x,y)
            + w_{\text{eff}} r_{\text{eff}}(x,y)
  \]
  can be fed into Equation 5. The advantage estimates become directional controls over multiple axes simultaneously.

Incorporating these richer reward structures demands careful consideration of how they affect variance in advantage estimates and whether clustering them into groups still yields stable grouping statistics as required by GRPO’s relative advantage formulation.

# 13. Summary and Takeaways

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a cornerstone of large language‑model (LLM) scaling, but its application to massive models—especially sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures—has been historically plagued by unstable training dynamics. The Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm, introduced in the preceding chapters, offers a principled and empirically validated remedy to these challenges. This final chapter consolidates the key insights, crystallizes the stability mechanisms, and distills actionable guidelines for practitioners who wish to deploy GSPO in production RL pipelines.

## Key Contributions Recap

1. **Sequence‑level importance weighting**  
   GSPO replaces the token‑wise importance ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) of GRPO with a single sequence‑level ratio
   \[
   s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|},
   \]
   thereby adhering to the foundational principle of importance sampling: re‑weighting expectations over the full sequence distribution rather than over individual token events.

2. **Consistent reward–gradient alignment**  
   The advantage used in GSPO,
   \[
   \widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\sigma},
   \]
   is computed on a per‑group basis, ensuring that each entire response receives a single scalar advantage that matches its reward signal. This alignment eliminates the mismatch that plagues GRPO’s token‑wise clipping.

3. **Stabilization of MoE training**  
   By focusing exclusively on sequence likelihoods, GSPO becomes immune to the volatile expert activations that distort token‑wise ratios in MoE models. Consequently, it removes the need for auxiliary techniques such as Routing Replay, simplifying both codebase and memory requirements.

4. **Infrastructure simplification**  
   Sequence likelihoods are more robust to numerical precision differences between training engines (e.g., Megatron) and inference engines (e.g., SGLang or vLLM). GSPO can therefore directly consume likelihoods from inference back‑ends during optimization, obviating costly recomputation and easing deployment in distributed or disaggregated settings.

5. **Empirical superiority**  
   Across multiple benchmarks—AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces—GSPO consistently outperforms GRPO in both training efficiency (higher reward trajectories per GPU epoch) and final capability (larger Pass@1 scores or Elo ratings). It played a pivotal role in advancing Qwen3 models to new performance plateaus.

## Stability Analysis Summary

The instability of GRPO originates from a misapplication of importance sampling at the token level:

- Token‐wise ratios \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) are computed using only a single sample from the old policy’s next-token distribution, violating the “many samples” requirement that enables variance reduction in importance weighting.
- These ratios fluctuate over long sequences and are aggressively altered by clipping, causing cumulative gradient noise that escalates with response length.
- In MoE settings, expert activation shifts between old and new policies incite additional variance since each token’s probability depends on a different set of experts.

GSPO resolves these issues through three intertwined mechanisms:

1. **Uniform weighting across tokens**: The sequence ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) assigns effectively equal weight to each token within a response when expanding \(\log s_i(\theta)\) into token‑wise log‐probabilities.
2. **Clipping at the sequence level**: By clipping \(s_i(\theta)\) rather than per‑token ratios, GSPO ensures that only entire responses that are too far off-policy are discounted.
3. **Length normalization**: Raising the sequence ratio to \(1/|y_i|\) controls variance across responses of different lengths and keeps clipping thresholds meaningful across varied generation horizons.

The gradient derivation underpins these observations:
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}} =
\mathbb{E}\!\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta}\!\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\Bigg],
\]
showing that all tokens contribute proportionally to a common scalar weight \(s_i(\theta)\). In contrast, GRPO’s gradient includes heterogenous weights \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), leading to unbalanced updates and amplified noise.

## Practical Guidelines for Practitioners

### 1. Dataset and Query Management
- **Query set refresh**: Regularly update \(\mathcal{D}\) during RL training; stale query distributions can reduce exploration diversity.
- **Batch sizing**: Use large rollout batches to maximize GPU utilization but partition them into 3–4 mini‑batches for gradient updates; this introduces off‑policy data while keeping the importance sampling assumptions intact.

### 2. Clipping Parameters
- **Sequence clipping window**: Set left/right clipping bounds in Equation (𝑕 ) within an order of magnitude smaller than GRPO’s typical values (e.g., \(3\times10^{-4}\)–\(4\times10^{-4}\)). Because \(s_i(\theta)\) is length‑normalized, these narrow ranges suffice to bound extreme off‑policy ratios without discarding useful samples.
- **Adaptive clipping**: Optionally monitor average \(s_i(\theta)\) per epoch; if it systematically drifts above 1 + ε, consider tightening bounds or scaling down learning rates.

### 3. Advantage Computation
- **Group advantage**: Compute \(\widehat{A}_i\) using per–query rewards over all generated responses (\(G>1\)). Standard practice sets \(G=8\), but larger \(G\) improves variance reduction at the cost of memory.
- **Reward scaling**: Rewards lie in \([0,1]\); standardize with mean-\(0\), std-\(1\) before applying advantages so that gradients are numerically stable across training stages.

### 4. MoE Handling
- **No routing replay needed**: Omit any caching or replay of expert activations; rely on GSPO’s sequence weights which do not depend on specific token-level routing decisions.
- **Expert volatility monitoring**: While optional, tracking expert activation overlap between successive policies can provide diagnostic insight into MoE health; significant fluctuations may signal architectural issues unrelated to RL.

### 5. Infrastructure Integration
- **Inference engine for likelihoods**: When using heterogeneous inference backends (SGLang/vLLM), rely on their returned sequence probabilities as inputs for \(s_i(\theta)\). This bypasses recomputation overhead on the training engine.
- **Batching strategy**: For partial rollouts or multi‑turn RL settings where entire responses cannot be generated at once, accumulate per‑turn likelihood segments and reconstruct total \(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\).

### 6. Hyperparameter Scheduling
- **Learning rate decay**: Start with a moderate LR (\(3\times10^{-5}\)) and decay linearly every few epochs; track loss curves autonomously to detect early divergence.
- **Catalytic learning rate adjustments around resets or policy migrations**: If a policy snapshot is swapped mid-training (e.g., checkpoint replay), warm up LR before resuming updates.

### 7. Monitoring & Diagnostics
- **Clipping fraction metrics**: Record proportion of responses clipped per update; persistent high clipping suggests misaligned ratio ranges or drifting policies.
- **Return distribution tracking**: Plot reward histograms per epoch; anticipate gradual leftward shift as policy improves — consistent with an effective advantage estimator.

---

By aligning gradients with rewards at the natural granularity of language generation—the full sequence—GSPO offers a mathematically sound solution to long-standing stability problems in LLM RL training. Its empirical success across dense and sparse models alike validates this design choice and underscores its suitability as a foundation for future RL scaling efforts. Practitioners adopting GSPO can expect smoother convergence trajectories, reduced engineering overhead for MoE-specific hacks, and greater flexibility in leveraging diverse inference infrastructures—all while pushing LLM capabilities toward unprecedented levels such as those achieved by Qwen3.*
