\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 2: # 2. Background Concepts

Large language models (LLMs) have become indispensable engines for natural‐language generation, instruction following, and a variety of downstream tasks. Yet, the sheer scale of these models raises several practical challenges at training time: memory consumption dominates the cost, and traditional fine‑tuning—updating every weight in a 10‑billion‑parameter network—many times consumes hundreds of gigabytes of GPU memory. The **QLoRA** framework addresses these constraints by marrying two powerful ideas: *block‑wise k‑bit quantization* of pretrained weights and *low‑rank adapters* (LoRA) that restrict learning to a small set of additional parameters. Understanding these components—and their interaction with the memory budget—is essential for appreciating why QLoRA can reliably fine‑tune extremely large models on a single consumer GPU.

In this chapter we unpack the foundational concepts that QLoRA leverages:

- **Block‑wise k‑bit quantization**: how tensors are discretised, how per‑block scaling constants are computed and stored, and the inverse dequantization process.
- **Low‑Rank Adapters (LoRA)**: the rank‑decomposition of linear projections, hyperparameters that govern capacity and regularisation, and the dynamics of gradient flow during training.
- **Memory footprint analysis**: a comparative study between full finetuning and LoRA as applied to a large language model, revealing where storage is saved and where it remains necessary.
- **Key limitations of existing PEFT methods** when applied to models in the tens of billions of parameters range.

With these pieces in place we can later see precisely how QLoRA lowers the bar for research teams that would otherwise be unable to experiment with state‑of‑the‑art LLMs.

## # Block–wise k–bit Quantization

Quantisation is the act of mapping a continuous or high‑precision representation into a discrete set with fewer bits per element. A typical scenario in deep learning is converting from 32‑bit floating point (\textsf{FP32}) weights into an 8– or 4–bit format so that the model fits on limited hardware or achieves faster inference throughput.

### Normalising by blockwise constants

When a tensor $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}$ is subdivided into contiguous blocks of size $B$, we obtain $n=\frac{b\,h}{B}$ blocks $\{\mathbf{X}_i\}_{i=1}^n$. For each block we compute an absolute maximum
\[
c_i = \operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{X}_i),
\]
which captures the largest magnitude value inside that block. This constant scales all entries in $\mathbf{X}_i$ into a target numeric range, chosen here as $[-127,127]$ for an 8–bit signed integer representation. The blockwise scaling guarantees that *all* possible 8–bit values are utilised; otherwise outlier values would saturate a few bins and leave many others unused—a wasteful allocation especially for highly skewed distributions.

The quantised tensor $\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}}$ is then computed as
\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{X}^{ \text{Int8}} = \operatorname{round}\!\bigl(c_i^{-1}\,\mathbf{X}_i\bigr)
}
\tag{2}
\]
where $c_i^{-1} = \frac{127}{\,\operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{X}_i)}$ is sometimes called the *quantisation constant* or *scale*. Importantly, this operation is applied independently per block; no inter‐block normalisation takes place.

To recover approximate real values during inference or backward passes we perform *dequantisation*:
\[
\boxed{
   \operatorname{dequant}(c_i,\mathbf{b}) = c_i\,\mathbf{b},
}
\tag{3}
\]
where $\mathbf{b}$ are the stored low‑bit integers and $c_i$ restores them to floating point space (often BF16 or FP16). Equation (3) is exact whenever the quantiser was perfect; in practice it introduces some small rounding error.

### Why block size matters

Choosing $B$ trades off precision against memory overhead. Larger blocks mean fewer scaling constants but potentially less accurate mapping: all values in a big block share one scale which can lead to bin underutilisation when there is high variance across elements. Conversely, small blocks drastically increase the number of constants—each requiring extra storage but keeping scaling granular.

For LLMs, it has been empirically observed that a blocksize around $64$ works well for a 4–bit quantisation while maintaining model performance when combined with LoRA training techniques such as QLoRA’s double quantisation scheme described later.

## # Low‑Rank Adapters (LoRA)

Parameter‑efficient finetuning trains only a small set of auxiliary weights while keeping the pretrained model frozen—an attractive strategy when GPU memory is scarce or privacy concerns forbid altering public weights.

### Rank‐decomposition form

A standard linear projection within an LLM maps input $\mathbf{X}\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times h}$ through weight $\mathbf{W}\in \mathbb{R}^{h\times o}$:
\[
    \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{W}.
\]
The LoRA trick augments this projection with an extra low–rank term,
\[
    \boxed{
   \mathbf{\widetilde Y} = \underbrace{\mathbf X\,\mathbf W}_{\textstyle pretrain}}
   + 
   s\, 
   {\underbrace{\bigl(\mathbf X\, \mathbf L_1\bigr)}_{\mathbb R^{b\times r}}
   {\underbrace{\bf L_2}_{r\times o}}},
}
\tag{4}
\]
where:
- $\mathbf L_1 \in \mathbb R^{h\times r}$ and $\mathbf L_2 \in \mathbb R^{r\times o}$ are learned adapters.
- $r$ is the *rank*, typically far smaller than $h$ and $o$ (often 8–64).
- $s$ is a scalar that rescales the adapter so its contribution at initialization matches typical weight values (often set to $1/r$).

Because $\|{\bf W}\|_F$ dominates training dynamics before learning begins, LoRA keeps ${\bf W}$ frozen; gradients flow only through ${\bf L}_1$ and ${\bf L}_2$, making adaptation extremely lightweight physically and computationally.

### Hyperparameters guiding capacity

Several LoRA hyperparameters dictate how strongly and flexibly adapters can modulate the pretrained representations:

| Hyperparameter | Typical range | Effect |
|----------------|---------------|--------|
| Rank $r$ | $8 - 128$ | Higher ranks increase expressiveness but add memory & compute |
| Scale $s$ | $(0,\infty)$ | Adjusts magnitude of adapter update |
| Adapter placement | Layer-wise vs selective | Mapping more layers yields better performance but increases parameters |

In practice QLoRA applies an adapter to virtually every weight matrix inside transformer blocks—q/qkv projections, feed-forward dense layers—rather than saving on adapters sparingly; because LoRA's parameter count stays negligible compared to base memories this “full coverage” strategy eliminates most accuracy loss present in earlier sparse-adapter schemes.

### Gradient flow during training

During backpropagation gradients must propagate between frozen weights $\bf W$, adapter matrices ${\bf L}_i$, and upstream activations $\bf X$. The forward pass uses dequantised representations $\bf W$, so gradients with respect to ${\bf X}$ are computed exactly as if real precision were used:
$\frac{\partial E}{\partial {\bf X}} = \frac{\partial E}{\partial {\bf Y}}\,{\bf W}^\top$.  
However, because ${\bf W}$ remains unmodified during updates, no weight decay or adaptive optimizer step needs be computed for it; only Adam/W optimizer updates loTra parameters (${L}_1,L_2$). Hence LoRA reduces both memory footprint due to tiny gradient tensors for active weights and compute overhead tied to Adam steps.

## # Memory Footprint Analysis for Full Finetuning vs. LoRA

*(All numbers refer loosely to GPU VRAM assuming typical BF16/BFloat16 usage.)*

### Full 16-bit finetuning

For a generic Transformer such as LLaMA’s decoder head:

- **Model parameters**: A 7B parameter example occupies about $7B\times16/8=14~GB$, disregarding overhead such as optimizer slots or activation tensors.
- **Gradient buffers**: During training each weight needs paired activation gradients (~50% overhead). With checkpointing enabled many frameworks store only activations at output ends of layers which leads to additional memory usage up to ~70% of raw weight storage.
- **Optimiser state**: Adam’s per-parameter momentum adds another ~128 % on top of base weight size at BF16 precision (~18 GB).

Overall a full finetuning run can thus exceed 60 GB even before accounting for batch tensors or hidden state buffers.

### LoRA Finetuning

Assume we keep all base weights frozen but add adapters:

- **Adapter parameters**: Adding two matrices per linear layer with rank $r=8$ yields roughly \(2\times r\times (h+o)\) parameters per layer—a tiny share ($<0.2$\%) relative to base weights.
    - For a model with ~30 layers each handling matrices sized ~\(8192\times8192$, this comes out around \(30\times2\times8\times8192~≈~3~MB.\)
- **Gradient buffers**: Because only adapters are updated and stored as BF16/FP16 quantities, their gradients occupy equivalent size (~3 MB).
- **Optimizer state**: Similar logic applies; Adam slots are tied only to adapters, providing another few megabytes.

The critical takeaway showcased by QLoRA is that *the majority* of GPU budget continues to be consumed by storing activations during forward passes (\(~5~GB embryos for 4-bit bases*)—the freszoned map forces them up briefly—but by freezing all other parameters we eliminate billions of bytes from integer storage previously allocated for weight updates.

When combined with QLoRA’s quantisation (downscaling base tensors from ~54 GB down to ~5 GB) plus double quantisation savings (~3 GB), you move from >780 GB down to <48 GB on a single modern card—a transformation achieved without any loss in performance according once adapters learn freely through backpropagation across frozen rhythmble quantised kernels.

## # Key Limitations of Existing PEFT Methods in Context of Large Models

As PEFT approaches grew popular (prompt tuning, prefix tuning), certain weaknesses became apparent when strictly applied to 10⁹+ sized models:

1. **Sparse Placement Leads To Accuracy Gap**  
   Traditional LoRA variants often inserted adapters into select layers (e.g., only query/key/value projections). For mid-size models this marginal approach balances cost vs accuracy; however, at scaling >33 B the removed layers hold legible amount of information whose fine-tuning cannot be approximated by narrow local changes—leading noticeably lower final perplexity or instruction-following fidelity compared to full finetuning.

2. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity Overheads**  
   Because higher ranks involve more parameters scattered across many layers, many frameworks require exhaustive grid searches over learning rate schedules, batch sizes etc., which becomes polynomially expensive when repeats are forced across numerous dataset/scale permutations as done in QLoRA’s experiment sweep (>1 000 models).  

3. **Backwards Compatibility With Quantized Bases**  
   Earlier PEFT libraries often expect full precision weights during forward computation; when fetching from ∼4– bit tensors there exists mismatched dtype propagation causing spontaneous NaNs or overflow if not explicitly handled via dequant – compute – re-upload pipelines that most open-source toolkits simply omit until recently.

4. **Compatibility With Advanced Optimises (\emph e.g., AdamW under distributed mixed precision)**  
   Using BF16 accumulators when some partitions reside at INT4 necessitates complex mixed precision handling not standardized across frameworks; even small GPU fragmentation can cause OOM if not internally patched (as Paged Optimisers highlight).

QLoRA addresses these shortcomings systematically:

- By training adapters *everywhere* while keeping them minuscule participants.
- By leveraging specialised NF4 data types that match pretrained weight statistics.
- By introducing Double Quantisation that reduces constant overhead fivefold.
- And by employing paged optimisation memory allocation so gradient checkpointing never exceeds VRAM capacities even under long sequence lengths.

Thus QLoRA opens up fully scalable PEFT on large parameter regimes without dexterous hyperparameter hunting or fragile upstream frameworks.
