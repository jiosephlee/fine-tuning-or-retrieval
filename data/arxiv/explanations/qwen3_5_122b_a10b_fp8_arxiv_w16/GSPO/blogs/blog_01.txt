# Why Your AI Model Might Be Crashing: Solving the Instability of Large-Scale Reinforcement Learning

Training massive language models with Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the gold standard for pushing the boundaries of what AI can do, enabling models to solve competition-level math and complex coding problems. However, as researchers scale these efforts to train gigantic models, they face a terrifying hurdle: **model collapse**. This is a catastrophic failure mode where the model's performance degrades irreversibly, often leaving engineers unable to recover even after reverting to previous checkpoints or tweaking hyperparameters.

Recent research identifies that this instability isn't just bad luck; it stems from a fundamental flaw in how popular algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) handle probability corrections. By shifting the focus from fixing individual words to evaluating entire sentences, a new method called Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) offers a robust solution that stabilizes training and unlocks superior performance.

## The Hidden Flaw in Token-Level Correction

To understand why models crash, we first need to look at how current RL algorithms try to learn from past mistakes. When training a model, we generate responses using an older version of the model (the "behavior" policy) and then optimize a newer version (the "target" policy). Because we are comparing distributions that have changed over time, we rely on **importance sampling** to correct for this mismatch.

Mathematically, importance sampling works by re-weighting samples so that expectations under the target distribution can be estimated using samples from the behavior distribution. The formula looks like this:

$$ \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)} \, f(z) \right] $$

The critical assumption here is that you must average over many samples for this weighting to work correctly. However, GRPO attempts to apply this correction at the **token level**—meaning it calculates an importance weight for every single word in a response independently. Since each word is essentially a single sample from its specific next-token distribution, this approach fails to satisfy the statistical requirements of importance sampling.

Instead of correcting the distribution mismatch, calculating weights token-by-token injects high-variance noise into the training gradients. As the response gets longer, this noise accumulates exponentially. When combined with the clipping mechanisms designed to stabilize training, this accumulated noise eventually triggers a feedback loop that causes the model to collapse completely. Once this happens, the damage is often permanent because the model has learned incorrect patterns that cannot be easily overwritten.

## The Core Principle: Match Units of Reward and Optimization

The root cause of GRPO's instability lies in a mismatch between units. In RL for language models, rewards are assigned to **entire sequences** (e.g., "Did this code compile?" or "Is this math proof correct?"). Yet, GRPO tries to optimize these sequence-level rewards by adjusting probabilities one token at a time using unstable weights.

The solution proposed by GSPO is simple yet profound: **the unit of optimization must match the unit of reward.** If we give credit or blame for an entire answer, we should calculate our importance weights based on the likelihood of that entire answer occurring as a sequence.

By defining the importance ratio based on sequence likelihood rather than individual token likelihoods, GSPO aligns perfectly with how rewards are actually distributed in practice. This shift eliminates the high-variance noise inherent in token-level calculations and ensures that the clipping mechanism operates on meaningful data rather than statistical artifacts.

## How GSPO Stabilizes Training

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) replaces the token-level objective with a sequence-level one. Instead of summing up weighted losses for every token $t$ in a response $y$, GSPO evaluates the response as a whole block $y_i$. The optimization objective becomes:

$$ \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min \left( s_{i}(\theta)  \widehat{A}_{i},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i} 
\right) 
\right] $$

Here, $s_i(\theta)$ represents the sequence-level importance ratio, calculated as:

$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ 
\pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} 
\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$

Notice two key differences here compared to previous methods:
*   **Sequence Clipping:** GSPO clips entire responses if they deviate too far from the old policy's distribution. This prevents outlier sequences from skewing the gradient updates.
*   **Length Normalization:** The exponent $1/|y_i|$ normalizes the ratio by sequence length. Without this step, short sequences might dominate or fluctuate wildly compared to long ones simply due to probability multiplication effects over many tokens.

In practice, this means GSPO treats every token in a successful response equally when calculating gradients once it passes stability checks via clipping at the sequence level. This removes the erratic weighting scheme that plagued GRPO and leads to smooth, continuous learning curves even as models grow larger and responses get longer.

## A Game Changer for Mixture-of-Experts Models

One of the most compelling benefits of GSPO is its ability to handle Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures without special tricks. MoE models are highly efficient because they activate only specific parts ("experts") of their neural network for each input token depending on routing decisions. However, these routing decisions can change drastically between training steps—a phenomenon known as **expert-activation volatility**.

When using GRPO on MoE models, if an expert used by $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ changes in $\pi_\theta$, calculating token-level probabilities becomes meaningless because you are comparing apples to oranges across different sub-networks active at each step. Previously, researchers had to implement complex workarounds like **Routing Replay**, where they forced both policies to use identical routing paths during calculation just to keep numbers comparable. This added significant memory overhead and limited model capacity.

GSPO sidesteps this entirely because it relies on total sequence likelihood rather than individual token probabilities driven by specific expert routes. Even if internal routing shifts slightly between steps, the overall probability of generating a coherent sentence remains relatively stable enough for effective optimization. Consequently, GSPO trains MoE models natively without needing Routing Replay or other stabilization hacks, simplifying infrastructure and allowing models to utilize their full architectural potential freely.

## Simpler Infrastructure and Better Performance beyond Theory

Beyond stability theory, GSPO offers practical advantages for engineering teams building RL pipelines today:
*   **Reduced Precision Sensitivity:** Since GSPO uses aggregate sequence likelihoods rather than precise per-token values computed across thousands of operations sequentially, it tolerates floating-point precision differences between training engines (like Megatron-LM) and inference engines (like vLLM or SGLang).
