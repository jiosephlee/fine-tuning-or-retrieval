\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### If GSPO clips entire sequences, doesn't it discard too much useful signal compared to GRPO?
Question:
The paper highlights in Section 4.2 that GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (by sequence count) than GRPO—often two orders of magnitude more—and yet claims this leads to better efficiency.

Intuitively, if I generate 16 responses and clip 14 of them entirely because their sequence likelihood shifted too much, I am throwing away almost all my data for that batch step. In contrast, GRPO might clip only specific bad tokens within otherwise good sequences.

How can discarding nearly every single sample in a batch result in superior training efficiency? Is it because GRPO's retained tokens contain misleading noise that actively harms learning more than simply ignoring them would? Or is there something about the 'signal-to-noise' ratio of sequence-level updates vs token-level updates that I'm failing to grasp?
Answer:
Your intuition that discarding 90% of your samples seems wasteful is understandable, but the paper argues that this apparent "loss" is actually a feature, not a bug, because the discarded data in GRPO is often providing **misleading signals** rather than useful ones. The core issue isn't the quantity of data used for the update, but the **quality and variance** of the gradient estimates provided by that data.

According to Section 3 ("Motivation") and Section 4.2 ("Curious Observation on Clipping Fractions"), the inefficiency of GRPO stems from a fundamental misapplication of importance sampling at the token level. In GRPO, the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is calculated for every single token independently. The authors argue that since a single token sample cannot reliably estimate a distribution shift (which requires averaging over many samples), these token-level weights introduce high-variance noise. When you combine this noisy weighting with a shared advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ across all tokens in a sequence, you get gradients where some tokens are artificially up-weighted or down-weighted based on random fluctuations rather than true policy deviation. This noise accumulates over long sequences and, critically, is amplified by the clipping mechanism itself, eventually leading to model collapse.

In contrast, GSPO shifts the unit of analysis from the token to the entire sequence. As defined in Equation (5), GSPO calculates a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ based on the geometric mean of token probabilities. This metric correctly reflects whether the *entire* response was likely under the new policy compared to the old one. When GSPO clips a sequence (as noted in Section 4.2), it is rejecting samples where this global probability ratio has drifted too far outside the trust region $(1-\epsilon \text{ to } 1+\epsilon)$. While this results in clipping a massive fraction of tokens (because if one sequence is clipped, all its tokens are excluded), it ensures that **every remaining gradient step is computed on statistically valid samples**.

The reason this leads to superior efficiency despite lower sample usage is explained in Section 4.2: GRPO's retained tokens contain "inherently noisy" gradient estimates that actively harm learning by pulling parameters in conflicting directions due to unstable weights. By aggressively filtering out these unreliable sequences, GSPO achieves a much higher signal-to-noise ratio in its updates. Essentially, ten clean updates from GSPO are more valuable than one hundred noisy updates from GRPO because the latter introduces instability that can derail training entirely (a phenomenon observed as "catastrophic model collapse"). Therefore, discarding sequences isn't throwing away signal; it is removing noise that would otherwise corrupt the optimization landscape.
