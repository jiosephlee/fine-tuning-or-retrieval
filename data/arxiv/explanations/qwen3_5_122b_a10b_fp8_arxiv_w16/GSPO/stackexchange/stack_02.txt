\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Does GSPO-token actually optimize differently than standard GSPO when advantages are uniform?
Question:
Section 3.2 introduces 'GSPO-token' as a variant allowing for token-wise advantage adjustments. The paper states in Equation (9) that $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ involves a stop-gradient operator ($\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$) on both the sequence ratio and the token probability.

Specifically, it says: "Note that the term ... has a numerical value of 1..." and concludes that GSPO-token and GSPO are "numerically identical" if all token advantages are equal ($\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$).

However, looking at Equation (8), $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ seems to decouple the sequence-level importance weight from the token-level gradient calculation. If I set all advantages equal, why do we need this complex formulation with stop-gradients? Isn't this just adding unnecessary computational overhead compared to simply summing up token gradients weighted by a constant scalar? Am I missing a subtle point about how backpropagation flows through these terms?
Answer:
Your intuition that the formulation might seem redundant when advantages are uniform is understandable, but the complexity involving stop-gradients is not merely computational overhead; it is a deliberate architectural choice to enforce a specific **separation of concerns** between global sequence stability and local token flexibility. While it is true that when $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$ for all $t$, the numerical value of the objective function and the final gradient vector are identical to standard GSPO, the *computational graph* constructed during backpropagation differs fundamentally.

In standard GSPO, the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ depends on the product of probabilities across all tokens in the sequence. Consequently, the gradient of $s_i(\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$ involves a sum over all tokens (as seen in Eq. 14), meaning every token's log-probability contributes to the scaling factor itself. If you were to naively implement "token-wise" weighting without stop-gradients by multiplying a global scalar by local terms, you would inadvertently create a situation where changing a single token's probability alters the global importance weight used for *every other* token in that sequence. This creates a dense coupling where optimizing one part of the sequence affects the importance scaling of unrelated parts, potentially reintroducing variance issues similar to those found in GRPO.

The `gs` (stop-gradient) operators in Eq. 16 break this coupling. By writing $s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|\dots)}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|\dots)]}$, the algorithm ensures that the global sequence likelihood ratio acts as a fixed coefficient (a static multiplier) for every token's gradient update within that specific backward pass. The term $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta]}$ evaluates to 1 numerically but preserves the gradient path for only that specific token's log-probability derivative $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$. As derived in Eq. 19, this results in a gradient where each token receives an update scaled by the pre-computed sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$, but crucially, $s_i(\theta)$ itself does not receive gradients from individual token updates during that step.

When advantages are uniform ($\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$), this structure collapses mathematically to exactly Eq. 14 because factoring out constants yields the same result: $\sum_t s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i \nabla \log \pi_t = s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i \sum_t \nabla \log \pi_t$. However, this setup is essential for non-uniform advantages (the primary motivation for GSPO-token). Without stop-gradients, attempting to assign different $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ values would cause the global weight $s_i(\theta)$ to fluctuate dynamically based on which tokens have high or low advantages during optimization, destabilizing the very sequence-level consistency GSPO aims to preserve. Thus, while computationally slightly heavier due to detached operations, this formulation guarantees that when you eventually switch to non-uniform advantages (e.g., in multi-turn RL), you retain the stability benefits of sequence-level clipping without needing to redesign the gradient flow logic later. It essentially prepares the computation graph for variable advantages while remaining mathematically equivalent to GSPO under uniform conditions.
