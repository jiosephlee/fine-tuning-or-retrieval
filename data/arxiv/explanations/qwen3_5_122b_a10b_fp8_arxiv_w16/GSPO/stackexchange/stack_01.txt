\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why is the sequence-level importance ratio raised to the power of $1/|y_i|$ in GSPO?
Question:
In Section 3.1, Equation (6), the authors define the sequence-level importance ratio $s_{i}(	heta)$ as:
$$ s_{i}(	heta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
I understand that this represents a geometric mean of the token-level ratios, but I am confused about why this specific exponentiation is necessary. If we just used the raw product of probabilities, wouldn't that simply reflect the total likelihood change? Does raising it to $1/|y_i|$ change the direction of the gradient, or is it purely for numerical stability and variance reduction as hinted in the text? How does this affect the clipping mechanism if we don't normalize by length?
Answer:
The exponentiation by $1/|y_i|$ in the GSPO sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is primarily a variance reduction technique designed to ensure numerical stability and maintain a consistent clipping regime across sequences of varying lengths, rather than merely reflecting the total likelihood change. As explicitly stated in Section 3.1 of the paper, without this length normalization, the raw sequence probability ratio $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}$ would behave multiplicatively over the sequence length. Consequently, even minor deviations in token-level probabilities would compound exponentially as the sequence grows, causing the importance ratio to fluctuate dramatically for long responses. This would render a fixed clipping range $\epsilon$ ineffective; short sequences might never trigger clipping while long sequences would almost always be clipped to zero, breaking the optimization signal.

By raising the ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$, GSPO effectively computes the geometric mean of the token-level importance ratios, transforming the multiplicative accumulation of errors into an additive accumulation in log-space (as seen in the exponential form provided in Equation 6). This ensures that $s_i(\theta)$ remains within a unified numerical range regardless of whether the response contains 10 or 1000 tokens. Crucially, this normalization does not alter the direction of the gradient update regarding *which* sequences are favored versus penalized, because taking a positive root preserves monotonicity; however, it fundamentally changes the *magnitude* of the weighting applied to each token's gradient contribution.

As derived in Section 3.2 (Equations 9 and 10), this formulation results in every token within a specific response receiving an identical weight factor determined by $s_i(\theta)$, whereas GRPO assigns potentially divergent weights to each token based on local likelihood ratios. The paper argues that this uniform weighting eliminates the "instability factor" present in GRPO where high-variance token-level weights accumulate unpredictably over long trajectories. Therefore, while using the raw product would technically reflect total likelihood change, it would fail to provide a stable learning signal for variable-length generations and would necessitate dynamic or impractically wide clipping bounds that could either ignore important updates or discard valid samples indiscriminately.
