\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How exactly does 'expert-activation volatility' invalidate token-level importance ratios in MoE models?
Question:
In Section 4.3, the authors argue that GRPO fails on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models because of 'expert-activation volatility.' They claim that since different experts might be active for $πθ$ versus $πθ_old$ for the same input sequence, the token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ becomes invalid.

My confusion is: Importance sampling mathematically requires evaluating $π(y|x)$ regardless of *which* parameters were used to compute it. If I calculate $πθ(y_t|x, y<t)$ using the new router and $πθ_old(y_t|x, y<t)$ using the old router, isn't that exactly what importance sampling demands?

Why does changing which experts are active make the ratio 'invalid' rather than just 'high variance'? Is there a discontinuity in MoE outputs when routers switch that breaks continuity assumptions required for gradient descent?
Answer:
Your intuition regarding the mechanics of importance sampling is partially correct: mathematically, one can indeed evaluate probabilities $\pi_\theta(y_t|x, y_{<t})$ using a new router configuration and $\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_t|x, y_{<t})$ using an old one. However, the paper argues that in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, this specific calculation renders the token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ **structurally invalid** rather than merely high-variance due to a fundamental disconnect between the numerator and denominator.

The core issue described in Section 4.3 ("Benefit of GSPO for MoE Training") is that MoE layers utilize a routing mechanism to select a subset of experts for each token. When the policy parameters $\theta$ are updated, the router's decision boundary shifts, causing "expert-activation volatility." Consequently, for the exact same input sequence $y$, the set of active experts in $\pi_\theta$ may differ significantly (roughly 10% in their experiments) from those active in $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. This creates a scenario where you are comparing probabilities computed by two entirely different sub-networks. The ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|\dots)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_t|\dots)}$ ceases to represent a smooth correction for distribution shift; instead, it captures arbitrary differences caused by structural changes in which experts contributed to the output.

This violates the implicit assumption of continuity required for stable gradient descent. In standard dense models or when using techniques like "Routing Replay" (caching old expert paths), the network structure remains constant, allowing the probability ratio to reflect genuine changes in token preference. Without such constraints, the sudden switch in active experts introduces massive discontinuities where a small parameter update causes a drastic jump in the calculated likelihood ratio. As noted in Section 3 ("Motivation"), applying these unstable weights at every token step amplifies noise exponentially over long sequences, eventually overwhelming the clipping mechanism and leading to model collapse.

By shifting to sequence-level optimization as proposed in GSPO, the algorithm aggregates these fluctuations over the entire response length $|y|$. The sequence likelihood ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y|x)} \right)^{1/|y|}$ effectively averages out local expert-switching artifacts because the overall generative capability of the model remains relatively stable even if internal routing paths diverge slightly. Thus, GSPO avoids relying on volatile token-level comparisons that depend on consistent expert activation patterns, rendering it robust to MoE-specific instabilities without needing auxiliary strategies like Routing Replay.
