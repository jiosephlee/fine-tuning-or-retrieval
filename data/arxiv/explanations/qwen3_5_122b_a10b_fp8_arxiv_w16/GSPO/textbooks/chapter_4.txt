Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: The GSPO Algorithm – Core Principles and Formulation

The evolution of Reinforcement Learning (RL) for Large Language Models (LLMs) has reached a critical juncture where the stability of the training algorithm dictates the ceiling of model capability. As models grow in size and complexity, particularly with the adoption of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, traditional algorithms that rely on token-level approximations begin to fail. This chapter delves into the foundational architecture of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), an algorithm designed to rectify the fundamental instabilities inherent in previous methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). The central thesis of GSPO is a rigorous adherence to the statistical principle that the unit of optimization must align perfectly with the unit of reward. By shifting the locus of control from individual tokens to entire sequences, GSPO establishes a theoretically sound framework that enables stable, efficient, and scalable training for state-of-the-art language models.

## The Principle of Matching Units: Sequence Reward Requires Sequence Optimization

To understand why GSPO was necessary, one must first appreciate the structural flaw in its predecessors regarding the application of importance sampling. In standard RL settings for language models, a verifier assigns a scalar reward $r(x, y)$ to an entire response $y$ generated for a query $x$. This reward is inherently holistic; it evaluates the coherence, correctness, and quality of the complete sequence as a single entity. However, algorithms like GRPO attempted to optimize this global signal by breaking it down into local components. They assigned this single sequence-level advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ to every individual token $y_{i,t}$ within that sequence and then applied token-level importance weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to correct for off-policy sampling errors.

This approach creates a fundamental mismatch between the unit of reward and the unit of optimization. The principle of importance sampling dictates that to estimate an expectation under a target distribution $\pi_\theta$ using samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, one must re-weight samples based on their joint probability ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(z)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(z)}$. Crucially, this correction relies on averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution to effectively cancel out distributional mismatches. In GRPO, however, the importance weight is calculated at each token position $t$ based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ drawn from the conditional distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$. Because this weight is derived from a single realization rather than an aggregate expectation, it fails to perform its intended role as a valid distribution-correcting factor. Instead, it acts as a source of high-variance noise.

As training progresses and response lengths increase, this noise accumulates exponentially along the sequence. When combined with the clipping mechanism—which truncates extreme weights—the result is not merely reduced variance but distorted gradients that can lead to catastrophic model collapse. Once such collapse occurs in GRPO-trained models, it is often irreversible; reverting checkpoints or tuning hyperparameters yields no recovery because the underlying gradient estimator has become fundamentally broken. GSPO addresses this by recognizing that since the reward is granted to the entire sequence, the off-policy correction must also be applied at the sequence level. By treating the response $y$ as an atomic unit for both weighting and clipping, GSPO ensures that the optimization objective respects the granularity of the feedback signal provided by the verifier. This alignment eliminates the accumulation of token-level noise and provides a stable foundation for scaling RL to massive models and long-context tasks.

## Defining the Sequence-Level Importance Ratio $s_i(\theta)$ via Geometric Mean Normalization

With established motivation for moving away from token-level weights, we turn to the mathematical formulation of GSPO's core component: the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$. In GRPO, the importance ratio for a token was defined simply as $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$. If we were naively extended this logic to sequences without modification, we might consider using raw likelihood ratios $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}$. However, such raw ratios present significant numerical challenges when dealing with autoregressive generation over hundreds or thousands of tokens. The product of probabilities across a long chain tends toward zero extremely rapidly due to floating-point underflow or explosive growth depending on implementation specifics. More critically for optimization stability, small changes in likelihoods at just a few positions can cause dramatic fluctuations in these raw products as sequence length varies.

To resolve these issues while maintaining theoretical validity regarding importance sampling theory (as referenced in prior literature), GSPO defines $s_i(\theta)$ using a length-normalized geometric mean approach. The formula is expressed as:
$$ s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
Expanding this definition reveals its relationship to token-level probabilities:
$$ s_i(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right) $$
This formulation essentially calculates the exponential of the average log-probability ratio across all tokens in the sequence. By raising likelihood ratios to their reciprocal length ($1/|y_i|$), GSPO transforms what would be an unstable multiplicative accumulation into a stable additive average in log-space before exponentiating back out. This geometric mean normalization serves two vital purposes aligned with intuition first described in Section 3a). First, it controls $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range regardless of whether a response consists of 10 tokens or 1000 tokens. Without this normalization, longer sequences would naturally produce vastly different magnitudes for their importance ratios compared to shorter ones solely due to length differences rather than actual policy divergence rates per step. Secondly—and perhaps most importantly—it reduces variance dramatically compared to summing independent random variables across long chains; averaging dampens outliers whereas multiplying them amplifies them exponentially over time steps tending towards infinity if not corrected appropriately beforehand through division inside exponentiation domain operations themselves!
