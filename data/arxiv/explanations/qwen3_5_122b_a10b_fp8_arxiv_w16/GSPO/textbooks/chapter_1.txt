Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Introduction to Reinforcement Learning in Large Language Models

Reinforcement learning (RL) has rapidly emerged as a pivotal paradigm for scaling the capabilities of large language models (LLMs). As these models grow in size and complexity, static supervised fine-tuning alone proves insufficient for unlocking their full potential in tackling sophisticated, open-ended problems. Through large-scale RL, language models develop the ability to engage in deeper and longer reasoning processes, enabling them to solve competition-level mathematics, write complex code, and navigate intricate logical chains that were previously beyond their reach. This shift represents a fundamental evolution in how we train AI systems, moving from merely predicting the next token to actively optimizing for high-quality outcomes verified by external evaluators. However, as researchers attempt to push these boundaries by investing greater computational resources into RL training, they encounter a formidable barrier: the requirement for extremely stable and robust training dynamics. Without stability, the massive investments in compute yield diminishing returns or, worse, catastrophic failures.

## The Role of RL in Scaling LLM Capabilities

The primary driver behind integrating reinforcement learning into the training pipeline of modern large language models is the necessity to handle tasks that require extended reasoning horizons. Traditional autoregressive training objectives optimize for local coherence—predicting the most likely next token based on immediate history—but this myopic view often fails when a task requires planning several steps ahead or correcting early mistakes. RL addresses this by treating text generation as a sequential decision-making process where the model acts as an agent interacting with an environment defined by a query and a verifier.

In this framework, an autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$ is defined as a policy $\pi_\theta$. When presented with a query $x$, the model generates a response $y$, which can be viewed as a sequence of tokens $y_1, y_2, \dots, y_{|y|}$. The likelihood of generating this specific response under the policy is calculated as the product of conditional probabilities at each step: $\pi_\theta (y | x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t} )$, where $|y|$ denotes the total number of tokens in the response. Crucially, once a complete response $y$ is generated for a query $x$, it is evaluated by a verifier function $r$. This verifier assigns a scalar score $r(x, y) \in [ 0, 1 ]$ representing the quality or correctness of the entire sequence. By maximizing the expected reward over many generations, RL guides the policy toward producing responses that are not just linguistically fluent but logically sound and factually accurate regarding complex domains like advanced mathematics and software engineering. This capability allows models to undertake "deeper" reasoning processes that are essential for solving problems where intermediate steps must be coherent before reaching a final conclusion.

## The Crisis of Training Stability: Model Collapse and Irreversibility

Despite the promise of RL for scaling intelligence, practical implementation at massive scales has revealed severe fragility in current training methodologies. The foremost prerequisite for successfully scaling RL with increased computational investment is maintaining stable training dynamics; however, state-of-the-art algorithms often fail to meet this requirement when applied to gigantic language models. The consequence of this instability is frequently "catastrophic model collapse," a phenomenon where the model's performance degrades rapidly and irreversibly during training. Once this collapse occurs, it manifests as an irreversible loss of capability that cannot be remedied simply by reverting to previous checkpoints or meticulously tuning hyperparameters such as clipping ranges or extending generation lengths. Even changing the set of queries used during training has been observed to be ineffective once collapse sets in.

This instability creates a critical bottleneck in pushing the capability boundaries of language models. If every increase in model size or dataset complexity carries a non-negligible risk of triggering an irreversible collapse that wastes weeks of computation and energy reserves, then true scaling becomes impossible. The root cause of these failures often lies hidden within the algorithmic design choices made during optimization. Specifically, when training very large models or Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures on long-response tasks using standard approaches like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), researchers have observed that small variances in gradient estimates can compound over time. This accumulation leads to erratic policy updates that eventually destabilize the entire system. Understanding why these collapses happen is not merely an academic exercise but a necessary step toward designing algorithms that can reliably harness massive compute clusters without fear of self-destruction during training runs.

## Limitations of Existing Algorithms: The Flaws in GRPO

To understand why current methods struggle with stability at scale, one must examine the mechanics of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which was designed to eliminate the need for separate value networks required by earlier methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). GRPO attempts to simplify RL by estimating advantages relatively within a group of responses generated for the same query rather than relying on an auxiliary value model. In GRPO, given a query $x$, $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ are sampled from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The advantage $\widehat{A}_{i}$ assigned to each response is computed based on its reward relative to other responses in the group:
$$ \widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) }{ \mathrm{std} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) } $$
All tokens within a specific response $y_i$ share this same advantage value $\widehat{A}_{i}$. The optimization objective then sums over all tokens across all groups:
$$ \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G }
\left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{!|y_i|} 
\min \left( w_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i},  \mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon})\widehat{A}_{i}\right)\right] $$
Here lies the fundamental flaw identified in recent research: GRPO applies importance sampling weights at the **token level**. The importance ratio for each token $t$ is defined as:
$$ w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t}) }{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) } $$
The principle of importance sampling dictates that re-weighting samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$ to approximate expectations under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ requires averaging over many samples ($N \gg 1$). Mathematically:
$$ \mathbb{E}_{z ~sim~ pi_\text {tar }}[f(z)] = E_z sim pi _ {beh}[ frac { pi _ {tar }( z )}{ pi _ {beh }( z ) } f(z)] $$
However, GRPO calculates this weight based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ drawn from each next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text {old}}( . | x , y _ { i , < t })$. Because there is only one sample per token position being considered at any moment during backpropagation relative to that specific distribution slice, the weight fails to perform its intended role of correcting distributional mismatches effectively. Instead of reducing bias introduced by off-policy data collection (where responses are generated by $\pi_{\theta_\text {old}}$ but updated using gradients from $\pi_\theta$), these token-level weights introduce high-variance noise into the training gradients.

This noise becomes particularly destructive because it accumulates over long sequences and interacts poorly with the clipping mechanism inherent in PPO-style algorithms. As training progresses on long-response tasks common in reasoning benchmarks like AIME or CodeForces, these accumulated errors amplify until they trigger model collapse. Furthermore, this issue is exacerbated in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models due to "expert-activation volatility." In MoE architectures, different subsets of experts are activated depending on input routing decisions driven by gating networks trained via softmax distributions subject to floating-point precision limits and stochasticity between inference engines and training engines.
