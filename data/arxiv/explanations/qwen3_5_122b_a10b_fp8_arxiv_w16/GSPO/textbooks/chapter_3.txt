Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Diagnosing Instability – Why Token-Level Weighting Fails

The pursuit of scaling reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models has revealed a critical bottleneck: the inherent instability of current state-of-the-art algorithms when applied to massive architectures and long-horizon tasks. While algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) were designed to eliminate the need for auxiliary value models, empirical evidence has shown that they frequently suffer from catastrophic and irreversible model collapse, particularly when training gigantic models or Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures. This chapter dissects the root causes of this failure. We argue that the instability is not merely a hyperparameter tuning issue or a side effect of architectural complexity, but rather stems from a fundamental theoretical misapplication of importance sampling within the GRPO framework. By examining the mathematical disconnect between token-level weighting and sequence-level rewards, we will understand how high-variance noise accumulates over long sequences and interacts destructively with clipping mechanisms to destabilize training dynamics.

## Theoretical Requirements for Valid Importance Sampling vs. Token-Level Application

To understand why GRPO fails, one must first revisit the foundational principles of importance sampling. In statistical estimation, importance sampling allows us to approximate the expectation of a function $f$ under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ by drawing samples from a different behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. The mathematical identity governing this relationship is expressed as:

$$ \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh}(z)} \, f(z) \right] $$

Here, the term $\frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ acts as an importance weight that corrects for the discrepancy between the two distributions. Crucially, for this estimator to be unbiased and effective, it relies on averaging over a sufficiently large number of independent samples ($N \gg 1$) drawn from $\pi_\text{beh}$. As the number of samples increases, the law of large numbers ensures that the weighted sum converges to the true expectation under $\pi_\text{tar}$. The validity of this method depends entirely on the ability to aggregate many samples to smooth out stochastic variations in individual weights.

GRPO attempts to apply this logic at the granularity of individual tokens within a generated sequence. In this formulation, for every token $y_{i,t}$ in a response $y_i$, an importance weight is calculated as:

$$ w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} $$

This weight represents the ratio of probabilities assigned by the current policy $\pi_\theta$ versus the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for generating exactly that single token given its history. However, this application violates the core requirement of importance sampling because it treats each token position as if it were an independent sample sufficient for correction. In reality, at any specific time step $t$, we only have **one** sample ($y_{i,t}$) drawn from the conditional distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$. There is no ensemble of tokens at position $t$ to average over; we are attempting to estimate a distributional shift based on a single data point. Consequently, the weight $w_{i,t}$ does not function as a reliable correction factor but rather acts as a source of high-variance noise. Because there is no aggregation over multiple tokens at a specific position to cancel out stochastic fluctuations, these weights fail to perform their intended distribution-correction role, rendering the objective function ill-posed for single-sample token-level updates.

## Analysis of High-Variance Noise Accumulation in Long-Sequences Generation

The failure of token-level importance weights becomes increasingly severe as sequence length grows. In language generation tasks involving complex reasoning or coding, responses can span hundreds or thousands of tokens. When GRPO calculates gradients using token-level weights $w_{i,t}$ multiplied by advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$, it implicitly assumes that these weights accurately reflect how likely a trajectory would be under the new policy relative to the old one. However, since each weight is derived from a single sample without proper averaging, they contain significant stochastic error.

As training progresses through long sequences, these errors do not remain isolated; they compound multiplicatively and additively depending on how gradients are aggregated across tokens. Consider a response consisting of $|y|$ tokens. If each token's probability ratio carries even slight variance due to sampling noise or minor shifts in local context distributions, multiplying these ratios together—or summing their logarithmic contributions—amplifies the total variance exponentially with respect to sequence length. The paper notes that this high-variance training noise progressively accumulates with increased response length. In short responses, this accumulation might be negligible enough for training to appear stable temporarily. However, in long-response tasks typical of advanced reasoning benchmarks like competition-level mathematics or programming code generation, the accumulated noise becomes dominant over any genuine learning signal derived from rewards.

This accumulation creates a feedback loop where unstable gradients cause erratic updates to model parameters $\theta$. These erratic updates further distort future probability distributions $\pi_\theta$, which in turn generates even more volatile importance weights in subsequent steps because the divergence between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ increases unpredictably at every step along with longer contexts eventually leads to what researchers observe as catastrophic model collapse. Once this collapse initiates—a state where performance degrades irreversibly—it cannot be recovered simply by reverting checkpoints or tuning hyperparameters like clipping ranges because the underlying statistical assumption required for stable gradient estimation has been fundamentally violated throughout the entire trajectory generation process.
