Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Foundations of Policy Optimization Algorithms

Autoregressive language models function fundamentally as probabilistic policies, mapping inputs to sequences of outputs. When we speak of training these models via reinforcement learning (RL), we are essentially refining a policy $\pi_\theta$ parameterized by weights $\theta$. In this framework, an input query is denoted as $x$, drawn from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$. The model generates a response $y$, which consists of a sequence of tokens $y_1, y_2, \dots, y_{|y|}$, where $|y|$ represents the total number of tokens in the response. The probability of generating this specific sequence under the current policy is defined as the product of conditional probabilities for each token given the preceding context. Mathematically, this sequence likelihood is expressed as $\pi_\theta (y | x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t} )$. Here, $y_{<t}$ denotes all tokens in the response prior to index $t$. This autoregressive structure implies that the probability of the entire sequence is decomposable into a chain of local token predictions. Each complete query-response pair $(x, y)$ is subsequently evaluated by a verifier or reward model $r$, yielding a scalar reward $r(x, y) \in [ 0, 1 ]$ that quantifies the quality or correctness of the response. Understanding this foundational notation is critical before examining how different algorithms optimize these policies.

## Autoregressive Policies and Likelihood Notation

The core mechanism driving modern large language models is their ability to predict the next token in a sequence based on all previous tokens. This process transforms the model into a stochastic policy $\pi_\theta$ used in reinforcement learning contexts. When analyzing the behavior of such a policy, we must distinguish between the probability assigned to individual tokens and the joint probability assigned to an entire generated sequence. The notation $\pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t})$ refers to the probability mass assigned by the model to a specific token $y_t$ at step $t$, conditioned on the user query $x$ and all previously generated tokens up to step $t-1$. Because language generation is sequential, the likelihood of producing a full response $y$ is not simply an independent sum of token probabilities but a multiplicative accumulation. As defined earlier, this global sequence likelihood is calculated as $\pi_\theta (y | x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t} )$.

This distinction between token-level and sequence-level probabilities becomes increasingly significant as response lengths grow. In short responses, the product involves few terms, keeping values relatively manageable. However, for long-chain reasoning tasks common in advanced RL applications—such as solving competition-level mathematics or writing complex code—the product spans hundreds or thousands of terms. Consequently, small variations in individual token probabilities can compound exponentially when calculating the total sequence likelihood. This compounding effect creates a highly non-linear landscape for optimization algorithms. Any method attempting to adjust policy parameters must account for whether it treats every token independently or views the generation as a holistic event governed by its aggregate likelihood. The choice between these perspectives dictates how importance sampling weights are constructed and how gradients flow back through time during training.

## Review of PPO: Importance Sampling, Clipping, and the Value Model Bottleneck

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) stands as one of the most influential algorithms in deep reinforcement learning and serves as a primary reference point for subsequent developments like GRPO and GSPO. The central philosophy behind PPO is to perform policy updates that stay within a "proximal" region relative to an older version of itself, denoted as $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. This constraint prevents destructive updates where new policies diverge too rapidly from old ones, which could lead to catastrophic forgetting or unstable training dynamics. PPO achieves this through an objective function that incorporates an importance sampling ratio and a clipping mechanism. The standard PPO objective seeks to maximize expected returns while penalizing deviations from the old policy beyond a certain threshold $\varepsilon$. Formally, this objective is written as:

$$
\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} 
\min \left( w_{t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{t},  \mathrm{clip} \left( w_{t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon}\right) \widehat{A}_{t} \right)
\right]
$$

In this equation, several components interact dynamically. The term $w_{t}(\theta)$ represents the importance ratio at token level $t$, defined as $w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t}) }$. This ratio measures how much more likely (or unlikely) the current policy considers generating token $y_t$ compared to what was predicted by the old policy during data collection. The variable $\widehat{A}_{t}$ denotes the advantage estimate for that specific token position, indicating how much better or worse that action was compared to average behavior at that state. Crucially, PPO requires estimating these advantages using an auxiliary value model—a separate neural network trained alongside the policy to approximate expected future rewards from any given state.

The reliance on this value model constitutes a significant bottleneck in scaling PPO for large language models. Firstly, constructing a value model with comparable capacity to the massive policy network doubles memory consumption and computational costs during training batches. Secondly—and perhaps more critically—the efficacy of PPO hinges entirely on the accuracy of these value estimates. Estimating values reliably for long-horizon tasks involving complex reasoning chains is notoriously difficult; errors in value estimation propagate through advantage calculations ($\widehat{A}_{t}$), introducing noise that can destabilize training even if other hyperparameters are tuned perfectly. As models scale in size and task complexity increases toward multi-step reasoning problems requiring extensive rollouts, acquiring precise value estimates becomes increasingly challenging, limiting PPO's practical utility for training giant language models efficiently.
