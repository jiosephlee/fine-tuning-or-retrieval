# Why Your AI Model Might Be Crashing: Solving the Instability of Large-Scale Reinforcement Learning

Training massive language models with Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the gold standard for pushing the boundaries of what AI can do, enabling models to solve competition-level math and complex coding problems. However, as researchers scale these efforts to train gigantic models, they face a terrifying hurdle: **model collapse**. This is a catastrophic failure mode where the model's performance degrades irreversibly, often leaving engineers unable to recover even after reverting to previous checkpoints or tweaking hyperparameters.

Recent research identifies that this instability isn't just bad luck; it stems from a fundamental flaw in how popular algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) handle probability corrections. By shifting the focus from fixing individual words to evaluating entire sentences, a new method called Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) offers a robust solution that stabilizes training and unlocks superior performance.

## The Hidden Flaw in Token-Level Correction

To understand why models crash, we first need to look at how current RL algorithms try to learn from past mistakes. When training a model, we generate responses using an older version of the model (the "behavior" policy) and then optimize a newer version (the "target" policy). Because we are comparing distributions that have changed over time, we rely on **importance sampling** to correct for this mismatch.

Mathematically, importance sampling works by re-weighting samples so that expectations under the target distribution can be estimated using samples from the behavior distribution. The formula looks like this:

$$ \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)} \, f(z) \right] $$

The critical assumption here is that you must average over many samples for this weighting to work correctly. However, GRPO attempts to apply this correction at the **token level**—meaning it calculates an importance weight for every single word in a response independently. Since each word is essentially a single sample from its specific next-token distribution, this approach fails to satisfy the statistical requirements of importance sampling.

Instead of correcting the distribution mismatch, calculating weights token-by-token injects high-variance noise into the training gradients. As the response gets longer, this noise accumulates exponentially. When combined with the clipping mechanisms designed to stabilize training, this accumulated noise eventually triggers a feedback loop that causes the model to collapse completely. Once this happens, the damage is often permanent because the model has learned incorrect patterns that cannot be easily overwritten.

## The Core Principle: Match Units of Reward and Optimization

The root cause of GRPO's instability lies in a mismatch between units. In RL for language models, rewards are assigned to **entire sequences** (e.g., "Did this code compile?" or "Is this math proof correct?"). Yet, GRPO tries to optimize these sequence-level rewards by adjusting probabilities one token at a time using unstable weights.

The solution proposed by GSPO is simple yet profound: **the unit of optimization must match the unit of reward.** If we give credit or blame for an entire answer, we should calculate our importance weights based on the likelihood of that entire answer occurring as a sequence.

By defining the importance ratio based on sequence likelihood rather than individual token likelihoods, GSPO aligns perfectly with how rewards are actually distributed in practice. This shift eliminates the high-variance noise inherent in token-level calculations and ensures that the clipping mechanism operates on meaningful data rather than statistical artifacts.

## How GSPO Stabilizes Training

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) replaces the token-level objective with a sequence-level one. Instead of summing up weighted losses for every token $t$ in a response $y$, GSPO evaluates the response as a whole block $y_i$. The optimization objective becomes:

$$ \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min \left( s_{i}(\theta)  \widehat{A}_{i},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i} 
\right) 
\right] $$

Here, $s_i(\theta)$ represents the sequence-level importance ratio, calculated as:

$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ 
\pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} 
\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$

Notice two key differences here compared to previous methods:
*   **Sequence Clipping:** GSPO clips entire responses if they deviate too far from the old policy's distribution. This prevents outlier sequences from skewing the gradient updates.
*   **Length Normalization:** The exponent $1/|y_i|$ normalizes the ratio by sequence length. Without this step, short sequences might dominate or fluctuate wildly compared to long ones simply due to probability multiplication effects over many tokens.

In practice, this means GSPO treats every token in a successful response equally when calculating gradients once it passes stability checks via clipping at the sequence level. This removes the erratic weighting scheme that plagued GRPO and leads to smooth, continuous learning curves even as models grow larger and responses get longer.

## A Game Changer for Mixture-of-Experts Models

One of the most compelling benefits of GSPO is its ability to handle Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures without special tricks. MoE models are highly efficient because they activate only specific parts ("experts") of their neural network for each input token depending on routing decisions. However, these routing decisions can change drastically between training steps—a phenomenon known as **expert-activation volatility**.

When using GRPO on MoE models, if an expert used by $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ changes in $\pi_\theta$, calculating token-level probabilities becomes meaningless because you are comparing apples to oranges across different sub-networks active at each step. Previously, researchers had to implement complex workarounds like **Routing Replay**, where they forced both policies to use identical routing paths during calculation just to keep numbers comparable. This added significant memory overhead and limited model capacity.

GSPO sidesteps this entirely because it relies on total sequence likelihood rather than individual token probabilities driven by specific expert routes. Even if internal routing shifts slightly between steps, the overall probability of generating a coherent sentence remains relatively stable enough for effective optimization. Consequently, GSPO trains MoE models natively without needing Routing Replay or other stabilization hacks, simplifying infrastructure and allowing models to utilize their full architectural potential freely.

## Simpler Infrastructure and Better Performance beyond Theory

Beyond stability theory, GSPO offers practical advantages for engineering teams building RL pipelines today:
*   **Reduced Precision Sensitivity:** Since GSPO uses aggregate sequence likelihoods rather than precise per-token values computed across thousands of operations sequentially, it tolerates floating-point precision differences between training engines (like Megatron-LM) and inference engines (like vLLM or SGLang). # From Words to Stories: How GSPO Changes the Rules of AI Training

When we train modern large language models (LLMs) to solve complex math problems or write code, we aren't just teaching them to predict the next word; we are teaching them to construct coherent stories and logical arguments. However, for a long time, the underlying algorithms used to teach these models operated on a granular level that often didn't match the nature of the task. They treated every single token—the smallest unit of text—as an independent decision point. This approach worked reasonably well for short tasks, but as models grew larger and began generating longer, more intricate responses, this method started to break down. Enter Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), a new algorithm that shifts the focus from individual words to entire narratives, unlocking unprecedented stability and performance in AI training.

## The Problem with Counting Every Step

To understand why GSPO was necessary, we first need to look at how previous algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) functioned. In traditional reinforcement learning for LLMs, the goal is to adjust the model's parameters so it generates better responses. To do this safely without destabilizing the model, algorithms use a concept called *importance sampling*. This involves comparing how likely a response is under the new policy versus the old policy.

Older methods calculated this probability ratio at every single token position. Imagine you are grading a student's essay. If you were using a token-level approach, you would assign a score and an importance weight to every single letter or punctuation mark independently. If one specific comma happened to be slightly unexpected compared to what the model predicted yesterday, it might receive a massive penalty or reward based on statistical noise. When you multiply these tiny fluctuations together across hundreds or thousands of tokens in a long response, the errors compound rapidly.

The research paper identifies this as a fundamental flaw: applying importance weights at the token level creates high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences. As training progresses, this noise amplifies until it causes "catastrophic model collapse," where the model forgets everything it learned and stops improving entirely. This is particularly dangerous when training massive Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, where different parts of the network activate dynamically for different inputs, adding another layer of instability to token-level calculations.

## Shifting Focus: From Tokens to Sequences

GSPO solves this by changing the unit of analysis from the token to the sequence. Instead of asking "How important was this specific word?", GSPO asks "How important was this entire story?"

Mathematically, while older algorithms computed an importance ratio $w_{t}(\theta)$ for each token $y_t$, GSPO calculates an importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ based on the likelihood of the entire response $y_i$. The formula looks like this:

$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$

Here, $\pi_{\theta} (y_i | x)$ represents the probability of generating that whole sequence under the current policy. By raising this ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$ (where $|y_i|$ is the length of the response), GSPO effectively averages out the fluctuations across all tokens in that sequence. This normalization ensures that no single weirdly placed word can derail the entire training step. It aligns perfectly with how rewards are actually given in real-world applications: we don't reward an AI for getting one comma right; we reward it for solving a math problem correctly or writing bug-free code as a whole package.

## Why Less Clipping Can Mean Better Learning

One might assume that looking at fewer data points—treating an entire sentence as one block rather than twenty separate words—would make learning slower or less precise. Surprisingly, experiments showed the opposite occurred. Because GSPO operates on sequence likelihoods, it requires much stricter clipping thresholds than its predecessors yet achieves higher efficiency.

In GRPO and similar algorithms, researchers had to clip individual tokens aggressively because they were prone to extreme outliers caused by noise paradoxically amplified by long contexts. Even then, many tokens provided unreliable signals for learning. In contrast, GSPO clips entire responses if they deviate too far from expected behavior based on their overall likelihood statistics. While this means discarding a larger percentage of raw data points during training (as measured by clipped tokens), each remaining data point carries a much cleaner, lower-noise signal regarding what constitutes good reasoning versus bad reasoning. Consequently, GSPO trains faster and reaches higher performance benchmarks using less computational effort than GRPO could ever achieve with equivalent resources.

## Stabilizing Mixture-of-Experts Models Without Workarounds

Perhaps most critically for future AI development is how GSPO handles Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures. These models contain specialized sub-networks ("experts") that turn on or off depending on what part of a prompt is being processed. In older systems using GRPO, this dynamic routing created chaos during training; if different experts fired up between generating a sample and evaluating it later in training steps, token-level probabilities would swing wildly and invalidate importance weights completely. To fix this previously required complex engineering hacks like "Routing Replay," which forced old routing patterns onto new computations—a process that added memory overhead and limited model capacity.

GSPO renders these workarounds obsolete because it ignores individual token routing volatility entirely since it cares only about total sequence probability regardless of which internal path generated it internally as long as final output remains consistent with general language capabilities required by human feedback loops themselves! This allows engineers to deploy massive MoE models confidently knowing their RL pipelines won't crash due to internal architectural quirks simply because they're designed around holistic outcomes # Unlocking the Power of Mixture-of-Experts: Why GSPO Makes Complex AI Models Easier to Train

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures have become a cornerstone of modern large language models, allowing systems to scale massive parameter counts while maintaining efficient inference. By activating only a subset of "experts" for any given input, these models achieve remarkable performance. However, this very strength introduces a notorious weakness: training instability. When applying Reinforcement Learning (RL) to these sparse models, the dynamic nature of expert routing often causes training to diverge or collapse entirely. Until recently, solving this required complex engineering hacks like "Routing Replay." Today, we explore how Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) fundamentally resolves these issues by shifting the focus from unstable token-level details to robust sequence-level quality.

## The Hidden Instability in MoE Training

To understand why MoE training is so fragile, we must first look at how standard RL algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) operate. These methods rely heavily on calculating "importance ratios," which measure how likely a specific output was under the current policy versus the old policy used to generate it. In traditional implementations, this calculation happens at every single token level.

The problem arises with MoE models because their internal routing decisions are highly volatile. As the model learns and its parameters update slightly, the specific experts selected to process a token can change dramatically between the time data is generated and when it is updated. In deep MoE models, studies show that roughly 10% of active experts can differ even after a single gradient update for the exact same input sequence.

When an algorithm calculates importance ratios token-by-token ($w_{t}(\theta)$), it assumes that the underlying network structure remains relatively consistent between steps. In an MoE model where the active sub-network changes frequently, this assumption breaks down. The resulting importance weights fluctuate wildly, introducing high-variance noise into the training gradients. Over long sequences, this noise accumulates, often leading to catastrophic model collapse where performance irreversibly degrades regardless of hyperparameter tuning.

## From Tokens to Sequences: The GSPO Solution

The core innovation of GSPO lies in recognizing that rewards in RL are typically assigned to entire responses, not individual words or tokens. Therefore, the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. Instead of trying to stabilize noisy token-level probabilities, GSPO defines importance ratios based on **sequence likelihood**.

Mathematically, while GRPO calculates an importance weight for each token $t$ as:
$$ w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t})} $$
which is prone to instability in MoEs, GSPO calculates a sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
By aggregating probabilities across the entire sequence and applying length normalization, GSPO smooths out the erratic fluctuations caused by changing expert routes. Even if specific experts swap out mid-generation, the overall probability of generating that specific sequence remains relatively stable because the model's general language capabilities do not vanish overnight. This allows GSPO to apply clipping and optimization at the sequence level, effectively filtering out "off-policy" samples without getting derailed by microscopic structural shifts in the network.

## Eliminating "Routing Replay" and Simplifying Infrastructure

Before GSPO, practitioners attempting to train MoE models with RL faced a significant hurdle: they had to implement **Routing Replay**. This strategy involved caching exactly which experts were activated during data generation and forcing those same experts to activate during the subsequent optimization step when calculating token-level weights. While effective at stabilizing GRPO training, Routing Replay introduced substantial complexity and overhead. It required extra memory storage for routing states and added communication costs between training nodes. More critically, it artificially constrained the model's capacity by preventing it from exploring new expert configurations naturally during learning.

GSPO renders Routing Replay obsolete. Because GSPO relies on sequence likelihood rather than token-level consistency within specific expert paths, it is naturally immune to expert-activation volatility. Empirical results confirm that GSPO converges smoothly on MoE architectures without any special routing constraints or replay buffers. This simplification means engineers no longer need to maintain complex synchronization logic between generation and optimization phases. Consequently, RL infrastructure becomes leaner and more scalable; in fact, because GSPO tolerates precision discrepancies better than token-level methods, it opens the door for using inference engines directly for likelihood computation without needing expensive recomputation on training engines—a massive win for distributed training frameworks involving partial rollouts or multi-turn interactions.

## Real-World Impact on Qwen3 Models

The theoretical benefits of GSPO translate directly into tangible performance gains in production environments. The developers behind Qwen3 utilized GSPO as a foundational component for their latest Reinforcement Learning pipeline. By removing the instability inherent in previous algorithms like GRPO when applied to large-scale MoE models, they achieved continuous performance improvements simply by increasing compute resources and extending generation lengths—something previously impossible due to frequent collapses requiring manual intervention or rollback strategies.

Furthermore, experiments showed that despite clipping a significantly larger fraction of tokens compared to GRPO (indicating stricter rejection of poor-quality samples), GSPO achieved higher training efficiency and final benchmark scores on tasks ranging from competitive mathematics (AIME'24) to coding challenges (LiveCodeBench). This counter-intuitive result highlights that discarding noisy gradient signals leads to faster convergence than trying to force-feed unreliable data into an optimizer struggling with high variance.

## Conclusion

The journey toward more intelligent AI systems requires pushing beyond dense models into vast Mixture-of-Expert architectures capable of handling increasingly complex reasoning tasks. However, unlocking this potential has been bottlenecked by training instability caused by dynamic routing behaviors inside these networks. Group Sequence Policy Optimization offers a elegant solution by realigning our mathematical approach with reality: treating sequences as atomic units rather than collections of fragile tokens has eliminated the need for brittle workarounds like Routing Replay and simplified RL infrastructure significantly as demonstrated by Qwen3's success story moving forward with confidence knowing that future scaling efforts won't be hampered by internal architectural volatility but empowered by robust optimization principles designed specifically for modern LLM realities



# Less Clipping, More Gains: How GSPO Flips the Script on Reinforcement Learning

It sounds almost paradoxical in the world of machine learning: to train a model faster and more effectively, you sometimes need to throw away more data. For years, the prevailing wisdom in Reinforcement Learning (RL) for Large Language Models (LLMs) suggested that aggressive clipping—discarding data points that deviate too far from the expected policy—was a necessary evil that inevitably slowed down convergence. However, a new algorithm called Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is turning this intuition on its head. By shifting the focus from individual tokens to entire sequences, GSPO demonstrates that filtering out a massive amount of "noisy" data can actually accelerate learning and stabilize training in ways previously thought impossible.

## The Problem with Token-Level Noise

To understand why GSPO is revolutionary, we first need to look at its predecessor, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO was designed to eliminate the need for a separate value model by comparing groups of responses to the same query. It calculates an advantage score for each response and applies it uniformly across every token in that response. However, GRPO relies on **token-level importance ratios**. This means it tries to correct for differences between the old policy and the new policy one word at a time.

The paper identifies a critical flaw in this approach: applying importance sampling weights at the token level creates high-variance noise. When generating long sequences, these small errors accumulate exponentially. As training progresses, this accumulated noise causes unstable gradients, which can eventually lead to "catastrophic model collapse," where the model irreversibly loses its capabilities. This instability is particularly severe in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, where the dynamic routing of experts causes token probabilities to fluctuate wildly between training steps. To combat this with GRPO, engineers had to implement complex workarounds like "Routing Replay," which caches expert activations to artificially stabilize calculations—a solution that adds significant memory overhead and complexity.

## The GSPO Solution: Think Sequences, Not Tokens

GSPO addresses these fundamental instabilities by adhering strictly to the principles of importance sampling: if you want to estimate an expectation under a target distribution using samples from a behavior distribution, you must weigh by the ratio of their densities. In language modeling, since rewards are assigned to entire responses (sequences), the importance ratio should also be calculated at the **sequence level**, not the token level.

Instead of calculating a separate weight for every single word $y_t$, GSPO computes a single importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ for the whole response $y_i$:

$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$

Here, $\pi_{\theta}(y|x)$ represents the likelihood of generating the entire sequence under the current policy. By raising this ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$ (length normalization), GSPO ensures that longer sequences don't dominate simply because they have more tokens, keeping values within a manageable numerical range. Crucially, this approach treats all tokens within a response equally regarding their contribution to the gradient update once weighted by this global sequence factor. This eliminates the erratic fluctuations caused by individual token mismatches in MoE architectures.

## The Counter-Intuitive Power of Aggressive Clipping

The most striking discovery in evaluating GSPO lies in how it handles outliers through clipping. In traditional algorithms like GRPO or Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), clipping acts as a safety valve; if an importance ratio exceeds a certain threshold (e.g., $1 + \epsilon$), it is capped to prevent drastic updates. Because these algorithms operate on thousands of individual tokens per batch, they tend to clip only those specific tokens that are extreme outliers while retaining others from potentially flawed responses.

GSPO takes a different stance: it clips based on whether an *entire sequence* is too far off-policy. If a generated response has an importance ratio outside the safe range ($1 - \epsilon$ to $1 + \epsilon$), **every token** in that response is discarded from the gradient calculation for that step. Empirical results show that GSPO clips significantly more data than GRPO—often by two orders of magnitude in terms of total clipped tokens per batch.

One might assume discarding such vast amounts of data would cripple learning efficiency due to poor sample utilization. Yet, experiments reveal the opposite: **GSPO trains faster and reaches higher performance ceilings.** Why? Because GRPO's retained tokens often carry high-variance noise that misleads the optimizer. By aggressively filtering out entire low-quality or off-distribution sequences before they ever influence the gradient update, GSPO provides a cleaner, more reliable learning signal. It trades raw quantity for high quality, allowing the model to converge more rapidly toward optimal policies without getting derailed by statistical noise.

## Stabilizing Mixture-of-Experts and Scaling Up

The benefits of GSPO extend beyond just speed; they fundamentally solve stability issues inherent in modern large-scale architectures like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE). In MoE models, different parts of the network activate depending on input content. Under GRPO's token-level logic, slight shifts in expert activation between training steps cause massive swings in probability ratios for individual tokens, breaking training stability unless mitigated by complex strategies like Routing Replay.

Because GSPO operates on sequence likelihoods rather than individual token probabilities, it is remarkably robust against these internal architectural fluctuations. The overall probability of generating a coherent sentence remains relatively stable even if specific expert paths shift slightly during training updates. Consequently, GSPO renders techniques like Routing Replay obsolete for MoE models entirely. This not only simplifies RL infrastructure but also removes artificial constraints on model capacity, allowing MoE models to utilize their full potential without needing special engineering hacks just to stay stable during training cycles.

Furthermore, this sequence-level approach simplifies deployment pipelines involving separate training and inference engines (like Megatron paired with vLLM or SGLang). Traditional methods often required re-computing token-level likelihoods using precise training-engine arithmetic because inference engines might introduce floating-point discrepancies at every step. Since GSPO relies on aggregate sequence likelihoods—which are far more tolerant of minor precision variations—it allows practitioners to directly use likelihoods computed by fast inference engines for optimization updates without costly recomputation steps on slower training hardware.

## From Theory to Qwen3 Success

The theoretical elegance of GSPO has translated into tangible real-world results within Alibaba's Qwen3 series models trained with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Benchmarks including AIME'24 for mathematics and LiveCodeBench/CodeForces for programming demonstrate that models trained with GSPO achieve superior pass rates compared to those trained with GRPO under identical compute budgets and query sets. Most importantly, these gains were achieved while maintaining stable training dynamics over extended periods and longer generation lengths—conditions where other algorithms frequently fail or collapse completely.

By recognizing that rewards belong at the sequence level and designing optimization objectives accordingly, researchers have unlocked a new frontier in RL stability and efficiency less clipping isn't just acceptable; it's essential when done correctly at scale." As we continue pushing towards larger models and more complex reasoning tasks, algorithms like GSPO provide the robust foundation needed to harness reinforcement learning safely and effectively at unprecedented scales."
