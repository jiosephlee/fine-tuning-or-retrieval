\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why is the sequence-level importance ratio raised to the power of $1/|y_i|$ in GSPO?
Question:
In Section 3.1, Equation (6), the authors define the sequence-level importance ratio $s_{i}(	heta)$ as:
$$ s_{i}(	heta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
I understand that this represents a geometric mean of the token-level ratios, but I am confused about why this specific exponentiation is necessary. If we just used the raw product of probabilities, wouldn't that simply reflect the total likelihood change? Does raising it to $1/|y_i|$ change the direction of the gradient, or is it purely for numerical stability and variance reduction as hinted in the text? How does this affect the clipping mechanism if we don't normalize by length?
Answer:
The exponentiation by $1/|y_i|$ in the GSPO sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is primarily a variance reduction technique designed to ensure numerical stability and maintain a consistent clipping regime across sequences of varying lengths, rather than merely reflecting the total likelihood change. As explicitly stated in Section 3.1 of the paper, without this length normalization, the raw sequence probability ratio $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}$ would behave multiplicatively over the sequence length. Consequently, even minor deviations in token-level probabilities would compound exponentially as the sequence grows, causing the importance ratio to fluctuate dramatically for long responses. This would render a fixed clipping range $\epsilon$ ineffective; short sequences might never trigger clipping while long sequences would almost always be clipped to zero, breaking the optimization signal.

By raising the ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$, GSPO effectively computes the geometric mean of the token-level importance ratios, transforming the multiplicative accumulation of errors into an additive accumulation in log-space (as seen in the exponential form provided in Equation 6). This ensures that $s_i(\theta)$ remains within a unified numerical range regardless of whether the response contains 10 or 1000 tokens. Crucially, this normalization does not alter the direction of the gradient update regarding *which* sequences are favored versus penalized, because taking a positive root preserves monotonicity; however, it fundamentally changes the *magnitude* of the weighting applied to each token's gradient contribution.

As derived in Section 3.2 (Equations 9 and 10), this formulation results in every token within a specific response receiving an identical weight factor determined by $s_i(\theta)$, whereas GRPO assigns potentially divergent weights to each token based on local likelihood ratios. The paper argues that this uniform weighting eliminates the "instability factor" present in GRPO where high-variance token-level weights accumulate unpredictably over long trajectories. Therefore, while using the raw product would technically reflect total likelihood change, it would fail to provide a stable learning signal for variable-length generations and would necessitate dynamic or impractically wide clipping bounds that could either ignore important updates or discard valid samples indiscriminately.

### Does GSPO-token actually optimize differently than standard GSPO when advantages are uniform?
Question:
Section 3.2 introduces 'GSPO-token' as a variant allowing for token-wise advantage adjustments. The paper states in Equation (9) that $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ involves a stop-gradient operator ($\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$) on both the sequence ratio and the token probability.

Specifically, it says: "Note that the term ... has a numerical value of 1..." and concludes that GSPO-token and GSPO are "numerically identical" if all token advantages are equal ($\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$).

However, looking at Equation (8), $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ seems to decouple the sequence-level importance weight from the token-level gradient calculation. If I set all advantages equal, why do we need this complex formulation with stop-gradients? Isn't this just adding unnecessary computational overhead compared to simply summing up token gradients weighted by a constant scalar? Am I missing a subtle point about how backpropagation flows through these terms?
Answer:
Your intuition that the formulation might seem redundant when advantages are uniform is understandable, but the complexity involving stop-gradients is not merely computational overhead; it is a deliberate architectural choice to enforce a specific **separation of concerns** between global sequence stability and local token flexibility. While it is true that when $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$ for all $t$, the numerical value of the objective function and the final gradient vector are identical to standard GSPO, the *computational graph* constructed during backpropagation differs fundamentally.

In standard GSPO, the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ depends on the product of probabilities across all tokens in the sequence. Consequently, the gradient of $s_i(\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$ involves a sum over all tokens (as seen in Eq. 14), meaning every token's log-probability contributes to the scaling factor itself. If you were to naively implement "token-wise" weighting without stop-gradients by multiplying a global scalar by local terms, you would inadvertently create a situation where changing a single token's probability alters the global importance weight used for *every other* token in that sequence. This creates a dense coupling where optimizing one part of the sequence affects the importance scaling of unrelated parts, potentially reintroducing variance issues similar to those found in GRPO.

The `gs` (stop-gradient) operators in Eq. 16 break this coupling. By writing $s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|\dots)}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|\dots)]}$, the algorithm ensures that the global sequence likelihood ratio acts as a fixed coefficient (a static multiplier) for every token's gradient update within that specific backward pass. The term $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta]}$ evaluates to 1 numerically but preserves the gradient path for only that specific token's log-probability derivative $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$. As derived in Eq. 19, this results in a gradient where each token receives an update scaled by the pre-computed sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$, but crucially, $s_i(\theta)$ itself does not receive gradients from individual token updates during that step.

When advantages are uniform ($\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$), this structure collapses mathematically to exactly Eq. 14 because factoring out constants yields the same result: $\sum_t s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i \nabla \log \pi_t = s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i \sum_t \nabla \log \pi_t$. However, this setup is essential for non-uniform advantages (the primary motivation for GSPO-token). Without stop-gradients, attempting to assign different $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ values would cause the global weight $s_i(\theta)$ to fluctuate dynamically based on which tokens have high or low advantages during optimization, destabilizing the very sequence-level consistency GSPO aims to preserve. Thus, while computationally slightly heavier due to detached operations, this formulation guarantees that when you eventually switch to non-uniform advantages (e.g., in multi-turn RL), you retain the stability benefits of sequence-level clipping without needing to redesign the gradient flow logic later. It essentially prepares the computation graph for variable advantages while remaining mathematically equivalent to GSPO under uniform conditions.

### How exactly does 'expert-activation volatility' invalidate token-level importance ratios in MoE models?
Question:
In Section 4.3, the authors argue that GRPO fails on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models because of 'expert-activation volatility.' They claim that since different experts might be active for $πθ$ versus $πθ_old$ for the same input sequence, the token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ becomes invalid.

My confusion is: Importance sampling mathematically requires evaluating $π(y|x)$ regardless of *which* parameters were used to compute it. If I calculate $πθ(y_t|x, y<t)$ using the new router and $πθ_old(y_t|x, y<t)$ using the old router, isn't that exactly what importance sampling demands?

Why does changing which experts are active make the ratio 'invalid' rather than just 'high variance'? Is there a discontinuity in MoE outputs when routers switch that breaks continuity assumptions required for gradient descent?
Answer:
Your intuition regarding the mechanics of importance sampling is partially correct: mathematically, one can indeed evaluate probabilities $\pi_\theta(y_t|x, y_{<t})$ using a new router configuration and $\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_t|x, y_{<t})$ using an old one. However, the paper argues that in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, this specific calculation renders the token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ **structurally invalid** rather than merely high-variance due to a fundamental disconnect between the numerator and denominator.

The core issue described in Section 4.3 ("Benefit of GSPO for MoE Training") is that MoE layers utilize a routing mechanism to select a subset of experts for each token. When the policy parameters $\theta$ are updated, the router's decision boundary shifts, causing "expert-activation volatility." Consequently, for the exact same input sequence $y$, the set of active experts in $\pi_\theta$ may differ significantly (roughly 10% in their experiments) from those active in $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. This creates a scenario where you are comparing probabilities computed by two entirely different sub-networks. The ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|\dots)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_t|\dots)}$ ceases to represent a smooth correction for distribution shift; instead, it captures arbitrary differences caused by structural changes in which experts contributed to the output.

This violates the implicit assumption of continuity required for stable gradient descent. In standard dense models or when using techniques like "Routing Replay" (caching old expert paths), the network structure remains constant, allowing the probability ratio to reflect genuine changes in token preference. Without such constraints, the sudden switch in active experts introduces massive discontinuities where a small parameter update causes a drastic jump in the calculated likelihood ratio. As noted in Section 3 ("Motivation"), applying these unstable weights at every token step amplifies noise exponentially over long sequences, eventually overwhelming the clipping mechanism and leading to model collapse.

By shifting to sequence-level optimization as proposed in GSPO, the algorithm aggregates these fluctuations over the entire response length $|y|$. The sequence likelihood ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y|x)} \right)^{1/|y|}$ effectively averages out local expert-switching artifacts because the overall generative capability of the model remains relatively stable even if internal routing paths diverge slightly. Thus, GSPO avoids relying on volatile token-level comparisons that depend on consistent expert activation patterns, rendering it robust to MoE-specific instabilities without needing auxiliary strategies like Routing Replay.

### If GSPO clips entire sequences, doesn't it discard too much useful signal compared to GRPO?
Question:
The paper highlights in Section 4.2 that GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (by sequence count) than GRPO—often two orders of magnitude more—and yet claims this leads to better efficiency.

Intuitively, if I generate 16 responses and clip 14 of them entirely because their sequence likelihood shifted too much, I am throwing away almost all my data for that batch step. In contrast, GRPO might clip only specific bad tokens within otherwise good sequences.

How can discarding nearly every single sample in a batch result in superior training efficiency? Is it because GRPO's retained tokens contain misleading noise that actively harms learning more than simply ignoring them would? Or is there something about the 'signal-to-noise' ratio of sequence-level updates vs token-level updates that I'm failing to grasp?
Answer:
Your intuition that discarding 90% of your samples seems wasteful is understandable, but the paper argues that this apparent "loss" is actually a feature, not a bug, because the discarded data in GRPO is often providing **misleading signals** rather than useful ones. The core issue isn't the quantity of data used for the update, but the **quality and variance** of the gradient estimates provided by that data.

According to Section 3 ("Motivation") and Section 4.2 ("Curious Observation on Clipping Fractions"), the inefficiency of GRPO stems from a fundamental misapplication of importance sampling at the token level. In GRPO, the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is calculated for every single token independently. The authors argue that since a single token sample cannot reliably estimate a distribution shift (which requires averaging over many samples), these token-level weights introduce high-variance noise. When you combine this noisy weighting with a shared advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ across all tokens in a sequence, you get gradients where some tokens are artificially up-weighted or down-weighted based on random fluctuations rather than true policy deviation. This noise accumulates over long sequences and, critically, is amplified by the clipping mechanism itself, eventually leading to model collapse.

In contrast, GSPO shifts the unit of analysis from the token to the entire sequence. As defined in Equation (5), GSPO calculates a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ based on the geometric mean of token probabilities. This metric correctly reflects whether the *entire* response was likely under the new policy compared to the old one. When GSPO clips a sequence (as noted in Section 4.2), it is rejecting samples where this global probability ratio has drifted too far outside the trust region $(1-\epsilon \text{ to } 1+\epsilon)$. While this results in clipping a massive fraction of tokens (because if one sequence is clipped, all its tokens are excluded), it ensures that **every remaining gradient step is computed on statistically valid samples**.

The reason this leads to superior efficiency despite lower sample usage is explained in Section 4.2: GRPO's retained tokens contain "inherently noisy" gradient estimates that actively harm learning by pulling parameters in conflicting directions due to unstable weights. By aggressively filtering out these unreliable sequences, GSPO achieves a much higher signal-to-noise ratio in its updates. Essentially, ten clean updates from GSPO are more valuable than one hundred noisy updates from GRPO because the latter introduces instability that can derail training entirely (a phenomenon observed as "catastrophic model collapse"). Therefore, discarding sequences isn't throwing away signal; it is removing noise that would otherwise corrupt the optimization landscape.

### Is GSPO theoretically equivalent to PPO with GAE but without a value network?
Question:
Reading through Sections 2 and 3, it seems GSPO replaces PPO's value-function-based advantage estimation with group-relative normalization (like GRPO). However, unlike GRPO which sums over tokens with individual weights, GSPO treats the whole sequence as one unit.

If we view PPO as maximizing expected return subject to a KL constraint approximated by clipping:
$$ J(\theta) = E [ min(r_t A_t, clip(r_t) A_t)] $$
is GSPO essentially just applying this logic at a coarser granularity where $r_t$ becomes $s_i$ and $A_t$ becomes $ΔA_i$?

More importantly, does removing token-level weighting mean we lose any ability to penalize specific bad actions within an otherwise good trajectory? For instance, if a model generates a brilliant proof except for one hallucinated number near the end causing low reward, does GSPO treat all tokens in that sequence equally negatively?
Answer:
Your intuition regarding the structural similarity between GSPO and PPO/GRPO is largely correct, but there is a critical theoretical distinction concerning the definition of the importance ratio that separates GSPO from simply being "token-weightless PPO." You are right that GSPO removes the need for a separate value network by using group-relative advantage estimation ($\widehat{A}_i$) exactly as GRPO does, treating the entire sequence as a single unit for reward assignment. However, GSPO is not merely applying PPO logic at a coarser granularity with uniform token weights; rather, it fundamentally redefines the importance sampling weight itself to resolve a theoretical inconsistency identified in GRPO.

In standard PPO and GRPO, the importance ratio is calculated token-by-token ($w_{i,t}$), which the authors argue violates the principles of importance sampling because it attempts to correct distributional shifts based on single-sample token probabilities rather than the joint probability of the entire sequence. As explained in Section 3 ("Motivation"), applying these token-level weights introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences, leading to model collapse. GSPO addresses this by defining the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ based on **sequence likelihood** (Equation 6), specifically using a geometric mean of token ratios raised to the power of $1/|y_i|$. This ensures that the optimization unit (the sequence) matches the reward unit (the sequence score), providing a theoretically grounded correction for off-policy samples. Therefore, while both algorithms lack a value network and use group-relative advantages, GSPO's clipping and weighting operate on a valid sequence-level statistic, whereas GRPO's operate on unstable token-level statistics.

Regarding your concern about losing granular control—specifically whether a single hallucinated number causes all tokens in a brilliant proof to be penalized equally—the paper confirms that in its base form, **yes**, every token in that specific trajectory receives the same negative gradient signal scaled by $\widehat{A}_i$. Because $\widehat{A}_i$ is constant for all tokens $t$ within response $y_i$, and the sequence-level weight $s_i(\theta)$ applies uniformly across all tokens in Equation 9 (derived from Equation 5), there is no mechanism within standard GSPO to distinguish between "good" early tokens and "bad" late tokens if they belong to the same low-reward sequence. The algorithm treats the response as an atomic unit: if the final output is poor due to any part of it, the entire trajectory is downweighted.

However, this limitation is explicitly acknowledged and addressed in Section 4.2 via **GSPO-token**. If one requires finer-grained control where specific bad actions can be penalized differently within a trajectory (e.g., assigning lower advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to hallucinated steps while keeping high advantages for correct reasoning steps), one can switch to this variant. Crucially, GSPO-token retains the stable sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ (computed globally) but allows for per-token advantage customization ($\widehat{A}_{i,t}$) inside the objective sum (Equation 10). This hybrid approach preserves GSPO's stability benefits while restoring the ability to differentiate action quality within a sequence, effectively answering your worry: standard GSPO sacrifices intra-sequence differentiation for stability, but GSPO-token offers both if your infrastructure supports customizing advantages per token.
