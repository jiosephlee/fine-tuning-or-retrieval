# Smarter, Lighter Reinforcement Learning: Introducing Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a critical tool for unlocking the latent reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly in complex domains like mathematics. However, traditional RL methods often come with a prohibitive cost: they require training a massive secondary model, known as a "critic" or value function, alongside the main policy model. This dual-model architecture doubles the memory footprint and computational overhead, making high-quality RL fine-tuning inaccessible for many researchers and organizations. In our recent work on **DeepSeekMath**, we tackled this bottleneck head-on by introducing **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, an algorithm that eliminates the need for a separate critic model entirely while delivering state-of-the-art performance.

## The Bottleneck of Traditional RL

To understand why GRPO matters, we must first look at how standard RL algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) operate. In PPO, the goal is to maximize a reward signal by adjusting the policy model's parameters. To do this stably, PPO relies on an advantage function ($A_t$), which estimates how much better a specific action is compared to the average expectation. Calculating this advantage traditionally requires a learned value function ($V_\psi$)—essentially another neural network of similar size to the policy itself—that predicts the expected future reward for every token generated.

This setup creates significant engineering hurdles. Training two large models simultaneously consumes double the GPU memory and compute resources. Furthermore, in the context of LLMs where rewards are often sparse (assigned only at the very end of a generation), training a value function that accurately estimates token-level advantages becomes notoriously difficult and unstable. We asked ourselves: *Is there a way to estimate this baseline without training an extra model?*

## How GRPO Changes the Game

The core insight behind GRPO is simplicity rooted in comparison rather than absolute prediction. Instead of relying on an external critic to tell us what the "average" reward should be, GRPO generates a group of multiple outputs for the same input question and uses them to establish their own baseline.

Here is how it works conceptually:
1.  For a given question $q$, we sample a group of $G$ distinct outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \dots, o_G\}$ from our current policy model.
2.  We pass these outputs through a reward model to get individual scores $\{r_1, r_2, \dots, r_G\}$.
3.  Rather than comparing each output to an external value network, we compare each output against the average performance of its peers in that specific group.

By normalizing these rewards—subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation—we derive an advantage estimate $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for every token in every output within that group. If an answer performs better than its siblings in the same batch, it receives positive reinforcement; if it performs worse, it is penalized relative to the group norm. This approach completely obviates the need for a separate value function $V_\psi$, slashing memory requirements while maintaining training stability.

## A Unified View of Alignment Methods

Beyond just saving resources, GRPO helps clarify the landscape of modern alignment techniques. By analyzing various methods—including Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and PPO—through a unified mathematical lens, we found they all share a common structure involving three components: Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm logic that converts rewards into gradient updates.

GRPO fits neatly into this paradigm as an online RL technique that leverages relative scoring within groups. Unlike Offline RFT or DPO which rely on static datasets generated by older models, GRPO continuously samples fresh data from the evolving policy ("online"). More importantly, unlike Online RFT which treats all correct answers equally and ignores incorrect ones beyond rejection, GRPO utilizes the full spectrum of reward values within a group to apply nuanced gradients. This means it doesn't just learn "what is right," but learns "how much better one right answer is compared to another."

## Impact on Mathematical Reasoning

The practical application of GRPO was central to building **DeepSeekMath**. Starting from our instruction-tuned DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B model—which already performed competitively against much larger open-source models—we applied GRPO using only chain-of-thought data from GSM8K and MATH benchmarks. Despite this narrow training scope focused on just two datasets in English, the results were striking:

*   **Performance Leap:** The final DeepSeekMath-RL model achieved **51.7%** accuracy on the competition-level MATH benchmark without using any external tools or voting ensembles at test time. This surpassed all other open-source models ranging from 7B to 70B parameters and approached the performance of proprietary giants like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra at that time.
*   **Generalization:** Remarkably, despite being trained only on English math problems via RL, the model showed significant improvements on out-of-domain tasks as well, such as Chinese math benchmarks (CMATH), jumping from 84.6% to 88.8%.
*   **Efficiency:** By removing the critic model, we reduced memory usage significantly compared to standard PPO implementations, allowing us to scale up sampling strategies effectively without hitting hardware limits immediately encountered with traditional methods.

Our analysis revealed that this boost wasn't necessarily due to teaching the model fundamentally new arithmetic facts it didn't know before (as measured by Pass@1 metrics staying relatively stable). Instead, RL with GRPO made the model's output distribution more robust and consistent (improving Max@K metrics). It helped align the model's internal preferences so that when generating multiple solutions for hard problems, it was far more likely to land on a correct path consistently across attempts.

## Looking Ahead

The success of DeepSeekMath demonstrates that high-quality mathematical reasoning does not strictly require massive parameter counts or prohibitively expensive training setups if data selection and optimization algorithms are handled correctly. The introduction of Group Relative Policy Optimization proves that simpler mechanisms can sometimes outperform complex architectures by leveraging relative comparisons inherent in grouped generations rather than absolute predictions from auxiliary networks.

While challenges remain—particularly in geometry reasoning where data biases persist—the path forward involves refining these RL paradigms further. Future work will focus on exploring advanced sampling strategies like tree search during generation and developing reward models that are robust against noise and uncertainty in complex reasoning chains。For now, GRPO stands as a testament to how rethinking fundamental algorithmic assumptions can push open-source models closer to—and sometimes beyond—the frontiers of AI capability with significantly fewer resources.cw
