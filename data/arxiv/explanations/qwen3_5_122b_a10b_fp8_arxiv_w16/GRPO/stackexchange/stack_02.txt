\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Does mixing code and math tokens cause catastrophic forgetting in small models?
Question:
In the 'Lessons Learnt in Pre-Training' section, the authors discuss training strategies involving code and math data. They mention:

1. Two-stage training (Code $	o$ Math) improves reasoning but degrades coding performance.
2. One-stage training (mixed Code + Math) mitigates catastrophic forgetting and synergizes both skills.

However, they also note: 'combining code tokens and math tokens for one-stage training compromises mathematical reasoning without tool use.'

This seems contradictory. If mixing prevents forgetting of coding skills, why does it compromise pure math reasoning (without tools)? Is this specific to the 1.3B model size mentioned in that experiment ('due to its limited scale')? I want to clarify if this trade-off is inherent to mixed-data training or just a limitation of small parameter counts.
Answer:
The apparent contradiction you observed stems from the distinction between **catastrophic forgetting** (losing previously acquired skills) and **capacity saturation** (the inability to learn new skills alongside existing ones due to limited model size). According to the "Lessons Learnt in Pre-Training" section of the DeepSeekMath paper, mixing code and math tokens in a one-stage training setup indeed prevents the catastrophic forgetting of coding skills that occurs in a two-stage approach (Code $ \to $ Math), where the model essentially overwrites its coding knowledge to focus on math. This confirms that interleaving data preserves the representation space necessary for coding.

However, the paper explicitly states that this mixed strategy "compromises mathematical reasoning without tool use" specifically because of the **limited scale** of the 1.3B model used in those experiments. The authors hypothesize that a model of this size lacks the sufficient parameter capacity to fully assimilate and internalize the distinct logical structures required for both rigorous mathematical reasoning and code generation simultaneously within a single training pass. In essence, while mixing prevents the *erasure* of coding weights, it forces the small model to split its finite representational capacity between two demanding domains, resulting in a "jack of all trades, master of none" scenario for pure math reasoning.

Crucially, this trade-off is presented as an artifact of scaling rather than an inherent flaw in mixed-data training itself. The paper's primary success story involves DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, which is initialized with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B and trained on a massive mixture of data (56% math corpus, 20% GitHub code, plus others). Unlike the 1.3B experiment where mixing hurt pure math performance, the 7B model successfully maintains high coding proficiency (matching its predecessor on HumanEval and MBPP) while achieving state-of-the-art mathematical reasoning scores on benchmarks like MATH and GSM8K without tool use. This demonstrates that once the parameter count crosses a certain threshold, the model gains enough capacity to synergize both skills without compromising either, validating that the compromise seen in the 1.3B experiments was strictly a limitation of model scale rather than a fundamental incompatibility between code and math tokens.
