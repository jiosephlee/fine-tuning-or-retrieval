\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Confused about the 'baseline' in GRPO vs. PPO: How does averaging group scores replace the critic model entirely?
Question:
I am reading the section on Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). The paper states that GRPO 'foregoes the critic model' and estimates the baseline from group scores. In standard PPO, the advantage $A_t$ is calculated using a learned value function $V_{	heta}$ (the critic) via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE).

In GRPO, Equation 3 defines the objective using $	ilde{A}_{i,t}$, which is derived from normalizing rewards within a group of $G$ outputs for the same question. My confusion is: If we remove the critic, aren't we losing the ability to estimate the expected return accurately for every single token step? How does simply taking $(r_i - 	ext{mean}(	ext{group})) / 	ext{std}(	ext{group})$ serve as a valid replacement for a learned value function that predicts future returns? Does this imply GRPO assumes all tokens in an output have equal value once normalized, unlike GAE?
Answer:
Your intuition touches on a fundamental distinction between standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and the specific design choices made in Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) as presented in the DeepSeekMath paper. You are correct that GRPO replaces the learned value function $V_\psi$ (critic) with a statistical baseline derived from a group of outputs, but this does not imply that all tokens are assumed to have equal value in a vacuum; rather, it shifts the definition of "advantage" from an absolute prediction of future return to a **relative ranking** within a specific context.

In standard PPO, Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) computes $A_t = \sum_{l=0}^\infty (\gamma \lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}$, where $\delta$ relies on the temporal difference error involving the learned critic $V(s_t)$. This critic attempts to predict the *expected* cumulative reward from state $t$ onwards. In contrast, GRPO operates under the assumption that for a fixed question $q$, the optimal strategy is to maximize the likelihood of generating outputs that yield higher rewards relative to other outputs generated for that same question. Instead of asking "What is the expected return of this token sequence?", GRPO asks "Is this output better or worse than its peers?"

The paper explicitly defines the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ in Equation 3 and the surrounding text by normalizing rewards within a group of $G$ sampled outputs $\{o_1, \dots, o_G\}$ for the same prompt. Specifically, under outcome supervision (Section 3.1.2), the advantage for every token $t$ in output $o_i$ is set to $\tilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$, where $r_i$ is the scalar reward assigned to the entire output. While this assigns a uniform scalar advantage across all tokens within a single trajectory $o_i$, it creates a highly discriminative signal *across* trajectories: if one output is mathematically correct ($r_i > 0$ after normalization), every token contributing to it receives positive reinforcement; conversely, if an output fails ($r_i < 0$), every token is penalized. This effectively treats the entire generation path as an atomic unit for credit assignment relative to others, bypassing the need for a critic to estimate intermediate values which can be notoriously difficult to train accurately when rewards are sparse (only provided at the end).

Regarding your concern about losing accuracy for individual token steps: GRPO addresses this by leveraging **process supervision** when necessary (Section 3.1.3). In this mode, instead of assigning one global reward per output, a process reward model scores each reasoning step. The advantage for token $t$ becomes $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{j: \text{index}(j) \ge t} \tilde{r}_i^{\text{index}(j)}$, which sums normalized rewards from all future steps in that specific trajectory. Here, earlier tokens receive advantages proportional to the quality of subsequent steps they led toward, reintroducing step-level granularity without requiring a separate value network. The core insight from the paper is that by grouping samples and using relative normalization, GRPO reduces variance and eliminates the memory overhead of training a critic model (which must match or exceed policy size), while still providing sufficient gradient signal because reward models are inherently trained on pairwise comparisons of outputs for identical questions. Thus, GRPO does not assume tokens have equal intrinsic value; it assumes that for optimization purposes within a group comparison framework, attributing success or failure uniformly across a complete trace (or accumulating step-wise traces) is more stable and computationally efficient than maintaining an independent estimator for expected future returns at every step.

### Does mixing code and math tokens cause catastrophic forgetting in small models?
Question:
In the 'Lessons Learnt in Pre-Training' section, the authors discuss training strategies involving code and math data. They mention:

1. Two-stage training (Code $	o$ Math) improves reasoning but degrades coding performance.
2. One-stage training (mixed Code + Math) mitigates catastrophic forgetting and synergizes both skills.

However, they also note: 'combining code tokens and math tokens for one-stage training compromises mathematical reasoning without tool use.'

This seems contradictory. If mixing prevents forgetting of coding skills, why does it compromise pure math reasoning (without tools)? Is this specific to the 1.3B model size mentioned in that experiment ('due to its limited scale')? I want to clarify if this trade-off is inherent to mixed-data training or just a limitation of small parameter counts.
Answer:
The apparent contradiction you observed stems from the distinction between **catastrophic forgetting** (losing previously acquired skills) and **capacity saturation** (the inability to learn new skills alongside existing ones due to limited model size). According to the "Lessons Learnt in Pre-Training" section of the DeepSeekMath paper, mixing code and math tokens in a one-stage training setup indeed prevents the catastrophic forgetting of coding skills that occurs in a two-stage approach (Code $ \to $ Math), where the model essentially overwrites its coding knowledge to focus on math. This confirms that interleaving data preserves the representation space necessary for coding.

However, the paper explicitly states that this mixed strategy "compromises mathematical reasoning without tool use" specifically because of the **limited scale** of the 1.3B model used in those experiments. The authors hypothesize that a model of this size lacks the sufficient parameter capacity to fully assimilate and internalize the distinct logical structures required for both rigorous mathematical reasoning and code generation simultaneously within a single training pass. In essence, while mixing prevents the *erasure* of coding weights, it forces the small model to split its finite representational capacity between two demanding domains, resulting in a "jack of all trades, master of none" scenario for pure math reasoning.

Crucially, this trade-off is presented as an artifact of scaling rather than an inherent flaw in mixed-data training itself. The paper's primary success story involves DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, which is initialized with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B and trained on a massive mixture of data (56% math corpus, 20% GitHub code, plus others). Unlike the 1.3B experiment where mixing hurt pure math performance, the 7B model successfully maintains high coding proficiency (matching its predecessor on HumanEval and MBPP) while achieving state-of-the-art mathematical reasoning scores on benchmarks like MATH and GSM8K without tool use. This demonstrates that once the parameter count crosses a certain threshold, the model gains enough capacity to synergize both skills without compromising either, validating that the compromise seen in the 1.3B experiments was strictly a limitation of model scale rather than a fundamental incompatibility between code and math tokens.

### Clarification on the KL divergence estimator used in GRPO's objective function
Question:
Looking at Equation 4 in the paper, DeepSeekMath proposes an unbiased estimator for the KL divergence $	ext{D}_{KL}[ar{	heta} || 	heta_{ref}]$: $$\frac{\pi_{ref}(o)}{\pi_{\theta}(o)} - \log\frac{\pi_{ref}(o)}{\pi_{\theta}(o)} - 1$$

Usually, KL divergence is defined as $ar{	heta}\log(\frac{\bar{	heta}}{\theta})$. The paper mentions this form is 'guaranteed to be positive'. Could someone explain why this specific formulation ($x - \ln x - 1$ where $x = \pi_{ref}/\pi_{	heta}$) is preferred here over the standard log-ratio definition? Also, since this term appears directly in the loss function (Equation 3) rather than as part of the reward signal like in standard PPO (Equation 2), how does this affect the stability of training compared to adding a KL penalty to rewards?
Answer:
The specific formulation `$\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o)}{\pi_{\theta}(o)} - \log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o)}{\pi_{\theta}(o)} - 1$` is preferred in the DeepSeekMath paper because it serves as an **unbiased estimator** of the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence that remains numerically stable and strictly non-negative, whereas the standard definition involves expectations that can be difficult to approximate accurately in stochastic optimization settings.

To understand why this form is used, consider the mathematical definition of KL divergence between two distributions `$P$` and `$Q$`, which is `$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim P} \left[\log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right]$`. In the context of reinforcement learning fine-tuning, we want to penalize deviations of the current policy `$\pi_\theta$` from a reference policy `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`. If we were to use the standard definition directly as a per-sample loss term, we would encounter issues because the expectation is taken over the *current* policy `$\pi_\theta$`, but we are evaluating terms involving ratios where `$\pi_\theta$` might be very small or zero for certain tokens, leading to numerical instability or undefined values (division by zero).

The expression `$f(x) = x - \ln x - 1$`, where `$x = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o)}{\pi_{\theta}(o)}$`, is derived from a known inequality property of convex functions. Specifically, for any `$x > 0$`, the function `$x - \ln x - 1$` is always greater than or equal to zero, achieving its minimum of 0 exactly when `$x=1$`. This guarantees that the penalty term added to the loss function (as seen in Equation 3 of the paper) is **guaranteed to be positive**, satisfying the requirement that a divergence measure must be non-negative. The authors cite this specific estimator (referencing [kl_approx]) because it provides an unbiased estimate of the KL divergence when averaged over samples drawn from `$\pi_\theta$`, unlike simply taking `\$\log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}\$` which would estimate a cross-entropy difference rather than KL unless properly weighted.

Regarding your second question about stability, placing this term directly in the objective function (Equation 3) rather than embedding it within the reward signal (as done in standard PPO via Equation 2) fundamentally changes how the gradient flows and how the baseline interacts with regularization. In standard PPO, adding `\$-\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\$` to the reward means that every token's advantage calculation includes this penalty immediately. This can sometimes lead to "reward hacking" where the model learns to manipulate small variations in log-probabilities to game the reward model before converging on optimal behavior, or it can cause instability if the reward model itself is noisy.

By moving the KL term outside and treating it as a direct regularizer on the objective function as GRPO does ("instead of adding KL penalty in the reward... GRPO regularizes by directly adding..."), DeepSeekMath simplifies the computation of advantages (`$\hat{A}_{i,t}$`). The paper explicitly states this avoids "complicating the calculation of `$\hat{A}_{i,t}$`." Since GRPO estimates advantages using group-relative normalization (subtracting group mean and dividing by group std), keeping the KL term separate ensures that these relative advantage calculations remain pure reflections of outcome quality relative to peers in the batch, while still enforcing proximity to `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` through a clean, additive penalty on total loss. This separation often leads to more stable training dynamics because it decouples the variance reduction strategy (the group-relative advantage) from the constraint enforcement (the KL penalty), preventing potential conflicts between scaling rewards for comparison and scaling penalties for regularization.

### Why did arXiv papers fail to improve performance despite being a staple in other math models?
Question:
The paper explicitly states that training on arXiv papers resulted in 'no notable improvements' or even deterioration on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH, contrasting with models like Minerva or Llemma which rely heavily on them.

The authors suggest arXiv might be ineffective because it contains too much preamble/macro noise or lacks problem-solving structure compared to web-scraped data. However, they admit they haven't tested arXiv combined with other data types or at larger scales.

Given that Minerva 540B performed well with arXiv-heavy data (as noted in Section Validating Quality), is it possible that arXiv data requires a much larger model capacity (>7B) to be useful? Or does their finding suggest that for *reasoning* specifically (vs. knowledge retrieval), raw problem-solution pairs from Common Crawl are strictly superior to academic papers?
Answer:
Based on the findings presented in the DeepSeekMath paper, the ineffectiveness of arXiv data appears to stem primarily from data quality and structural characteristics rather than a strict requirement for larger model capacities, although the authors acknowledge that scale remains an open variable. The paper explicitly states that training on arXiv-only corpora (such as MathPile, which is $>85\%$ arXiv, or ArXiv-RedPajama) resulted in "no notable improvements" or even "deterioration" on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH for both 1.3B and 7B models. The authors hypothesize that arXiv papers suffer from excessive "noise," including preambles, macros, and bibliographies, and crucially lack the explicit **problem-solving structure** found in web-scraped data. Unlike Common Crawl sources which often contain tutorials, forum discussions (like MathOverflow), or worked-out examples that mimic the step-by-step reasoning required for benchmarks, arXiv papers are optimized for presenting finished proofs and theoretical results rather than teaching the process of deriving them.

Regarding your comparison with Minerva 540B, it is important to distinguish between correlation and causation in their respective data strategies. While Minerva relied heavily on arXiv data, DeepSeekMath argues that its own success comes from a meticulously engineered pipeline extracting 120B high-quality tokens from Common Crawl, which proved superior to existing corpora like Proof-Pile-2 (which mixes arXiv) even at lower token counts (outperforming Proof-Pile-2 at just 50B tokens). The paper suggests that for **reasoning**—specifically the ability to generate coherent chains of thought—raw problem-solution pairs from web data are indeed strictly superior to academic papers in their current processed forms. The "noise" in arXiv LaTeX files may confuse smaller models (like the 7B architecture used here) by introducing irrelevant syntactic structures or dense notation without pedagogical scaffolding.

However, you correctly note the authors' caveats: they have not tested arXiv data combined with other types (though their final mix included only 10% arXiv alongside code and web data) nor at scales significantly larger than 7B. The possibility remains that very large models ($>7$B) possess the capacity to filter out macro noise and extract latent reasoning patterns from raw papers more effectively than smaller models. Nevertheless, within the experimental bounds of this study, the evidence points toward **data curation quality** being a more critical factor than sheer parameter count; a smaller model trained on high-quality web-derived reasoning traces outperformed a massive model trained on noisier academic archives. Thus, for open-source mathematical reasoning focused on benchmarks like MATH, prioritizing structurally sound problem-solution pairs from web scrapes appears more effective than relying on traditional academic repositories unless those repositories undergo rigorous restructuring to match the format of instructional content.

### Interpretation of RL improving Maj@K but not Pass@K: What does this say about model capability?
Question:
In the 'Why RL Works?' section, Figure 
ef{fig:maj-pass} shows that while Reinforcement Learning (GRPO) significantly boosts Maj@K (Majority Vote) accuracy, it does not improve Pass@K accuracy compared to the SFT baseline.

The authors conclude: 'it seems that RL enhances... by rendering the output distribution more robust... boosting the correct response from TopK rather than enhancing fundamental capabilities.'

I am trying to reconcile this with my understanding of RLHF. Usually, we expect RL to teach new reasoning patterns. If Pass@K doesn't change, isn't it true that RL didn't actually learn *new* solutions but just made existing correct solutions slightly more probable while suppressing wrong ones? Does this mean DeepSeekMath-RL essentially acts as a better filter/sampler rather than acquiring new mathematical logic?
Answer:
Your interpretation aligns closely with the authors' explicit conclusions in the "Why RL Works?" section of the DeepSeekMath paper. The observation that Reinforcement Learning (specifically GRPO) significantly boosts Majority Vote ($\text{Maj}@K$) accuracy while leaving $\text{Pass}@K$ unchanged indicates that the primary mechanism of improvement is indeed refining the output distribution rather than expanding the model's fundamental solution space.

In technical terms, $\text{Pass}@K$ measures whether *at least one* correct answer exists within $K$ independent samples generated by the model. Since this metric remained static between the SFT baseline and the RL model, it implies that the set of unique correct solutions the model can generate did not grow; if a problem was unsolvable by generating $K$ distinct paths under SFT, it remains unsolvable under RL with the same sampling budget. Conversely, $\text{Maj}@K$ relies on consistency: it requires that a correct answer appears frequently enough to win a majority vote among $K$ samples. The boost here signifies that RL has increased the probability mass assigned to correct trajectories ($\pi_\theta(\text{correct} \mid q)$) while suppressing incorrect ones, making correct answers more reproducible across multiple generations.

This phenomenon supports the authors' argument that RL acts as a "filter" or "stabilizer." During Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), models often suffer from misalignment where they possess latent knowledge of a solution but fail to consistently retrieve it due to noise in their generation distribution. By optimizing via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which uses group-relative advantages to reinforce high-scoring outputs relative to peers, the policy shifts from exploring diverse but erratic reasoning paths to converging reliably on known correct paths. Essentially, the model learns to suppress "lucky guesses" or hallucinated chains of thought and amplify valid reasoning patterns it already knew how to produce during SFT.

Therefore, your intuition is correct: DeepSeekMath-RL does not appear to be acquiring entirely new mathematical logic or discovering novel solution methods for previously unsolvable problems within this experimental setup. Instead, it is performing a form of distributional sharpening, ensuring that when a correct path exists in the model's capability envelope, it becomes significantly more probable than incorrect alternatives. This distinction is crucial for understanding scalability limits; if $\text{Pass}@K$ were to improve, it would suggest genuine capability expansion (learning new things), whereas an isolated $\text{Maj}@K$ gain suggests optimization of existing competencies (reliability). The authors explicitly attribute this to rendering the output distribution "more robust," confirming that for these specific benchmarks and data subsets, RL served primarily to maximize confidence in existing knowledge rather than expand the frontier of solvable problems.

### How exactly does Process Supervision differ from Outcome Supervision in calculating $	ilde{A}_{i,t}$?
Question:
The paper describes two modes for GRPO: Outcome Supervision and Process Supervision.

For Outcome Supervision: $	ilde{A}_{i,t} = 	ilde{r}_i$ (the normalized final reward applies to all tokens).
For Process Supervision: $	ilde{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \tilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$.

My confusion lies in the summation indices. In process supervision, do we assign a reward $r^{index(j)}$ only at specific step boundaries? If so, how are intermediate tokens between steps handled? Are they assumed to inherit the cumulative sum of future step rewards?
Also, since normalization happens globally across all steps and all outputs ($	ext{mean}(\mathbf{R})$, $	ext{std}(\mathbf{R})$), doesn't this risk diluting strong signals from critical steps if there are many low-value steps elsewhere?
Answer:
Your intuition regarding the assignment of rewards to specific tokens versus steps is largely correct, and the summation formula in the paper reflects a specific design choice for credit assignment in Process Supervision. In the DeepSeekMath paper, **Process Supervision** differs from Outcome Supervision by assigning rewards only at the end of logical reasoning steps (denoted by $index(j)$), rather than at every single token generation. Consequently, for any token $t$ generated *between* two step boundaries (i.e., after $index(j-1)$ but before $index(j)$), the immediate reward is effectively zero until the next step boundary is reached.

The formula $\tilde{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \tilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$ implements a "cumulative future reward" logic. Here, $\tilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$ represents the normalized reward assigned specifically to the end of step $j$. If token $t$ falls within step $j$, it inherits the value of $\tilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$ plus all subsequent step rewards ($j+1, j+2, \dots$). If token $t$ is part of an earlier step, say step $k < j$, it accumulates the rewards of all future steps starting from $k$. Essentially, every token in a sequence acts as if it contributed to the success of all subsequent reasoning steps; thus, tokens in an early, crucial step receive a very high advantage score if all later steps are correct, because they carry the weight of the entire successful trajectory. Conversely, if a mistake occurs at step $j$, making $\tilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$ negative (or low), that negative signal propagates backward to all preceding tokens via this summation, penalizing them proportionally to their distance from the error.

Regarding your concern about normalization diluting strong signals: you are right that normalizing globally across all steps and all outputs ($\text{mean}(\mathbf{R})$, $\text{std}(\mathbf{R})$) treats every step as a data point in a single pool. This can indeed dampen extreme values if there are many trivial or neutral steps (low variance) interspersed with critical reasoning steps. However, this global normalization serves a specific purpose in Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): it ensures stability by centering the advantage estimates around zero within each group of sampled outputs for a given question. The paper relies on the fact that GRPO operates on groups of outputs generated for the *same* question; therefore, even if global statistics include many low-value steps from other questions or easy steps within a solution, the relative comparison remains valid because it evaluates whether a specific path (sequence of steps) was better or worse than alternative paths generated for that same problem. The "dilution" effect is somewhat mitigated by the fact that mathematical reasoning chains usually have distinct structural breaks where errors compound; a failure in an early critical step often renders subsequent steps irrelevant or incorrect anyway, leading to consistently lower accumulated sums for those trajectories compared to successful ones. Thus, while individual step signals might be smoothed by global normalization, the *relative* ordering between successful and failed trajectories generally remains preserved enough to drive effective policy updates.
