# How a Tiny 7B Model Outsmarted Giants: The Power of High-Quality Math Data

For years, the prevailing wisdom in artificial intelligence suggested that to solve complex mathematical problems, you needed massive scale. If you wanted to compete with giants like GPT-4 or Gemini-Ultra, your model had to be huge, often requiring hundreds of billions of parameters. But a new study from DeepSeek challenges this assumption head-on. They introduced **DeepSeekMath**, a 7-billion parameter model that achieves performance on competition-level math benchmarks approaching that of the most powerful closed-source systems.

The secret sauce wasn't simply scaling up compute or parameters; it was a relentless focus on data quality and a novel approach to reinforcement learning. By mining 120 billion math-related tokens from the public web and refining them through a rigorous selection pipeline, the researchers proved that high-quality data can outweigh sheer model size. Here is how they did it and what it means for the future of open-source AI.

## The Myth of Size vs. Quality

When building DeepSeekMath, the team started with an interesting observation: existing open-source models lagged significantly behind proprietary ones in mathematical reasoning. Most attempts to catch up involved training on massive datasets like arXiv papers or specialized academic corpora. However, DeepSeek took a different path. Instead of relying heavily on academic papers, they turned their attention to the vast expanse of **Common Crawl**.

The result is the **DeepSeekMath Corpus**, a dataset comprising 120 billion tokens dedicated entirely to mathematics. To put this in perspective, this dataset is roughly seven times larger than the web-based math data used by Google's Minerva and nine times larger than OpenWebMath. The critical insight here is that publicly available web data contains far more valuable mathematical information than previously thought, provided you know how to extract it.

To validate this approach, the researchers trained a smaller 1.3B parameter model on various corpora for comparison. They found that models trained on their curated DeepSeekMath Corpus consistently outperformed those trained on established datasets like MathPile or Proof-Pile-2, even though those competitors relied heavily on arXiv papers. Surprisingly, training exclusively on arXiv papers showed little to no improvement in mathematical reasoning capabilities across their benchmarks, suggesting that academic rigor does not automatically translate to better problem-solving skills for language models without careful curation.

## Mining Gold from the Web: The Data Pipeline

Building a dataset of 120 billion tokens from the chaotic internet requires more than just downloading pages; it demands precision engineering. The DeepSeek team developed an iterative pipeline centered around a **fastText-based classifier**.

The process began with a small seed corpus of high-quality math texts from OpenWebMath. They trained their classifier to distinguish between these positive examples and random web pages acting as negatives. Once trained, this classifier scoured Common Crawl to find similar mathematical content. But they didn't stop there. Recognizing that their initial classifier might miss diverse types of math content, they analyzed which domains were being captured (like *mathoverflow.net*) and manually expanded their seed corpus with new sources from those domains before retraining the classifier for subsequent iterations.

This cycle ran four times, allowing them to harvest millions of high-quality pages while filtering out noise through ranking and human annotation checks. Crucially, they also implemented strict decontamination protocols to ensure none of the evaluation benchmark questions leaked into the training data, guaranteeing that their test scores reflected genuine reasoning ability rather than memorization.

Another fascinating discovery emerged during this phase: **multilingualism matters**. Unlike many existing math corpora that are strictly English-centric, the DeepSeekMath Corpus includes significant amounts of Chinese mathematical content derived from global web sources without translation artifacts. Consequently, their model showed superior performance on Chinese benchmarks compared to other models trained only on English data, proving that diverse linguistic exposure enhances general mathematical reasoning capabilities across languages.

## From Base Model to Master Solver: Code and RL

Once the base model was pre-trained using this massive corpus (initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5), it already outperformed much larger models like Minerva 540B on standard benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH without any tool use or voting techniques alone scoring 36%. However, pushing past 50% accuracy required two final ingredients: supervised fine-tuning and a new reinforcement learning algorithm called **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**.

The team observed that initializing with a code-trained model was vital; code training appeared to boost logical reasoning capabilities significantly before any specific math training occurred. After instruction tuning with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Program-of-Thought (PoT) data, they applied GRPO to refine the model further.

Traditional reinforcement learning methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) rely on a separate "critic" model—a value function roughly equal in size to the main policy—to estimate baselines for training stability. This doubles memory requirements and computational costs deepseek aimed to eliminate this bottleneck with GRPO. Instead of training an extra critic network, GRPO estimates the baseline by comparing groups of outputs generated for the same question against each other essentially normalizing rewards within a group rather than against an external value function estimate ($V_{\psi}$). This approach drastically reduces memory usage while maintaining or improving performance stability during optimization steps defined by equations involving group-relative advantages $\hat{A}_{i,t}$.

# Beyond Code: Why Teaching AI Programming Makes It Better at Math

For years, researchers have debated whether teaching Large Language Models (LLMs) to code actually helps them reason better in other domains. Is programming just another skill, or does it fundamentally rewire a model's logic? The team behind DeepSeekMath has finally provided compelling evidence that the answer is yes. Their latest work reveals a surprising synergy: initializing a mathematical reasoning model with code training significantly boosts its ability to solve complex math problems.

This insight comes from the development of **DeepSeekMath**, a 7-billion parameter model that pushes the boundaries of what open-source models can achieve in mathematics. Without relying on external toolkits or voting techniques, DeepSeekMath achieved a score of 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark, rivaling performance from massive closed-source systems like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra. But the secret sauce wasn't just more data; it was the strategic decision to start with a coder.

## The Power of Starting with Code

DeepSeekMath is not built from scratch. Instead, it begins as **DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B**. The authors hypothesized that a model already proficient in programming would possess a structural advantage when tackling mathematical reasoning. Their experiments confirmed this intuition, offering a partial answer to the long-standing question: *Does code training improve reasoning abilities?*

The results were starkly positive. When comparing models trained on general data versus those trained on code before moving to mathematics, the code-initialized models consistently outperformed their peers. This holds true for two distinct modes of mathematical problem-solving:
*   **Step-by-step logical arguments:** Even without using external tools, models exposed to code showed enhanced capabilities in generating coherent chain-of-thought reasoning.
*   **Tool-integrated reasoning:** When asked to write Python scripts to perform calculations, the code-trained models demonstrated superior proficiency in generating executable programs that accurately solved math problems.

Interestingly, the timing of this training matters. In two-stage training scenarios where a model learns code first and then math, the transition led to significant improvements in both coding retention and mathematical prowess. However, simply mixing code and math tokens in a single-stage training run had mixed results; while it prevented "catastrophic forgetting" of coding skills, it sometimes compromised pure mathematical reasoning without tools for smaller models like the 1.3B parameter version tested in their ablation studies. Ultimately, for the 7B scale DeepSeekMath model, starting with code proved to be the optimal path forward.

## Scaling Up with Web Data

While code initialization provided the foundation, scaling up required massive amounts of high-quality data. Traditional approaches often rely heavily on arXiv papers or curated textbook datasets like MathPile or OpenWebMath. DeepSeek took a different route by mining **Common Crawl**, the vast repository of public web data.

Using an iterative pipeline involving fastText classifiers and human annotation, they constructed the **DeepSeekMath Corpus**, comprising 120 billion math-related tokens. This dataset is roughly seven times larger than the web data used by Google's Minerva and nine times larger than OpenWebMath. Surprisingly, their experiments suggested that arXiv papers might not be as impactful as previously thought for improving general mathematical reasoning benchmarks like GSM8K or MATH when compared to high-quality web-sourced content. The DeepSeekMath Corpus not only surpassed these traditional sources in quantity but also delivered higher quality results during pre-training evaluations, helping their base model beat Minerva 540B despite having far fewer parameters (7B vs 540B).

## Refining Reasoning with GRPO

Once pre-trained and instruction-tuned via Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), DeepSeek needed one final push to reach elite status: Reinforcement Learning (RL). Standard RL algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) are powerful but computationally expensive because they require training a separate "critic" model (a value function) alongside the main policy model to estimate baselines.

To solve this bottleneck, DeepSeek introduced **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**. GRPO eliminates the need for a critic model entirely. Instead of learning a value function token-by-token, GRPO samples a group of outputs (e.g., 64 different solutions) for a single question from the current policy model. It then estimates the baseline by calculating the average reward across this group of outputs relative to each other. If one solution performs better than its peers in the same group, it gets reinforced; if it performs worse, it gets penalized.

This approach drastically reduces memory usage while maintaining effectiveness. Applying GRPO allowed DeepSeekMath-Instruct to jump from 46.8% to 51.7% on the MATH benchmark using only English instruction-tuning data derived from GSM8K and MATH itself—a remarkable feat considering no new problem types were introduced during this phase. Furthermore, this optimization improved performance on out-of-domain tasks as well, suggesting that RL helps generalize reasoning patterns rather than just memorizing specific problems.

## What This Means for Open Source AI

The success of DeepSeekMath demonstrates that parameter count isn't everything; data quality and architectural choices matter immensely by leveraging code pre-training and efficient RL algorithms like GRPO). By combining these elements—starting with a coder's logic engine and refining it with millions of math-heavy web pages—the team managed to create an open-source model that competes with giants costing orders of magnitude more to train and run).

This work opens new doors for researchers everywhere). It proves that we don't necessarily need billions of dollars or trillions of tokens to break ground-breaking barriers in AI reasoning). Sometimes, all you need is smart data engineering and a little bit of Python know-how)). As we move forward), expect more models adopting similar hybrid strategies where coding serves as a catalyst for broader cognitive capabilities))



# Smarter, Lighter Reinforcement Learning: Introducing Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a critical tool for unlocking the latent reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly in complex domains like mathematics. However, traditional RL methods often come with a prohibitive cost: they require training a massive secondary model, known as a "critic" or value function, alongside the main policy model. This dual-model architecture doubles the memory footprint and computational overhead, making high-quality RL fine-tuning inaccessible for many researchers and organizations. In our recent work on **DeepSeekMath**, we tackled this bottleneck head-on by introducing **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, an algorithm that eliminates the need for a separate critic model entirely while delivering state-of-the-art performance.

## The Bottleneck of Traditional RL

To understand why GRPO matters, we must first look at how standard RL algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) operate. In PPO, the goal is to maximize a reward signal by adjusting the policy model's parameters. To do this stably, PPO relies on an advantage function ($A_t$), which estimates how much better a specific action is compared to the average expectation. Calculating this advantage traditionally requires a learned value function ($V_\psi$)—essentially another neural network of similar size to the policy itself—that predicts the expected future reward for every token generated.

This setup creates significant engineering hurdles. Training two large models simultaneously consumes double the GPU memory and compute resources. Furthermore, in the context of LLMs where rewards are often sparse (assigned only at the very end of a generation), training a value function that accurately estimates token-level advantages becomes notoriously difficult and unstable. We asked ourselves: *Is there a way to estimate this baseline without training an extra model?*

## How GRPO Changes the Game

The core insight behind GRPO is simplicity rooted in comparison rather than absolute prediction. Instead of relying on an external critic to tell us what the "average" reward should be, GRPO generates a group of multiple outputs for the same input question and uses them to establish their own baseline.

Here is how it works conceptually:
1.  For a given question $q$, we sample a group of $G$ distinct outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \dots, o_G\}$ from our current policy model.
2.  We pass these outputs through a reward model to get individual scores $\{r_1, r_2, \dots, r_G\}$.
3.  Rather than comparing each output to an external value network, we compare each output against the average performance of its peers in that specific group.

By normalizing these rewards—subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation—we derive an advantage estimate $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for every token in every output within that group. If an answer performs better than its siblings in the same batch, it receives positive reinforcement; if it performs worse, it is penalized relative to the group norm. This approach completely obviates the need for a separate value function $V_\psi$, slashing memory requirements while maintaining training stability.

## A Unified View of Alignment Methods

Beyond just saving resources, GRPO helps clarify the landscape of modern alignment techniques. By analyzing various methods—including Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and PPO—through a unified mathematical lens, we found they all share a common structure involving three components: Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm logic that converts rewards into gradient updates.

GRPO fits neatly into this paradigm as an online RL technique that leverages relative scoring within groups. Unlike Offline RFT or DPO which rely on static datasets generated by older models, GRPO continuously samples fresh data from the evolving policy ("online"). More importantly, unlike Online RFT which treats all correct answers equally and ignores incorrect ones beyond rejection, GRPO utilizes the full spectrum of reward values within a group to apply nuanced gradients. This means it doesn't just learn "what is right," but learns "how much better one right answer is compared to another."

## Impact on Mathematical Reasoning

The practical application of GRPO was central to building **DeepSeekMath**. Starting from our instruction-tuned DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B model—which already performed competitively against much larger open-source models—we applied GRPO using only chain-of-thought data from GSM8K and MATH benchmarks. Despite this narrow training scope focused on just two datasets in English, the results were striking:

*   **Performance Leap:** The final DeepSeekMath-RL model achieved **51.7%** accuracy on the competition-level MATH benchmark without using any external tools or voting ensembles at test time. This surpassed all other open-source models ranging from 7B to 70B parameters and approached the performance of proprietary giants like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra at that time.
*   **Generalization:** Remarkably, despite being trained only on English math problems via RL, the model showed significant improvements on out-of-domain tasks as well, such as Chinese math benchmarks (CMATH), jumping from 84.6% to 88.8%.
*   **Efficiency:** By removing the critic model, we reduced memory usage significantly compared to standard PPO implementations, allowing us to scale up sampling strategies effectively without hitting hardware limits immediately encountered with traditional methods.

Our analysis revealed that this boost wasn't necessarily due to teaching the model fundamentally new arithmetic facts it didn't know before (as measured by Pass@1 metrics staying relatively stable). Instead, RL with GRPO made the model's output distribution more robust and consistent (improving Max@K metrics). It helped align the model's internal preferences so that when generating multiple solutions for hard problems, it was far more likely to land on a correct path consistently across attempts.

## Looking Ahead

The success of DeepSeekMath demonstrates that high-quality mathematical reasoning does not strictly require massive parameter counts or prohibitively expensive training setups if data selection and optimization algorithms are handled correctly. The introduction of Group Relative Policy Optimization proves that simpler mechanisms can sometimes outperform complex architectures by leveraging relative comparisons inherent in grouped generations rather than absolute predictions from auxiliary networks.

While challenges remain—particularly in geometry reasoning where data biases persist—the path forward involves refining these RL paradigms further. Future work will focus on exploring advanced sampling strategies like tree search during generation and developing reward models that are robust against noise and uncertainty in complex reasoning chains。For now, GRPO stands as a testament to how rethinking fundamental algorithmic assumptions can push open-source models closer to—and sometimes beyond—the frontiers of AI capability with significantly fewer resources.cw
