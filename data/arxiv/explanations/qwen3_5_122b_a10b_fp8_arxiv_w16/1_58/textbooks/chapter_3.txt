Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Mathematical Mechanics and Training Dynamics

The transition from traditional floating-point Large Language Models (LLMs) to the 1-bit regime represented by BitNet b1.58 necessitates a rigorous re-examination of the underlying mathematical mechanics. Unlike standard neural networks where weights are continuous variables updated via gradient descent, BitNet b1.58 operates under strict discrete constraints, forcing every parameter to reside within a ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This architectural shift fundamentally alters how gradients flow through the network and how numerical stability is maintained during the training process. The core of this transformation lies in the precise formulation of the quantization functions applied to both weights and activations. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for grasping why BitNet b1.58 can achieve parity with full-precision models while drastically reducing computational complexity. The following sections dissect the specific mathematical operations that define this new class of models, focusing on the *absmean* quantization strategy for weights and the distinct handling of activations.

## The absmean Quantization Function: Scaling by Average Absolute Value ($\gamma$)

At the heart of constraining weights to a ternary distribution is the *absmean* quantization function. In conventional deep learning, weights are initialized randomly and updated continuously without inherent bounds on their magnitude during forward passes, relying on regularization techniques later to prevent divergence. In contrast, BitNet b1.58 must explicitly project real-valued weight matrices onto a discrete grid at every relevant stage of computation or update cycle. The method employed does not rely on fixed thresholds or learned scaling factors alone; instead, it utilizes a dynamic normalization based on the statistical properties of the weight matrix itself at any given moment.

The process begins by calculating a global scaling factor, denoted as $\gamma$, derived from the weight matrix $W$. Rather than computing variance or maximum absolute values, which might be sensitive to outliers or require sorting operations incompatible with high-throughput training pipelines, BitNet b1.58 computes $\gamma$ as the mean absolute value of all elements in the matrix. Mathematically, if $W$ is an $n \times m$ matrix representing a layer's weights, $\gamma$ is defined as:

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|
$$

Here, $|W_{ij}|$ denotes the absolute value of each individual weight element $W_{ij}$ within the matrix dimensions $n$ rows and $m$ columns. By dividing every element in $W$ by this scalar $\gamma$, the algorithm normalizes the distribution such that its average absolute magnitude becomes unity (ignoring small perturbations). This normalization step effectively centers the dynamic range of the weights around values that are easily separable into integers close to -1, 0, or 1 after rounding. If a weight element is large relative to others in its group, dividing by $\gamma$ brings it closer to an integer boundary; conversely, small weights are suppressed toward zero. This adaptive scaling ensures that regardless of whether a layer naturally learns larger magnitudes or smaller ones during early training epochs, it maintains a consistent probability mass near the target ternary states $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

Following this division by $\gamma$, an infinitesimal constant $\epsilon$ is added to the denominator to ensure numerical stability. While rare in idealized theoretical scenarios where no exact zeros exist in denominators during initialization phases involving random Gaussian distributions with non-zero variance, practical implementations face edge cases where gradients might vanish or specific layers might temporarily exhibit near-zero activity. Without $\epsilon$, attempting to divide by a vanishingly small $\gamma$ could result in numerical overflow or undefined behavior in floating-point arithmetic units. Thus, the term $(\gamma + \epsilon)$ acts as a safeguard against singularities while having negligible impact on valid scaling factors where $\gamma > \epsilon$. # Chapter 3: Mathematical Mechanics and Training Dynamics

The transition from traditional floating-point Large Language Models (LLMs) to the extreme efficiency regime of BitNet b1.58 necessitates a rigorous reformulation of neural network mechanics. Standard Transformers rely heavily on floating-point matrix multiplications involving dense arrays of real numbers ($\mathbb{R}$), which incur substantial computational costs in terms of both energy and silicon area when scaled to billions of parameters. BitNet b1.58 disrupts this paradigm by enforcing strict discreteness constraints on its learnable parameters while maintaining competitive performance with full-precision counterparts like FP16 LLaMA models. This chapter provides a comprehensive analysis of the mathematical foundations that enable this feat: specifically detailing how continuous gradients interact with discrete ternary weights through specialized quantization functions and how activation tensors are handled differently than in previous architectures like vanilla BitNet or standard Transformers. Understanding these mechanical details is crucial for interpreting why this architecture succeeds despite operating outside conventional differentiable spaces typically assumed in deep learning theory.*
