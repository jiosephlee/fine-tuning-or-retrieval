\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Does the absmean quantization function allow gradients to flow through the rounding operation during backpropagation?
Question:
In Section 2 under 'Quantization Function', Equation 1 defines $	ilde{W} = 	ext{RoundClip}(rac{W}{eta + 	au}, -1, 1)$. Since the $	ext{RoundClip}$ operation is non-differentiable (the derivative is zero everywhere except undefined points), how does the model train effectively? Are they using Straight-Through Estimator (STE) approximations for the gradient of the rounding function? The paper mentions training from scratch but doesn't explicitly detail how gradients pass through this hard constraint.
Answer:
Yes, the model trains effectively despite the non-differentiable nature of the `RoundClip` operation because the authors implicitly rely on the **Straight-Through Estimator (STE)**, a standard technique in quantization-aware training that is consistent with the methods used in their predecessor, BitNet. Although the paper titled *The Era of 1-bit LLMs* does not explicitly define the STE equation in Section 2, it states that BitNet b1.58 is built upon the BitNet architecture and trained from scratch using a specific quantization function. In the original BitNet framework and virtually all modern quantization literature, when a discrete operation like $\mathrm{Round}$ appears inside a computational graph during backpropagation, its gradient is defined as 1 within a certain clipping range (typically $[-1, 1]$) and 0 outside, effectively bypassing the discontinuity.

Intuitively, this works because during forward propagation, the weights $W$ are indeed clamped to the set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ via Equation 1: $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$, ensuring the model operates with ternary weights. However, during backward propagation, treating the derivative of $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ as zero would prevent any learning signal from reaching the underlying full-precision weights $W$, causing training to stall. By applying STE, we approximate $\frac{\partial \widetilde{W}}{\partial W} \approx 1$ whenever $\left|\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}\right| < 1$. This allows gradients to flow through the "hard" rounding operation as if it were an identity function locally, updating $W$ toward values that minimize loss while keeping the actual stored weights ternary.

This approach connects to broader concepts in deep learning where we optimize a continuous relaxation of a discrete problem. The scaling factor $\gamma$, defined as $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$, acts as a learnable or dynamic threshold that adapts to the distribution of weights. Without STE (or a similar approximation like Gumbel-Softmax), there would be no mechanism for $W$ to evolve toward regions where rounding yields optimal ternary configurations ($\pm 1$ or $0$). Therefore, while Equation 2 describes a non-differentiable mapping for inference and forward passes, successful training necessitates this gradient approximation strategy to bridge the gap between continuous optimization and discrete constraints.
