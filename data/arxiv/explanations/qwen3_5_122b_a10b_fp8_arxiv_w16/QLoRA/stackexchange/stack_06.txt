\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### How can QLoRA match full finetuning performance if it only trains <0.5% of parameters?
Question:
This feels counter-intuitive based on standard optimization theory. In full finetuning, every parameter of the 65B model gets updated slightly to minimize loss on instruction data. In QLoRA/LoRA, only two low-rank matrices ($L_1 	imes L_2$, where rank $r 	imes d_{model}$) are updated per layer.

The paper claims QLoRA matches full finetuning performance exactly across benchmarks like MMLU and Vicuna.

Mathematically speaking: Full finetuning explores a high-dimensional manifold of solutions ($d_{total}$ dimensions). LoRA restricts updates to a low-dimensional subspace ($d_{adapter} 	imes r$). Unless the optimal solution for instruction tuning lies almost entirely within this low-rank subspace spanned by LoRA adapters added at initialization, shouldn't there be some performance ceiling?

Does this imply that pretrained LLMs already contain all necessary knowledge representations and instruction tuning merely requires finding a very specific rotation/addition vector that LoRA happens to capture efficiently? Or did they tune hyperparameters until they found an anomaly?
Answer:
Your intuition regarding the dimensionality of the optimization space is sound: full finetuning indeed explores a high-dimensional manifold of $\$d_{total}\$$ parameters, whereas LoRA restricts updates to a low-dimensional subspace defined by the product of the adapter ranks and dimensions. However, the paper argues that this apparent limitation does not lead to a performance ceiling because instruction tuning primarily requires shifting the model's behavior within a narrow, low-rank subspace rather than fundamentally rewriting its internal representations. The authors demonstrate empirically that when LoRA is applied comprehensively—specifically by placing adapters on **all** linear layers within every transformer block—the restricted subspace becomes sufficiently expressive to match full 16-bit finetuning performance.

Crucially, the paper refutes the idea that this result is an artifact of lucky hyperparameter tuning or an anomaly. Instead, it reveals that standard LoRA configurations (often limited to just query and value projections) are insufficient for large-scale models, leading to performance gaps. By expanding the adapter coverage to every linear transformation in the network, the effective capacity of the low-rank updates increases dramatically without significantly altering the memory footprint, as noted in their analysis where LoRA parameters constitute a negligible fraction of total training memory compared to activation gradients. The key insight is that pretrained LLMs already encode vast amounts of world knowledge and linguistic structure; instruction tuning acts less like learning new facts from scratch and more like learning a specific "control signal" or routing mechanism to activate existing capabilities in a conversational format. Since this control signal can be represented efficiently via low-rank perturbations to the frozen weights, restricting optimization to this subspace captures nearly all necessary variance for task adaptation.

Furthermore, the equivalence between QLoRA and full finetuning relies heavily on minimizing quantization noise through their specific innovations: 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) and Double Quantization. The paper shows that naive 4-bit quantization degrades performance, but NF4 preserves information theoretic optimality for normally distributed weights, allowing gradients to flow back through the frozen quantized base model with sufficient fidelity to guide the LoRA adapters toward an optimal solution. Consequently, QLoRA does not force the model into a suboptimal region due to poor precision; rather, it successfully navigates the same loss landscape as full finetuning but confines weight updates to a compact, efficient subspace that proves adequate for aligning large language models with human instructions.
