Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Deriving Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

The prevailing paradigm for aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human values has long relied on Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). This traditional pipeline involves a multi-stage process: first, collecting human preferences over model outputs; second, training an explicit reward model to predict these preferences; and third, using complex reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to fine-tune the language model policy to maximize the predicted reward while staying close to the original model. While effective, this approach introduces significant complexity, instability, and computational overhead due to the necessity of sampling from the policy during training and managing separate networks for the reward model and the policy. In this chapter, we dismantle this conventional wisdom by deriving Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a method that eliminates the intermediate reward modeling stage entirely. We demonstrate that by leveraging a specific parameterization of the reward function derived from optimal control theory, we can map the preference learning problem directly onto a simple binary classification objective. This transformation allows us to optimize a language model policy to satisfy human preferences without ever explicitly fitting a reward model or running a reinforcement learning loop.

## Analytical Solution to KL-Constrained Reward Maximization

To understand how DPO bypasses reinforcement learning, we must first revisit the fundamental objective that standard RLHF seeks to optimize. The goal of aligning a language model is not merely to maximize raw scores assigned by a reward function, but to do so while ensuring the resulting policy does not drift arbitrarily far from a reference distribution. If we allow the model to maximize reward without constraint, it risks collapsing into degenerate solutions—producing nonsensical text that happens to score highly on narrow criteria—or losing the linguistic fluency acquired during pre-training. Consequently, the standard formulation frames alignment as a constrained optimization problem where we seek a policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes expected reward subject to a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty against a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Mathematically, this objective is expressed as:

$$
\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi(y|x)} [r(x, y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}} [\pi(y|x) || \pi_\text{ref}(y|x)]
$$

Here, $x$ represents an input prompt drawn from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$, $y$ is a generated response sampled from the current policy $\pi$, and $r(x, y)$ is an underlying reward function reflecting human preferences. The term $\beta > 0$ acts as a Lagrange multiplier controlling the strictness of the constraint; smaller values of $\beta$ enforce tighter adherence to the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (typically an earlier Supervised Fine-Tuned model), while larger values permit greater deviation in pursuit of higher rewards. The KL divergence term serves as a regularizer, penalizing deviations in probability mass between the new policy and the reference distribution across all possible tokens.

The critical breakthrough in deriving DPO lies in recognizing that this constrained optimization problem admits an exact analytical solution for any given reward function $r$. Unlike typical non-linear optimization problems solved iteratively via gradient ascent or actor-critic methods, this specific structure—a linear expectation minus a convex regularization term—allows us to solve for the optimal policy $\pi^*$ in closed form. By applying variational calculus or utilizing results from control-as-inference frameworks, one can show that for any fixed reward function $r$, there exists a unique optimal policy that satisfies this objective. This optimal policy takes the form of an exponential tilting of the reference distribution weighted by the exponentiated reward:

$$
\pi^*(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_\text{ref}(y|x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)
$$

In this equation, $Z(x)$ is known as the partition function or normalization constant. Its role is purely functional: it ensures that $\pi^*(y|x)$ sums to unity over all possible sequences $y$, thereby constituting a valid probability distribution. Explicitly defined as $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_\text{ref}(y|x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$ (or integrated over continuous spaces), $Z(x)$ depends solely on the input prompt $x$ and acts as an offset that varies with every new prompt encountered during generation. The presence of this partition function presents a significant hurdle for standard RL approaches because calculating it requires summing over exponentially many possible token sequences at every step of training or inference. Standard RL algorithms circumvent this difficulty by approximating gradients using samples (e.g., REINFORCE or PPO), effectively avoiding explicit computation of $Z(x)$ but introducing high-variance estimators and requiring complex stabilization techniques like clipping and value-function critics. However, if we accept this analytical form as our starting point rather than treating it as something to be approximated numerically via sampling loops, we unlock a path toward direct optimization. The existence of this closed-form relationship implies that if we know what our target optimal policy looks like relative to our reference model, we can immediately deduce what shape any compatible reward function must take. This invertibility is the cornerstone upon which DPO is built; it suggests that instead of searching for weights in an independent neural network acting as a reward predictor ($r_\phi$), we can treat our language model itself ($\pi_\theta$) as sufficient statistics for defining both the behavior and its associated utility landscape simultaneously.

## Reparameterization: Expressing Rewards as Log-Ratio of Policies

Having established that there exists an analytic mapping from rewards to policies via Equation 3 above ($\pi^* = Z^{-1} \pi_\text{ref} e^{r/\beta}$), our strategy now shifts toward inversion: Can we express any arbitrary reward function in terms of its corresponding optimal policy? If yes, then perhaps we can substitute this expression back into our preference learning objectives and eliminate $r$ entirely from our equations before they are ever minimized. To proceed with this "change of variables," let us manipulate Equation 3 algebraically to isolate $r(x,y)$. Taking the natural logarithm of both sides yields:

$$
\log \left( \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y|x)} Z(x)^{-1} e^{r(x,y)/\beta} \right) = 0
$$

Expanding this logarithmic identity separates terms dependent on individual outcomes from those depending only on context:

$$
-\log Z(x) + r(x,y)/\beta + \log \left( \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y|x)} \right) = 0
$$

By rearranging terms and multiplying through by $\beta$, we arrive at an elegant reparameterization formula expressing any valid reward function in terms of its induced optimal policy:

$$
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y|x)} + c(x)
$$

where $c(x) = -\beta \log Z(x)$ represents an additive term that depends exclusively on the prompt $x$. Crucially, notice that for any fixed prompt $x$, regardless of which response $y$ is considered good or bad relative to others *within* that context window (assuming they share identical prefixes leading up to decision points), adding or subtracting any function solely dependent on $x$ does not alter relative differences between completions generated for that same prompt. In other words, if you add an arbitrary constant vector indexed by inputs—but uniform across all outputs for each input—the ordering implied by rewards remains unchanged when comparing two candidates generated from identical contexts. This observation connects deeply with theoretical properties inherent in rank-based preference models discussed shortly; specifically under models like Bradley-Terry where only pairwise differences matter rather than absolute magnitudes.
