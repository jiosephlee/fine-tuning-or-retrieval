\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Clarification on initialization when no SFT model is available
Question:
In Section 3 under 'DPO outline', the authors state: 'Since the preference datasets are sampled using $
ho^{SFT}$, we initialize $
ho_{ref} = 
ho^{SFT}$ whenever available.' However, they immediately follow with: 'when $
ho^{SFT}$ is not available, we initialize $
ho_{ref}$ by maximizing likelihood of preferred completions...'

Wait, isn't maximizing likelihood of preferred completions exactly what Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) *is*? So why distinguish between '$
ho^{SFT}$' and '$
ho_{ref}$ initialized by max likelihood'? Are they implying that sometimes you don't have an SFT checkpoint file saved from before you collected preferences?

Also, if you create your own reference policy by doing SFT on just the preferred data ($y_w$), isn't there now a massive distributional shift between your new $
ho_{ref}$ and whatever policy originally generated those preferences? If humans labeled pairs generated by Model A (unknown), but you use Model B (trained on winners) as your reference for DPO equations like Eq. 7 ($	ext{log}(rac{	heta}{	ext{Ref}})$), won't this mismatch break the theoretical guarantee that DPO solves Eq. 6?
Answer:
You have correctly identified a subtle but critical distinction in the DPO pipeline regarding the definition of the reference policy, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, versus the starting point for optimization, $\pi_{\theta}$. The confusion arises from conflating the *source* of the preference data with the *initialization* of the reference model required by the DPO loss function.

First, let's address whether maximizing likelihood on preferred completions constitutes SFT. Technically, yes; training a model on $(x, y_w)$ pairs via maximum likelihood estimation is precisely what Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) is. However, the paper distinguishes between $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ (the specific checkpoint that actually generated the preference data) and a surrogate $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ constructed when that original checkpoint is missing. In standard RLHF pipelines described in Section 3 ("Preliminaries"), preference pairs are sampled from a specific SFT model: $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pi^{\text{SFT}}(\cdot \mid x)$. The theoretical derivation of DPO in Equation 6 assumes that $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ in the KL constraint matches this sampling distribution. When you possess the original $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ checkpoint, you set $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi^{\text{SFT}}$. When you do not (as in the Anthropic HH dialogue dataset case mentioned in Section 5), you cannot simply guess what distribution generated those pairs. Instead, you approximate this missing reference by training a fresh model solely on the preferred completions $y_w$ to get close to that unknown sampling distribution. This is done to "mitigate the distribution shift," as stated in Section 3 ("DPO outline").

Regarding your concern about distributional shift breaking theoretical guarantees: you are right to worry about mismatches here, but the impact is nuanced. The theoretical guarantee in Equation 6 relies on $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ being the policy used to define the reward function's baseline. If you use a $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ trained only on winners ($y_w$), it will indeed differ from Model A (the unknown generator). However, notice that DPO optimizes a relative score: $\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}$. By initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ via SFT on $y_w$, you ensure that for "good" responses $y_w$, the denominator $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)$ is reasonably high. If you had used a random base model as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, it might assign near-zero probability to high-quality outputs $y_w$, causing numerical instability or forcing $\pi_\theta$ to overfit aggressively to compensate for a vanishing denominator.

The "breakage" of theory occurs if $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is so far from the true sampling distribution that it fails to act as a proper anchor for regularization. However, because DPO effectively learns an implicit reward $r(x,y) = \beta \log (\pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{ref}})$, using an SFT-initialized $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ ensures that this implicit reward starts from a sensible baseline where good responses already have non-negligible probability mass relative to bad ones. The authors argue in Section 5 that despite this approximation (using Preferred-FT as a proxy for SFT), DPO still outperforms PPO and matches Best-of-N baselines, suggesting that while perfect alignment between $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and the true generator is ideal, a strong SFT approximation suffices for stability and performance in practice. Essentially, you are trading strict adherence to Equation 6's assumptions for practical feasibility when historical checkpoints are lost, relying on the robustness of the Bradley-Terry formulation to handle slight deviations in the reference baseline.
