Answer:
Your concern stems from conflating the **expressivity of the mathematical reparameterization** with the **approximation capacity of the specific neural network architecture** used to implement it. Theorem 1 in the paper guarantees that the *form* of the reward function $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ does not restrict the class of learnable preferences, provided we have a sufficiently expressive policy $\pi$.

The core insight lies in Lemma 1 and Definition 1, which establish that preference models like Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce are invariant to adding any function $f(x)$ to the reward. Specifically, if two rewards differ only by a term dependent solely on the prompt $x$, they induce identical preference distributions because the comparison $r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)$ cancels out any such $f(x)$. Consequently, there is an entire equivalence class of reward functions that map to the same optimal policy and preference data. Theorem 1 proves that for *any* arbitrary reward function $r^*$, there exists a member within its equivalence class that can be exactly represented by your proposed reparameterization. The proof constructs this specific member by subtracting the log-partition function (which depends only on $x$) from $r^*$, effectively projecting it onto a subspace where it matches $\beta \log \frac{\pi_r}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$.

Therefore, theoretically, you lose no generality regarding what preferences can be modeled. If there exists a complex non-linear ground-truth reward surface, there is mathematically guaranteed to be a corresponding policy $\pi^*$ such that taking its log-ratio against $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ recovers a valid reward function within that equivalence class. The limitation you identified regarding finite neural network capacity is real but distinct from the theoretical claim of Theorem 1; it is an approximation error common to all deep learning methods (including standard RLHF), not a structural flaw unique to DPO's reparameterization. In standard RLHF, if your explicit reward model $r_\phi$ lacks capacity to fit the complex ground truth, you fail; similarly, in DPO, if your policy network $\pi_\theta$ lacks capacity to represent the optimal policy $\pi^*$ required to generate those preferences via the log-ratio formula, you will also fail. However, since modern LMs are highly expressive and trained specifically to approximate complex conditional distributions $\pi(y|x)$, they are generally capable of representing these implicit rewards just as well as an explicit MLP-based reward head can represent explicit scores. The advantage of DPO is simply that it forces the model to learn this representation directly through classification loss rather than requiring a separate training loop for an intermediate reward model and subsequent RL optimization.
