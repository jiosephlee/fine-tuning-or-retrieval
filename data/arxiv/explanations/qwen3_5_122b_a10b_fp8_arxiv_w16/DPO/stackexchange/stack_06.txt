They argue that without this term (or approximating it poorly), gradients have high variance. However, in DPO (Eq. 7), there is absolutely no such summation over all possible outputs $y$. We only care about two specific tokens/sequences ($y_w$ and $y_l$).

How can DPO avoid needing this global normalization term entirely? Isn't maximizing relative log-probabilities without knowing how likely other sequences are risky? The paper suggests DPO yields a reward function where this term cancels out naturally due to parameterization. Answer:
The reason DPO avoids the explicit normalization term (the "soft value function") required in PPO lies in the fundamental difference between optimizing a reward function versus optimizing a policy directly through a change of variables. In standard PPO, the objective involves maximizing expected reward minus a KL penalty, which mathematically necessitates a partition function $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp(r(x,y)/\beta)$ to ensure the resulting policy is a valid probability distribution. As noted in Section 4.2 of the paper, omitting this term or approximating it poorly leads to high-variance gradients because the advantage estimation becomes unstable without a proper baseline that accounts for the entire output space.

However, DPO circumvents this by leveraging an analytical mapping derived from the optimal solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization problem. As shown in Equation 9 of the paper (derived from Eq. 5), any optimal policy $\pi^*$ can be expressed as $\pi^*(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)$. By taking the logarithm and rearranging, we get $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. The critical insight here is that human preference models, specifically the Bradley-Terry model used in Eq. 1, depend only on the *difference* between rewards for two sequences ($r(y_w) - r(y_l)$). When you substitute the reparameterized reward expression into this difference, the term $\beta \log Z(x)$ appears in both parts of the subtraction and cancels out perfectly:

$$ r(y_w) - r(y_l) = \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right) - \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right) $$

This cancellation eliminates the need to ever compute or approximate $Z(x)$, which is computationally prohibitive as it requires summing over all possible outputs $y$. Consequently, DPO transforms the problem from estimating an absolute reward function (which requires global normalization for stability in actor-critic updates) to directly fitting a policy that satisfies pairwise preferences via a simple binary cross-entropy loss (Eq. 7). The "variance risk" associated with missing baselines in PPO arises because PPO tries to estimate values relative to an unknown global expectation; DPO sidesteps this entirely by operating solely on relative log-ratios within observed pairs, effectively embedding the necessary normalization constraint directly into the policy parameterization itself rather than treating it as an external correction term.
