\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Confused about why the stabilizer state $m_t$ doesn't change the final output in sLSTM
Question:
I am reading the section on sLSTM stability (Equation 5). The paper defines a stabilizer state $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$ and then modifies the gates to $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ by subtracting $m_t$. The authors claim that replacing $f_t$ and $i_t$ with these stabilized versions 'does neither change the output... nor the derivatives'. 

However, looking at the cell state update equation $c_t = f'_t c_{t-1} + i'_t z_t$, if I plug in the definitions, it seems like I am multiplying everything by exponential factors involving differences of logs. Why exactly does this normalization cancel out perfectly so that the effective value of $c_t$ remains identical to the unstable version? Intuitively, shouldn't changing the magnitude of the gates affect how much information flows into the cell?
Answer:
The reason the stabilizer state $m_t$ does not alter the final output or the derivatives lies in the fact that the stabilization procedure is essentially a global rescaling of the numerator ($c_t$) and the denominator ($n_t$) by the exact same factor, which cancels out completely in the calculation of the hidden state $\tilde{h}_t$.

To see this intuitively, recall that in sLSTM, the hidden state is computed as a normalized ratio: $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$. Both the cell state $c_t$ and the normalizer state $n_t$ are defined recursively with multiplicative forget gates ($f$) and additive input gates ($i$). If you expand these recursions backward in time, you find that every term contributing to $c_t$ (which represents accumulated information) and every term contributing to $n_t$ (which represents accumulated normalization weight) shares a common history of forgetting. Specifically, a contribution from time step $\tau < t$ appears in both $c_t$ and $n_t$ multiplied by the product of all subsequent forget gates: $\prod_{k=\tau+1}^t f_k$.

When exponential gating is used, these products can become extremely large or small, causing numerical overflow or underflow. The stabilizer $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$ effectively tracks the logarithm of this cumulative scaling factor. By defining the stabilized gates as $i'_t = \exp(\log(i_t) - m_t)$ and $f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)$, you are mathematically subtracting this log-scaling factor from every gate operation at step $t$. Consequently, when you propagate these stabilized gates forward to compute $\tilde{c}_t$ and $\tilde{n}_t$, every single term in both expansions is divided by exactly $\exp(m_t)$.

Because both the numerator (accumulated content) and the denominator (accumulated normalization weight) are scaled down by the identical factor $\exp(m_t)$ relative to their unstable counterparts, their ratio remains invariant:
$$ \frac{\tilde{c}_t}{\tilde{n}_t} = \frac{\exp(-m_t) c_t}{\exp(-m_t) n_t} = \frac{c_t}{n_t} $$
Thus, while the internal magnitudes of $c'_t$ and $n'_t$ differ significantly from $c_t$ and $n_n$, their quotient—the actual signal passed to the activation function—is numerically identical. Since the output is unchanged, and since differentiation follows standard calculus rules where constants cancel out in ratios (or more formally, because this transformation is just a re-parameterization that preserves functional equivalence), the gradients flowing back through time remain unaffected. The "effective value" of information flow is preserved because you are simply dividing both sides of an equation by a non-zero constant at every step.

### How does mLSTM achieve O(L) complexity if it maintains a full dxd matrix memory?
Question:
The abstract claims xLSTM has 'constant memory complexity' regarding sequence length, unlike Transformers. In Section 3.3 (mLSTM), the cell state is defined as a matrix $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ updated via a covariance rule: $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. 

If I need to store this entire $d \times d$ matrix for every layer, isn't that actually quadratic in terms of hidden dimension ($O(d^2)$)? While this is constant with respect to sequence length $L$, usually 'linear complexity' refers to both dimensions. Furthermore, updating a dense matrix at every time step involves computing an outer product and adding it. If I have a batch size of 1, isn't this operation just as heavy per token as a standard attention calculation? How is this considered 'parallelizable' in practice if I still have to iterate through time steps to update this accumulating matrix?
Answer:
Your confusion stems from conflating the complexity dependence on sequence length ($L$) with the complexity dependence on hidden dimension ($d$), as well as overlooking the distinction between sequential recurrence and parallelizable matrix operations. The paper explicitly clarifies that mLSTM achieves **linear complexity in $L$** (often referred to as $O(L)$ in the context of sequence processing) while maintaining a **constant memory footprint relative to $L$**, even though the memory itself scales quadratically with the hidden dimension ($O(d^2)$).

When the authors claim "constant memory complexity" regarding sequence length, they strictly mean that the memory required to store the state $\mathbf{C}_t$ does not grow as the input sequence gets longer. Unlike Transformers, which require storing an attention map of size $L \times L$ (leading to $O(L^2)$ memory growth), mLSTM stores a single fixed-size matrix $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ regardless of whether $L=100$ or $L=100,000$. Consequently, for inference or streaming scenarios where $L$ grows indefinitely, mLSTM's memory usage remains bounded by $O(d^2)$, whereas a Transformer's usage explodes. This is why it is termed "constant" with respect to $L$, even if it is technically quadratic with respect to $d$.

Regarding your concern about computational heaviness and parallelization: while updating $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ involves an outer product that costs $O(d^2)$ per token, this operation is fundamentally different from standard recurrent units because it lacks **memory mixing**. As stated in Section 3.3 and Section 4 ("Memory and Speed Considerations"), mLSTM abandons hidden-hidden recurrent connections ($h_{t-1}$ dependencies in the gates). Instead, all gates ($i_t, f_t, o_t$) and projections ($\mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t, \mathbf{q}_t$) depend solely on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$. This decoupling allows the entire sequence of updates to be computed in parallel using associative scans (similar to how FlashAttention or State Space Models operate), rather than iterating sequentially token-by-token.

You mentioned that updating a dense matrix seems as heavy as attention; however, standard self-attention computes interactions between *every pair* of tokens simultaneously ($O(L^2 d)$ total work), which prevents true parallelism across time steps without approximation. In contrast, mLSTM performs a localized $O(d^2)$ update for each token independently. While the per-token cost is indeed higher than a scalar RNN cell due to the matrix nature of $\mathbf{C}$, this cost is incurred exactly once per token and can be massively parallelized across a batch or sequence using GPU tensor cores. The paper notes in Section 4 that these computations "can be done in parallel on GPUs," resulting in wall-clock times comparable to other linear-complexity models despite the heavier arithmetic per step. Thus, mLSTM trades increased parameter-independent computation ($d^2$ ops/token) for reduced sequence-dependent memory ($O(1)$ vs $O(L)$) and full temporal parallelization during training.

### What is the exact role of memory mixing in sLSTM vs mLSTM regarding expressivity?
Question:
The paper emphasizes that sLSTM includes 'memory mixing' (recurrent connections between hidden states) while mLSTM explicitly removes it to gain parallelizability. In Section 4.1, it mentions that models without memory mixing (like pure Transformers or SSMs) cannot solve certain formal language tasks like parity because they lack state tracking capabilities.

My confusion lies in the distinction: Does removing memory mixing in mLSTM strictly limit its theoretical capability to recognize non-regular languages? If mLSTM uses a massive matrix memory ($\mathbf{C}$) effectively acting as associative recall, why isn't that sufficient for state tracking? Is it true that only sLSTM can theoretically solve tasks requiring precise sequential state updates (like counting or matching parentheses), making xLSTM architectures dependent on having *some* sLSTM blocks (like xLSTM[7:1]) rather than being purely mLSTM?
Answer:
Your intuition is correct: according to the paper, removing memory mixing in mLSTM strictly limits its theoretical capability to solve tasks requiring precise sequential state tracking, such as recognizing non-regular languages or solving the parity problem. While mLSTM possesses a massive matrix memory $\mathbf{C}$ that excels at associative recall, it lacks the mechanism to dynamically update a global state based on previous hidden representations, which is essential for counting or matching nested structures.

The paper explicitly distinguishes these capabilities in Section 4.1 ("Synthetic Tasks") and the "Most Related" subsection of Section 3. It cites prior work (Merrill et al., Deletang et al.) establishing that models without memory mixing—specifically Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs)—cannot solve formal language tasks like parity because they lack "state tracking" capabilities. The authors argue that memory mixing (the recurrent hidden-hidden connections present in sLSTM) is the critical component that enables a model to evolve its internal state representation sequentially, thereby allowing it to track entities or count items over time. Without this feedback loop where $h_{t-1}$ influences the calculation of $h_t$ directly through recurrent weights $\mathbf{R}$, the system behaves more like a finite-state automaton with limited expressivity regarding sequential dependencies.

In contrast, mLSTM replaces this sequential dependency with a parallelizable covariance update rule $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. This structure creates an associative memory akin to Bidirectional Associative Memories (BAMs), which is highly effective for retrieving stored key-value pairs (as demonstrated in the Multi-Query Associative Recall experiments). However, this mechanism stores information as a superposition of outer products rather than maintaining a coherent, evolving state vector that encodes the history of operations. Consequently, while mLSTM can remember *what* was seen (associative recall), it struggles to remember *how* things relate sequentially if that relationship requires iterative state updates (e.g., incrementing a counter for every open parenthesis).

Therefore, the paper posits that xLSTM architectures depend on including sLSTM blocks (such as in xLSTM[7:1]) precisely to bridge this gap. The experimental results in Figure 1 ("formal-main") confirm this hypothesis: xLSTM[0:1] (pure sLSTM) achieves near-perfect accuracy on parity and other formal grammar tasks where pure mLSTM [1:0] fails significantly. The hybrid approach allows xLSTM to leverage the parallel efficiency and high storage capacity of mLSTM for general language modeling while retaining the necessary expressive power of sLSTM's memory mixing to handle complex logical constraints and state-tracking requirements inherent in natural language and code generation.

### Clarification on the denominator normalization in mLSTM hidden state calculation
Question:
In Equation 7 for mLSTM, the hidden state calculation involves dividing by $\max(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1)$. Here, $\mathbf{n}_t$ is described as a 'normalizer state' representing the weighted sum of keys.

In standard attention mechanisms (softmax), we divide by a sum of exponentials to ensure probabilities sum to 1. Here, we seem to be doing something different: taking an absolute dot product and lower-bounding it by 1. 

Is this division intended to approximate an L2 norm normalization or simply prevent division by zero/near-zero values when keys are orthogonal to queries? Also, why take the absolute value inside the max function instead of just ensuring positivity elsewhere? Does this specific choice affect gradient flow differently than standard LayerNorm applied after retrieval?
Answer:
The denominator $\max(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1)$ in the mLSTM hidden state calculation serves primarily as a numerical stability mechanism to prevent division by zero or near-zero values, rather than functioning as an L2 norm approximation or a probability normalization akin to softmax. As explicitly stated in the paper under Section 3.3 (mLSTM), the authors note that "since the dot product between query and normalizer state can be close to zero," they employ this specific bounding strategy referencing prior work by Sun et al. [Sun:23arxiv]. The normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$ accumulates the weighted sum of keys over time, analogous to the denominator in standard attention mechanisms but adapted for the exponential gating scheme used in xLSTM. Unlike softmax, which normalizes weights to sum to unity across a distribution, this operation ensures that the magnitude of the retrieved signal remains bounded relative to the accumulated "strength" of the gates without enforcing a probabilistic interpretation.

The inclusion of the absolute value $|\cdot|$ is crucial because it treats negative correlations symmetrically with positive ones regarding magnitude. In this context, if the query $\mathbf{q}_t$ aligns negatively with the accumulated key history $\mathbf{n}_t$, the dot product would be negative; however, for retrieval purposes, a strong negative alignment implies significant information content that should still scale the output appropriately. By taking the absolute value before applying the maximum lower bound, the model ensures that whether the interaction is strongly positive or strongly negative, it contributes meaningfully to scaling, while strictly preventing scenarios where orthogonality (resulting in a dot product near zero) causes numerical instability or exploding gradients. This design choice effectively decouples the scaling factor from sign-specific cancellation effects that could occur if one simply ensured positivity elsewhere without considering magnitude symmetry.

Furthermore, this formulation differs significantly from applying Layer Normalization after retrieval. While LayerNorm would normalize across feature dimensions post-retrieval based on variance and mean statistics of that specific vector, this denominator acts as a dynamic gain control based on temporal accumulation history ($\mathbf{n}_t$). It stabilizes the gradient flow during backpropagation by ensuring that updates related to the memory matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ are never divided by infinitesimal numbers, which could otherwise lead to unstable learning dynamics in recurrent settings with exponential gates. The lower bound of 1 essentially acts as a floor for this gain control, ensuring that even when no strong correlation exists between current query and historical keys (orthogonal case), the system defaults to a stable identity-like scaling rather than amplifying noise through division by near-zero values.

### Why use pre-up-projection for mLSTM but post-up-projection for sLSTM blocks?
Question:
Section 3.4 discusses xLSTM Blocks and references Cover's Theorem regarding separating patterns in higher-dimensional spaces. It states:
"For an xLSTM block containing an sLSTM, we use the post up-projection block... For an xLSTM block containing an mLSTEM, we use the pre up-projection block since the memory capacity becomes larger in the high-dimensional space."

Can someone explain the intuition here? Usually, MLP blocks follow either Pre-Norm/Pre-Projections or Post-Norm/Post-Projections consistently across architectures (like standard Transformers). Why does switching from scalar memory (sLSTM) to matrix memory (mLSTM) necessitate flipping whether we project up *before* or *after* applying non-linearity/recurrence? Specifically, how does projecting inputs into high-dim space *before* feeding them into an mLSTM cell improve its capacity compared to projecting after?
Answer:
The distinction between pre-up-projection for mLSTM and post-up-projection for sLSTM stems directly from how each variant handles the dimensionality of its internal memory state relative to the input feature space, specifically invoking Cover's Theorem to maximize pattern separability.

As outlined in Section 3.4, the primary goal of the xLSTM block is to non-linearly summarize past history in a high-dimensional space to facilitate the linear separation of distinct contexts. Cover's Theorem suggests that patterns become more easily separable when mapped into a sufficiently high-dimensional space. The decision to place the projection (the "up" step) before or after the recurrent unit depends on where that high-dimensional expansion actually occurs within the cell's mechanics.

For **mLSTM**, the authors explicitly design a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ where $d$ represents the hidden dimension. As described in Section 3.3, this memory stores key-value pairs via an outer product update rule ($\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$). Crucially, if you project the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ into this higher-dimensional space *before* entering the mLSTM cell, you effectively expand the rank and capacity of this matrix memory immediately. By projecting first, the keys $\mathbf{k}_t$ and values $\mathbf{v}_t$ exist in a richer, higher-dimensional manifold before being written into $\mathbf{C}$. This ensures that the "memory capacity becomes larger in the high-dimensional space," allowing for finer-grained associative recall and better separation of complex patterns within the matrix itself. If you were to use post-up-projection (processing in low dimensions first), you would be constraining the information stored in the matrix memory to a lower-rank subspace before expanding it later, negating the benefit of having a full $d \times d$ storage mechanism designed for high-capacity retrieval.

Conversely, **sLSTM** retains a scalar (or vector) memory structure similar to traditional LSTMs, where information is compressed into a single cell state per neuron (or head). In this configuration, expanding dimensions *before* processing does not inherently increase the storage capacity of a scalar state; instead, it risks diluting information across redundant dimensions without adding structural richness to the memory update mechanism itself. Therefore, sLSTM follows the standard Transformer-like "post-up-projection" architecture: it processes temporal dependencies and updates its scalar memory within its native dimensionality (often with memory mixing via recurrent weights), and only projects to a higher dimension *after* this summary has been computed. This aligns with your observation that sLSTM relies on exponential gating and memory mixing rather than raw dimensional expansion for its expressiveness.

In essence, mLSTM flips the standard order because its unique advantage lies in utilizing high-dimensional matrix algebra for storage; thus, it requires high-dimensional inputs to populate that matrix effectively. sLSTM maintains standard ordering because its power comes from sophisticated gating dynamics on compact states rather than dimensional scaling of the storage medium itself.

### Does exponential gating imply numerical instability despite stabilization techniques?
Question:
The paper introduces exponential gating ($i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$) as a key innovation over standard sigmoid gates to allow revisiting storage decisions. However, exponentials grow extremely fast.

Even with the stabilization technique using $m_t$, are there practical scenarios where gradients could still explode or vanish more easily than with sigmoids? Specifically, if $	ilde{i}_t$ becomes slightly negative large numbers during early training stages before stabilization kicks in effectively, does $	ilde{i}_t - m_t$ guarantee safety? Also, how sensitive is this architecture to weight initialization compared to standard LSTMs where sigmoid gates naturally clamp values between 0 and 1?
Answer:
Your concern regarding the numerical stability of exponential gating is well-founded, as the raw exponential function $\exp(x)$ indeed grows rapidly and poses risks of overflow or vanishing gradients if left unchecked. However, the xLSTM paper explicitly addresses this through a log-space stabilization mechanism that guarantees safety regardless of whether $\tilde{i}_t$ becomes a large negative number during early training. The key lies in the fact that the architecture operates on the difference between the current gate logits and a running maximum stabilizer state $m_t$, rather than applying the exponential directly to unstable raw values.

Specifically, the stabilization technique defined in Equation (3) of the paper computes a stabilizer state $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$. When calculating the stabilized input gate $i'_t$, the formula is $\exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)$. Mathematically, since $m_t$ is defined as the maximum of all previous log-gate values (accumulated with forget factors), it follows by definition that $\tilde{i}_t \le m_t$ at every time step. Consequently, the exponent $(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)$ is always less than or equal to zero. This ensures that $i'_t$ is strictly bounded within the interval $(0, 1]$, effectively clamping the value just as a sigmoid does, but derived from an unbounded exponential distribution. Even if $\tilde{i}_t$ starts as a large negative number (e.g., $-100$) before training converges, $m_t$ will either remain at its initial safe value or decrease appropriately to ensure the difference remains non-positive, preventing any explosion in magnitude.

Regarding sensitivity to weight initialization compared to standard LSTMs, xLSTM requires careful initialization primarily for the forget gate biases to prevent immediate saturation or collapse of memory retention, a requirement shared with standard LSTMs where forgetting too quickly leads to vanishing gradients. However, unlike standard LSTMs where sigmoid gates naturally constrain outputs between 0 and 1 immediately upon initialization regardless of weight magnitudes, xLSTM relies on this dynamic subtraction by $m_t$. If initialized poorly such that $m_t$ fails to track correctly or if numerical underflow occurs in intermediate calculations before stabilization updates, transient instability could theoretically occur. Yet, because the final operation involves subtracting a quantity guaranteed to be greater than or equal to $\tilde{i}_t$, the system inherently resists gradient explosion in the forward pass. The gradients flowing backward are also controlled because they depend on these bounded stabilized values $i'_t$ and $f'_t$, meaning the derivative magnitudes do not scale exponentially with depth as they would with raw exponentials. Thus, while initialization strategies matter for convergence speed and avoiding local minima (particularly ensuring forget gates start near 1), the architectural design itself prevents catastrophic numerical failure during training phases where raw activations might otherwise diverge.
