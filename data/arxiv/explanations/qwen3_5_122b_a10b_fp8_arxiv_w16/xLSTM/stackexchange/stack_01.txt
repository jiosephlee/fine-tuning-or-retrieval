\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Confused about why the stabilizer state $m_t$ doesn't change the final output in sLSTM
Question:
I am reading the section on sLSTM stability (Equation 5). The paper defines a stabilizer state $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$ and then modifies the gates to $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ by subtracting $m_t$. The authors claim that replacing $f_t$ and $i_t$ with these stabilized versions 'does neither change the output... nor the derivatives'. 

However, looking at the cell state update equation $c_t = f'_t c_{t-1} + i'_t z_t$, if I plug in the definitions, it seems like I am multiplying everything by exponential factors involving differences of logs. Why exactly does this normalization cancel out perfectly so that the effective value of $c_t$ remains identical to the unstable version? Intuitively, shouldn't changing the magnitude of the gates affect how much information flows into the cell?
Answer:
The reason the stabilizer state $m_t$ does not alter the final output or the derivatives lies in the fact that the stabilization procedure is essentially a global rescaling of the numerator ($c_t$) and the denominator ($n_t$) by the exact same factor, which cancels out completely in the calculation of the hidden state $\tilde{h}_t$.

To see this intuitively, recall that in sLSTM, the hidden state is computed as a normalized ratio: $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$. Both the cell state $c_t$ and the normalizer state $n_t$ are defined recursively with multiplicative forget gates ($f$) and additive input gates ($i$). If you expand these recursions backward in time, you find that every term contributing to $c_t$ (which represents accumulated information) and every term contributing to $n_t$ (which represents accumulated normalization weight) shares a common history of forgetting. Specifically, a contribution from time step $\tau < t$ appears in both $c_t$ and $n_t$ multiplied by the product of all subsequent forget gates: $\prod_{k=\tau+1}^t f_k$.

When exponential gating is used, these products can become extremely large or small, causing numerical overflow or underflow. The stabilizer $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$ effectively tracks the logarithm of this cumulative scaling factor. By defining the stabilized gates as $i'_t = \exp(\log(i_t) - m_t)$ and $f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)$, you are mathematically subtracting this log-scaling factor from every gate operation at step $t$. Consequently, when you propagate these stabilized gates forward to compute $\tilde{c}_t$ and $\tilde{n}_t$, every single term in both expansions is divided by exactly $\exp(m_t)$.

Because both the numerator (accumulated content) and the denominator (accumulated normalization weight) are scaled down by the identical factor $\exp(m_t)$ relative to their unstable counterparts, their ratio remains invariant:
$$ \frac{\tilde{c}_t}{\tilde{n}_t} = \frac{\exp(-m_t) c_t}{\exp(-m_t) n_t} = \frac{c_t}{n_t} $$
Thus, while the internal magnitudes of $c'_t$ and $n'_t$ differ significantly from $c_t$ and $n_n$, their quotient—the actual signal passed to the activation function—is numerically identical. Since the output is unchanged, and since differentiation follows standard calculus rules where constants cancel out in ratios (or more formally, because this transformation is just a re-parameterization that preserves functional equivalence), the gradients flowing back through time remain unaffected. The "effective value" of information flow is preserved because you are simply dividing both sides of an equation by a non-zero constant at every step.
