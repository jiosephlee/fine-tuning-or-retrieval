\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Clarification on the denominator normalization in mLSTM hidden state calculation
Question:
In Equation 7 for mLSTM, the hidden state calculation involves dividing by $\max(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1)$. Here, $\mathbf{n}_t$ is described as a 'normalizer state' representing the weighted sum of keys.

In standard attention mechanisms (softmax), we divide by a sum of exponentials to ensure probabilities sum to 1. Here, we seem to be doing something different: taking an absolute dot product and lower-bounding it by 1. 

Is this division intended to approximate an L2 norm normalization or simply prevent division by zero/near-zero values when keys are orthogonal to queries? Also, why take the absolute value inside the max function instead of just ensuring positivity elsewhere? Does this specific choice affect gradient flow differently than standard LayerNorm applied after retrieval?
Answer:
The denominator $\max(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1)$ in the mLSTM hidden state calculation serves primarily as a numerical stability mechanism to prevent division by zero or near-zero values, rather than functioning as an L2 norm approximation or a probability normalization akin to softmax. As explicitly stated in the paper under Section 3.3 (mLSTM), the authors note that "since the dot product between query and normalizer state can be close to zero," they employ this specific bounding strategy referencing prior work by Sun et al. [Sun:23arxiv]. The normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$ accumulates the weighted sum of keys over time, analogous to the denominator in standard attention mechanisms but adapted for the exponential gating scheme used in xLSTM. Unlike softmax, which normalizes weights to sum to unity across a distribution, this operation ensures that the magnitude of the retrieved signal remains bounded relative to the accumulated "strength" of the gates without enforcing a probabilistic interpretation.

The inclusion of the absolute value $|\cdot|$ is crucial because it treats negative correlations symmetrically with positive ones regarding magnitude. In this context, if the query $\mathbf{q}_t$ aligns negatively with the accumulated key history $\mathbf{n}_t$, the dot product would be negative; however, for retrieval purposes, a strong negative alignment implies significant information content that should still scale the output appropriately. By taking the absolute value before applying the maximum lower bound, the model ensures that whether the interaction is strongly positive or strongly negative, it contributes meaningfully to scaling, while strictly preventing scenarios where orthogonality (resulting in a dot product near zero) causes numerical instability or exploding gradients. This design choice effectively decouples the scaling factor from sign-specific cancellation effects that could occur if one simply ensured positivity elsewhere without considering magnitude symmetry.

Furthermore, this formulation differs significantly from applying Layer Normalization after retrieval. While LayerNorm would normalize across feature dimensions post-retrieval based on variance and mean statistics of that specific vector, this denominator acts as a dynamic gain control based on temporal accumulation history ($\mathbf{n}_t$). It stabilizes the gradient flow during backpropagation by ensuring that updates related to the memory matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ are never divided by infinitesimal numbers, which could otherwise lead to unstable learning dynamics in recurrent settings with exponential gates. The lower bound of 1 essentially acts as a floor for this gain control, ensuring that even when no strong correlation exists between current query and historical keys (orthogonal case), the system defaults to a stable identity-like scaling rather than amplifying noise through division by near-zero values.
