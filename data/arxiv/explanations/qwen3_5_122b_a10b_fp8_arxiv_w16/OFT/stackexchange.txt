\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Confused about the definition of 'Hyperspherical Energy': Why is it defined as the inverse of distances rather than the sum of cosines?
Question:
In Section 3.2, Equation (1), the paper defines Hyperspherical Energy (HE) as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$. Earlier in the abstract and introduction, it mentions HE captures 'pairwise neuron relationship' and 'uniformity'. Usually, measures of uniformity or repulsion involve maximizing distances or minimizing similarities (cosine similarity). Here, the formula sums the *inverse* of the Euclidean distance between normalized vectors. If two neurons are close together (distance $\to 0$), this term explodes to infinity. Is this formulation intended to act as a repulsive force where we want to *maximize* this energy to spread neurons out? Or is there a sign convention I am missing? The text says we want to 'preserve' this value, but doesn't explicitly state whether high or low energy corresponds to better semantic preservation in this specific context.
Answer:
Your confusion stems from interpreting "energy" in the thermodynamic sense (where systems seek minimum energy states) versus its usage here as a specific **repulsive potential** derived from electrostatics, specifically the Thomson problem. You are correct that the formula sums the inverse of distances, but the goal is indeed to **maximize** this quantity (or rather, maintain a high, stable value) to ensure uniform distribution, though the paper frames the objective as **preserving** this value relative to the pretrained model.

To understand why $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ is used instead of cosine similarity directly, consider the geometry of the unit hypersphere. The Euclidean distance between two normalized vectors $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$ is related to their dot product (cosine similarity) by the law of cosines: $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^2 = 2 - 2\langle \hat{\bm{w}}_i, \hat{\bm{w}}_j \rangle$. Consequently, minimizing cosine similarity (making vectors orthogonal or anti-correlated) is roughly equivalent to maximizing Euclidean distance. However, defining energy as the sum of inverse distances creates a **singular repulsive force**. As two neurons get arbitrarily close ($\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\| \to 0$), their contribution to the total energy explodes toward infinity. This mathematical singularity acts as a hard barrier preventing neuron collapse; if any pair of neurons were to cluster together, the "energy" would become prohibitively large.

Therefore, in physics-inspired terminology used in this field (referenced from Liu et al., cited in your paper), a configuration with high uniformity corresponds to a state where neurons are maximally separated, which paradoxically results in a **large** Hyperspherical Energy value because every pairwise term contributes significantly without any term vanishing or becoming singularly dominant due to proximity. Conversely, if neurons clump together, the inverse-distance terms for those pairs explode, making the total energy undefined or extremely unstable depending on regularization, but conceptually, we view "low energy" in this specific repulsive potential context as undesirable clustering if we were minimizing it. However, the authors clarify in Section 4 ("Intriguing Insights") that unlike classification tasks where one might *minimize* hyperspherical energy to encourage uniform spacing from scratch (reducing redundancy), finetuning requires **preservation**.

The critical insight in Section 3.2 and Equation (1) is not whether high or low absolute values are "better," but that **orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles**, and thus preserve pairwise Euclidean distances on the unit sphere. Since $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ depends solely on these distances, applying an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ such that $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ ensures that $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \|\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0}_i - \widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0}_j\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|$. Consequently, $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$. The paper argues that this specific structural invariant—the exact distribution of pairwise angles captured by this energy metric—is what encodes the semantic knowledge of the pretrained model. If you were to simply minimize or maximize this energy during finetuning (as done in training-from-scratch classification), you would distort these angles and destroy the pre-trained semantic relationships. Thus, OFT treats Hyperspherical Energy not as a loss function to be optimized toward zero or infinity, but as an invariant quantity that must remain constant ($\|\text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|=0$) to guarantee that the fundamental "shape" of the feature space remains intact while allowing for directional shifts via rotation.

### Does Cayley parameterization restrict OFT from modeling reflections, and does this matter for image generation?
Question:
Section 3.3 states that using Cayley parameterization ($\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(I-\bm{Q})^{-1}$) allows us to efficiently enforce orthogonality, but notes a limitation: it can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$ (special orthogonal group $SO(d)$), excluding reflections (determinant $-1$). The paper claims this limitation 'does not affect performance in practice.' However, mathematically, an orthogonal transformation includes both rotations and reflections. If a downstream task theoretically requires flipping the orientation of features (a reflection) to match the target distribution, OFT with Cayley parameterization might be fundamentally incapable of learning that specific transformation. Has anyone tested if allowing reflections actually helps in diffusion model finetuning, or is the feature space robust enough that rotations alone suffice?
Answer:
Your observation is mathematically precise: the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ indeed restricts the learned transformation $\bm{R}$ to the Special Orthogonal Group $SO(d)$, meaning it can only represent rotations (determinant $+1$) and excludes reflections (determinant $-1$). Consequently, strictly speaking, OFT cannot learn a pure reflection transformation. However, the authors assert this limitation does not impact practical performance because the primary mechanism for semantic modification in this context relies on rotating the neuron coordinate system rather than reflecting it.

The theoretical justification lies in the nature of hyperspherical energy and how semantic information is encoded. As argued in Section 3.1 and supported by their toy experiment in Figure 2, the angular relationships between neurons (pairwise angles on the unit hypersphere) are the dominant carriers of semantic information, while magnitudes are secondary. An orthogonal transformation preserves these pairwise angles regardless of whether it is a rotation or a reflection. Since the goal of finetuning is to adjust the *orientation* of the feature space to align with new data distributions while maintaining the internal geometric structure (hyperspherical energy) of the pretrained model, a rotation is generally sufficient to map one valid configuration of features to another. In high-dimensional spaces typical of deep neural networks ($d \gg 2$), any orientation-preserving mapping required to adapt to a new domain can be approximated effectively by a sequence of rotations within $SO(d)$. The "flip" associated with a reflection often corresponds to an arbitrary choice of coordinate handedness that does not necessarily alter the functional output or semantic alignment in a way that a rotation cannot also achieve through re-orienting the basis vectors.

Furthermore, Section 3.3 explicitly addresses this trade-off between mathematical completeness and computational efficiency. The authors chose Cayley parameterization specifically because it offers a differentiable and efficient way to enforce orthogonality without requiring iterative procedures like Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization. They acknowledge that this sacrifices access to $O(d) \setminus SO(d)$ (the set containing reflections), but they found empirically that this restriction did not hinder performance in their extensive experiments on subject-driven and controllable generation tasks. The block-diagonal structure introduced later in Section 3.3 further reduces parameter complexity but maintains orthogonality; if reflections were critical for convergence or quality, one would expect significant degradation in tasks requiring drastic feature flips, yet OFT consistently outperformed baselines like LoRA and DreamBooth in both stability and generation quality across diverse datasets (COCO, ADE20K, CelebA-HQ). Therefore, while OFT technically lacks the expressivity to perform global reflections, the rotational degrees of freedom provided by $SO(d)$ appear robust enough to cover the necessary manifold adaptations for text-to-image diffusion finetuning without compromising semantic fidelity or controllability.

### How does Re-scaled OFT preserve Hyperspherical Energy if magnitudes are changed?
Question:
Section 3.4 introduces Re-scaled OFT with a diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ containing learnable positive scalars $s_i$. The paper argues that 're-scaling neurons does not change the hyperspherical energy (the magnitude will be normalized out).' Looking at Eq (1), HE depends on $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. If we update weights as $\bm{W}_{new} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$, then each column vector becomes scaled by its corresponding $s_i$. When we normalize these new columns to calculate HE, indeed the scalar factors cancel out ($s_i \bm{v} / \| s_i \bm{v} \| = \hat{\bm{v}}$). So strictly speaking, HE is preserved regardless of $s_i$. But if HE is purely angular and invariant to scale, why do we need to learn these scales at all? Doesn't this contradict the premise that preserving angles is sufficient? Or is the magnitude information critical for something else in diffusion models that isn't captured by HE?
Answer:
Your intuition regarding the mathematical definition of Hyperspherical Energy (HE) is entirely correct: since HE is calculated using normalized vectors $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$, multiplying any neuron $\bm{w}_i$ by a positive scalar $s_i$ cancels out during normalization, leaving the angular relationships and thus the HE value unchanged. The paper explicitly acknowledges this in Section 3.4, stating that "re-scaling neurons does not change the hyperspherical energy."

However, the necessity of learning these scales in Re-scaled OFT arises because preserving HE is a **constraint** designed to protect semantic structure, not a complete description of the model's functional behavior. While the *angles* between neurons dictate the relative orientation of features (which corresponds to semantic content, as argued via the Fourier phase analogy in Section 3.1), the *magnitudes* of these neurons determine the strength or sensitivity of those features in the final output. In a linear layer $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x}$, if we replace $\bm{W}$ with $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$, the output becomes a weighted sum where each transformed neuron contributes according to its new scale $s_i$.

If we were restricted to pure orthogonal transformations (where all $s_i=1$), we would be limited to rotating or reflecting the entire subspace spanned by the neurons without altering their individual "gain." This might be insufficient for specific downstream tasks where certain feature channels need to be amplified or attenuated independently to match new data distributions or control signals. By introducing $\bm{D}$, Re-scaled OFT decouples two objectives: maintaining the **structural integrity** of the pretrained model (via $\bm{R}$ preserving angles/HE) and optimizing **feature responsiveness** (via $\bm{D}$ adjusting magnitudes). The paper notes in Section 3.4 that while they primarily report results using standard OFT to isolate the effects of orthogonality, "we find that the re-scaled OFT is generally better," suggesting that allowing magnitude adjustment provides necessary flexibility for convergence and performance without sacrificing the stability guarantees offered by preserving hyperspherical energy. Essentially, HE preservation ensures we don't break what we already know, while learning $s_i$ allows us to tune how strongly we apply that knowledge.

Answer:
Your confusion stems from conflating the exact algebraic inversion of the Cayley transform with the local linear approximation used specifically for deriving the constraint in the COFT framework. The paper does not claim that bounding $\bm{R}$ near the identity *exactly* linearly bounds $\bm{Q}$ via a factor of 2 for all values; rather, it relies on the **Neumann series expansion** valid strictly in the regime where $\bm{Q}$ is small (i.e., close to zero).

Mathematically, the Cayley transform is defined as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. The term $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ can be expanded as a Neumann series $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \bm{Q}^k = \bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q}^2 + \dots$, provided that the spectral radius of $\bm{Q}$ is less than 1. Substituting this back into the expression for $\bm{R}$ yields:
$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)) = \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + 2\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)
$$
Thus, locally around $\bm{Q}=0$, the relationship is indeed approximately linear: $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \approx \|2\bm{Q}\| = 2\|\bm{Q}\|$. Consequently, enforcing $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ is asymptotically equivalent to enforcing $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon/2$ (which corresponds to your $\epsilon'$). The authors explicitly state this derivation holds "under the assumption that the Neumann series converges," meaning this equivalence is an approximation intended for small deviations where higher-order terms $\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ are negligible. You are correct that solving exactly for $\bm{Q}$ gives $\smash[t]{\displaystyle{\boldsymbol Q=(\boldsymbol R-\boldsymbol I)(\boldsymbol R+\boldsymbol I)^{-1}}}$. However, projecting onto a ball in this nonlinear space would require computing this inverse at every step or using complex manifold projections. The innovation of COFT is precisely avoiding this complexity by operating in the tangent space (via $\smash[t]{\displaystyle{\boldsymbol Q}}$) and applying a simple Euclidean projection, justified by the fact that finetuning requires only small rotations.

Regarding your second concern about maintaining skew-symmetry during Projected Gradient Descent (PGD): there is no violation here because of how PGD interacts with subspaces. The set of skew-symmetric matrices forms a **linear subspace** of all square matrices (since if $A$ and $B$ are skew-symmetric, $A+B$ and $cA$ are also skew-symmetric). When you perform an unconstrained gradient step on $\smash[t]{\displaystyle{\boldsymbol Q}}$, you might technically drift outside the feasible region if you were optimizing over general matrices with constraints. However, in practice, if you initialize $\smash[t]{\displaystyle{\boldsymbol Q}}$ as skew-symmetric and ensure your gradient updates respect this symmetry (or simply enforce it by symmetrizing), you remain in the subspace. Crucially, projecting a skew-symmetric matrix onto a Frobenius norm ball (the constraint $\|\smash[t]{\displaystyle{\boldsymbol Q}} - \mathbf 0 \| < \epsilon'$) involves simply scaling the entire matrix by a scalar factor if its norm exceeds $\epsilon'$. Since scalar multiplication preserves skew-symmetry ($(-cA)^\top = c(-A^\top) = c(A) = -(cA)$), the projection operation **automatically** maintains the required property without needing specialized manifold optimization techniques. The constraint acts radially on the vector space of skew-symmetric matrices, keeping you safely within both the norm bound and the algebraic structure required for Cayley parameterization.

Section 3.3 compares parameter efficiency between LoRA and OFT. For LoRA with rank $r'$, parameters are roughly $(d+n)r'$. For OFT with block size determined by $r$, parameters are roughly $d(d/r - 1)/2$. The paper simplifies this by assuming both ranks depend on dimension ($r=r'=\\alpha d$) leading to complexities of $O(d^2)$ vs $O(d)$. However, in practical implementations like Stable Diffusion's cross-attention layers, input dimension $d_{in}$ and output dimension $d_{out}$ are often different (e.g., projection layers). Answer:
Your confusion stems from conflating the dimension of the orthogonal transformation matrix `$\mathbf{R}$` with the dimensions of the weight matrix `$\mathbf{W}$` itself. In the OFT formulation presented in Section 3.2, specifically Equation (2), the transformation is defined as `$\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$`. Here, if the pretrained weight matrix `$\mathbf{W}^0$` has dimensions `$d_{out} \times d_{in}$` (mapping inputs of size `$d_{in}$` to outputs of size `$d_{out}$`), then `$\mathbf{W}$` must also be `$d_{out} \times d_{in}$`. Consequently, the orthogonal matrix `$\mathbf{R}$` must operate on the rows of `$\mathbf{W}^0$`, meaning `$\mathbf{R}$` is strictly a square matrix of size `$d_{out} \times d_{out}$`. The multiplication occurs as `$(\mathbf{R}_{d_{out} \times d_{out}})(\mathbf{W}^0_{d_{out} \times d_{in}})$`, leaving the column dimension `$d_{in}$` untouched by `$\mathbf{R}$`.

This distinction resolves your concern regarding non-square weight matrices common in Transformer attention projections (such as query/key/value or output projections where `$d_{in} \neq d_{out}$`). The block-diagonal parameterization described in Section 3.3 applies exclusively to this square transformation matrix `$\mathbf{R}$`. Therefore, when applying OFT to a linear layer with mismatched input and output dimensions, you simply construct a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix sized according to `$d_{out}$`, regardless of whether `$d_{in}$` is equal to, greater than, or less than `$d_{out}$`. The "blocks" partition the output neuron space, grouping them into sets of size `$d_{out}/r$` to reduce the degrees of freedom in rotating these neurons relative to one another.

Regarding parameter count comparability with LoRA, the paper's complexity analysis holds because it isolates the cost of learning the *transformation* versus learning an *additive update*. For LoRA applied to a layer with dimensions `$d_{out} \times d_{in}$` and rank `$r'$`, you learn two matrices: a down-projection (`$r' \times d_{in}$`) and an up-projection (`$d_{out} \times r'$`), totaling approximately `$(d_{in} + d_{out})r'$` parameters. For OFT with block size determined by `$r$`, you learn a single sparse orthogonal matrix `$\mathbf{R}$` of size `$d_{out} \times d_{out}$`. As derived in Section 3.3, if we assume a block structure with no shared blocks across layers (standard OFT), the parameter count is roughly `\$d_out(d_out/r - 1)/2\$`. If we assume both methods scale their ranks proportionally to their respective dominant dimensions (e.g., setting LO rank `\$r' = \alpha d\$` and OFT block factor such that effective rank scales similarly), LoRA scales linearly with dimension (`\$O(d)\$`) while standard unstructured OFT would scale quadratically (`\$O(d^2)\$`). However, by enforcing the block-diagonal structure on `\$\mathbf{R}\$` as shown in Equation (3), OFT reduces its complexity from `\$O(d^2)\$` to `\$O(d^2/r)\$`. The paper argues that for appropriate choices of hyperparameters (specifically choosing a sufficiently large number of blocks or sharing them), OFT can achieve comparable or lower parameter counts than LoRA while offering superior preservation of hyperspherical energy. Crucially, unlike LoRA which adds parameters that grow with both input and output dimensions (`\$O(r'd + rd')\$`), OFT's parameter growth depends primarily on the output dimension squared divided by the number of blocks (`\$O(d^2/r)\$`), making it highly competitive when applied to layers where one might otherwise need a large LoRA rank to capture sufficient expressivity.
