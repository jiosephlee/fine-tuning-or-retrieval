# Chapter 1: Introduction to Controllable Text-to-Image Generation

Over the past few years, the field of artificial intelligence has witnessed a paradigm shift driven by large-scale text-to-image diffusion models. Systems such as Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 2, and Imagen have demonstrated an unprecedented ability to synthesize high-fidelity, photorealistic images from natural language descriptions. These models operate by learning a complex mapping between textual embeddings and visual representations, effectively internalizing vast amounts of world knowledge regarding objects, styles, lighting conditions, and artistic techniques. For instance, Stable Diffusion leverages a latent space constructed by a variational autoencoder (VQ-GAN), allowing it to generate images efficiently in a compressed representation before decoding them back to pixels. Similarly, DALL-E 2 utilizes CLIP encoders to create a joint embedding space where text and images coexist. Despite these remarkable achievements in raw generative capability, a critical bottleneck remains: the inability to exert precise, fine-grained control over the generation process using text prompts alone. While text offers broad semantic direction, it is often ambiguous and insufficient for specifying exact spatial arrangements, structural boundaries, or specific subject identities required for many practical applications. This chapter explores these foundational limitations and defines the two primary downstream tasks—subject-driven generation and controllable generation—that necessitate advanced fine-tuning strategies like Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT).

## Overview of State-of-the-Art Diffusion Models

To appreciate the necessity of controlled generation methods, one must first understand the architecture and training philosophy behind contemporary diffusion models. These systems typically employ denoising processes where noise is iteratively removed from a random Gaussian distribution until a coherent image emerges. Recent advancements have moved beyond training solely in pixel space, which is computationally prohibitive at high resolutions. Instead, models like Stable Diffusion utilize latent spaces derived from Vision-Language Pre-training (VLP) or Variational Autoencoders. By operating in a lower-dimensional latent space that captures essential perceptual features rather than raw pixel values, these models achieve higher resolution outputs with greater computational efficiency. Furthermore, they rely heavily on cross-modal alignment; for example, Imagen employs frozen text encoders trained on massive web-scraped datasets to ensure that the textual prompt guides the diffusion trajectory toward visually relevant outcomes. The result is a model that possesses immense "world knowledge," capable of imagining anything described verbally. However, this strength is also its weakness regarding precision; the model relies entirely on statistical correlations found in its training data rather than explicit instructions about geometry or identity preservation unless explicitly guided through architectural modifications or fine-tuning procedures.

## The Problem of Ambiguity in Text Guidance

While text-guided control allows for high-level semantic manipulation—for instance, changing "a cat" to "a dog"—it struggles with low-level specificity and identity retention. Natural language is inherently vague; prompting for "a red ball" might yield balls of various shapes, textures, or positions depending on how the model interprets the phrase relative to its learned priors. More critically, text cannot reliably enforce strict adherence to external structural inputs like edge maps or segmentation outlines without specialized mechanisms added post-hoc. Even more problematic is the issue of concept drift during attempts to customize content. When a user wishes to generate images containing a specific object not present in the base training distribution (such as a custom-designed chair or their own pet), standard text conditioning often fails to maintain consistency across different contexts or backgrounds. The model tends to revert to generic representations associated with words describing that category rather than adhering to unique visual details provided by reference images. This ambiguity creates a fundamental disconnect between user intent and generated output, driving the need for methodologies that go beyond passive prompting to active model adaptation where structural relationships and specific identities are rigidly preserved while allowing contextual variation.

## Defining Task 1: Subject-Driven Generation

One prominent application area addressing these limitations is subject-driven generation. In this scenario, the objective shifts from generating generic instances of a class (e.g., "dogs") to creating high-quality depictions of a *specific* entity given only a handful of reference images—often referred to as few-shot learning—and accompanied by descriptive text prompts for new contexts. This task was popularized by frameworks such as DreamBooth, which treats customization as an optimization problem where unique identifiers are learned alongside existing vocabulary tokens within the diffusion model itself. The goal here is twofold: first, to bind a specific token name (like "[V]") exclusively to the visual appearance of the input subject; second, to retain the model's ability to render that subject in diverse scenes without altering its core identity features like facial structure or texture patterns. Traditional fine-tuning approaches struggle with this balance; aggressive updates tend to cause catastrophic forgetting where the model loses its ability to generate other classes (concept collapse), while overly conservative updates fail to imprint enough detail about the new subject into weights already saturated with general knowledge effectively rendering it incapable of distinguishing between different dogs when asked for "Dog [V]" versus just "dog." # Chapter 2: Theoretical Foundations of Neural Representation

To appreciate the novelty and efficacy of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), one must first step back from the mechanics of gradient descent and examine the geometric nature of neural representations themselves. Large text-to-image diffusion models possess immense generative capabilities, yet adapting them to specific downstream tasks without degrading their pre-trained semantic abilities presents a profound challenge. Traditional finetuning strategies rely heavily on heuristic assumptions about model proximity, often assuming that keeping the updated weights close to the original weights in Euclidean space is sufficient to preserve knowledge. However, recent insights suggest that this Euclidean perspective is inadequate for capturing the true essence of semantic retention in deep networks. Instead, the critical information resides in the angular relationships between neurons. This chapter establishes the theoretical groundwork necessary to understand why manipulating neuron directions through orthogonal transformations offers a superior path for finetuning, focusing on hyperspherical geometry, the definition of hyperspherical energy, and the distinction between magnitude and directional information in encoding visual semantics.

## Neuron Geometry on the Unit Hypersphere

At the heart of modern deep learning theory lies a shift in perspective regarding how information is stored within neural network layers. Rather than viewing neurons merely as vectors with arbitrary magnitudes residing in a flat Euclidean space, contemporary analysis treats them as points on a unit hypersphere. This conceptual reframing is supported by empirical observations indicating that hyperspherical similarity serves as a robust encoder of semantic information. When neurons are normalized to lie on a unit sphere, their positions are defined entirely by their direction relative to one another, stripping away absolute scale. This normalization reveals that the "knowledge" within a trained network is not primarily encoded in how large a particular weight vector grows, but rather in where it points relative to its neighbors.

The geometry of this unit hypersphere dictates how features interact. Consider a single layer containing $N$ neurons represented by weight vectors $\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_N$. Each vector can be mapped to a point on the surface of a $d$-dimensional unit sphere by normalizing its magnitude: $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. The configuration of these $N$ points forms a constellation on the hypersurface. The spatial arrangement of this constellation—specifically, the distribution of distances or angles between every pair of points—constitutes the structural fingerprint of that layer's learned representation. If we view each neuron as defining a decision boundary or a feature detector, then the relative orientation of these detectors determines how complex patterns are synthesized from simpler inputs. Consequently, altering this geometric structure fundamentally changes how the network interprets data. Maintaining integrity during finetuning thus requires careful management not just of individual vector lengths, but predominantly of this collective angular architecture embedded within the unit hypersphere geometry.

This geometric viewpoint challenges the conventional wisdom derived from standard optimization practices which prioritize minimizing Euclidean norms. While moving a single vector slightly might seem benign in Euclidean terms if measured against an origin point, doing so arbitrarily can disrupt delicate angular balances established during pre-training. The unit hypersphere acts as a rigid scaffold; deviations from optimal configurations here can lead to catastrophic interference where previously learned associations break down. Therefore, any successful adaptation strategy must respect this spherical topology, treating updates as rotations or reflections within this curved manifold rather than linear shifts in unbounded space. Understanding this topological constraint is prerequisite to grasping why orthogonal transformations—which operate precisely as rigid rotations/reflections—are theoretically sound candidates for preserving pre-trained capabilities while enabling new functionality.

## Hyperspherical Energy: Definition and Significance in Semantic Preservation

To quantify the structural integrity described above, researchers utilize a metric known as **Hyperspherical Energy**. Defined formally for a set of normalized neurons $\{\hat{\bm{w}}_i\}_{i=1}^n$ within a layer, Hyperspherical Energy ($\text{HE}$) measures the cumulative pairwise similarity among all neurons in that layer. Mathematically, it is expressed as:

$$ \text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1} $$

Here, $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ represents the Euclidean distance between two normalized vectors on the unit sphere. Since these vectors lie on a sphere with radius 1, this distance corresponds directly to twice the sine half-angle between them ($2\sin(\theta_{ij}/2)$). Minimizing or stabilizing Hyperspherical Energy effectively forces neurons to maintain uniform separation across the sphere's surface; conversely, maximizing it would cause clustering (though typically we care about maintaining an equilibrium state). Crucially, lower values indicate higher uniformity (greater repulsion), while higher values indicate tighter clustering depending on normalization conventions used in literature; however, regardless of minimization or maximization goals per se in training from scratch versus finetuning, **preserving** this value relative to a pre-trained baseline is paramount for semantic continuity.

# Chapter 3: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Core Methodology

# Why Angular Information Matters in Semantic Preservation

To understand the necessity of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), one must first grasp the fundamental mechanism by which neural networks encode semantic information. Traditional intuitions suggest that the magnitude of neuron activations carries significant weight in determining outputs. However, recent theoretical and empirical observations indicate that angular relationships between neurons—specifically their directions on a unit hypersphere—are far more critical for preserving semantic content than their magnitudes. This insight draws heavily from analogies found in signal processing, particularly the properties of the 2D Fourier transform. Just as an image can be reconstructed with high fidelity using its phase spectrum (which represents angular relationships between frequencies) combined with random magnitudes, neural networks rely primarily on the relative angles between neuron vectors to maintain the integrity of learned concepts. If one alters these angles, the semantic meaning of the representation changes drastically; conversely, altering magnitudes while keeping angles constant tends to preserve the underlying semantics while affecting scale or intensity.

In the context of finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models, this distinction is paramount. When adapting a pretrained model to a specific downstream task, such as generating images of a particular subject or adhering to specific control signals like edge maps, we require modifications to the model's behavior. However, aggressive updates often degrade the rich generative priors acquired during pretraining, leading to issues like mode collapse or loss of visual realism. Existing methods often attempt to mitigate this by restricting updates through small learning rates or low-rank assumptions, operating on the premise that a small Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights and the pretrained weights implies good preservation of capability. Yet, Euclidean distance fails to capture the structural geometry of how neurons interact. Two sets of weights can be close in Euclidean space but possess vastly different pairwise angular relationships, thereby destroying the delicate hyperspherical structure that encodes semantic knowledge. Consequently, a robust finetuning strategy must prioritize preserving these angular relationships while still allowing sufficient flexibility to learn new tasks.

The solution proposed by Orthogonal Finetuning lies in explicitly manipulating these angles rather than treating them as side effects of weight adjustments. Empirical evidence supports that training neural networks where all neurons are normalized onto a unit hypersphere yields superior generalization capabilities. Furthermore, studies on autoencoders have demonstrated that replacing standard inner products with purely angular comparisons allows for near-perfect recovery of input data features, whereas relying solely on magnitude yields little to no useful reconstruction power. This suggests that for any successful finetuning procedure aimed at modifying semantics—such as teaching a model about a new subject—the primary vehicle for change should be the rotation or reflection of neuron directions within their respective layers. By focusing exclusively on changing directions through controlled rotations, OFT ensures that the model adapts its semantic output without dismantling the foundational geometric arrangement established during pretraining.

Hyperspherical energy serves as a quantitative measure characterizing the pairwise relational structure among neurons within a layer. The output vector $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ for an input $\bm{x}$ is traditionally computed via $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x}$.
