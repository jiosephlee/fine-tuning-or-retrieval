Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Introduction to Controllable Text-to-Image Generation

Over the past few years, the field of artificial intelligence has witnessed a paradigm shift driven by large-scale text-to-image diffusion models. Systems such as Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 2, and Imagen have demonstrated an unprecedented ability to synthesize high-fidelity, photorealistic images from natural language descriptions. These models operate by learning a complex mapping between textual embeddings and visual representations, effectively internalizing vast amounts of world knowledge regarding objects, styles, lighting conditions, and artistic techniques. For instance, Stable Diffusion leverages a latent space constructed by a variational autoencoder (VQ-GAN), allowing it to generate images efficiently in a compressed representation before decoding them back to pixels. Similarly, DALL-E 2 utilizes CLIP encoders to create a joint embedding space where text and images coexist. Despite these remarkable achievements in raw generative capability, a critical bottleneck remains: the inability to exert precise, fine-grained control over the generation process using text prompts alone. While text offers broad semantic direction, it is often ambiguous and insufficient for specifying exact spatial arrangements, structural boundaries, or specific subject identities required for many practical applications. This chapter explores these foundational limitations and defines the two primary downstream tasks—subject-driven generation and controllable generation—that necessitate advanced fine-tuning strategies like Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT).

## Overview of State-of-the-Art Diffusion Models

To appreciate the necessity of controlled generation methods, one must first understand the architecture and training philosophy behind contemporary diffusion models. These systems typically employ denoising processes where noise is iteratively removed from a random Gaussian distribution until a coherent image emerges. Recent advancements have moved beyond training solely in pixel space, which is computationally prohibitive at high resolutions. Instead, models like Stable Diffusion utilize latent spaces derived from Vision-Language Pre-training (VLP) or Variational Autoencoders. By operating in a lower-dimensional latent space that captures essential perceptual features rather than raw pixel values, these models achieve higher resolution outputs with greater computational efficiency. Furthermore, they rely heavily on cross-modal alignment; for example, Imagen employs frozen text encoders trained on massive web-scraped datasets to ensure that the textual prompt guides the diffusion trajectory toward visually relevant outcomes. The result is a model that possesses immense "world knowledge," capable of imagining anything described verbally. However, this strength is also its weakness regarding precision; the model relies entirely on statistical correlations found in its training data rather than explicit instructions about geometry or identity preservation unless explicitly guided through architectural modifications or fine-tuning procedures.

## The Problem of Ambiguity in Text Guidance

While text-guided control allows for high-level semantic manipulation—for instance, changing "a cat" to "a dog"—it struggles with low-level specificity and identity retention. Natural language is inherently vague; prompting for "a red ball" might yield balls of various shapes, textures, or positions depending on how the model interprets the phrase relative to its learned priors. More critically, text cannot reliably enforce strict adherence to external structural inputs like edge maps or segmentation outlines without specialized mechanisms added post-hoc. Even more problematic is the issue of concept drift during attempts to customize content. When a user wishes to generate images containing a specific object not present in the base training distribution (such as a custom-designed chair or their own pet), standard text conditioning often fails to maintain consistency across different contexts or backgrounds. The model tends to revert to generic representations associated with words describing that category rather than adhering to unique visual details provided by reference images. This ambiguity creates a fundamental disconnect between user intent and generated output, driving the need for methodologies that go beyond passive prompting to active model adaptation where structural relationships and specific identities are rigidly preserved while allowing contextual variation.

## Defining Task 1: Subject-Driven Generation

One prominent application area addressing these limitations is subject-driven generation. In this scenario, the objective shifts from generating generic instances of a class (e.g., "dogs") to creating high-quality depictions of a *specific* entity given only a handful of reference images—often referred to as few-shot learning—and accompanied by descriptive text prompts for new contexts. This task was popularized by frameworks such as DreamBooth, which treats customization as an optimization problem where unique identifiers are learned alongside existing vocabulary tokens within the diffusion model itself. The goal here is twofold: first, to bind a specific token name (like "[V]") exclusively to the visual appearance of the input subject; second, to retain the model's ability to render that subject in diverse scenes without altering its core identity features like facial structure or texture patterns. Traditional fine-tuning approaches struggle with this balance; aggressive updates tend to cause catastrophic forgetting where the model loses its ability to generate other classes (concept collapse), while overly conservative updates fail to imprint enough detail about the new subject into weights already saturated with general knowledge effectively rendering it incapable of distinguishing between different dogs when asked for "Dog [V]" versus just "dog."
