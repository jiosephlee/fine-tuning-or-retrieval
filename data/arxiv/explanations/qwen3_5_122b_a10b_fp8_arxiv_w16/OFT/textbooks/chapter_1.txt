Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Introduction to Controlling Diffusion Models

Large text-to-image diffusion models have revolutionized the field of generative artificial intelligence, demonstrating an unprecedented ability to synthesize photorealistic and diverse images from textual descriptions alone. Models such as Stable Diffusion, Imagen, and DALL-E 2 leverage massive datasets and sophisticated architecture designs to map the complex latent space of visual concepts to corresponding textual inputs. These systems operate by progressively denoising a random noise distribution into a coherent image, guided by the semantic information embedded in a text prompt. The result is a powerful engine capable of creating high-fidelity visuals that were previously impossible to generate programmatically. However, despite their impressive capabilities in broad text-guided control, these models face significant limitations when tasked with precise, fine-grained manipulation. Text prompts are often ambiguous and insufficient for specifying exact attributes, spatial arrangements, or specific identities required for specialized applications. Consequently, the community has identified a critical open problem: how to effectively guide or control these powerful pre-trained models to perform specific downstream tasks without compromising their inherent generative prowess.

The core difficulty lies in the process of adapting these massive models. When researchers attempt to specialize a general-purpose diffusion model for a narrow task, they must navigate a delicate trade-off between learning new behaviors and retaining old ones. If the adaptation is too aggressive, the model suffers from catastrophic forgetting, where it loses its ability to generate diverse, high-quality images unrelated to the new task. Conversely, if the adaptation is too conservative, the model fails to learn the necessary nuances of the specific task at hand. Current strategies often rely on heuristic approaches, such as updating neuron weights with extremely small learning rates or adding low-rank components to the weights. While these methods offer simplicity, they lack a principled theoretical foundation for ensuring that the semantic generation ability—the "knowledge" encoded in the pre-trained network—is preserved during finetuning. There is currently no robust measure that explicitly quantifies how much of the original generative capability remains intact after adaptation, leading to unstable training dynamics and unpredictable outcomes.

To address these challenges systematically, this chapter focuses on two primary downstream tasks that exemplify the need for controlled finetuning: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. Both tasks require modifying a pre-trained diffusion model using limited data—either a few images of a specific object or an auxiliary control signal like edges or segmentation maps—while strictly maintaining the model's original ability to interpret text prompts and generate realistic imagery. Understanding these tasks provides the necessary context for introducing Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a novel method designed specifically to solve the tension between adaptability and stability by preserving the geometric structure of neuronal relationships within the network.

## Overview of Large Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

The foundation of modern image synthesis lies in diffusion probabilistic models, which have rapidly advanced alongside improvements in vision-language representation learning. These models generally fall into two categories based on their operational space: pixel-space diffusion and latent-space diffusion. Early approaches like GLIDE trained diffusion models directly in pixel space using paired text-image data or jointly trained text encoders with diffusion priors. Similarly, Imagen utilized frozen pre-trained text encoders to guide pixel-level denoising processes. While effective, operating in pixel space is computationally expensive due to the high dimensionality of raw images. To mitigate this cost while maintaining quality, subsequent architectures like Stable Diffusion shifted operations into a compressed latent space learned via variational autoencoders (VQ-GAN) or constructed joint latent embedding spaces using CLIP representations as seen in DALL-E 2. This shift allows for efficient training and inference while leveraging powerful vision-language alignment techniques developed through extensive research on transformers and contrastive learning frameworks like BLIP and Flamingo.

Despite these architectural advancements enabling photorealistic generation across vast domains of objects and scenes governed by natural language prompts, the control mechanism remains fundamentally coarse-grained. The mapping from text tokens to visual features relies on statistical correlations learned during pre-training on billions of image-text pairs. While this allows for creative freedom—for instance, generating "a cat wearing sunglasses"—it struggles with specificity when asked to render "that specific cat I own" or "this exact scene layout." The ambiguity arises because many distinct visual configurations can satisfy a single textual description according to probability distributions learned during pre-training. Furthermore, standard conditioning mechanisms cannot easily enforce strict structural constraints such as adhering precisely to an input edge map or preserving unique identity features across varying contexts without significant intervention in the model's internal parameters. This limitation underscores why mere prompting is insufficient for professional-grade content creation where precision is paramount, necessitating more direct forms of model intervention through finetuning procedures tailored to specific requirements rather than relying solely on prompt engineering tricks or inversion methods that struggle with identity retention over complex edits.

## The Fine-tuning Challenge: Balancing Adaptation vs Catastrophic Forgetting

Adapting large-scale pre-trained models presents a fundamental dilemma known as catastrophic forgetting versus plasticity retention. In traditional machine learning paradigms involving smaller networks, finetuning typically involves initializing weights from a pre-trained checkpoint and continuing gradient descent updates on new data with reduced learning rates or regularization terms designed to penalize deviations from original weights ($||\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0||$). Implicitly assuming that minimizing Euclidean distance correlates with preserving semantic knowledge seems logical; however, recent investigations suggest this assumption fails to capture structural integrity within deep neural networks responsible for generative capabilities. Simply keeping weights close numerically does not guarantee that pairwise relationships between neurons—which encode higher-order semantic abstractions—are maintained effectively enough to sustain diverse generation post-adaptation.

Existing strategies attempting to circumvent this issue include Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which freezes original weights $\bm{W}^0$ while learning additive low-rank updates $\Delta \bm{W}$ such that total weights become $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \Delta \bm{W}$.
