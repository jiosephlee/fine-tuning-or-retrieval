Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Theoretical Foundations of Neural Semantics

Before delving into the specific mechanics of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), it is essential to establish the theoretical underpinnings that justify its design. The efficacy of OFT relies on a fundamental shift in perspective regarding how neural networks store and process semantic information. Traditional finetuning methods often treat weight updates as arbitrary adjustments in Euclidean space, assuming that keeping the total change small is sufficient to preserve a model's pre-trained knowledge. However, recent insights suggest that the geometric arrangement of neurons—specifically their relative orientations—is far more critical to semantic integrity than their absolute magnitudes or simple Euclidean proximity to initial weights. This chapter dissects these concepts, exploring why angular information dominates semantic representation, defining the metric known as Hyperspherical Energy, and elucidating why orthogonal transformations serve as the ideal mechanism for adapting large-scale text-to-image diffusion models without catastrophic forgetting.

## Angular Information vs. Magnitude in Neural Representations

To understand why OFT focuses exclusively on rotating neurons rather than scaling or shifting them arbitrarily, we must first examine what constitutes "meaning" within a deep neural network. Intuitively, one might assume that both the strength (magnitude) and direction of a neuron's response contribute equally to the features it extracts. However, empirical evidence challenges this assumption, suggesting that angular information—the direction of neuron vectors on a unit hypersphere—encodes the vast majority of semantic content. This insight draws upon observations from prior research indicating that hyperspherical similarity is a robust indicator of semantic relationships between data points.

Consider a standard convolutional autoencoder trained on image data, such as flowers. During training, the network learns filters through standard inner products, optimizing for reconstruction loss based on these interactions. Once trained, we can analyze the contribution of different components of these learned filters by separating their magnitude from their direction (angle). Experiments reveal a striking disparity: if one retains only the magnitude information of the neuron weights while discarding their directional orientation (replacing directions with random noise), the reconstructed images lose all recognizable structure and semantic meaning. Conversely, if one retains only the angular information (normalizing all weights to unit length) while replacing magnitudes with random values or even constant values, the network can almost perfectly recover the original input images. This phenomenon holds true even though the model was never explicitly trained using cosine activation or angular constraints; it emerged purely from optimizing inner products during training.

This experimental result implies that during the pre-training phase of large models like Stable Diffusion, the network organizes itself such that the *directions* of its neurons capture the complex manifolds required for high-fidelity generation. The magnitudes act primarily as scaling factors but do not carry the core identity or semantic essence of the features. Consequently, when attempting to adapt a model to a new task—such as generating images of a specific subject or adhering to control signals like edge maps—the primary lever for modification should be these neuron directions. Modifying angles allows us to steer the model's internal representations toward new semantic territories while retaining the foundational knowledge encoded in those angles' relative configurations. If we were to alter magnitudes indiscriminately or change angles without regard for their pairwise relationships, we risk destroying the delicate geometric structure that enables coherent image synthesis. Therefore, an effective finetuning strategy must prioritize manipulating neuron directions while rigorously preserving their inter-neuronal geometry.

## Hyperspherical Energy: Defining Pairwise Neuron Relationships

If neuron directions are paramount, how do we quantify whether a finetuned model has successfully preserved its pre-trained semantic structure? Simply measuring the Euclidean distance between pre-trained weights and finetuned weights proves insufficient because it fails to capture structural similarities; two sets of weights can be close in Euclidean space yet have drastically different angular relationships between neurons, leading to degraded performance. To address this limitation, researchers utilize a concept called Hyperspherical Energy (HE). HE serves as a structural measure that characterizes the pairwise relational geometry among neurons within a single layer on a unit hypersphere.

Mathematically, for a layer containing $n$ neurons represented by weight vectors $\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_n \in \mathbb{R}^d$, we first normalize each vector to lie on the unit sphere: $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. The Hyperspherical Energy is then defined as the sum of inverse distances between all pairs of these normalized vectors:
$$
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}
$$
Alternatively expressed through cosine similarity measures found in related literature, this quantity captures how uniformly distributed or clustered neurons are relative to one another on the hypersphere surface. A lower energy value typically corresponds to neurons being more evenly spread out (maximizing separation), which is often associated with better generalization in classification tasks where redundancy must be minimized. However, in the context of generative models like diffusion networks trained on massive datasets, neurons already possess a specific energy profile determined by their extensive pre-training history. This profile represents an equilibrium state where pairwise angles encode rich semantic correlations necessary for generating diverse and coherent images.

The significance of HE lies in its sensitivity to rotational changes versus structural distortions. If we rotate all neurons in a layer simultaneously by an orthogonal matrix, every pairwise angle remains identical; consequently, $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ remains invariant because $\|\hat{\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}}_i - \hat{\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}}_j\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$. In contrast, arbitrary weight updates that stretch or shear individual neurons will alter these pairwise distances and thus change the HE value significantly. Therefore, maintaining $\text{HE}(\bm{W}_{finetuned}) \approx \text{HE}(\bm{W}_{pretrained})$ acts as a proxy for ensuring that the intrinsic geometric structure governing semantic generation has not been corrupted during adaptation. By targeting this metric directly rather than relying on loose Euclidean bounds OFT ensures that any modifications made to accommodate downstream tasks respect the fundamental topology established during pre-training. This provides a principled foundation for distinguishing between beneficial adaptations and destructive overfitting phenomena common in naive fine-tuning approaches where excessive parameter shifts lead to mode collapse or loss of diversity.

## The Invariance Property of Orthogonal Transformations

Having established that preserving pairwise neuronal relationships is crucial for maintaining generative capability, we arrive at the core mechanism enabling Orthogonal Finetuning: orthogonal transformations possess an inherent mathematical property known as rotational invariance regarding dot products and angles between vectors. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ (where $\bm{I}$ is identity), implying that applying such a transformation corresponds strictly to rotating or reflecting vectors within Euclidean space without altering lengths or relative angles between them. This property offers an elegant solution to our dilemma: how can we modify neuron weights enough to learn new tasks while guaranteeing zero distortion to their internal geometric configuration?

Formally, let $\bm{W}^0$ denote the weight matrix of a fully connected layer before fine-tuning, composed of columns representing individual neuron vectors $\{\bm{w}_1^0, \dots, \bm{n}\}$. Instead of updating each weight independently via gradient descent which risks disrupting pairwise relations OFT proposes learning a single shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ per layer such that updated weights become $\tilde{\boldsymbol W} = \boldsymbol R\boldsymbol W^0$.
