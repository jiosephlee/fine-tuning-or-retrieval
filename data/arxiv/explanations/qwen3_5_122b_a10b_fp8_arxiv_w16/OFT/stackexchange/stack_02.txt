\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Does Cayley parameterization restrict OFT from modeling reflections, and does this matter for image generation?
Question:
Section 3.3 states that using Cayley parameterization ($\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(I-\bm{Q})^{-1}$) allows us to efficiently enforce orthogonality, but notes a limitation: it can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$ (special orthogonal group $SO(d)$), excluding reflections (determinant $-1$). The paper claims this limitation 'does not affect performance in practice.' However, mathematically, an orthogonal transformation includes both rotations and reflections. If a downstream task theoretically requires flipping the orientation of features (a reflection) to match the target distribution, OFT with Cayley parameterization might be fundamentally incapable of learning that specific transformation. Has anyone tested if allowing reflections actually helps in diffusion model finetuning, or is the feature space robust enough that rotations alone suffice?
Answer:
Your observation is mathematically precise: the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ indeed restricts the learned transformation $\bm{R}$ to the Special Orthogonal Group $SO(d)$, meaning it can only represent rotations (determinant $+1$) and excludes reflections (determinant $-1$). Consequently, strictly speaking, OFT cannot learn a pure reflection transformation. However, the authors assert this limitation does not impact practical performance because the primary mechanism for semantic modification in this context relies on rotating the neuron coordinate system rather than reflecting it.

The theoretical justification lies in the nature of hyperspherical energy and how semantic information is encoded. As argued in Section 3.1 and supported by their toy experiment in Figure 2, the angular relationships between neurons (pairwise angles on the unit hypersphere) are the dominant carriers of semantic information, while magnitudes are secondary. An orthogonal transformation preserves these pairwise angles regardless of whether it is a rotation or a reflection. Since the goal of finetuning is to adjust the *orientation* of the feature space to align with new data distributions while maintaining the internal geometric structure (hyperspherical energy) of the pretrained model, a rotation is generally sufficient to map one valid configuration of features to another. In high-dimensional spaces typical of deep neural networks ($d \gg 2$), any orientation-preserving mapping required to adapt to a new domain can be approximated effectively by a sequence of rotations within $SO(d)$. The "flip" associated with a reflection often corresponds to an arbitrary choice of coordinate handedness that does not necessarily alter the functional output or semantic alignment in a way that a rotation cannot also achieve through re-orienting the basis vectors.

Furthermore, Section 3.3 explicitly addresses this trade-off between mathematical completeness and computational efficiency. The authors chose Cayley parameterization specifically because it offers a differentiable and efficient way to enforce orthogonality without requiring iterative procedures like Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization. They acknowledge that this sacrifices access to $O(d) \setminus SO(d)$ (the set containing reflections), but they found empirically that this restriction did not hinder performance in their extensive experiments on subject-driven and controllable generation tasks. The block-diagonal structure introduced later in Section 3.3 further reduces parameter complexity but maintains orthogonality; if reflections were critical for convergence or quality, one would expect significant degradation in tasks requiring drastic feature flips, yet OFT consistently outperformed baselines like LoRA and DreamBooth in both stability and generation quality across diverse datasets (COCO, ADE20K, CelebA-HQ). Therefore, while OFT technically lacks the expressivity to perform global reflections, the rotational degrees of freedom provided by $SO(d)$ appear robust enough to cover the necessary manifold adaptations for text-to-image diffusion finetuning without compromising semantic fidelity or controllability.
