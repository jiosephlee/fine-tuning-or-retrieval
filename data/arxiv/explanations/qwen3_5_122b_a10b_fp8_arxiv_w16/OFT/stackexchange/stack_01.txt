\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Confused about the definition of 'Hyperspherical Energy': Why is it defined as the inverse of distances rather than the sum of cosines?
Question:
In Section 3.2, Equation (1), the paper defines Hyperspherical Energy (HE) as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$. Earlier in the abstract and introduction, it mentions HE captures 'pairwise neuron relationship' and 'uniformity'. Usually, measures of uniformity or repulsion involve maximizing distances or minimizing similarities (cosine similarity). Here, the formula sums the *inverse* of the Euclidean distance between normalized vectors. If two neurons are close together (distance $\to 0$), this term explodes to infinity. Is this formulation intended to act as a repulsive force where we want to *maximize* this energy to spread neurons out? Or is there a sign convention I am missing? The text says we want to 'preserve' this value, but doesn't explicitly state whether high or low energy corresponds to better semantic preservation in this specific context.
Answer:
Your confusion stems from interpreting "energy" in the thermodynamic sense (where systems seek minimum energy states) versus its usage here as a specific **repulsive potential** derived from electrostatics, specifically the Thomson problem. You are correct that the formula sums the inverse of distances, but the goal is indeed to **maximize** this quantity (or rather, maintain a high, stable value) to ensure uniform distribution, though the paper frames the objective as **preserving** this value relative to the pretrained model.

To understand why $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ is used instead of cosine similarity directly, consider the geometry of the unit hypersphere. The Euclidean distance between two normalized vectors $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$ is related to their dot product (cosine similarity) by the law of cosines: $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^2 = 2 - 2\langle \hat{\bm{w}}_i, \hat{\bm{w}}_j \rangle$. Consequently, minimizing cosine similarity (making vectors orthogonal or anti-correlated) is roughly equivalent to maximizing Euclidean distance. However, defining energy as the sum of inverse distances creates a **singular repulsive force**. As two neurons get arbitrarily close ($\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\| \to 0$), their contribution to the total energy explodes toward infinity. This mathematical singularity acts as a hard barrier preventing neuron collapse; if any pair of neurons were to cluster together, the "energy" would become prohibitively large.

Therefore, in physics-inspired terminology used in this field (referenced from Liu et al., cited in your paper), a configuration with high uniformity corresponds to a state where neurons are maximally separated, which paradoxically results in a **large** Hyperspherical Energy value because every pairwise term contributes significantly without any term vanishing or becoming singularly dominant due to proximity. Conversely, if neurons clump together, the inverse-distance terms for those pairs explode, making the total energy undefined or extremely unstable depending on regularization, but conceptually, we view "low energy" in this specific repulsive potential context as undesirable clustering if we were minimizing it. However, the authors clarify in Section 4 ("Intriguing Insights") that unlike classification tasks where one might *minimize* hyperspherical energy to encourage uniform spacing from scratch (reducing redundancy), finetuning requires **preservation**.

The critical insight in Section 3.2 and Equation (1) is not whether high or low absolute values are "better," but that **orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles**, and thus preserve pairwise Euclidean distances on the unit sphere. Since $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ depends solely on these distances, applying an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ such that $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ ensures that $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \|\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0}_i - \widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0}_j\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|$. Consequently, $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$. The paper argues that this specific structural invariant—the exact distribution of pairwise angles captured by this energy metric—is what encodes the semantic knowledge of the pretrained model. If you were to simply minimize or maximize this energy during finetuning (as done in training-from-scratch classification), you would distort these angles and destroy the pre-trained semantic relationships. Thus, OFT treats Hyperspherical Energy not as a loss function to be optimized toward zero or infinity, but as an invariant quantity that must remain constant ($\|\text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|=0$) to guarantee that the fundamental "shape" of the feature space remains intact while allowing for directional shifts via rotation.
