Answer:
Your confusion stems from conflating the exact algebraic inversion of the Cayley transform with the local linear approximation used specifically for deriving the constraint in the COFT framework. The paper does not claim that bounding $\bm{R}$ near the identity *exactly* linearly bounds $\bm{Q}$ via a factor of 2 for all values; rather, it relies on the **Neumann series expansion** valid strictly in the regime where $\bm{Q}$ is small (i.e., close to zero).

Mathematically, the Cayley transform is defined as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. The term $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ can be expanded as a Neumann series $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \bm{Q}^k = \bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q}^2 + \dots$, provided that the spectral radius of $\bm{Q}$ is less than 1. Substituting this back into the expression for $\bm{R}$ yields:
$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)) = \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + 2\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)
$$
Thus, locally around $\bm{Q}=0$, the relationship is indeed approximately linear: $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \approx \|2\bm{Q}\| = 2\|\bm{Q}\|$. Consequently, enforcing $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ is asymptotically equivalent to enforcing $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon/2$ (which corresponds to your $\epsilon'$). The authors explicitly state this derivation holds "under the assumption that the Neumann series converges," meaning this equivalence is an approximation intended for small deviations where higher-order terms $\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ are negligible. You are correct that solving exactly for $\bm{Q}$ gives $\smash[t]{\displaystyle{\boldsymbol Q=(\boldsymbol R-\boldsymbol I)(\boldsymbol R+\boldsymbol I)^{-1}}}$. However, projecting onto a ball in this nonlinear space would require computing this inverse at every step or using complex manifold projections. The innovation of COFT is precisely avoiding this complexity by operating in the tangent space (via $\smash[t]{\displaystyle{\boldsymbol Q}}$) and applying a simple Euclidean projection, justified by the fact that finetuning requires only small rotations.

Regarding your second concern about maintaining skew-symmetry during Projected Gradient Descent (PGD): there is no violation here because of how PGD interacts with subspaces. The set of skew-symmetric matrices forms a **linear subspace** of all square matrices (since if $A$ and $B$ are skew-symmetric, $A+B$ and $cA$ are also skew-symmetric). When you perform an unconstrained gradient step on $\smash[t]{\displaystyle{\boldsymbol Q}}$, you might technically drift outside the feasible region if you were optimizing over general matrices with constraints. However, in practice, if you initialize $\smash[t]{\displaystyle{\boldsymbol Q}}$ as skew-symmetric and ensure your gradient updates respect this symmetry (or simply enforce it by symmetrizing), you remain in the subspace. Crucially, projecting a skew-symmetric matrix onto a Frobenius norm ball (the constraint $\|\smash[t]{\displaystyle{\boldsymbol Q}} - \mathbf 0 \| < \epsilon'$) involves simply scaling the entire matrix by a scalar factor if its norm exceeds $\epsilon'$. Since scalar multiplication preserves skew-symmetry ($(-cA)^\top = c(-A^\top) = c(A) = -(cA)$), the projection operation **automatically** maintains the required property without needing specialized manifold optimization techniques. The constraint acts radially on the vector space of skew-symmetric matrices, keeping you safely within both the norm bound and the algebraic structure required for Cayley parameterization.
