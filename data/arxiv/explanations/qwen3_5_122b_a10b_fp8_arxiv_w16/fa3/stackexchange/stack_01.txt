\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### Does 'pingpong scheduling' imply that we lose half the throughput because warps alternate between doing nothing and working?
Question:
I am trying to understand the 'pingpong scheduling' concept described in Section 3.1. The text says Warpgroup 1 does softmax while Warpgroup 2 does GEMM, and then they swap roles. My confusion is: during the phase where Warpgroup 1 is doing softmax, isn't Warpgroup 2 effectively idle regarding its *own* softmax task? And vice versa? If the goal is to maximize utilization, doesn't alternating roles mean we are serializing the work of two groups rather than running them fully in parallel? How exactly does this result in a net gain if one group is always waiting for the other to finish its 'slow' part?
Answer:
Your intuition that one warpgroup might appear "idle" during the other's work is understandable if we view the operations as strictly sequential blocks of time, but the "pingpong" scheduling described in Section 3.1 of the paper actually achieves parallelism by exploiting hardware heterogeneity and asynchrony, rather than serialization. The key insight is that the two warpgroups are not competing for the same resource; instead, they are utilizing distinct hardware units simultaneously to hide latency.

In the standard synchronous model (like in earlier FlashAttention versions), a single warpgroup would compute a GEMM ($\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$), then immediately switch to computing the softmax (which involves expensive non-matmul operations like `exp` and `rowmax`), and finally compute the second GEMM ($\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$). During the softmax phase, the powerful Tensor Cores responsible for GEMMs sit idle because they cannot process the exponential functions required for softmax. Conversely, during GEMM phases, the specialized multi-function units handling exponentials sit idle. Pingpong scheduling breaks this bottleneck by splitting the work: while Warpgroup 1 performs the computationally intensive GEMM operations using Tensor Cores, Warpgroup 2 concurrently executes the softmax operations on its own set of resources (specifically utilizing multi-function units or scalar cores).

The paper explicitly notes that non-matmul operations have significantly lower throughput than matmul operations on modern hardware like Hopper; for instance, FP16 matmul throughput is roughly 989 TFLOPS while special function throughput is only about 3.9 TFLOPS. Without interleaving, these slow softmax steps become a major fraction of the total execution time because they block the fast Tensor Cores. By assigning Warpgroup 2 to handle softmax while Warpgroup 1 handles GEMM, you ensure that both types of hardware units are active simultaneously. When Warpgroup 1 finishes its GEMM block and needs to start its own softmax (to prepare for its next accumulation step), Warpgroup 2 has likely finished its previous softmax task and can begin its next GEMM block. The "swap" or "pingpong" refers to this handoff where one group starts what the other was doing previously, ensuring continuous utilization of both Tensor Cores and functional units throughout the entire kernel execution.

Therefore, no throughput is lost due to idleness; rather, throughput is gained by preventing the fast Tensor Cores from stalling while waiting for slower scalar operations to complete. The synchronization barriers mentioned in Section 3.1 (`bar.sync`) are used specifically to orchestrate this overlap without violating data dependencies (e.g., ensuring $\mathbf{S}$ is ready before softmax starts), effectively turning what would be a serial dependency into a parallel pipeline across two warpgroups. This results in a net gain because it hides the latency of the "slow" parts (softmax) under the computation of the "fast" parts (GEMM), pushing utilization closer to the theoretical peak of both hardware components rather than being limited by whichever component happens to be executing sequentially last in a monolithic schedule.
