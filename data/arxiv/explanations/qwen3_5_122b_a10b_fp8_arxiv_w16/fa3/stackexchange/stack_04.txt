\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### If multiplying by an orthogonal matrix M preserves QK^T mathematically, why does it reduce quantization error?
Question:
The paper introduces 'incoherent processing' where we multiply Q and K by a random orthogonal matrix M before quantizing to FP8. Mathematically, $(QM)(KM)^T = QKK^TM^T = QK^T$, so the exact attention score shouldn't change. However, I'm struggling to grasp *how* this helps with quantization error specifically caused by outliers. If M just rotates the vectors, wouldn't the magnitudes of individual elements remain roughly similar (just mixed)? Why does spreading out outliers via rotation make them easier to represent with only 3 bits of mantissa in FP8 compared to leaving them concentrated? Does this rely on statistical properties of Gaussian distributions that aren't immediately obvious?
Answer:
Your intuition regarding the preservation of the dot product is mathematically correct: since $M$ is orthogonal ($M M^T = I$), the identity $(QM)(KM)^T = Q M M^T K^T = Q K^T$ holds exactly in infinite precision arithmetic. However, the reduction in quantization error arises because quantization is a non-linear operation that breaks this algebraic equivalence. When you quantize before computing the dot product, the order of operations matters significantly, particularly in the presence of outliers.

The core issue lies in how Fixed-Point or Floating-Point representations handle dynamic range. In Large Language Models, activation vectors often contain "outliers"—a small number of features with extremely large magnitudes compared to the bulk of the distribution. When you apply per-block or per-tensor quantization to these raw vectors, the scaling factor (the multiplier used to map float values to discrete levels) is dictated by these few extreme values. Consequently, the vast majority of smaller values are compressed into a very narrow range of discrete steps, effectively losing resolution and introducing significant rounding noise for the typical features that carry semantic meaning.

Multiplying by a random orthogonal matrix $M$ acts as a "whitening" or decorrelation step that spreads these outliers across all dimensions. As explained in Section 3.3 under "Accuracy," each entry in the transformed vector $QM$ becomes a random linear combination (specifically, a random sum) of the original entries in $Q$. Due to concentration of measure phenomena inherent in high-dimensional spaces (similar to properties observed in Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma contexts), this mixing process tends to flatten the distribution of magnitudes. Instead of having a few coordinates with massive norms and many with tiny norms, all coordinates in $QM$ tend toward a more uniform magnitude profile closer to a Gaussian distribution with reduced variance spread.

By distributing the energy of the outliers across all dimensions, no single element dominates the dynamic range required for quantization. This allows the quantizer's scaling factor to be smaller (tighter), which increases the density of representable levels for the average-sized elements without clipping or saturating on the former outliers. Essentially, you are trading a few large errors on specific dimensions for many small errors distributed evenly across all dimensions; since attention scores are sums of products ($Q \cdot K^T$), these small, uncorrelated errors tend to cancel out or accumulate less destructively than systematic truncation errors caused by poor scaling on dominant features. The paper notes they implement this efficiently using a product of random diagonal matrices and a Hadamard matrix ($O(d \log d)$ complexity), ensuring this statistical smoothing happens without negating performance gains from FP8 acceleration.
