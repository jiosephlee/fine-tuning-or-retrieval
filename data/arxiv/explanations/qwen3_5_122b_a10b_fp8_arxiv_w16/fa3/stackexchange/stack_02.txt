\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### How can asynchronous WGMMA instructions depend on previous results if they don't wait?
Question:
In Algorithm 2 (FlashAttention-3 consumer warpgroup forward pass), line 14 states 'Compute S_next... Commit but do not wait,' followed later by 'Wait for the WGMMA.' I understand that WGMMA is asynchronous on Hopper. However, line 19 ('Compute m_i... based on S_next') logically requires S_next to be finished. If we commit without waiting at line 14, how does the hardware ensure that the subsequent operations (like calculating max or exp) don't read garbage data from registers before the matrix multiplication completes? Is there an implicit barrier inserted by the compiler that I'm missing, or does the 'Wait' at line 18 strictly enforce this dependency?
Answer:
Your confusion stems from conflating the *issuance* of an asynchronous instruction with the *availability* of its result, a distinction that is central to the warp-specialized design in FlashAttention-3. When line 14 states "Commit but do not wait," it refers specifically to the `wgmma` instruction initiating the matrix multiplication `$\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_{j}^T$`. On Hopper architecture, this instruction tells the Tensor Core to begin fetching operands and performing the calculation in the background, returning control to the warp immediately without stalling. Crucially, at this precise moment, the destination registers holding `$\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$` contain undefined or stale data; they are effectively "garbage" until the operation completes.

The hardware ensures correctness not through an implicit compiler barrier at line 14, but through explicit synchronization instructions placed exactly where data dependency becomes critical. You correctly identified line 18 ("Wait for the WGMMA") as the enforcement mechanism. The CPU-like scheduler within the GPU warp will simply ignore any attempt to read from the specific registers allocated for `$\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$` (used subsequently in line 19 for computing `$m_i$` and `$\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{next}}$`) until that specific `wait` instruction resolves. In PTX assembly terms, this is often realized via a `wgmma.commit_group` followed by a `wgmma.wait_group`. The `commit` fires off the operation, and the `wait` acts as a fence that blocks further execution on that register file subset until the Tensor Core signals completion.

Therefore, there is no risk of reading garbage because line 19 is structurally impossible to execute before line 18 resolves. The "pipelining" mentioned in Section 3.2 relies on this gap: while one instance of WGMMA (computing `$\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$`) runs asynchronously in the background during lines 15–17 (which handle loading `$\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$` and executing a different WGMMA for updating `$\mathbf{O}_i$`), the current warp continues with independent work. Only when the warp reaches line 18 does it pause to guarantee that `$\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$` is fully written before proceeding to line 19 to extract statistics like row-maxima and exponents. This explicit separation allows FlashAttention-3 to overlap low-latency softmax arithmetic with high-throughput matrix multiplication without violating data dependencies, maximizing Tensor Core utilization as described in their "Hiding softmax under asynchronous block-wise GEMMs" contribution.
