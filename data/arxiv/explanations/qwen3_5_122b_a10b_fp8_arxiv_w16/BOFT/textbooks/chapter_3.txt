Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: The Butterfly Structure and Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)

Having established the information transmission framework in the previous chapter, we now turn our attention to the central innovation of this work: Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). The primary challenge identified was the inefficiency of traditional Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which relies on block-diagonal structures that impose rigid sparsity patterns and fail to capture the rich interactions between all neurons in a layer. While OFT preserves pairwise angles between neurons—a crucial property for retaining pre-trained knowledge—the cost of maintaining a fully dense orthogonal matrix scales quadratically with the dimension $d$, requiring $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. This chapter details how we overcome this limitation by drawing inspiration from classical signal processing algorithms. Specifically, we leverage the butterfly structures inherent in the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm to construct a dense orthogonal matrix through a product of sparse matrices. This approach allows us to achieve parameter efficiency scaling as $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ while maintaining the expressivity required for complex downstream tasks. We will formally define the mathematical building blocks of BOFT, explore its recursive construction, and discuss critical implementation strategies such as identity initialization and multiplicative dropout that ensure stable training.

# From Signal Processing to Matrix Factorization

The conceptual leap from standard finetuning methods to BOFT lies in recognizing a deep parallel between information propagation in neural networks and efficient computation in signal processing. In the context of OFT, we aim to transform a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by multiplying it with an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. The goal is for $\bm{R}$ to be dense—allowing every input dimension to influence every output dimension—to maximize expressivity, yet parameter-efficient. A naive dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is prohibitive for large foundation models. However, history offers a solution: the Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm demonstrates that a global transformation (like the Discrete Fourier Transform) can be decomposed into a sequence of local operations arranged in a specific topology known as a butterfly graph.

In the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, an $N$-point transform is recursively broken down into two $N/2$-point transforms until reaching base cases. This recursion induces a computational graph where data flows through stages of "butterfly" operations, connecting pairs of nodes across layers. Crucially, this structure allows for efficient information exchange between any source node and any destination node using only $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ operations, rather than the $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ operations required by a direct calculation. We adapt this insight to our information transmission framework described previously. Just as the butterfly graph ensures that information from any input frequency bin reaches any output time-domain sample efficiently, we posit that arranging sparse orthogonal matrices in a butterfly topology allows us to construct a globally connected (dense) transformation matrix using minimal parameters. This shifts our paradigm from viewing finetuning as learning arbitrary weights to viewing it as learning efficient pathways for information flow within a constrained grid structure. By adopting this topology, we satisfy our desiderata: achieving dense connectivity (every node communicates with every other node eventually) while minimizing free edges (trainable parameters).

# Mathematical Foundations: Butterfly Factors and Recursive Matrices

To formalize BOFT, we must rigorously define its constituent parts: the butterfly factor, the recursive butterfly matrix, and finally, how these are combined into an orthogonal parameterization. Let us assume the dimension $d$ is a power of 2, such that $d = 2^N$. We begin with the definition of a **butterfly factor**, denoted as $\bm{B}^F(k)$ for some integer $k \geq 2$. A butterfly factor is a square matrix of size $k \times k$ constructed from four diagonal blocks derived from vectors $\bm{d}_1, \bm{d}_2, \bm{d}_3, \bm{d}_4 \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. Mathematically, it is expressed as:

$$
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}.
$$

Here, $\text{diag}(\cdot)$ creates a diagonal matrix from its vector argument. This structure implies that each row interacts with exactly two rows from the next stage in terms of non-zero entries if viewed sequentially, but structurally within this block, it mixes inputs locally before passing them on. This local mixing is analogous to the basic arithmetic operation in an FFT butterfly diagram where two inputs are combined linearly to produce two outputs.

Building upon these factors, we define the **butterfly matrix** $\bm{B}(d)$ recursively for dimension $d = 2^N$. The recursion proceeds by splitting the problem into halves until reaching base cases. Formally:

$$
\!\!\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\!\cdot\!\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2}) & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0}&\bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})
\end{bmatrix}= \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{T}}(d,\frac{n}{2})\cdots\tilde{\mathtt{T}}(d,n),
$$

where $\tilde{\mathtt{T}}(n,k)$ represents what we term **block-butterfly components**. Specifically, let us refine this definition for clarity regarding orthogonality constraints later. The term $\tilde{\mathtt{T}}(n,k)$ is defined as $\texttt{textbf}{diag}(\mathtt{textbf}{B}^F_1(k),\dots,\mathtt{textbf}{B}^F_{n/k}(k))$, which constructs a block-diagonal matrix of size $n \times n$ composed of multiple disjoint butterfly factors of size $k$. In this construction, $\mathtt{textbf}{B}_1(n/2)$ and $\mathtt{textbf}{B}_2(n/2)$ are themselves independent butterfly matrices acting on half-dimensional subspaces. The full matrix $\mathtt{textbf}{B}(n)$ is thus formed by multiplying these hierarchical components together: first combining pairs at scale 2 ($k=2$), then merging those results at scale 4 ($k=4$), and so on up to scale $n$. This recursive multiplication effectively unrolls into a chain of sparse matrices where each step refines the connectivity pattern established by previous steps.

The elegance of this construction lies in its sparsity profile relative to density achievement. While a single dense orthogonal matrix would require storing roughly $n^2$ values (minus constraints), our recursive product involves only logarithmic depth ($m = \log n$ stages). At each stage $i$, we operate with block sizes that grow geometrically or shrink depending on perspective, but critically, each component matrix contains only non-zero entries corresponding to its specific block-diagonal or permuted-block-diagonal structure derived from the butterfly factors. Consequently, instead of learning one massive dense interaction map, BOFT learns a sequence of localized interactions that propagate globally through composition. This mirrors how FFT computes global correlations via local additions and multiplications repeated across log-depth layers.

# Constructing Orthogonal Matrices via Block-Butterfly Components
