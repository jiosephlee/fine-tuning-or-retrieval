# Beyond LoRA: Why 'Orthogonal' Might Be the Key to Efficient AI Tuning

The era of massive foundation models has arrived, bringing with it capabilities that were once science fiction. From chatbots that hold coherent conversations to image generators that dream up photorealistic scenes, these models are incredibly powerful. However, this power comes at a steep cost: training a model from scratch is now prohibitively expensive for most researchers and organizations. Consequently, the industry has shifted toward **finetuning**, the practice of adapting a pre-trained giant to specific downstream tasks without rebuilding it from the ground up.

Among the various strategies for efficient finetuning, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) has become a household name. LoRA works by freezing the original model weights and injecting small, trainable low-rank matrices into the architecture. While effective, LoRA relies on an additive update mechanism that changes the weight matrix by adding a perturbation. But what if there was a fundamentally different way to think about updating these weights—one that prioritizes preserving the internal geometry of the model rather than just minimizing parameter count? This is where **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** and its newer evolution, **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**, enter the conversation.

## Preserving Geometry Over Just Parameters

To understand why Orthogonal Finetuning is unique, we must first look at what happens inside a neural network during training. Neurons in a layer don't operate in isolation; they interact through their relative orientations. The angle between two neuron vectors carries semantic meaning about how those features relate to one another. Standard finetuning methods often use small learning rates to ensure the updated weights stay close to the original ones, hoping this preserves knowledge implicitly.

OFT takes a more principled approach. Instead of trying to keep weights numerically close, it explicitly ensures that the **pairwise angles between neurons remain unchanged**. Mathematically, this means transforming the original weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by multiplying it with an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. The new weight matrix becomes $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because orthogonal transformations preserve lengths and angles, this method guarantees that the relational structure learned during pre-training is mathematically intact throughout the finetuning process.

This geometric preservation offers distinct advantages over low-rank approaches like LoRA. While LoRA approximates updates via low-rank additions, OFT applies a rotation or reflection to the feature space. This multiplicative update tends to yield better spectral properties, preserving the singular values of the original weights and leading to more stable training dynamics and improved generalization on downstream tasks.

## The Bottleneck: Too Many Parameters?

If OFT is so theoretically sound, why hasn't it replaced LoRA everywhere? The answer lies in computational efficiency regarding parameters. A standard $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix requires roughly $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters to describe fully. For modern foundation models where $d$ can be thousands or tens of thousands, storing and training these dense matrices is impractical compared to LoRA's compact low-rank updates.

Early attempts to fix this involved imposing a **block-diagonal structure** on the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. By breaking $\bm{R}$ into smaller independent blocks (e.g., treating groups of neurons separately), researchers could drastically cut down the number of trainable parameters. However, this solution introduced new problems. The rigid block-diagonal pattern creates artificial boundaries between groups of neurons that shouldn't exist logically. It limits expressiveness because it cannot approximate classic linear transforms that mix information across these arbitrary blocks effectively essentially forcing an unnatural sparsity pattern onto the data flow.

## Enter BOFT: Efficiency Through Information Transmission

The breakthrough came when researchers stopped viewing parameter reduction solely as a matrix algebra problem and started looking at it as an **information transmission problem**. Imagine you need to send information from $d$ source nodes to $d$ destination nodes in a network grid. Your goal is for every source node to reach every destination node (dense connectivity) while using as few wires (edges/parameters) as possible.

In traditional block-diagonal OFT, information can only travel within isolated clusters; cross-cluster communication is impossible unless you pay for dense connections everywhere else ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$). The authors realized they could compose a dense orthogonal matrix by multiplying together several *sparse* orthogonal matrices sequentially. If designed correctly, information can hop from node to node across layers until full connectivity is achieved with far fewer total connections than a single dense matrix would require.

Inspired by this logic and drawing parallels from signal processing history—specifically the **Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform**—the paper introduces **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**. The Cooley-Tukey algorithm famously uses "butterfly" structures to compute Fourier transforms efficiently by recursively breaking down large problems into smaller ones connected in specific patterns. BOFT adopts this topology for its orthogonal matrices.

Instead of one huge dense matrix or disjointed blocks, BOFT constructs $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple sparse butterfly matrices:
$$ \bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \cdot \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1 $$
Each factor $\bm{B}_i$ is sparse but orthogonal. When multiplied together over $m$ stages (typically proportional to $\log d$), they create a dense transformation capable of mixing information globally across all neurons. Remarkably, this construction reduces the parameter complexity from quadratic $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ down to nearly linear-logarithmic $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$.

## Why Structure Matters More Than Size

One might ask: isn't restricting ourselves to butterfly structures limiting our ability to learn? Surprisingly, no. In fact, structured constraints often act as beneficial **inductive biases**. Just as convolutional layers assume translation invariance which helps image recognition, BOFT assumes that valid transformations share structural similarities with classic fast linear transforms like Fourier or Hadamard transforms found throughout nature and engineering signals processing literature shows these structures are surprisingly expressive subsets of all possible orthogonal matrices). Empirical studies confirm that BOFT can approximate random dense orthogonal matrices with significantly fewer parameters than block-diagonal OFT while maintaining higher accuracy on diverse tasks ranging from Large Language Models (LLMs) like LLaMA and BERT to Vision Transformers and Text-to-Image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion). Furthermore because BOFT subsumes standard block-diagonal OFT as a special case (when only one stage is used), it offers a flexible continuum between extreme regularization and maximum expressivity allowing practitioners fine-tune exactly how much freedom their model has during adaptation). Additionally unlike some other methods requiring careful initialization schemes BOFT initializes cleanly using identity matrices ensuring zero deviation from pre-trained weights at start training phase while introducing multiplicative dropout techniques tailored specifically handle stochasticity without disrupting orthogonality constraints). Ultimately while there slight computational overhead during training due sequential multiplication steps once trained these learned rotations merged directly back into base model resulting no extra inference latency—a critical advantage deployment scenarios where speed matters most). By reframing parameter efficiency through lens information theory leveraging elegant mathematical structures borrowed decades-old algorithms researchers have unlocked new frontier adaptive AI systems proving sometimes best way move forward revisiting old ideas fresh perspective rather chasing newest trend alone).



# From FFT to Fine-Tuning: How Butterfly Structures Supercharge AI Models

Large foundation models like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have revolutionized artificial intelligence, but their sheer scale presents a massive challenge: training them from scratch is prohibitively expensive. Consequently, the field has shifted toward efficiently adapting these pre-trained giants to specific downstream tasks. While methods like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) have gained traction, they rely on low-rank approximations that can limit expressiveness. Another approach, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), offers superior stability and generalization by preserving the geometric relationships between neurons through orthogonal transformations. However, standard OFT suffers from a critical bottleneck: it requires a vast number of trainable parameters to maintain dense orthogonal matrices, scaling quadratically with the model dimension ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$).

Enter **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**, a novel method that bridges the gap between mathematical elegance and practical efficiency. By borrowing a decades-old concept from signal processing—the butterfly structure used in the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)—researchers have created a parameter-efficient finetuning technique that generates dense orthogonal matrices with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. This blog post explores how this clever mathematical trick solves the scalability issues of previous orthogonal methods and unlocks new potential for adapting large-scale AI models.

## The Challenge of Dense Orthogonal Matrices

To understand why BOFT is necessary, we must first look at the mechanics of Orthogonal Finetuning. The core idea behind OFT is to preserve the "relational information" of a pre-trained model—specifically, the pairwise angles between neurons—during adaptation. Mathematically, this involves transforming the pre-trained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by multiplying it with an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, resulting in new weights $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and angles, this ensures that the rich semantic knowledge learned during pre-training remains intact while allowing for task-specific adjustments.

The problem lies in representing $\bm{R}$. A fully dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix contains $d^2$ degrees of freedom, requiring $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters. For modern models where $d$ can be thousands or even tens of thousands, this parameter count becomes unwieldy. Previous attempts to solve this involved using block-diagonal structures, where the large matrix is split into smaller independent blocks. While this reduces parameters significantly, it introduces rigid constraints: neurons in different blocks cannot interact during finetuning. This lack of cross-block communication limits the model's expressiveness and imposes arbitrary inductive biases based on how dimensions are grouped. Ideally, we want a method that maintains the flexibility of a dense matrix without paying the quadratic cost in parameters.

## Reimagining Matrix Multiplication as Information Transmission

The breakthrough behind BOFT comes from reframing how we think about constructing these matrices. Instead of viewing a dense orthogonal matrix as a monolithic block of numbers, the authors propose an **information transmission framework**. Imagine a grid with $d$ nodes on one side and $d$ nodes on the other. A dense connection implies that every node on the left can send information to every node on the right. If we were to build this connection using just one step (a single dense matrix), we would need roughly $d^2$ links (edges).

However, what if we break this process down into multiple steps? If we allow information to hop through intermediate layers via sparse connections, we might achieve full connectivity with far fewer total edges. In this view, generating a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ becomes equivalent to finding a sequence of sparse matrices $\bm{B}_1, \dots, \bm{B}_m$ such that their product $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \cdots \bm{B}_1$ creates paths from every source node to every destination node. The goal is to minimize the number of trainable edges while ensuring that information can flow freely across the entire system—a problem strikingly similar to designing efficient routing networks in computer science or signal propagation circuits in physics.

## Borrowing Wisdom from the Fast Fourier Transform

This is where history meets innovation. The authors realized that nature had already solved this optimization problem centuries ago in algorithms designed for speed: specifically, the **Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)**. The FFT relies on a recursive structure known as "butterfly operations," which efficiently mixes signals across different frequencies using only $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ operations instead of $N^2$.

By adopting this **butterfly topology** for their matrix factorization, BOFT constructs a dense orthogonal transformation as a product of multiple sparse butterfly matrices. Each butterfly component connects pairs of inputs in a structured way that ensures global mixing after several layers are stacked together. Crucially, because each individual butterfly component is sparse and structured (often consisting mostly of zeros), it requires very few parameters to define its non-zero entries while maintaining orthogonality constraints through techniques like Cayley parameterization or simple rotations on small blocks.

Mathematically, if we let $d = 2^N$, BOFT can represent an approximately dense orthogonal matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters by stacking roughly $\log d$ layers of these butterfly components. This stands in stark contrast to standard OFT ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$) and even block-diagonal variants which struggle with expressiveness at lower parameter counts ($\mathcal{O}(bd)$). The butterfly structure acts as a "smoother" interpolation between identity matrices (no change) and full orthogonal transformations (maximum change), providing just enough complexity to adapt to new tasks without overfitting or exploding computational costs.

## Why Structure Matters for Generalization

One might wonder if restricting ourselves to such highly structured matrices limits what BOFT can learn compared to fully flexible methods like LoRA or full fine-tuning. Surprisingly, theory suggests otherwise. The butterfly structure belongs to a family of matrices capable of recovering many classic linear transforms efficiently, including Discrete Fourier Transforms (DFT), Discrete Cosine Transforms (DCT), and Hadamard transforms. This inherent ability suggests that BOFT possesses an **implicit inductive bias** aligned with fundamental patterns found in real-world data distributions (such as periodicities or correlations).

Furthermore, because BOFT operates multiplicatively rather than additively like LoRA, it preserves the spectral properties of the original weight matrix perfectly. This means the singular values—and thus the stability dynamics—of the pre-trained model remain untouched during adaptation leading to more robust training convergence across diverse architectures ranging from Large Language Models (LLMs) to Vision Transformers and Text-to-Image diffusion models. Empirical studies confirm that BOFT not only matches but often exceeds state-of-the-art methods in accuracy while utilizing significantly fewer trainable parameters per layer.

## Conclusion: A New Paradigm for Efficient Adaptation

The introduction of Orthogonal Butterfly marks a significant leap forward in parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT). By treating matrix construction as an information transmission problem and leveraging the proven efficiency of butterfly topologies from signal processing literature, BOFT successfully decouples expressiveness from parameter count. It allows practitioners to deploy dense-like adaptations on massive foundation models without breaking their memory budgets or sacrificing generalization capabilities. As foundation models continue to grow larger and more complex, techniques like BOFT remind us that sometimes looking back at classical algorithms provides the most innovative solutions for tomorrow's challenges



# The Information Highway: Visualizing How AI Models Learn New Tasks

Large foundation models like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have revolutionized artificial intelligence, but their sheer size—often boasting hundreds of billions of parameters—makes training them from scratch prohibitively expensive. Instead, the industry standard is to adapt these massive pre-trained models to specific downstream tasks through a process called fine-tuning. While methods like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) and Orthogonal Fine-tuning (OFT) have made this feasible, they face a critical bottleneck: balancing parameter efficiency with the ability for the model to learn complex, global relationships. A recent breakthrough in this area is Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), a method that reimagines how we visualize and execute model adaptation by treating it as an information transmission problem across a network graph.

## The Challenge of Isolated Learning

To understand why BOFT is necessary, we must first look at the limitations of its predecessor, Orthogonal Fine-tuning (OFT). OFT operates on a brilliant premise: instead of changing the values of weights arbitrarily, it applies an orthogonal matrix to transform neurons within a layer. This transformation preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, effectively keeping the semantic knowledge learned during pretraining intact while allowing the model to adapt to new tasks.

However, traditional OFT struggles with scalability. A fully dense orthogonal matrix for a layer with $d$ neurons requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which defeats the purpose of efficient fine-tuning. To solve this, early versions of OFT adopted a block-diagonal structure. Imagine splitting your neural network's neurons into isolated groups where Group A talks only to Group A, and Group B talks only to Group B. While this drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters, it introduces a severe limitation: information cannot flow between these isolated blocks. This creates an "inductive bias" where the model assumes that certain features are independent when they might actually be deeply interconnected in reality. The result is a model that is efficient but potentially lacks the expressiveness needed for complex tasks.

## Reframing Fine-Tuning as Information Transmission

The authors of the BOFT paper proposed a novel way to think about this problem: viewing matrix factorization as **information transmission**. Instead of looking at weight matrices as static grids of numbers, imagine them as a dynamic graph where data flows from one set of nodes to another.

In this framework, generating a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is equivalent to ensuring that every node in the input layer can send information to every node in the output layer. If we simply multiply two matrices together ($\bm{R} = \bm{B}_2 \times \bm{B}_1$), we are essentially stacking two stages of communication. The first matrix $\bm{B}_1$ allows information to move from level 1 to level 2, and $\bm{B}_2$ moves it from level 2 to level 3. For the final result to be "dense"—meaning every input can influence every output—the path taken by this information must eventually connect every possible pair of start and end nodes.

This visualization reveals why simple block-diagonal approaches fail; they create disconnected islands in our graph where information from node 1 never reaches node 3 if they belong to different blocks. To fix this without exploding our parameter count, we need a network topology that connects everything efficiently using as few edges (trainable parameters) as possible.

## Enter the Butterfly Structure

The solution lies in borrowing ideas from computer science history: the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). Just as FFT algorithms use "butterfly" operations to compute transforms efficiently by breaking problems down recursively, BOFT uses **butterfly factorization** to construct its orthogonal matrices.

In this structure, instead of isolating neurons into rigid blocks or connecting everyone directly (which costs too much), we arrange connections in layers that resemble a butterfly diagram. In each stage of this multi-layered graph, nodes exchange information with just a few neighbors in a specific pattern before passing data to the next stage. After $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ such stages, information originating from any single input node has propagated across the entire system and reached every output node.

Mathematically, this allows BOFT to represent a dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix as a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices. Consequently, the total number of trainable parameters drops from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ down to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$. This is a massive improvement over standard OFT while maintaining full connectivity across the neural network layers. Unlike block-diagonal methods that force independence between feature groups, BOFT ensures that even distant parts of the network can communicate effectively through these intermediate butterfly steps.

## Why Connectivity Matters for Generalization

The shift from isolated blocks to connected butterfly paths offers more than just mathematical elegance; it fundamentally changes how the model learns new tasks. By enforcing dense connectivity through sparse factorization, BOFT introduces an implicit **inductive bias** toward structured linear transforms found in nature and signal processing, such as Discrete Fourier Transforms and Hadamard Transforms.

Because BOFT can approximate these classic transforms while remaining flexible enough for general adaptation, it finds a "sweet spot" between expressivity and regularization. It avoids overfitting by restricting the search space to structured subsets of orthogonal matrices, yet it retains enough freedom to capture complex dependencies that block-diagonal methods would miss entirely. Empirical studies show that when applied to Large Language Models (LLMs), Vision Transformers (ViTs), and text-to-image diffusion models, BOFT consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods like LoRA and standard OFT across various benchmarks. Furthermore, unlike some other efficient methods that introduce latency during inference because they add extra modules (like adapters), BOFT's learned transformations can be merged directly into the original weights after training, resulting in zero additional inference cost.

## Conclusion

The development of Orthogonal Butterfly marks a significant step forward in making large-scale AI adaptable without compromising on performance or efficiency. By visualizing fine-tuning not just as weight adjustment but as an **information highway**, researchers were able to identify that isolating parts of a model limits its potential growth compared to letting data flow freely through optimized pathways like butterfly structures. As foundation models continue to grow larger and more diverse in application, techniques like BOFT will likely become essential tools for ensuring these systems remain agile, communicative, and capable of mastering new domains with minimal computational overhead.



# One Method Fits All? Testing BOFT on Language, Vision, and Art Generation

Foundation models have revolutionized artificial intelligence, powering everything from chatbots like ChatGPT to image generators like Stable Diffusion. However, these massive models come with a significant cost: they are prohibitively expensive to train from scratch. Consequently, the community has shifted focus toward efficiently adapting these pre-trained giants to specific downstream tasks. While various Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods exist, many struggle to generalize beyond their intended niches. A recent breakthrough challenges this status quo with Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), a unified framework that demonstrates superior performance across language, vision, and generative art tasks.

## The Challenge of Efficient Adaptation

Adapting large foundation models typically falls into three categories: model fine-tuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. Model fine-tuning stands out because it modifies the existing architecture without introducing inference latency, making it highly practical for deployment. Early approaches relied on small learning rates to keep the fine-tuned model close to the original, preserving pre-training knowledge. More principled methods, such as Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT), emerged by enforcing that neurons maintain their pairwise angles during adaptation. This geometric constraint ensures that the semantic relationships learned during pre-training remain intact.

However, standard OFT faces a critical bottleneck: parameter efficiency. To preserve orthogonality in high-dimensional spaces, OFT traditionally requires a dense orthogonal matrix, which scales quadratically with dimension ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$). To mitigate this, previous versions of OFT adopted a block-diagonal structure. While this reduced parameters, it introduced rigid sparsity patterns that limited expressiveness and failed to approximate classic linear transforms effectively. The core challenge became clear: how can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix that remains parameter-efficient without sacrificing the flexibility needed for diverse tasks?

## Introducing Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)

The solution lies in reimagining how we generate dense orthogonal matrices. Instead of treating them as monolithic blocks of parameters, the authors propose viewing matrix generation through an **information transmission** lens. In this framework, creating a dense connection between inputs and outputs is analogous to transmitting information across a grid-structured graph. The goal is to find a topology where every node can reach every other node using the minimum number of "edges" or trainable parameters.

Inspired by the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform algorithm, BOFT utilizes **butterfly structures**. Just as the butterfly algorithm allows for efficient signal processing by decomposing operations into sparse steps, BOFT composes a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices. Mathematically, if we need a $d$-dimensional matrix, BOFT constructs it using $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse factors rather than one dense factor. This reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ down to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$.

This approach offers two distinct advantages over previous methods:
*   **Dense Connectivity:** Unlike block-diagonal OFT which isolates groups of neurons, BOFT creates smooth interactions across all dimensions while maintaining sparsity in individual layers of the factorization.
*   **Structured Inductive Bias:** The butterfly structure naturally aligns with many classic linear transforms (like Fourier and Hadamard transforms). This alignment acts as an implicit regularization mechanism, preventing overfitting and improving generalization—a crucial trait when moving between vastly different domains like text and images.

## Empirical Validation Across Domains

The true test of any general-purpose fine-tuning method is its ability to perform well outside its original context. While earlier studies focused primarily on controlling text-to-image diffusion models, this paper presents an extensive empirical study applying BOFT to Large Language Models (LLMs), Large Vision Transformers (ViTs), and text-to-image generators simultaneously.

In Natural Language Processing tasks involving LLMs such as GPT-style architectures, BOFT consistently outperformed state-of-the-art competitors like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation). LoRA relies on low-rank updates which assume that task-specific deviations lie in a low-dimensional subspace. In contrast, BOFT leverages its structured orthogonality to explore a richer hypothesis space within the orthogonal group while keeping parameter counts low. The results showed that BOFT achieved higher accuracy on benchmark datasets while utilizing fewer trainable parameters than traditional block-diagonal OFT variants.

The versatility extended equally into Computer Vision and Generative AI:
*   **Vision Tasks:** When adapting large ViTs for classification or detection tasks on standard benchmarks like ImageNet subsets or COCO object detection datasets using ResNet backbones or Swin Transformers were adapted successfully with minimal performance drop compared to full fine-tuning but with significantly lower memory footprints during training.*
*   **Text-to-Image Generation:** Perhaps most impressively, BOFT was applied to Stable Diffusion models for personalized image generation tasks (such as DreamBooth-style customization). Here, preserving the intricate geometric relationships within the UNet architecture is vital for maintaining image quality while learning new concepts like specific objects or artistic styles.**BOFT demonstrated superior convergence stability and generated higher-fidelity images compared to LoRA**, proving that orthogonal constraints are just as valuable in pixel-space generation as they are in token prediction.**

## Why One Framework Works Everywhere

What makes BOFT uniquely capable of handling such disparate tasks? The answer lies in its balance between expressivity and regularity. Full fine-tuning offers maximum expressivity but risks catastrophic forgetting or overfitting due to excessive freedom in parameter space.**LoRA offers extreme efficiency but restricts movement to low-rank subspaces.**BOFT strikes a middle ground by restricting updates to a structured subset of the orthogonal group defined by butterfly factorization.**This structure implicitly encodes knowledge about efficient transformations found in nature and signal processing**, allowing the model to adapt quickly without losing its foundational capabilities regardless of whether it is predicting words or pixels.**Furthermore,** because BOFT multiplies an orthogonal matrix onto the weights rather than adding low-rank matrices,**it perfectly preserves the spectral norm**of the original weight matrix.**This spectral preservation enhances training stability**, preventing issues like exploding gradients that often plague deep networks during fine-tuning.**Additionally,** once training is complete,**the learned butterfly components can be fused directly into the base model**, resulting in zero additional inference latency**—a critical advantage for real-time applications.**While there is a slight computational overhead during training due to repeated matrix multiplications**,this cost is negligible compared to the gains in sample efficiency and final model quality.**By subsuming block-diagonal OFT as a special case **(when only one factor is used)**,**BOFT provides a generalized framework that scales seamlessly from small adjustments to comprehensive adaptations.**The extensive experimental results confirm that this single methodology not only matches but frequently exceeds current state-of-the-art methods across language,vision,and generative modeling**,establishing itself as a robust candidate for future foundation model adaptation strategies.*
