# Chapter 1: Introduction to Efficient Adaptation of Foundation Models

## The Rise of Large Foundation Models and Training Costs

The landscape of artificial intelligence has undergone a seismic shift with the advent of large foundation models. Recent architectures, such as ChatGPT for natural language processing and Stable Diffusion for image generation, have demonstrated remarkable generalization abilities across a vast array of domains. These models possess an unprecedented capacity to learn complex patterns from massive datasets, enabling them to perform tasks ranging from creative writing to sophisticated visual synthesis. However, this surge in performance is inextricably linked to a dramatic increase in scale. Modern foundation models often contain billions of parameters; for instance, GPT-3 boasts approximately 175 billion parameters. This exponential growth in model size presents a formidable economic and computational barrier: training such models from scratch is prohibitively expensive for most researchers and organizations. Consequently, the field faces a critical imperative: how can we leverage the immense capabilities of these pre-trained giants without incurring the costs associated with retraining them entirely? The answer lies in the ability to efficiently adapt existing foundation models to specific downstream tasks, preserving their foundational knowledge while tailoring them to new objectives.

## Overview of Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) Paradigms

Given the constraints on computational resources, the research community has focused intensively on Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) strategies. The primary goal of PEFT is to adapt a large pre-trained model to a downstream task by updating only a small subset of its parameters or introducing a minimal number of new trainable parameters, thereby avoiding the modification of the entire model. Currently, there are three dominant paradigms for achieving this efficient task adaptation: model fine-tuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. Each approach offers distinct advantages regarding implementation complexity, storage requirements, and inference latency. Understanding these distinctions is crucial for selecting the appropriate method for a given application scenario and for appreciating the niche that newer methods like Orthogonal Fine-Tuning aim to fill.

Model fine-tuning represents the most direct approach within this triad. In this paradigm, the underlying architecture of the model remains completely unchanged. Instead of inserting new modules or modifying inputs, a subset of the original model's parameters is updated during training on the target dataset. This method distinguishes itself through its conceptual simplicity and its lack of inference latency; once trained, the adapted model runs exactly as fast as the original pre-trained version because no additional operations are required during inference. In contrast, adapter tuning involves inserting small neural network modules, known as adapters, into specific layers of the pre-trained model. These adapters are trained while the original weights remain frozen or partially frozen. While effective at isolating task-specific knowledge, this approach increases both memory usage during training and inference time due to the extra computations introduced by the adapter layers. Similarly, prompt tuning takes a different route by attaching additional trainable prefix tokens to the input sequence rather than altering internal weights or adding architectural components. While prompt tuning is highly parameter-efficient in terms of storage (often requiring only megabytes instead of gigabytes), it can sometimes struggle with stability compared to weight-based methods and may alter input representations in ways that are difficult to control precisely. Among these options, model fine-tuning stands out as a robust balance between simplicity and performance-free inference speed.

## Principles of Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT): Preserving Pairwise Angles

Within the realm of model fine-tuning, recent research has sought to move beyond ad-hoc heuristics toward more principled frameworks grounded in geometric theory. Traditional fine-tuning methods often rely on using small learning rates to ensure that the finetuned weights do not drift too far from their initial values. While empirically successful, this approach lacks a rigorous theoretical justification regarding what aspect of pre-training knowledge is being preserved. A more structured approach recognizes that weight matrices possess inherent relational information encoded in their geometry—specifically, the pairwise angles between neurons (represented as column vectors). Preserving these angles ensures that the semantic relationships learned during pre-training remain intact even as individual neuron magnitudes might shift slightly. This insight forms the bedrock of Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT).

In OFT, rather than directly updating weight matrices or adding low-rank perturbations as seen in Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), we reparameterize the weight update process using an orthogonal transformation matrix $\bm{R}$. Consider a pretrained linear layer represented by $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$. In standard fine-tuning or LoRA, one might compute $\bm{W}_{new} = \bm{W}^0 + \Delta\bm{W}$. In OFT, however, we define $\bm{W}_{new} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix ($\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$). Geometrically, multiplying by an orthogonal matrix corresponds to rotating or reflecting vectors without changing their lengths or relative angles. Consequently, if two neurons were separated by angle $\theta$ before finetuning ($(\bm{w}_i^0)^\top \bm{w}_j^0 / (\|\bm{w}_i^0\| \|\bm{w}_j^0\|)$), they will maintain exactly that angle after transformation ($(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0)^\top (\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0) / (\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0\| \|\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0\|)$). This property allows OFT to preserve rich semantic structures derived from pre-training while allowing sufficient flexibility for task adaptation through rotation in high-dimensional space. To ensure numerical stability and enforce orthogonality during optimization without resorting to projection steps at every gradient update step—which would be computationally expensive—OFT employs Cayley parameterization where $\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{T}-\bar{\alpha})^{-1}$ with $\bar{\alpha}$ being skew-symmetric ($\bar{\alpha}=-\bar{\alpha}^\top$). Furthermore, initialization plays a vital role; starting with $\bar{\alpha}=0$ implies $\bar{\alpha}=I$, ensuring zero initialization equivalent behavior where training begins exactly at pretrained weights before drifting toward optimal rotations for downstream tasks.

## Limitations of Block-Diagonal Structures in Current OFT Methods

Despite its theoretical elegance and demonstrated success in stabilizing training for tasks like controllable text-to-image generation via diffusion models, standard OFT faces a significant scalability challenge related to parameter efficiency. A fully dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix requires $d(d-1)/2$ independent parameters (roughly proportional to $d^2$). For modern foundation models where hidden dimensions $d$ can reach thousands or tens of thousands (e.g., 4096 or higher), storing and optimizing such dense matrices becomes impractical compared to other PEFT methods like LoRA which scales linearly with rank $r$. # Chapter 2: An Information Transmission Framework for Matrix Factorization

## Preliminaries: Reparameterizing Weights with Orthogonal Matrices

Before we can appreciate the novelty of the information transmission framework, we must first establish the foundational mechanics of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). The core challenge in adapting large foundation models lies in updating the massive weight matrices without destroying the rich semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining. Standard approaches like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) address this by adding a low-rank update matrix to the pretrained weights. In contrast, OFT adopts a multiplicative strategy. Instead of adding a perturbation, OFT reparameterizes the new weight matrix as the product of a learnable orthogonal matrix and the frozen pretrained weight matrix. Consider a pretrained linear layer represented by a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$. In standard inference, an input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ produces an output $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. Under OFT, this forward pass is modified to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix learned during fine-tuning.

The choice of an orthogonal matrix is not arbitrary; it is driven by a desire to preserve the geometric relationships inherent in the pretrained model. Specifically, orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles between neurons (columns of $\bm{W}^0$). By maintaining these angles, OFT ensures that the relative orientation of features learned during pretraining remains intact, thereby preserving semantic knowledge while allowing for task-specific adaptation. To implement this mathematically while ensuring numerical stability and ease of optimization, OFT employs Cayley parameterization. Rather than optimizing $\bm{R}$ directly, which would require enforcing complex orthogonality constraints at every step, we optimize a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ (where $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$) and map it to the orthogonal group via the formula $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. This guarantees that $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout training regardless of the values in $\bm{Q}$, provided $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is invertible.

However, a dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix contains $d(d-1)/2$ independent parameters (degrees of freedom), leading to a computational cost of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for storage and updates. For modern foundation models where $d$ can reach thousands or tens of thousands, this parameter count is prohibitive compared to low-rank methods like LoRA. To mitigate this, early iterations of OFT introduced a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$, decomposing it into smaller independent blocks such as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$. While this successfully reduced the parameter count to roughly proportional to $bd$ (where $b$ is the block size), it imposed severe structural limitations. The block-diagonal assumption implies that dimensions within different blocks never interact; information cannot flow across block boundaries. This creates rigid inductive biases that may hinder expressivity and fail to approximate essential linear transforms that require global mixing of dimensions. Consequently, we face a critical dilemma: how can we construct a *dense* orthogonal matrix—one that allows full interaction between all dimensions—without succumbing to the high parameter cost associated with density? The answer lies not in looking at matrices as static grids of numbers, but dynamic systems for moving information.

## The Information Transmission Analogy: Grid Graphs and Connectivity

To resolve the tension between density and efficiency, we shift our perspective from linear algebra to network theory by framing matrix factorization as an information transmission problem. Imagine we wish to generate a dense target matrix $\bm{R}$ by multiplying together $m$ sparse matrices: $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$. If we were to analyze this purely algebraically, determining which entries must be non-zero in each factor to yield a dense product would be computationally opaque. However, if we view each multiplication step as a stage in a communication network, the problem becomes visually intuitive and structurally clear. We can conceptualize this process as transmitting information across a grid-structured graph consisting of $d$ rows and $m+1$ columns (levels).

In this graphical representation, each row index corresponds to one of the $d$ dimensions (nodes) at any given level. The initial level (column 0) represents the input nodes corresponding to the source dimensions before any transformation occurs. The final level (column $m+1$) represents the destination nodes after passing through all $m$ transformations. Each intermediate level corresponds to the state of information after passing through one specific sparse factor matrix $\bm{B}_i$. The transition from level $i$ to level $i+1$ is governed entirely by the connectivity pattern defined by the sparse matrix $\BMathbf B_{i+1}. If an entry $(j_1, j_2)$ in matrix $\BMathbf B_{i+1}$ is non-zero ($(\BMathbf B_{i+1})_{j_1 j_2} \neq 0$), it signifies that there exists a directed edge allowing information to flow from node $j_2$ at level $i$ to node $j_1$ at level $i+1$. Conversely, if $(\BMathbf B_{i+1})_{j_1 j_2}$ is zero, no direct path exists between those specific nodes at that specific stage; information cannot jump directly from source dimension $j_2$ to target dimension $j_1$ in this single step.

This analogy transforms abstract matrix multiplication into a physical routing problem on a layered graph. When we compute the product $\BMathbf R = BMathbf B_m BMathbf B_{m-1} cdots BMathbf B_1$, we are essentially asking: "Is there at least one valid path from every node in Level 0 to every node in Level m+1?" For instance, consider a scenario where we want $(\BMathbf R)_{31}$ (information flowing from dimension 3 at input to dimension 3 at output? No wait let's correct indices for clarity). Let us say we want information from node 3 at Level 0 to reach node 5 at Level m+1. Even if there is no direct edge from node 3 at Level 0 to node 5 at Level 1 (perhaps $(\BMathbf B_1)_{53}=0$), there might be an indirect path: Node 3 connects to Node 7 at Level 2 via some intermediate steps involving other matrices like $(\BMathbf B_2)$ and $(\BMathbf B_3)$ eventually reaching Node 5 at Level m+1. If such paths exist for all pairs $(source\_node\_at\_L0$, destination\_node\_at\_L(m+1)), then the resulting product matrix will be fully dense; every output dimension receives aggregated information from every input dimension through some chain of dependencies across layers.

Therefore, constructing a dense orthogonal matrix via sparse factors becomes equivalent to designing a multi-layer network topology where global connectivity emerges from local interactions. A single dense orthogonal matrix corresponds trivially to having direct edges connecting every pair of nodes between two levels—a complete bipartite graph requiring $\mathcal O(d^2)$ edges per layer—and thus suffers from poor scalability regarding trainable parameters because almost every edge carries learnable weights under standard assumptions unless constrained otherwise . But crucially , our goal here isn't simply connectivity ; it's achieving full connectivity using minimal resources while respecting strict orthogonality rules governing what kinds of connections are permissible simultaneously within each layer-matrix pair . # Chapter 3: The Butterfly Structure and Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)

Having established the information transmission framework in the previous chapter, we now turn our attention to the central innovation of this work: Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). The primary challenge identified was the inefficiency of traditional Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which relies on block-diagonal structures that impose rigid sparsity patterns and fail to capture the rich interactions between all neurons in a layer. While OFT preserves pairwise angles between neurons—a crucial property for retaining pre-trained knowledge—the cost of maintaining a fully dense orthogonal matrix scales quadratically with the dimension $d$, requiring $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. This chapter details how we overcome this limitation by drawing inspiration from classical signal processing algorithms. Specifically, we leverage the butterfly structures inherent in the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm to construct a dense orthogonal matrix through a product of sparse matrices. This approach allows us to achieve parameter efficiency scaling as $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ while maintaining the expressivity required for complex downstream tasks. We will formally define the mathematical building blocks of BOFT, explore its recursive construction, and discuss critical implementation strategies such as identity initialization and multiplicative dropout that ensure stable training.

# From Signal Processing to Matrix Factorization

The conceptual leap from standard finetuning methods to BOFT lies in recognizing a deep parallel between information propagation in neural networks and efficient computation in signal processing. In the context of OFT, we aim to transform a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by multiplying it with an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. The goal is for $\bm{R}$ to be dense—allowing every input dimension to influence every output dimension—to maximize expressivity, yet parameter-efficient. A naive dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is prohibitive for large foundation models. However, history offers a solution: the Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm demonstrates that a global transformation (like the Discrete Fourier Transform) can be decomposed into a sequence of local operations arranged in a specific topology known as a butterfly graph.

In the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, an $N$-point transform is recursively broken down into two $N/2$-point transforms until reaching base cases. This recursion induces a computational graph where data flows through stages of "butterfly" operations, connecting pairs of nodes across layers. Crucially, this structure allows for efficient information exchange between any source node and any destination node using only $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ operations, rather than the $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ operations required by a direct calculation. We adapt this insight to our information transmission framework described previously. Just as the butterfly graph ensures that information from any input frequency bin reaches any output time-domain sample efficiently, we posit that arranging sparse orthogonal matrices in a butterfly topology allows us to construct a globally connected (dense) transformation matrix using minimal parameters. This shifts our paradigm from viewing finetuning as learning arbitrary weights to viewing it as learning efficient pathways for information flow within a constrained grid structure. By adopting this topology, we satisfy our desiderata: achieving dense connectivity (every node communicates with every other node eventually) while minimizing free edges (trainable parameters).

# Mathematical Foundations: Butterfly Factors and Recursive Matrices

To formalize BOFT, we must rigorously define its constituent parts: the butterfly factor, the recursive butterfly matrix, and finally, how these are combined into an orthogonal parameterization. Let us assume the dimension $d$ is a power of 2, such that $d = 2^N$. We begin with the definition of a **butterfly factor**, denoted as $\bm{B}^F(k)$ for some integer $k \geq 2$. A butterfly factor is a square matrix of size $k \times k$ constructed from four diagonal blocks derived from vectors $\bm{d}_1, \bm{d}_2, \bm{d}_3, \bm{d}_4 \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. Mathematically, it is expressed as:

$$
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}.
$$

Here, $\text{diag}(\cdot)$ creates a diagonal matrix from its vector argument. This structure implies that each row interacts with exactly two rows from the next stage in terms of non-zero entries if viewed sequentially, but structurally within this block, it mixes inputs locally before passing them on. This local mixing is analogous to the basic arithmetic operation in an FFT butterfly diagram where two inputs are combined linearly to produce two outputs.

Building upon these factors, we define the **butterfly matrix** $\bm{B}(d)$ recursively for dimension $d = 2^N$. The recursion proceeds by splitting the problem into halves until reaching base cases. Formally:

$$
\!\!\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\!\cdot\!\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2}) & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0}&\bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})
\end{bmatrix}= \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{T}}(d,\frac{n}{2})\cdots\tilde{\mathtt{T}}(d,n),
$$

where $\tilde{\mathtt{T}}(n,k)$ represents what we term **block-butterfly components**. Specifically, let us refine this definition for clarity regarding orthogonality constraints later. The term $\tilde{\mathtt{T}}(n,k)$ is defined as $\texttt{textbf}{diag}(\mathtt{textbf}{B}^F_1(k),\dots,\mathtt{textbf}{B}^F_{n/k}(k))$, which constructs a block-diagonal matrix of size $n \times n$ composed of multiple disjoint butterfly factors of size $k$. In this construction, $\mathtt{textbf}{B}_1(n/2)$ and $\mathtt{textbf}{B}_2(n/2)$ are themselves independent butterfly matrices acting on half-dimensional subspaces. The full matrix $\mathtt{textbf}{B}(n)$ is thus formed by multiplying these hierarchical components together: first combining pairs at scale 2 ($k=2$), then merging those results at scale 4 ($k=4$), and so on up to scale $n$. This recursive multiplication effectively unrolls into a chain of sparse matrices where each step refines the connectivity pattern established by previous steps.

The elegance of this construction lies in its sparsity profile relative to density achievement. While a single dense orthogonal matrix would require storing roughly $n^2$ values (minus constraints), our recursive product involves only logarithmic depth ($m = \log n$ stages). At each stage $i$, we operate with block sizes that grow geometrically or shrink depending on perspective, but critically, each component matrix contains only non-zero entries corresponding to its specific block-diagonal or permuted-block-diagonal structure derived from the butterfly factors. Consequently, instead of learning one massive dense interaction map, BOFT learns a sequence of localized interactions that propagate globally through composition. This mirrors how FFT computes global correlations via local additions and multiplications repeated across log-depth layers.
