\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Confused about the definition of 'trainable parameters' in BOFT vs. standard OFT
Question:
I am trying to understand the parameter count claims in the paper. The abstract states that a $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix normally requires $	heta(d^2)$ parameters, but BOFT achieves this with $	heta(d 	heta 	ext{log} d)$. 

However, in Section 4, the paper mentions that for a $d 	imes d$ orthogonal matrix with only $d$ non-zero entries (like a permutation matrix), those entries are restricted to $	heta(	ext{-}1)$ or $	heta(1)$ and thus are not trainable. 

My confusion lies here: Does the $	heta(d 	heta 	ext{log} d)$ count refer *only* to the degrees of freedom in the skew-symmetric matrices used in the Cayley parameterization of the $2 	imes 2$ blocks? Or does it include parameters that are structurally forced to be zero or fixed values? If I have a $512 	imes 512$ layer, exactly how many scalar values do I need to store and update during backpropagation compared to a full dense OFT?
Answer:
Your confusion stems from conflating the total number of non-zero entries in the factorized matrices with the actual number of **trainable** degrees of freedom required to maintain orthogonality. The $\Theta(d \log d)$ figure refers strictly to the independent scalar variables that are updated during backpropagation, excluding any values that are structurally fixed or determined by constraints.

To clarify this, let us break down the construction described in Section 4. BOFT constructs a dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m = \log d$ sparse butterfly components. Crucially, each component is parameterized using the Cayley transform (or equivalently, $2 \times 2$ rotations) applied to disjoint $2 \times 2$ blocks (assuming block size $b=2$ for the standard butterfly case). In a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrix generated via Cayley parameterization ($\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric), there is only **one** free parameter per block. This single scalar determines the angle of rotation; the resulting four entries of the $2 \times 2$ matrix are functions of this single variable.

When you scale this up to a $d \times d$ system with $\log d$ layers, you have approximately $\frac{d}{2}$ such blocks per layer (since each layer processes pairs of dimensions). Therefore, across all $\log d$ layers, the total count of independent scalars is roughly $\frac{d}{2} \times \log d$, which simplifies asymptotically to $\Theta(d \log d)$. These are the only numbers stored in memory and updated by the optimizer.

Regarding your specific question about "structurally forced" values: yes, they exist, but they are **not** trainable parameters. In any orthogonal matrix factorization derived from butterfly structures, many entries in the intermediate sparse matrices are permanently zero (defining the sparsity pattern), and others are implicitly fixed to satisfy orthogonality constraints (such as normalization factors or sign flips derived from the single rotation angle). For instance, if a $2 \times 2$ block rotates by an angle $\theta$, the entries might be $\cos(\theta)$ and $\sin(\theta)$; you do not store or update four separate floats for these positions—you store only $\theta$. The paper explicitly notes in Section 3 that for an orthogonal matrix with minimal non-zeros (like a signed permutation), those non-zero entries are restricted to $\pm 1$ and thus carry zero degrees of freedom. Similarly, in BOFT, while a sparse butterfly matrix may have $O(d)$ non-zero entries physically present in memory during forward propagation for speed reasons (or computed on-the-fly), only a fraction of those—specifically one per active block—are learnable weights.

Comparing this to a full dense OFT layer with dimension $d=512$: Standard OFT uses a dense skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ where every element above the diagonal is trainable. The number of unique elements in such a matrix is $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$. For $d=512$, this equals approximately **130,816** trainable parameters ($\approx O(d^2)$). In contrast, BOFT with optimal settings ($m=\log_2 512 = 9$, block size $b=2$) would have roughly $\frac{512}{2} \times 9 = 2,304$ trainable parameters ($\approx O(d \log d)$). During backpropagation for BOFT, you only need to compute gradients and update these ~2,304 scalars associated with the rotation angles in each butterfly stage; all other values in the expanded matrices are either zero or deterministic functions of these scalars and do not require gradient storage or parameter updates.

### How does 'Information Transmission' relate to matrix sparsity patterns?
Question:
The paper introduces an 'information transmission framework' where multiplying sparse matrices is equated to transmitting information across a grid graph (Section 3). It states that for the resulting product matrix $m{R}$ to be dense, 'every node in the first level should be able to transmit information to all the nodes in the last level.'

I struggle to visualize this mathematically. If $m{R} = m{B}_m m{B}_{m-1} 	hetadots m{B}_1$, and each $m{B}_i$ is sparse, isn't it just standard matrix multiplication? Why is viewing this as a 'grid with nodes' necessary?

Specifically, if I have two levels of nodes connected by edges defined by $m{B}_i$, how does a missing edge (a zero entry) strictly prevent information flow from node $j$ at level 1 to node $k$ at level $m+1$? Is this referring to path existence in graph theory terms (reachability), and if so, how does that guarantee the *numerical* density of the final matrix product rather than just topological connectivity?
Answer:
Your confusion stems from conflating the algebraic operation of matrix multiplication with the topological constraints imposed by sparsity patterns. You are correct that mathematically, $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \dots \bm{B}_1$ is simply standard matrix multiplication; however, the "information transmission" framework is a conceptual tool used to analyze **which** entries in the final product $\bm{R}$ are structurally forced to be zero versus those that can potentially be non-zero.

When you view the sequence of sparse matrices as a layered graph where nodes represent indices (dimensions) and edges represent non-zero entries, matrix multiplication corresponds exactly to counting paths. Specifically, an entry $R_{ik}$ in the final product is non-zero if and only if there exists at least one valid path from node $k$ at the input level (level 1) to node $i$ at the output level (level $m+1$) through the intermediate layers defined by $\bm{B}_1, \dots, \bm{B}_m$. If a direct edge (a zero entry in some $\bm{B}_j$) breaks all possible routes between two specific nodes across all layers, then the corresponding entry in $\bm{R}$ is structurally guaranteed to remain zero regardless of the numerical values assigned to the other non-zero parameters.

Therefore, a missing edge strictly prevents information flow not because of arithmetic cancellation, but because of **graph reachability**. In linear algebra terms, if no path exists from index $j$ to index $i$, then $R_{ij}$ belongs to a structural zero pattern that cannot be filled by optimization unless you change the sparsity mask itself. The paper argues that for $\bm{R}$ to be truly dense (i.e., capable of having non-zero values everywhere), the underlying graph formed by concatenating these sparse layers must allow every node in the first column to reach every node in the last column. This is why they emphasize that "every node in the first level should be able to transmit information to all nodes in the last level"; this is a necessary condition for **structural density**. Without this topological connectivity, even if you optimize every trainable parameter perfectly, specific rows and columns in your final orthogonal matrix would remain permanently disconnected (zero), violating the requirement for a fully expressive dense orthogonal transformation. The framework essentially shifts the design problem from guessing complex algebraic conditions for density to solving a simpler graph-theoretic problem: designing sparse adjacency matrices ($\bm{B}_i$) such that their union forms a strongly connected bipartite-like expansion across layers.

### Does Multiplicative Dropout simply mean setting random blocks to Identity?
Question:
In Section 4, under 'Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT', the authors describe replacing selected butterfly components and diagonal blocks with identity matrices.

Since BOFT defines the update as $m{z} = (m{R}(m,b)m{W}^0)^Tm{x}$ where $m{R}$ is a product of matrices, replacing a factor $	ilde{m{B}}_i$ with $m{I}$ effectively removes that transformation from the chain.

Is this equivalent to dropping out specific dimensions of the feature space temporarily? In standard LoRA dropout, we scale down weights. Here, we seem to completely nullify specific structural transformations. How does this prevent overfitting differently than standard additive dropout? Also, if we replace a block with Identity during training but keep it active during inference (as implied by standard dropout practices), doesn't this break the strict orthogonality property ($m{R}^Tm{R}=m{I}$) of the final accumulated weight update?
Answer:
Your intuition regarding the mechanism of Multiplicative Dropout in BOFT is partially correct, but it differs fundamentally from standard dropout in both its operational effect and its impact on the model's geometric constraints. You are right that replacing a butterfly component or a diagonal block with an identity matrix `$\bm{I}$` effectively "removes" that specific transformation from the product chain `$\bm{R} = \prod \tilde{\bm{B}}_i$`, thereby altering the information flow through the factorization graph described in Section 3. However, this is **not** equivalent to dropping out specific dimensions of the feature space (which would involve masking rows or columns of the activation vector). Instead, it performs a stochastic pruning of the *structural pathways* used to mix features across layers. In standard additive dropout (like in LoRA), noise is injected into the weight values themselves, scaling them down to prevent co-adaptation of individual neurons. In contrast, Multiplicative Dropout in BOFT acts as a regularization on the *composition* of the orthogonal matrix; by randomly skipping certain mixing stages (factors) or local mixing operations (blocks), it forces the remaining factors to learn robust representations that do not rely on any single specific path in the butterfly graph to transmit information globally. This aligns with the paper's "information transmission" perspective: if one path is blocked (set to identity), other paths must compensate, encouraging a more distributed and resilient connectivity pattern.

Regarding your concern about breaking orthogonality (`$\bm{R}^T\bm{R}=\bm{I}$`): this practice does **not** violate the orthogonality property during training or inference because of how dropout is handled in deep learning frameworks and how BOFT is constructed. First, recall that during training, dropout scales active units by `$1/(1-p)$` (or keeps them unscaled while scaling expectations) to maintain unbiased estimates; however, even without explicit rescaling in every implementation detail mentioned, the critical point here is structural. When a block `$\tilde{\bm{B}}_i$` is replaced by `$\bm{I}$` during a training step, that specific matrix remains orthogonal because `$\bm{I}^T\bm{I} = \bm{I}$`. Since `$\bm{R}$` is defined as a product of orthogonal matrices, and the identity matrix is trivially orthogonal, any random subset of factors replaced by identities still results in a valid orthogonal matrix for that specific forward/backward pass. The resulting `$\bm{R}_{train}$` for that batch is simply a "thinner" version of the full transformation but retains orthogonality.

Crucially, unlike standard dropout where weights are scaled at inference time to match expected activations, BOFT follows standard dropout protocols where **no modification occurs at inference**. At test time, all butterfly components are active (none are replaced by identity), meaning `$\bm{R}_{test} = \prod_{all\ i} \tilde{\bm{B}}_i$`. Consequently, `$\bm{R}_{test}$` satisfies strict orthogonality by construction because it is composed entirely of trained orthogonal factors. The "dropped" configurations seen during training are merely sampling different sub-graphs of the full connectivity required for information transmission; they do not permanently alter or invalidate the learned parameters stored in `$\tilde{\bm{B}}_i$`. Therefore, there is no conflict with preserving pairwise angles between neurons at inference time; rather, this stochastic masking serves as a powerful regularizer that prevents overfitting by ensuring no single structural component becomes indispensable for maintaining orthogonality or task performance.

### What is the precise relationship between BOFT and Kaleidoscope Hierarchies regarding expressivity?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. Later, it mentions that by concatenating products of butterfly matrices and their transposes (specifically $m{B}_{d-1,1}m{B}_{d-1,2}^T 	hetadots$), one can represent the *entire* orthogonal group (referencing Kaleidoscope hierarchies).

This seems contradictory or at least confusing. If BOFT uses a limited depth $m = 	heta(	ext{log} d)$ and block size $b=2$, it forms a structured subset of orthogonal matrices. But if we increase $l$ (the number of alternating products) sufficiently, we get universality.

So, is BOFT theoretically capable of approximating *any* orthogonal matrix if we allow enough depth/alternations? If yes, why do they claim BOFT finds a 'smaller hypothesis class'? Are they implying that even though it *can* represent everything eventually, using fewer layers forces it into a specific subspace that acts as an inductive bias? I'm struggling to reconcile 'universal approximation capability' with 'restricted hypothesis class' in this context.
Answer:
Your confusion stems from conflating the *theoretical capacity* of the parameterization family with the *practical hypothesis class* utilized during standard finetuning. There is no contradiction between BOFT having universal approximation capabilities in its limit and operating within a restricted, structured subspace during typical application; rather, this restriction is precisely what grants it its advantages.

Theorem 1 establishes that BOFT is strictly more expressive than standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) for a fixed block size because the butterfly structure allows information to propagate across the entire dimension $d$ through a sequence of sparse operations, whereas block-diagonal OFT isolates dimensions into independent groups. However, the "universality" you reference—where the product of alternating butterfly matrices and their transposes ($\bm{B}_{d-1,1}\bm{B}_{d-1,2}^T \dots$) can represent the *entire* orthogonal group—is achieved only when the depth parameter $l$ (the number of alternating pairs) scales linearly with the dimension $d$, specifically $l = d-1$. This construction corresponds to the "Kaleidoscope Hierarchy," which requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, effectively collapsing back to the complexity of a full dense orthogonal matrix.

In contrast, practical BOFT operates with a logarithmic depth $m = \Theta(\log d)$ and small block sizes (often $b=2$). At this configuration, BOFT generates a highly structured subset of the orthogonal group that can be represented with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. While this subset cannot theoretically represent *every* possible orthogonal matrix immediately (it lacks the degrees of freedom of a full $d \times d$ matrix), it possesses sufficient expressivity to approximate complex transformations relevant to downstream tasks while maintaining a massive reduction in trainable parameters compared to OFT or full fine-tuning.

The authors describe BOFT as finding a "smaller hypothesis class" not because it is incapable of expansion, but because they intentionally constrain the search space to this specific $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ manifold during training. This constraint acts as a powerful **inductive bias**. By forcing the learned transformation to adhere to the butterfly topology—which mirrors efficient signal propagation patterns found in classic linear transforms like the Fast Fourier Transform—the model avoids overfitting to noise that might occur if it were allowed to explore the vast, unstructured space of all possible orthogonal matrices. Full fine-tuning or deep Kaleidoscope constructions have higher capacity but often suffer from instability or poor generalization on downstream tasks because they lack this structural prior. Therefore, BOFT strikes a deliberate balance: it sacrifices theoretical universality (which requires prohibitive resources) for a compact, structured hypothesis class that empirically yields superior generalization and training stability by implicitly favoring smooth interpolations between identity and complex transforms.

### Clarification on Spectral Norm Preservation: Does BOFT preserve singular values exactly?
Question:
Under 'Spectral properties', the paper argues that because OFT and BOFT multiply by an orthogonal matrix $m{R}$ on the left ($	ilde{m{W}} = m{R}m{W}^0$), they preserve the spectral norm (largest singular value) of $m{W}^0$. The logic follows from SVD: if $m{W}^0 = m{U}sigmasigmasigma^T$, then $	ilde{m{W}} = (m{R}sigma)sigmasigmasigma^T$, and since $m{R}sigma$ is still orthogonal (product of two orthogonal matrices), singular values remain unchanged.

However, in practice, we optimize parameters within these butterfly structures using gradient descent. Numerical instability or optimization errors might mean our learned matrix $	ilde{sigma}$ isn't perfectly orthogonal at every step before projection or regularization.

Does BOFT enforce orthogonality constraints explicitly at every step (e.g., via retraction on manifolds or Cayley parameterization as mentioned)? Or does it rely on soft penalties? If it relies on soft penalties/skew-symmetric initialization without explicit projection steps during SGD steps, can we truly claim spectral norms are preserved for stability guarantees?
Answer:
Based on the provided paper, BOFT enforces orthogonality **explicitly** through its parameterization rather than relying on soft penalties or post-hoc projections during standard stochastic gradient descent (SGD) steps. The mechanism described in Section 4 ("Orthogonal Parameterization by Butterfly Factorization") ensures that the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ is theoretically guaranteed to be orthogonal at every step of optimization, regardless of numerical precision issues inherent to floating-point arithmetic.

Specifically, BOFT constructs the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple sparse butterfly components: $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. The paper states that to guarantee orthogonality, each individual butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}$ is parameterized to be orthogonal by construction. For the base case of $2\times 2$ blocks (which constitute the building blocks of the butterfly structure), the authors explicitly adopt the **Cayley parameterization**, where each block is defined as $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}$ being a skew-symmetric matrix ($\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$). Because the product of any number of orthogonal matrices is itself an orthogonal matrix, and because the Cayley transform maps any real skew-symmetric matrix to an orthogonal matrix (specifically one without eigenvalue $-1$), the composite matrix $\bm{R}$ remains strictly within the orthogonal manifold by definition.

Consequently, you do not need to worry about optimization errors causing $\tilde{\bm{R}}$ to drift away from orthogonality in a way that breaks spectral norm preservation. During training, gradient descent optimizes the underlying parameters of the skew-symmetric matrices $\bm{Q}$ within each block. Even if numerical instability occurs in deep learning libraries, it would manifest as minor floating-point deviations rather than a structural violation of orthogonality, unlike methods that rely on penalty terms which only encourage orthogonality asymptotically. Therefore, the claim regarding spectral norm preservation holds robustly: since $\|\tilde{\bm{W}}\|_2 = \|(\prod \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)})\bm{W}^0\|_2$ and each factor in the product is orthogonal (preserving singular values), the spectral norm of the finetuned weights remains identical to that of the pretrained weights $\|\bm{W}^0\|_2$. This strict enforcement via Cayley parameterization is what allows BOFT to inherit the stability guarantees associated with preserving pairwise angles and spectral norms discussed in Section 5 under "Spectral properties."

### Why choose Butterfly over other sparse factorizations like Monarch or Permutation-only?
Question:
The Related Work section mentions other works using butterfly structures (Monarch, Pixelated) for training from scratch or kernel approximation. The paper highlights that Butterfly allows recovering FFT/DCT/Hadamard transforms.

Given that Monarch also uses hierarchical sparse structures and claims similar efficiency ($	heta(d 	extup log d)$), what specifically makes Butterfly superior for *finetuning* foundation models compared to Monarch? Is it purely because Butterfly aligns better with preserving pairwise angles between neurons due to its specific recursive structure? Or is there an empirical result showing Butterfly converges faster than Monarch when initialized near Identity?

Also, since both aim for parameter efficiency via sparsity patterns on grids, could one theoretically swap Butterfly factors for Monarch factors within their proposed framework without breaking orthogonality?
Answer:
Based on the provided paper, the choice of the Butterfly structure over alternatives like Monarch or simple permutations for Orthogonal Finetuning (specifically in BOFT) stems from a deliberate balance between **expressivity**, **structural inductive bias**, and the specific requirements of initializing near the Identity matrix for stable finetuning, rather than just raw computational complexity which is similar across these hierarchical methods.

While it is true that both Butterfly and Monarch structures achieve $O(d \log d)$ parameter efficiency and can theoretically represent dense orthogonal matrices through products of sparse factors, the paper argues that the Butterfly structure offers a unique advantage in constructing a "smoother interpolation" between the identity matrix and the full orthogonal group. The authors posit that finetuning requires finding a solution close to the pretrained weights (initialized at Identity) to preserve semantic knowledge, meaning the optimization landscape must allow for gradual exploration. The recursive nature of the Cooley-Tukey butterfly factorization inherently supports this by enabling dense connectivity with minimal "free edges" in their information transmission framework, allowing every input node to reach every output node efficiently without imposing rigid block constraints. In contrast, while Monarch is also hierarchical, the paper emphasizes that the specific topology of the butterfly graph aligns better with recovering classic linear transforms (like FFT, DCT, and Hadamard) which serve as strong inductive biases. This alignment implies that BOFT implicitly favors transformations that mix frequencies or features globally in a manner consistent with signal processing principles, potentially preventing overfitting by restricting the hypothesis class to a structured subset of orthogonal matrices that generalizes well.

Regarding your question about swapping factors: theoretically, one could replace Butterfly factors with Monarch factors within this framework because both satisfy the core desiderata of dense connectivity and orthogonality constraints described in Section 3. However, doing so would alter the specific inductive bias. The paper explicitly states that "it is impossible for the block-diagonal structure... to recover any [classic linear transforms]," whereas the butterfly structure "can easily recover many." While Monarch might share some properties, the authors selected Butterflies specifically because their recursive decomposition mirrors algorithms proven to efficiently handle global mixing (information transmission), which they hypothesize benefits generalization more than other sparse topologies. Furthermore, regarding convergence speed when initialized near Identity, while the paper does not present a direct ablation comparing BOFT vs. Monarch convergence rates on identical tasks, it demonstrates empirically that BOFT outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods (including standard OFT and LoRA) across vision and language tasks. The authors attribute this success to the trade-off between expressivity and regularity: BOFT expands the parameter space beyond block-diagonal OFT but constrains it more than full fine-tuning via its structured prior, leading to better stability and generalization without necessarily requiring faster per-step convergence but rather reaching a better optimum within fewer epochs or with higher robustness. Thus, Butterflies were chosen not merely for efficiency but because their specific recursive geometry provides an optimal structural prior for preserving pairwise angles while introducing just enough flexibility to adapt to downstream tasks without catastrophic forgetting.
