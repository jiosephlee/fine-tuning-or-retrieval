\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What is the precise relationship between BOFT and Kaleidoscope Hierarchies regarding expressivity?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. Later, it mentions that by concatenating products of butterfly matrices and their transposes (specifically $m{B}_{d-1,1}m{B}_{d-1,2}^T 	hetadots$), one can represent the *entire* orthogonal group (referencing Kaleidoscope hierarchies).

This seems contradictory or at least confusing. If BOFT uses a limited depth $m = 	heta(	ext{log} d)$ and block size $b=2$, it forms a structured subset of orthogonal matrices. But if we increase $l$ (the number of alternating products) sufficiently, we get universality.

So, is BOFT theoretically capable of approximating *any* orthogonal matrix if we allow enough depth/alternations? If yes, why do they claim BOFT finds a 'smaller hypothesis class'? Are they implying that even though it *can* represent everything eventually, using fewer layers forces it into a specific subspace that acts as an inductive bias? I'm struggling to reconcile 'universal approximation capability' with 'restricted hypothesis class' in this context.
Answer:
Your confusion stems from conflating the *theoretical capacity* of the parameterization family with the *practical hypothesis class* utilized during standard finetuning. There is no contradiction between BOFT having universal approximation capabilities in its limit and operating within a restricted, structured subspace during typical application; rather, this restriction is precisely what grants it its advantages.

Theorem 1 establishes that BOFT is strictly more expressive than standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) for a fixed block size because the butterfly structure allows information to propagate across the entire dimension $d$ through a sequence of sparse operations, whereas block-diagonal OFT isolates dimensions into independent groups. However, the "universality" you reference—where the product of alternating butterfly matrices and their transposes ($\bm{B}_{d-1,1}\bm{B}_{d-1,2}^T \dots$) can represent the *entire* orthogonal group—is achieved only when the depth parameter $l$ (the number of alternating pairs) scales linearly with the dimension $d$, specifically $l = d-1$. This construction corresponds to the "Kaleidoscope Hierarchy," which requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, effectively collapsing back to the complexity of a full dense orthogonal matrix.

In contrast, practical BOFT operates with a logarithmic depth $m = \Theta(\log d)$ and small block sizes (often $b=2$). At this configuration, BOFT generates a highly structured subset of the orthogonal group that can be represented with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. While this subset cannot theoretically represent *every* possible orthogonal matrix immediately (it lacks the degrees of freedom of a full $d \times d$ matrix), it possesses sufficient expressivity to approximate complex transformations relevant to downstream tasks while maintaining a massive reduction in trainable parameters compared to OFT or full fine-tuning.

The authors describe BOFT as finding a "smaller hypothesis class" not because it is incapable of expansion, but because they intentionally constrain the search space to this specific $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ manifold during training. This constraint acts as a powerful **inductive bias**. By forcing the learned transformation to adhere to the butterfly topology—which mirrors efficient signal propagation patterns found in classic linear transforms like the Fast Fourier Transform—the model avoids overfitting to noise that might occur if it were allowed to explore the vast, unstructured space of all possible orthogonal matrices. Full fine-tuning or deep Kaleidoscope constructions have higher capacity but often suffer from instability or poor generalization on downstream tasks because they lack this structural prior. Therefore, BOFT strikes a deliberate balance: it sacrifices theoretical universality (which requires prohibitive resources) for a compact, structured hypothesis class that empirically yields superior generalization and training stability by implicitly favoring smooth interpolations between identity and complex transforms.
