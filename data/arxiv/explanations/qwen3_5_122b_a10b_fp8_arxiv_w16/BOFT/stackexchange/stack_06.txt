\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why choose Butterfly over other sparse factorizations like Monarch or Permutation-only?
Question:
The Related Work section mentions other works using butterfly structures (Monarch, Pixelated) for training from scratch or kernel approximation. The paper highlights that Butterfly allows recovering FFT/DCT/Hadamard transforms.

Given that Monarch also uses hierarchical sparse structures and claims similar efficiency ($	heta(d 	extup log d)$), what specifically makes Butterfly superior for *finetuning* foundation models compared to Monarch? Is it purely because Butterfly aligns better with preserving pairwise angles between neurons due to its specific recursive structure? Or is there an empirical result showing Butterfly converges faster than Monarch when initialized near Identity?

Also, since both aim for parameter efficiency via sparsity patterns on grids, could one theoretically swap Butterfly factors for Monarch factors within their proposed framework without breaking orthogonality?
Answer:
Based on the provided paper, the choice of the Butterfly structure over alternatives like Monarch or simple permutations for Orthogonal Finetuning (specifically in BOFT) stems from a deliberate balance between **expressivity**, **structural inductive bias**, and the specific requirements of initializing near the Identity matrix for stable finetuning, rather than just raw computational complexity which is similar across these hierarchical methods.

While it is true that both Butterfly and Monarch structures achieve $O(d \log d)$ parameter efficiency and can theoretically represent dense orthogonal matrices through products of sparse factors, the paper argues that the Butterfly structure offers a unique advantage in constructing a "smoother interpolation" between the identity matrix and the full orthogonal group. The authors posit that finetuning requires finding a solution close to the pretrained weights (initialized at Identity) to preserve semantic knowledge, meaning the optimization landscape must allow for gradual exploration. The recursive nature of the Cooley-Tukey butterfly factorization inherently supports this by enabling dense connectivity with minimal "free edges" in their information transmission framework, allowing every input node to reach every output node efficiently without imposing rigid block constraints. In contrast, while Monarch is also hierarchical, the paper emphasizes that the specific topology of the butterfly graph aligns better with recovering classic linear transforms (like FFT, DCT, and Hadamard) which serve as strong inductive biases. This alignment implies that BOFT implicitly favors transformations that mix frequencies or features globally in a manner consistent with signal processing principles, potentially preventing overfitting by restricting the hypothesis class to a structured subset of orthogonal matrices that generalizes well.

Regarding your question about swapping factors: theoretically, one could replace Butterfly factors with Monarch factors within this framework because both satisfy the core desiderata of dense connectivity and orthogonality constraints described in Section 3. However, doing so would alter the specific inductive bias. The paper explicitly states that "it is impossible for the block-diagonal structure... to recover any [classic linear transforms]," whereas the butterfly structure "can easily recover many." While Monarch might share some properties, the authors selected Butterflies specifically because their recursive decomposition mirrors algorithms proven to efficiently handle global mixing (information transmission), which they hypothesize benefits generalization more than other sparse topologies. Furthermore, regarding convergence speed when initialized near Identity, while the paper does not present a direct ablation comparing BOFT vs. Monarch convergence rates on identical tasks, it demonstrates empirically that BOFT outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods (including standard OFT and LoRA) across vision and language tasks. The authors attribute this success to the trade-off between expressivity and regularity: BOFT expands the parameter space beyond block-diagonal OFT but constrains it more than full fine-tuning via its structured prior, leading to better stability and generalization without necessarily requiring faster per-step convergence but rather reaching a better optimum within fewer epochs or with higher robustness. Thus, Butterflies were chosen not merely for efficiency but because their specific recursive geometry provides an optimal structural prior for preserving pairwise angles while introducing just enough flexibility to adapt to downstream tasks without catastrophic forgetting.
