\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Does Multiplicative Dropout simply mean setting random blocks to Identity?
Question:
In Section 4, under 'Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT', the authors describe replacing selected butterfly components and diagonal blocks with identity matrices.

Since BOFT defines the update as $m{z} = (m{R}(m,b)m{W}^0)^Tm{x}$ where $m{R}$ is a product of matrices, replacing a factor $	ilde{m{B}}_i$ with $m{I}$ effectively removes that transformation from the chain.

Is this equivalent to dropping out specific dimensions of the feature space temporarily? In standard LoRA dropout, we scale down weights. Here, we seem to completely nullify specific structural transformations. How does this prevent overfitting differently than standard additive dropout? Also, if we replace a block with Identity during training but keep it active during inference (as implied by standard dropout practices), doesn't this break the strict orthogonality property ($m{R}^Tm{R}=m{I}$) of the final accumulated weight update?
Answer:
Your intuition regarding the mechanism of Multiplicative Dropout in BOFT is partially correct, but it differs fundamentally from standard dropout in both its operational effect and its impact on the model's geometric constraints. You are right that replacing a butterfly component or a diagonal block with an identity matrix `$\bm{I}$` effectively "removes" that specific transformation from the product chain `$\bm{R} = \prod \tilde{\bm{B}}_i$`, thereby altering the information flow through the factorization graph described in Section 3. However, this is **not** equivalent to dropping out specific dimensions of the feature space (which would involve masking rows or columns of the activation vector). Instead, it performs a stochastic pruning of the *structural pathways* used to mix features across layers. In standard additive dropout (like in LoRA), noise is injected into the weight values themselves, scaling them down to prevent co-adaptation of individual neurons. In contrast, Multiplicative Dropout in BOFT acts as a regularization on the *composition* of the orthogonal matrix; by randomly skipping certain mixing stages (factors) or local mixing operations (blocks), it forces the remaining factors to learn robust representations that do not rely on any single specific path in the butterfly graph to transmit information globally. This aligns with the paper's "information transmission" perspective: if one path is blocked (set to identity), other paths must compensate, encouraging a more distributed and resilient connectivity pattern.

Regarding your concern about breaking orthogonality (`$\bm{R}^T\bm{R}=\bm{I}$`): this practice does **not** violate the orthogonality property during training or inference because of how dropout is handled in deep learning frameworks and how BOFT is constructed. First, recall that during training, dropout scales active units by `$1/(1-p)$` (or keeps them unscaled while scaling expectations) to maintain unbiased estimates; however, even without explicit rescaling in every implementation detail mentioned, the critical point here is structural. When a block `$\tilde{\bm{B}}_i$` is replaced by `$\bm{I}$` during a training step, that specific matrix remains orthogonal because `$\bm{I}^T\bm{I} = \bm{I}$`. Since `$\bm{R}$` is defined as a product of orthogonal matrices, and the identity matrix is trivially orthogonal, any random subset of factors replaced by identities still results in a valid orthogonal matrix for that specific forward/backward pass. The resulting `$\bm{R}_{train}$` for that batch is simply a "thinner" version of the full transformation but retains orthogonality.

Crucially, unlike standard dropout where weights are scaled at inference time to match expected activations, BOFT follows standard dropout protocols where **no modification occurs at inference**. At test time, all butterfly components are active (none are replaced by identity), meaning `$\bm{R}_{test} = \prod_{all\ i} \tilde{\bm{B}}_i$`. Consequently, `$\bm{R}_{test}$` satisfies strict orthogonality by construction because it is composed entirely of trained orthogonal factors. The "dropped" configurations seen during training are merely sampling different sub-graphs of the full connectivity required for information transmission; they do not permanently alter or invalidate the learned parameters stored in `$\tilde{\bm{B}}_i$`. Therefore, there is no conflict with preserving pairwise angles between neurons at inference time; rather, this stochastic masking serves as a powerful regularizer that prevents overfitting by ensuring no single structural component becomes indispensable for maintaining orthogonality or task performance.
