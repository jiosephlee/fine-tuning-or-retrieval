## The Rise of LLMs in Reasoning and Their Potential for Non-Textual Data

In recent years, Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as transformative tools capable of tackling challenging and unforeseen reasoning problems without the necessity of task-specific fine-tuning. Born from exposure to vast textual corpora, these models possess rich prior knowledge that allows them to serve as substitutes for expert domain expertise across a myriad of fields, ranging from genomic analysis to medical diagnosis and legal interpretation. Perhaps most critically for the advancement of artificial intelligence is the discovery that LLMs excel at in-context learning. By simply incorporating a task description alongside a handful of illustrative examples into their input prompts, LLMs can comprehend the nuances of a specific assignment and selectively attend to salient features and patterns within the provided context. This capacity highlights a profound shift in how machines interact with data; rather than requiring explicit programming or massive retraining cycles, these models demonstrate an inherent ability to analyze structural relationships and extrapolate logic from sparse information.

While initially celebrated for their prowess in natural language processing, the utility of LLMs extends far beyond text generation. Researchers have begun investigating how these models' latent world knowledge can be leveraged for tabular learning, a domain vital to countless real-world applications in finance, healthcare, and logistics. Traditional tabular datasets consist of rows representing entities and columns representing numerical or categorical attributes, often accompanied by descriptive metadata defining what each feature represents. The hypothesis driving recent innovation posits that the generalized knowledge encoded within LLMs—such as knowing that advanced age correlates with higher health risks—can provide invaluable inductive biases for prediction tasks where labeled data is scarce. By translating tabular elements into natural language descriptions and feeding them into an LLM, researchers aim to bridge the gap between raw structured data and deep semantic understanding, theoretically unlocking superior performance even when only a few labeled samples are available.

However, moving from theoretical potential to practical implementation reveals distinct hurdles when applying generic language models directly to tabular prediction tasks. Early attempts involved serialization strategies where entire records were flattened into text strings containing feature names, values, and task goals, which were then fed directly into the model for end-to-end inference. While this approach demonstrated that LLMs could indeed reason over tabular formats, it treated every prediction request as a unique query requiring immediate computation by the massive neural network. As the demand for scalable solutions grew, it became evident that reliance on continuous LLM inference per sample created bottlenecks regarding speed and cost. Furthermore, the sheer volume of information required to describe complex datasets often exceeded the strict token limits imposed by modern model architectures, forcing compromises on data richness or failing entirely when faced with high-dimensional tables containing hundreds of features. These constraints underscored the urgent need for a paradigm shift in how we architect systems that integrate large-scale foundation models with classical statistical learning tasks.

## Challenges in Few-Shot Tabular Learning: Spurious Correlations and Lack of Priors

The realm of few-shot learning seeks to construct robust predictors from extremely limited amounts of labeled data, a scenario increasingly common in specialized domains where acquiring annotations is prohibitively expensive or technically difficult. In image recognition or natural language understanding communities, few-shot methodologies have matured significantly by exploiting shared semantic spaces across modalities. Yet, transferring these successes to tabular data proves exceptionally arduous due to the heterogeneous nature of structured tables. Unlike images which share pixel-wise continuity or text which relies on sequential grammar laws across virtually all documents, tabular features are highly variable; one column might represent income in dollars while another encodes blood pressure units or boolean flags for medication usage. This heterogeneity complicates the creation of universal data augmentations or normalization schemes necessary for training deep neural networks effectively from scratch with minimal supervision.

When training data is restricted to only a few dozen examples—a typical constraint known as low-shot regimes—the primary danger facing any machine learning algorithm is overfitting through spurious correlations. With insufficient signal-to-noise ratios present in small datasets, algorithms often latch onto coincidental patterns that appear predictive within the tiny training slice but fail catastrophically upon encountering new test instances devoid of those accidental alignments. Conventional supervised learning methods struggle here because they rely almost exclusively on empirical evidence derived solely from the observed labels and features present in the training split. Without external guidance or strong assumptions about causal mechanisms connecting inputs to outputs purely drawn from historical statistics alone remains fragile against noise fluctuations inevitable in small sample sizes.
