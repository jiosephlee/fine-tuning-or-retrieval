Chapter 5: # Chapter 5: Downstream Modeling and Ensemble Strategies

Having established how Large Language Models (LLMs) act as expert feature engineers to derive semantically meaningful rules from tabular data, we must now address the critical phase of transforming these linguistic artifacts into quantitative predictors. The core innovation of the FeatLLM framework lies not solely in the generation of these rules, but in the sophisticated downstream pipeline that converts them into robust probabilistic classifications. Once an LLM produces a set of natural language rules—conditions determining whether a sample belongs to a specific class—the system must parse these strings into executable logic, transform raw data into binary indicators, and finally fit a predictive model capable of weighing the evidence provided by each rule. This chapter delves deep into the mechanics of this downstream modeling process. We will explore why a seemingly simple linear architecture is sufficient when paired with high-quality engineered features, examine the mathematical necessity of constraining weights to ensure monotonic contribution, and dissect how ensemble methodologies, specifically combining bagging with stochastic rule generation, serve as the primary defense against hallucinated rules and hardware-imposed context limits. Understanding this pipeline is essential for grasping how FeatLLM achieves state-of-the-art performance while maintaining computational efficiency comparable to classical tabular learning algorithms.

### The Linear Classifier Objective Function with Non-Negative Weight Constraints

Once the LLM has successfully inferred a series of discriminative rules for each class category, the immediate challenge is to quantify their collective influence on the final decision boundary. Unlike end-to-end deep learning approaches where hidden layers implicitly learn feature interactions through millions of parameters, FeatLLM decouples representation learning from classification. The former is handled entirely by the LLM's vast pre-trained knowledge base, leaving the latter to a transparent, interpretable statistical model. Specifically, FeatLLM employs a linear model devoid of bias terms to map the newly constructed binary feature vectors onto class logits. If we denote the generated binary feature vector derived from sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ for a specific class $k$ as $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0, 1\}^R$, where $R$ represents the fixed number of rules extracted per class (typically ten), the transformation relies fundamentally on a dot-product operation between these features and a set of trainable scalar weights $\mathbf{w}_k$.

The design choice to utilize such a parsimonious linear model is driven by several factors rooted in both theory and practice. Firstly, because the upstream LLM has already performed heavy lifting regarding non-linear transformations and contextual understanding—encoding complex dependencies between features and labels into discrete boolean flags—a simple linear combination is theoretically adequate to aggregate this signal. Secondly, and perhaps more critically in low-shot regimes where labeled data is scarce ($k < N$), complex non-linear models prone to capturing noise are highly susceptible to severe overfitting. A linear model minimizes hypothesis space complexity, forcing reliance on the quality of the engineered features themselves rather than fitting idiosyncrasies in tiny training sets. However, simply applying ordinary least squares or unconstrained logistic regression introduces risks inherent to automated feature engineering: what happens if an incorrectly generated rule negatively correlates with its intended target? To counteract this instability, FeatLLM imposes strict structural constraints on its optimization landscape by enforcing non-negativity on all trainable weights $\mathbf{w}_k$. This constraint dictates that every rule generated by the LLM contributes monotonically toward increasing or holding constant the predicted probability for its associated class; no single rule can ever actively decrease the likelihood of membership upon being triggered (i.e., evaluating to true). Mathematically, before computing the dot product with any incoming activation pattern $\mathbf{z}_k^i$, each element of $\mathbf{w}_k$ undergoes projection onto a strictly positive subspace via a rectification mechanism:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_k = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)
$$

This operation ensures that regardless of initial gradient updates or fluctuations during early training stages where signs might momentarily flip due to sparse gradients or numerical instabilities, only positive magnitudes contribute to final calculations. Conceptually aligns well with domain intuition regarding diagnostic heuristics: satisfying more medical symptoms generally raises suspicion rather than lowering it unless explicitly framed as exclusionary criteria—which here are captured by separate negative-consequence rules assigned distinct indices within another class grouping if necessary—but primarily acts as insurance against adversarial behaviors arising from imperfect prompt adherence or misinterpreted semantics during rule synthesis phases orchestrated earlier in our workflow pipeline today!
