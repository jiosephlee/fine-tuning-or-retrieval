Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Related Work and Methodological Evolution

The landscape of tabular machine learning has historically relied heavily on gradient-boosted decision trees and logistic regressions, which excel when abundant labeled data is available. However, the modern era demands algorithms capable of operating effectively in low-data regimes, known as few-shot learning. In this environment, the scarcity of labeled examples creates a precarious situation where models极易 overfit to noise or fail to capture meaningful patterns. Early attempts to solve this involved adapting deep learning architectures designed for images or text, but these adaptations revealed inherent structural weaknesses when applied to the rigid, heterogeneous nature of tabular data. This chapter traces the evolution from these early struggles to the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs), culminating in the shift from direct inference paradigms to feature-engineering frameworks like **FeatLLM**. Understanding this trajectory is essential for grasping why simply asking an LLM to predict a row-by-row basis is suboptimal and how redefining the LLM's role as a logic generator solves critical bottlenecks in scalability and reliability.

## Traditional Few-Shot Techniques: Pitfalls of Heterogeneity

The foundational philosophy behind few-shot learning is the extraction of generalizable representations from a minimal number of labeled samples, minimizing reliance on costly annotation processes. Initially pioneered in computer vision, where spatial hierarchies are consistent across images, researchers sought to transplant these successes onto tabular data. Methods initially focused on semi-supervised learning and contrastive learning objectives. In these approaches, models attempt to learn robust embeddings by leveraging vast amounts of easily available unlabeled data alongside sparse labeled samples. Techniques such as masking parts of the data and reconstructing them—similar to autoencoders—or applying contrastive losses to pull similar samples closer together in latent space were explored extensively.

However, the efficacy of these traditional methods falters significantly when confronted with the intrinsic heterogeneity of tabular datasets. Unlike images, where pixel adjacency carries universal semantic meaning regardless of the object being viewed (e.g., eyes are generally near noses in facial recognition), tabular features lack uniform spatial or relational continuity. A column representing "age" behaves fundamentally differently from a column representing "credit score," and there is no universal augmentation strategy that applies across disparate domains ranging from medical diagnostics to financial forecasting. Consequently, strategies that rely on arbitrary corruption or augmentation often fail to preserve the necessary statistical properties required for valid inference. The literature indicates that these unsupervised objectives mask or corrupt parts of the data without successfully capturing domain-invariant features in low-shot settings, leaving models vulnerable to spurious correlations derived purely from chance alignments in tiny datasets rather than genuine causal mechanisms.

Furthermore, recent advancements involving massive unlabeled datasets did not bridge this gap entirely because they struggle with variable schema alignment across different tables. When attempting to transfer knowledge from auxiliary datasets without explicit supervision regarding feature semantics, models often learn brittle associations that collapse upon encountering slight variations in feature distributions typical of new domains. Thus, while semi-supervised and contrastive techniques offered theoretical promise for reducing dependency on labeled data, their inability to account for the disjointed nature of tabular attributes rendered them insufficient for reliable few-shot performance compared to newer paradigm-shifting approaches that incorporate stronger priors.

## Transfer Learning Approaches: From Semantic Alignment to Synthetic Mixing

As traditional representation learning hit its ceiling on heterogeneous tabsles, attention shifted toward transfer learning methodologies that explicitly encoded domain knowledge before reaching the target task. Two distinct schools of thought emerged: those focusing on column semantics and those synthesizing diverse distributional profiles during pre-training. The former approach is exemplified by models like TransTab, which posits that understanding the *meaning* behind column headers is paramount for transferring knowledge across tables with different schemas but shared conceptual undercurrents rather than identical values. TransTab leverages transformer-based architectures trained on millions of rows spanning various public databases simultaneously. Its core innovation lies in teaching the model semantical relationships between columns solely through their textual names and metadata descriptors, independent of specific cell values. By doing so, it learns a generalized notion that "income level" correlates similarly with "default risk" regardless of whether it appears in a banking database or a retail loyalty program dataset acquired later for testing purposes.

Conversely, another powerful strand involves manipulating probability distributions themselves during pre-training phases rather than aligning static semantic vectors directly against observed realities alone—a methodology embodied prominently by TabPFN (Prior Network Fine-Tuned).
