\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Is there an upper bound on feature dimensionality imposed by the fixed number of rules per class?
Question:
The paper mentions handling datasets with over 100 attributes by using bagging to fit within prompt limits. However, Equation (2) and Section 3.2 imply that for every class $c$, we generate exactly $R$ rules (where $R=10$ according to Section 3.1). If we have a multi-class problem with $C=5$ classes, does this mean our final feature vector size is strictly capped at $5 \times 10 = 50$ dimensions regardless of whether we have 10 or 100 original input features? If so, how does reducing hundreds of original features down to just dozens of derived binary rules affect information retention compared to methods like TabPFN that might preserve more granular interactions?
Answer:
Your interpretation of the dimensional cap is partially correct regarding the *fixed* output of a single rule-generation trial, but it misses the critical role of **bagging** and **ensembling** described in Section 3.2, which allows the framework to scale beyond this limit and retain information from high-dimensional inputs.

While it is true that Equation (2) defines the intermediate feature vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ for a single trial as having a dimensionality of $R$ (where $R=10$ rules per class), this represents only one "weak learner" in an ensemble. The paper explicitly states in Section 3.1 and Section 3.2 that for datasets with over 100 attributes (like "Communities" or "Myocardial"), the authors employ **feature bagging**. In this process, the LLM is prompted with a *subset* of the original features rather than all of them simultaneously. By repeating this process $T$ times (the paper uses $T=20$ ensembles), each trial generates a distinct set of 10 rules based on different subsets of the original $d$ features. Consequently, while any single model instance operates on roughly 50 dimensions ($C \times R = 5 \times 10$), the full ensemble aggregates predictions from these multiple trials, effectively covering a much larger portion of the original feature space across the aggregate system.

Regarding information retention compared to methods like TabPFN, FeatLLM does not attempt to preserve every granular interaction in a dense vector representation; instead, it relies on **prior knowledge filtering**. As noted in Section 3.2 ("Reasoning instruction"), the LLM is guided to infer causal relationships before generating rules, which inherently filters out uninformative or spurious features. The paper argues in Section 4.2 that this mechanism makes FeatLLM robust against noise; when spurious correlations were artificially added to datasets, FeatLLM suffered minimal performance degradation because the LLM rarely generated rules based on irrelevant columns (only $1\text{--}2\%$). Therefore, rather than losing information by compressing hundreds of features into dozens of binary flags, FeatLLM discards low-signal noise early in the pipeline, transforming raw data into high-confidence binary indicators that are weighted by a downstream linear model (Eq. 2). This approach trades exhaustive feature preservation for high signal-to-noise ratio features derived from the LLM's internal knowledge base, which proves particularly effective in few-shot regimes where preserving every interaction might lead to overfitting rather than better generalization.
