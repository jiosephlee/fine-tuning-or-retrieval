Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: The Anatomy of Patching Strategies

The fundamental challenge in transitioning Large Language Models (LLMs) from token-based architectures to raw byte-level processing lies in computational efficiency. Training a Transformer directly on individual bytes requires processing sequences that are exponentially longer than their tokenized equivalents. Since the dominant computational cost in modern Transformers arises from the Feed-Forward Network (FFN) layers—which execute once for every unit of the sequence—processing billions of individual bytes renders scaling prohibitive. To resolve this, the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) introduces a novel mechanism known as *patching*. Rather than treating every byte as a discrete computational step, BLT aggregates contiguous sequences of bytes into units called **patches**. These patches become the primary entities fed into the global latent transformer, thereby drastically reducing the number of steps required for computation while preserving the richness of raw byte data. This chapter dissects the mechanics of this aggregation process, contrasting rigid static strategies with sophisticated dynamic algorithms that adapt to the informational complexity of the input stream.

## Static Segmentation: Fixed-Stride and Whitespace-Based Patching

Before exploring advanced adaptive techniques, it is instructive to examine two foundational methods of aggregating bytes: fixed-stride patching and whitespace-based patching. These represent the spectrum from purely structural grouping to linguistically motivated heuristics.

**Fixed-Stride Patching**, also referred to as strided patching, is the most intuitive approach to segmenting a continuous byte stream. In this methodology, a patching function divides a sequence of bytes $\pmb{x}=\{x_i \mid i=1,\dots,n\}$ into chunks of exactly $k$ consecutive bytes. If we view the sequence through a lens that marks every $(j \cdot k)^{th}$ byte as a potential boundary, we effectively force every $k$-length interval to undergo a dedicated step in the global transformer model. This approach offers significant engineering simplicity; it is trivial to implement during both training and inference pipelines, and it provides precise control over the average patch size, which directly correlates to Flop counts (floating-point operations). However, this rigidity imposes severe inefficiencies regarding information density. Consider a scenario where a sequence contains mostly comments in source code consisting solely of spaces or punctuation; assigning a full transformer step to these low-information regions wastes substantial computational resources conversely, complex mathematical expressions or rare symbols packed densely might be forced into arbitrary cuts that sever semantic coherence. Furthermore, fixed-striding lacks context-awareness: identical words appearing near different positions might be sliced mid-character depending on alignment, leading to inconsistent representations for semantically identical strings.

An evolution over pure striding is **Whitespace (or Space) Patching**, inspired by observations that natural delimiters often align with linguistic units. In this schema, new patches are initiated immediately following any "space-like" byte. Formally, space-like bytes are defined here not merely as ASCII space characters but encompass any byte that falls outside the sets of Latin letters, digits, or UTF-8 continuation markers. Crucially, constraints enforce that every valid patch must contain at least one non-space-like byte to prevent degenerate empty segments. This strategy leverages human linguistic structure by ensuring that whole words generally constitute single patches. The advantage here is twofold: it guarantees consistent parsing of words regardless of surrounding context (unlike fixed strides), and it naturally allocates more compute effort toward predicting tokens that begin after pauses—a phenomenon rooted in language dynamics where starting a new word involves higher uncertainty than continuing one.
