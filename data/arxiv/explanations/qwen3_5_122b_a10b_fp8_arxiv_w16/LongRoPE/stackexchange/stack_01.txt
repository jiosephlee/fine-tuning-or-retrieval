### Does the 'monotonically non-decreasing constraint' mean lambda_i must strictly increase, or just stay equal?
Question:
I am reading Section 3.2 regarding the evolution-based search. The paper states: "we impose a non-decreasing monotonicity constraint on the sampled RoPE rescaled factors: $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$." 

I am confused about the practical implication of this inequality. Does this mean that for every dimension $i$, the rescale factor must be strictly greater than or exactly equal to the previous one? Also, why is this specific direction ($\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$) chosen? Answer:
Your interpretation of the inequality $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ is correct: "non-decreasing" explicitly permits equality, meaning a solution where $\lambda_{10} = 5.0$ and $\lambda_{11} = 5.0$ is entirely valid. The constraint does not enforce strict increase; it merely forbids the sequence from decreasing (i.e., $\lambda_i > \lambda_{i+1}$ is invalid). In the context of the evolutionary search described in Section 3.2, this constraint serves as a heuristic pruning mechanism to reduce the vast search space by enforcing a smooth progression of rescale factors across RoPE dimensions, reflecting the underlying physical intuition that adjacent frequency bands should not behave erratically relative to one another.

Regarding your confusion about the directionality and the definition of $\lambda$, your intuition about the relationship between $\lambda$ and angle scaling is spot on, but you need to align it with how the paper defines $\lambda$ versus the actual multiplier applied to the angle. As stated in Equation 2 and clarified in Section 3.2 ("Search space"), the authors search for parameters denoted as $\lambda_i$, but these are defined such that the actual rescaling factor applied to the rotary angle is $1/\lambda_i$. The text explicitly notes: "we search $\lambda_i$ ... where $\hat{\lambda}_{i}=1/\lambda_i$," and later refers to applying "the rescale factor $1/\lambda_i$" to the angles for tokens $n \ge \hat{n}$.

Therefore, a **larger** value of $\lambda_i$ corresponds to a **smaller** scaling factor ($1/\lambda_i$) applied to the angle, which results in **less** interpolation (or effectively more compression/stretching of the position index $n$). Conversely, a **smaller** $\lambda_i$ (closer to 1) means $1/\lambda_i$ is closer to 1, implying minimal modification or direct extrapolation. The paper's logic follows NTK theory: lower dimensions (indices $i=0, 1, \dots$) correspond to higher frequencies and contain critical local information; these dimensions require "less interpolation" to preserve detail. Consequently, they are assigned **smaller** values of $1/\lambda_i$, which mathematically translates to assigning them **larger** values of $\lambda_i$.
