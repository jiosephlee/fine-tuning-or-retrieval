\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning

# What tabular learning is and why it matters in real-world applications

Tabular learning refers to machine learning on structured data organized in rows and columns, where each row is a sample and each column is a feature. In the paper, the authors focus on classification tasks over tabular datasets, where the goal is to predict one of a predefined set of classes from a small set of labeled examples. This setting is important because tabular data appear in many practical domains, including healthcare, finance, and other decision-making settings where each feature may encode a meaningful attribute such as age, income, medical measurements, or categorical descriptors.

The paper emphasizes that tabular learning is a central problem in real-world applications not only because tabular datasets are common, but also because labels are often costly or difficult to obtain. In such cases, the ability to make accurate predictions from just a few labeled examples can be extremely valuable. The authors frame their work around this low-data scenario and study whether Large Language Models, which have learned broad knowledge from large text corpora, can help solve tabular prediction tasks more effectively.

# The few-shot learning regime: definition, motivation, and challenges

The paper studies few-shot learning for tabular data, meaning the model is trained with only $k$ labeled samples, where $k < N$ and $N$ is the total number of labeled examples in the dataset. In this regime, the learner must extract useful structure from very limited supervision. The motivation is practical: labeling tabular data can be expensive, and in many domains only a handful of examples may be available for a new task.

This setting is challenging because small training sets make it easy to learn accidental patterns rather than truly meaningful ones. The paper explicitly notes the risk of learning spurious correlations, especially when there are few labeled samples. With too little data, conventional models may overfit or fail to identify which features really matter for the target. As a result, few-shot tabular learning requires methods that can inject useful prior knowledge or other forms of inductive bias to compensate for the scarcity of labels.

# Why tabular data are harder to generalize from than images or text

The paper argues that tabular data pose special difficulties compared with modalities like images or text. One reason is the heterogeneous nature of tabular features. Different columns can represent different types of quantities, categories, or measurements, and there is no single universal structure like spatial locality in images or word order in text that can be exploited in the same way across tasks.

Because of this heterogeneity, methods that work well in one tabular problem may not transfer reliably to another. The paper notes that even approaches based on unsupervised objectives, masking, corruption, or contrastive learning have not been notably effective in few-shot tabular settings. It also points out that data augmentation is particularly difficult because there is no universally applicable augmentation strategy for tabular data. These properties make generalization from small datasets especially difficult and motivate alternative sources of knowledge beyond the few labeled rows alone.

# The role of inductive bias and prior knowledge in low-data settings

When data are scarce, a model needs strong inductive bias: assumptions or prior structure that guide learning toward plausible solutions. The paper positions prior knowledge as a key ingredient in such settings. Existing tabular methods often introduce this bias through unlabeled data, pretraining, or transfer learning from many other tabular tasks. For example, some methods use large unlabeled datasets, unsupervised pretraining, or synthetic data to create better initial representations before adapting to the target task.

The central idea of the paper is that Large Language Models can provide another form of prior knowledge. Since LLMs are trained on extensive text corpora, they may encode useful facts and reasoning patterns about the world. The paper argues that such knowledge can substitute for expert domain knowledge in some applications. In tabular tasks like disease prediction, for instance, a model might benefit from the LLM’s understanding that certain patient characteristics are associated with certain outcomes. The paper treats this prior knowledge as something that can help identify which feature conditions are relevant to the target class.

# Capabilities of LLMs: in-context learning, reasoning, and use of world knowledge

The paper highlights three major abilities of LLMs that matter for this setting. First, LLMs can generalize to unseen tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. Second, they can use in-context learning, meaning that by placing a task description and a few examples in the prompt, the model can infer how to respond appropriately. Third, they can use reasoning and world knowledge learned from large-scale pretraining to make informed judgments about relationships among concepts.

These capabilities are particularly relevant for tabular learning because tabular features often correspond to meaningful semantic concepts, not just raw numeric values. The paper argues that by providing feature descriptions and example demonstrations in natural language, the LLM can attend to important features and examples and infer how the task should be solved. This makes the LLM a promising source of guidance in low-shot settings, even if it is not used in the usual end-to-end prediction role.

# Why LLMs may act as expert feature engineers rather than end-to-end predictors

A major contribution of the paper is a shift in perspective: instead of using the LLM to predict each sample directly, \textsf{FeatLLM} uses the LLM as a feature engineer. The authors argue that an LLM may be especially effective at discovering criteria or rules that distinguish classes. For example, in a disease prediction task, the model may infer conditions that indicate the presence of the disease. These inferred conditions can then be turned into features.

This is a natural role for LLMs because feature engineering is fundamentally about identifying which aspects of the input matter and how they relate to the target. The paper’s approach uses the LLM’s prior knowledge to generate such rules, then converts those rules into binary features indicating whether each sample satisfies them. Once these features are created, a simple downstream model can make the final prediction. This design is important because it uses the LLM at the stage where its reasoning may be most valuable, while avoiding the need to rely on it for every test-time prediction.

# Overview of the FeatLLM idea and its main claimed advantages

\textsf{FeatLLM} is the framework proposed in the paper. Its core idea is to prompt an LLM to generate rules associated with each class, parse those rules into executable code, and use the resulting binary features to train a low-complexity predictive model. The paper structures the method into two reasoning stages: first, the LLM is guided to understand the relationship between the task and the features; second, it uses that understanding plus a few examples to derive decisive rules for each class.

The main claimed advantages are practical as well as performance-related. First, \textsf{FeatLLM} does not require an LLM call for each test sample at inference time. Instead, once the rules have been generated and converted into features, prediction can be done with a simple model. Second, the method only requires API-level access to an LLM, rather than fine-tuning access or training code. Third, it is designed to handle prompt-size limitations by using ensemble-style feature bagging, so it remains usable even when tabular inputs have many columns. The paper argues that these properties make the method much more practical than existing LLM-based tabular learners.

# Preview of empirical findings: strong performance, low inference cost, and robustness

The paper reports experiments on 13 tabular datasets spanning multiple domains and task types. Across these datasets, \textsf{FeatLLM} is said to consistently rank first or second among the compared methods. The authors also report that using only 4 shots, their method can reach performance comparable to conventional tabular baselines that require 32 shots.

In addition to accuracy, the paper emphasizes computational benefits. Because the LLM is used to generate rules rather than to predict every sample directly, \textsf{FeatLLM} achieves relatively low inference time, comparable to conventional methods. The method is also reported to be robust in settings with many features, where other LLM-based approaches struggle with long serialized inputs. The ensemble and bagging design helps mitigate prompt-size limitations and improves stability, making the approach more reliable in challenging low-shot tabular scenarios.

Overall, the paper presents \textsf{FeatLLM} as a way to use LLMs not as black-box predictors, but as engines for extracting useful tabular rules. The empirical results are presented as evidence that this shift in role can yield both strong predictive performance and practical efficiency.

2. Background: Existing Approaches to Few-Shot and LLM-Based Tabular Learning

# Classical supervised methods for tabular data: logistic regression, random forests, and gradient boosting

Tabular learning traditionally relies on standard supervised models that directly map input features to labels. In the paper, the representative classical baselines include logistic regression, XGBoost, and random forests. These methods are widely used because they can work well when enough labeled data are available and when the data distribution is reasonably captured by the chosen model class. Logistic regression is a simple linear classifier that estimates class likelihood from a weighted combination of input features. Random forests aggregate many decision trees, while XGBoost builds boosted trees sequentially to improve predictive accuracy. In the setting of tabular data, these models provide strong, established reference points because they require no special language processing or external pretraining.

The paper treats these methods as the conventional starting point for tabular prediction. They operate directly on the structured features and do not require the data to be translated into text. This makes them computationally simpler than LLM-based methods at inference time, and they are often effective when enough labeled examples are available. However, the paper’s central motivation is that these conventional models become less reliable when labels are scarce. That low-shot regime is precisely where the need for additional prior knowledge becomes most important.

# Why conventional tabular models struggle in low-shot settings

The paper emphasizes that learning from only a few labeled examples creates a substantial risk of capturing spurious correlations rather than the true relationships between features and labels. When the number of training examples is small, even a flexible model may fit accidental patterns that do not generalize. This is a general challenge in low-shot learning, and the paper frames it as a major obstacle for tabular tasks.

Another issue is that tabular data are heterogeneous. Different datasets may contain numerical, categorical, and mixed-type variables, and the importance of features can vary widely from task to task. In low-shot conditions, conventional supervised learners do not have enough evidence to reliably determine which signals are truly meaningful. As a result, even well-established methods such as logistic regression, random forests, and gradient boosting can underperform when the labeled set is very small. This is why the paper turns to methods that can incorporate additional information beyond the few labeled samples themselves.

# Semi-supervised and self-supervised tabular learning methods

To address the limitations of low-shot supervised learning, earlier work has explored methods that use unlabeled data or auxiliary learning objectives. The paper describes semi-supervised approaches that exploit large quantities of unlabeled tabular data together with augmentation or other mechanisms to improve learning. The basic idea is to use unlabeled examples to provide structure that the labeled samples alone cannot supply. This can be especially appealing in tabular domains, where labels may be costly but raw records may be easier to collect.

The paper also discusses self-supervised methods that train models on pretext tasks rather than directly on labels. These approaches aim to learn representations that transfer better to downstream classification tasks. The text mentions masking or corrupting part of the data and then reconstructing it, as well as contrastive learning with data augmentation. Such methods are intended to force the model to learn useful patterns in the input structure rather than memorizing labels. However, the paper argues that tabular data are difficult for these strategies because the heterogeneous nature of tables makes it hard to define universally effective augmentations. As a result, the paper notes that these strategies have not been notably effective in few-shot settings.

# Representation learning for tabular data: masking, reconstruction, and contrastive learning

A major line of tabular representation learning attempts to improve downstream performance by learning useful internal representations before supervised training. In the paper’s account, this includes objectives based on masking or corrupting portions of the input and reconstructing them. The intuition is that if a model can recover missing information, it must learn latent structure in the data. Another family of methods uses contrastive learning, where the model is trained to distinguish between related and unrelated examples under data augmentations. In both cases, the goal is to acquire representations that are more transferable and more robust than those learned from scarce labels alone.

The paper places methods such as TabNet-style reconstruction and SCARF-style contrastive learning in this category. These methods can be helpful when large amounts of unlabeled data are available, but the paper stresses that they depend on design choices that may not generalize well across tables. Because tabular datasets differ so much in feature types and semantics, an augmentation that helps one dataset may be unhelpful or even harmful for another. This limits the reliability of these techniques in the low-shot regime the paper focuses on.

# Transfer learning across tabular datasets

Another strategy reviewed in the paper is transfer learning across multiple tabular datasets. Rather than learning only from the target dataset, these methods train on a broader collection of tabular tasks and then adapt to the new task. The motivation is that tabular domains may share useful patterns at a higher level, so a model exposed to diverse source tasks can develop generalizable knowledge that improves performance on a new target table.

The paper cites methods such as TransTab and related approaches in this category. TransTab, for instance, learns semantic relationships between columns using column names, not just values. The paper’s discussion suggests that this broader exposure can support zero-shot or few-shot inference on new tasks. In the same spirit, transfer learning methods seek to reduce dependence on large labeled sets in the target domain. Still, these approaches usually require a pretraining stage over many tabular datasets, which is a different practical setting from directly using a general-purpose LLM through an API.

# TabPFN and synthetic pretraining for few-shot tabular inference

The paper also discusses TabPFN as a notable few-shot tabular method. TabPFN uses synthetic data generation with diverse distributions to pretrain a Bayesian neural network. After pretraining, inference is carried out without further training by providing the model with both training and test samples from the target task. The point of this design is to bake strong inductive bias into the model ahead of time, so that it can perform well when only a few labeled examples are available.

In the paper’s framing, TabPFN is an example of how prior knowledge can improve few-shot tabular performance without relying on the usual supervised learning pipeline from scratch. By training on many synthetic distributions, the method aims to become broadly useful across tasks. The paper notes that this prior knowledge helped TabPFN outperform traditional tree-based methods and become beneficial in few-shot settings. Even so, it is conceptually different from the approach taken by FeatLLM, which does not pretrain a tabular predictor but instead uses an LLM to engineer features from the few-shot examples.

# Language-interfaced tabular learning: serializing rows and prompting LLMs

A newer direction, which the paper calls language-interfaced tabular learning, uses large language models as the basis for prediction. The central idea is to convert structured table rows into natural language text and then provide that text as part of an LLM prompt. The prompt typically includes the feature names, feature descriptions, task description, and a few labeled examples. The table is serialized into a textual form so that the LLM can process it in the same way it handles other language tasks.

The paper points out that this design lets the LLM infer relationships between features and labels from the prompt itself. Because LLMs are pretrained on very large and diverse text corpora, they may bring in external prior knowledge relevant to the task. For tabular learning, that prior knowledge can be especially valuable when labels are scarce. The paper therefore presents language-interfaced tabular learning as a promising way to inject broad knowledge into structured prediction problems.

# In-context learning versus parameter-efficient fine-tuning for tabular tasks

The paper distinguishes two main ways of using LLMs for tabular tasks. The first is in-context learning, in which the prompt contains a task description and a few example rows, but the model itself is not updated. The second is parameter-efficient fine-tuning, where the LLM is adapted using techniques such as LoRA or IA3. In-context learning uses the examples to steer the model’s behavior at inference time, while parameter-efficient tuning changes the model weights in a limited way to better fit the task.

The paper presents both as part of the recent trend in LLM-based tabular learning. In-context learning is attractive because it avoids training, while tuning can improve accuracy by adapting the model to the task. Yet the paper also highlights a key tradeoff: methods that rely on tuning are not usable when the LLM is accessible only through an inference API, and methods that rely only on in-context learning still require the LLM to be queried for each sample. These distinctions become central to understanding why FeatLLM is designed the way it is.

# Representative methods: TabLLM, TABLET, and related approaches

The paper identifies TabLLM, TABLET, and related systems as representative language-based tabular learning methods. TabLLM uses parameter-efficient tuning to adapt an LLM to tabular prediction. TABLET, by contrast, augments the prompt with additional hints from an external classifier, including rule sets and prototypes, to improve inference quality. Other approaches use in-context learning by adding few-shot demonstrations directly to the prompt without further training.

Across these methods, the common pattern is that the table is translated into language and then fed through the LLM to produce the prediction. The paper views this as a black-box style of end-to-end inference. The LLM is responsible for producing the final answer for each sample, rather than just extracting reusable structure from the data. This design has been useful, but the paper argues that it also creates practical limitations that become especially severe in real-world deployment.

# Limitations of prior LLM-based methods: per-sample inference cost, fine-tuning dependence, and prompt-length constraints

The paper is explicit about three main limitations of prior LLM-based tabular methods. First, they require at least one LLM inference per sample at test time. That makes inference computationally expensive, particularly when many samples must be scored. Second, many methods depend on fine-tuning or parameter-efficient training to obtain high performance, which is not always possible when only API-level access to an LLM is available. This matters because some of the strongest recent LLMs do not expose training code or tuning interfaces. Third, these methods are vulnerable to prompt-length limitations. As the number of features in a table grows, the serialized text can become too long for the available context window, or performance may degrade even before the limit is reached.

The paper argues that these constraints are not minor engineering inconveniences but fundamental barriers to practical use. If every prediction must pass through the LLM, inference cost remains high. If tuning is required, the method cannot be used with API-only models. If the prompt becomes too long, then large tables become difficult or impossible to handle. These limitations motivate the paper’s shift away from using the LLM as the end-to-end predictor.

# How FeatLLM differs conceptually from end-to-end LLM prediction

The paper’s central conceptual move is to stop treating the LLM as the final predictor and instead use it as a feature engineer. Rather than asking the LLM to output the class directly for every sample, FeatLLM asks it to infer the rules or criteria that distinguish classes. Those rules are then converted into binary features that can be reused by a simpler downstream model. In this way, the LLM contributes its prior knowledge and reasoning ability once during feature generation, while the downstream learner handles prediction.

This is a significant departure from end-to-end language-based tabular prediction. The paper’s approach reduces inference cost because the LLM does not need to be queried for every new sample. It also avoids the need for fine-tuning, since the framework relies on in-context learning to extract rules. Finally, by using bagging and repeated rule generation, the method can better handle long tables and prompt-size limitations. The background literature therefore sets up the paper’s main claim: that LLMs are not only useful as predictors, but can also act as automatic feature engineers for few-shot tabular learning.

3. Problem Formulation and Design Goals of FeatLLM

# Formal definition of the labeled tabular dataset

The paper studies supervised learning on tabular data, where the input is a collection of labeled examples and the output is a class prediction for each example. Formally, the dataset is written as $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$. Each pair $(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)$ consists of an input vector and a label. The input $\mathbf{x}^i$ is a $d$-dimensional feature vector, meaning that every sample is described by $d$ attributes. The label $\mathbf{y}^i$ belongs to a predefined set of classes, so the paper focuses on classification rather than regression.

This formulation is intentionally general. It covers binary classification problems, such as deciding whether a person earns over a certain income threshold or whether a patient has a particular disease, as well as multi-class problems, such as identifying one of several sequence types. The important point is that the method is designed to operate over standard tabular datasets in which each row is a sample and each column is a feature.

The paper emphasizes that the data are not just raw numbers. The tabular setting also includes human-readable metadata about the features. This metadata is part of what makes the problem suitable for language models, because it gives the LLM semantic information about what each column means. In the paper’s formulation, the model does not see only values; it also sees names and descriptions of those values.

# Feature vectors, class labels, and natural-language feature names/descriptions

In addition to the numerical or categorical values in $\mathbf{x}^i$, the paper assumes that each dataset comes with natural-language feature names, written as $F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^{d}$. These names identify each column and are accompanied by feature definitions provided separately. This is an important design assumption because FeatLLM relies on the LLM’s ability to connect symbolic tabular values to semantic concepts.

The paper also notes that the feature description can include the value type of the feature and, optionally, its definition. For categorical variables, examples of possible categories can be included as well. This means the LLM is given not only the raw column values but also enough metadata to interpret what kind of variable each feature is. That information is used later when the model is instructed to generate rules, because it helps avoid type mismatches in the generated conditions.

The class labels are treated as predefined answers. In the prompt design, the task itself is phrased as a question tied to these labels. For example, the paper gives a disease prediction task framed as a yes-or-no question. This choice reflects the overall method: the LLM is not asked to output an arbitrary free-form explanation, but rather to help determine which conditions support each class.

# The k-shot classification setting

FeatLLM is developed for the few-shot or $k$-shot setting. In this regime, only $k$ labeled samples are randomly selected from the available labeled dataset to train the method, with $k < N$. The central challenge is that the method must learn useful predictive behavior from very limited supervision.

This setting is especially important because the paper’s motivation is rooted in the difficulty of tabular learning when labeled data are scarce. With only a small number of examples, conventional models may struggle to learn stable patterns, and they are also vulnerable to spurious correlations. FeatLLM addresses this by using the LLM’s prior knowledge to supplement the small labeled set.

The paper’s experiments are specifically designed to evaluate performance in low-shot regimes. This means the method is not presented as a general-purpose solution for large-scale supervised training, but rather as a way to make better use of a very small number of examples.

# Assumptions about feature metadata and task descriptions

A key assumption in the paper is that the dataset can be serialized into natural language with meaningful feature names and definitions. The method depends on the availability of task descriptions and feature descriptions that can be inserted into the prompt. Without this metadata, the LLM would have much less context for reasoning about the data.

The task description is formulated as a question that reflects the prediction target. The paper explains that example demonstrations are serialized alongside this task description, so the LLM can connect the textual task instruction with the concrete values in the few labeled examples. This is part of the in-context learning setup.

The method also assumes that the feature type can be communicated to the LLM. This is used during rule generation to ensure that the conditions the LLM writes are consistent with the feature’s domain. For instance, numeric features should be handled differently from categorical ones, and the prompt explicitly encourages the model to attend to those differences.

# Practical constraints in API-based LLM usage

A major motivation for FeatLLM is that many practical LLM-based tabular methods are expensive or difficult to deploy. The paper highlights several constraints. First, end-to-end prediction methods typically require at least one LLM inference per sample at test time. That makes inference slow and costly, especially when a dataset has many samples.

Second, many strong LLMs are only accessible through inference APIs rather than through open training code or fine-tuning interfaces. The paper frames this as a practical obstacle, because some prior tabular methods depend on fine-tuning or parameter-efficient adaptation. FeatLLM is designed to work under API-level access only, which means it must extract useful behavior from prompting rather than from training the LLM itself.

Third, prompt length is a serious limitation. Tabular datasets can have many features, and serializing all of them into text can produce prompts that are too long for the model’s context window. The paper explicitly treats this as a constraint that many existing LLM-based methods do not handle well. FeatLLM therefore has to be designed so that it can still function when the data are large or feature-rich.

# Desiderata for a useful method: no fine-tuning, low inference-time cost, prompt scalability, and robustness

The paper turns those practical constraints into concrete design goals. FeatLLM is meant to avoid fine-tuning entirely. Instead of updating the language model’s parameters, it uses in-context learning to get the LLM to generate useful intermediate outputs. This makes the method compatible with models that only provide API access.

Another goal is low inference-time cost. FeatLLM is structured so that the expensive part of LLM usage happens during feature discovery, not for every individual prediction at test time. Once the rules are extracted and converted into features, the final prediction is performed by a simple downstream model. That means test-time inference no longer requires sending each sample through the LLM.

A third goal is prompt scalability. The paper addresses this by using bagging, which allows the method to work with subsets of features or instances rather than always including everything at once. This helps reduce prompt size when the dataset is too large to fit comfortably into the context window.

Finally, FeatLLM is designed to be robust. Because the LLM may occasionally produce imperfect rules, the method repeats the generation process multiple times and combines the results through ensembling. The paper presents this as a way to compensate for errors in individual trials and to improve stability across low-shot runs.

# From predictions to criteria: reframing tabular classification as rule discovery

The core conceptual shift in the paper is that the LLM is not treated as a direct predictor of class labels. Instead, it is treated as a feature engineer that discovers criteria, or rules, associated with each class. In other words, the method asks the LLM to identify what conditions tend to support a label, rather than asking it to produce the label directly.

This reframing is central to FeatLLM’s design goals. If the model can infer rules such as “a sample belongs to class $k$ when certain conditions hold,” those rules can be used to create new features. Each rule becomes a binary indicator that says whether a sample satisfies the condition. The downstream classifier then works over these derived features instead of the original raw table alone.

This design has two important consequences. First, it reduces dependence on the LLM at inference time, because the LLM is used mainly to discover the feature space. Second, it converts the open-ended reasoning power of the LLM into a form that a simple model can use. That is why the paper describes FeatLLM as a feature-engineering framework rather than a black-box predictor.

# High-level workflow of FeatLLM from prompts to final predictions

The overall workflow begins by constructing a prompt that contains task information, feature descriptions, and a few labeled demonstrations. The prompt is designed to guide the LLM through two reasoning stages. In the first stage, the LLM is asked to infer the relationship between the task and the features using general knowledge and common sense. In the second stage, it uses the examples to produce class-specific rules.

The generated rules are then parsed into executable program code. The paper does this because the rules are expressed in natural language and may contain syntactic variation. After parsing, the rules are applied to each sample to produce new binary features indicating whether each rule is satisfied.

Once these binary features are available, the method fits a simple downstream model with non-negative trainable weights. The paper uses these weights to estimate class likelihoods from the rule-based features. In this way, the final prediction does not require the LLM itself; instead, it uses the discovered features together with a lightweight classifier.

The process is repeated multiple times with bagging and then combined through ensembling. This repeated generation helps both with robustness and with prompt-size limitations. The final outcome is a pipeline in which the LLM contributes knowledge in the form of extracted rules, while the actual prediction stage remains computationally simple and efficient.

4. Prompting LLMs to Extract Predictive Rules

# Why rule extraction is central to the FeatLLM framework

FeatLLM does not use the language model in the usual end-to-end way, where the LLM directly outputs a class prediction for each tabular sample. Instead, the model is asked to act as a feature engineer. The key idea is to have the LLM infer rules that connect feature conditions to class labels, and then turn those rules into new binary features that can be used by a simple downstream classifier. This shift is central to the framework because it moves most of the reasoning work into a one-time feature discovery stage, rather than repeating a full LLM inference for every test sample.

This design has an important consequence: once the rules are generated, prediction can be carried out by a lightweight model rather than by the LLM itself. In the paper’s formulation, the rules are the bridge between the LLM’s prior knowledge and the tabular prediction task. They encode the kinds of conditions that distinguish classes, such as whether a sample satisfies a particular threshold or categorical condition. By extracting such rules, FeatLLM converts a difficult low-shot learning problem into a more standard classification problem over engineered binary features.

The paper emphasizes that this is especially valuable in few-shot tabular learning, where labeled data are scarce and conventional models can overfit or fail to identify useful structure. If the LLM can propose rules that reflect both prior knowledge and the limited examples, the downstream model can then estimate class likelihoods from these rules in a much simpler and more stable way. In that sense, rule extraction is not just a preprocessing step; it is the core mechanism through which the framework uses language models for tabular reasoning.

# The three-part prompt structure used in FeatLLM

To make the LLM generate useful rules, FeatLLM uses a structured prompt with three components: basic information description, reasoning instruction, and response instruction. The paper presents this organization as a way of guiding the LLM through a reasoning process that resembles how a human expert might approach a tabular task. Rather than leaving the model to infer everything from a raw list of examples, the prompt explicitly shapes what information is supplied, how the model should think, and how the output should be formatted.

The first component supplies the task and data context. The second component directs the model’s reasoning in two stages, first using prior knowledge and then using example demonstrations. The third component tells the LLM how to structure its answer so that the generated rules can be parsed automatically. These three parts are complementary: the first grounds the model in the problem, the second improves the quality of the inferred rules, and the third ensures that the results can be converted into code and used by the downstream pipeline.

This structure matters because FeatLLM depends on the output being both semantically meaningful and operationally usable. A rule that is useful in natural language is not enough if it cannot be parsed into a deterministic transformation. The prompt is therefore designed not only to ask for reasoning, but also to constrain the model’s response so the framework can reliably turn the generated text into binary features.

# Basic information description: task statement, label semantics, feature names, and feature definitions

The first part of the prompt provides the core information needed to understand the tabular task. This includes a natural-language description of the task, label information, feature descriptions, and example demonstrations. The task description is phrased as a question, which helps frame the problem as a decision task with explicit answer classes. The paper gives the example of asking whether a patient’s myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure, with the expected answers being yes or no.

The feature description section is equally important. Each feature is presented with its name, its value type, and optionally its definition. For categorical variables, examples of possible categories can also be included. This is meant to help the LLM understand not only what each column is called, but what kind of values it takes and how those values should be interpreted. In tabular data, names alone are often not enough, since a column may have a domain-specific meaning that is not obvious from the raw value. By explicitly including definitions, the prompt gives the model a better chance of recognizing which attributes are relevant for the task.

This part of the prompt provides the factual grounding from which the LLM can infer rules. It is the portion that tells the model what problem it is solving, what the labels mean, and what the available inputs represent. Without this context, the model would have much less chance of producing rules that align with the actual semantics of the dataset.

# Serializing tabular examples into natural language

FeatLLM represents few-shot examples by converting tabular rows into text. The paper follows a serialization format used in previous work, in which each feature is described as a natural-language statement of the form “$f_j$ is $x_j^i$,” followed by the answer label. In the paper’s notation, the serialization of a sample $(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)$ with feature names $F$ is written as:

$$
\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) = ``\ f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ ... \ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i\ "
$$

This serialization turns a table row into a small piece of text that can be inserted directly into a prompt. The benefit is that the LLM can process the sample in the same language format it was trained on, while still receiving the full feature-value information from the tabular row. Because the model is not given the raw table structure, the natural-language formatting acts as a bridge between tabular input and language-model inference.

The paper uses this serialization specifically for the few-shot demonstrations that accompany the prompt. These examples show the model how feature values correspond to labels in the target task. The demonstrations therefore play an important role in helping the LLM move from abstract feature descriptions to concrete decision patterns. They are not only examples of the task; they are part of the mechanism through which rules are inferred.

# Formatting demonstrations with ground-truth answers

The serialized examples in the prompt include the ground-truth label for each example. This is crucial because the LLM is being asked to infer class-specific rules from labeled demonstrations. By seeing a few samples together with their answers, the model can relate particular feature configurations to each class.

In FeatLLM, the demonstrations are not meant to train the LLM in the traditional sense. Instead, they support in-context learning: the model uses the examples as immediate context to infer patterns relevant to the current task. The paper’s design leverages this property to avoid parameter tuning while still allowing the model to use task-specific information. The demonstrations therefore act as both evidence and guidance.

The ground-truth answers are also important for disambiguating the rules the LLM should generate. Since the output goal is not to classify the given examples directly but to extract general rules for each class, the demonstrations help the model identify what kinds of feature conditions tend to correspond to each label. This makes the examples a necessary part of the prompt structure rather than a mere illustration.

# Reasoning instruction inspired by chain-of-thought prompting

The second prompt component gives the model a reasoning instruction. The paper describes this as being similar in spirit to chain-of-thought prompting, in the sense that it encourages the LLM to reason step by step rather than jumping directly to an answer. The aim is to improve the quality of the extracted rules by guiding the model through a structured inference process.

FeatLLM’s reasoning instruction splits the task into two steps. This is not just a stylistic choice; it is meant to control how the model uses its knowledge. The paper argues that tabular rule extraction benefits when the model first reflects on the broader relationship between the task and the features before turning to specific examples. The reasoning instruction therefore serves as an algorithmic scaffold, defining an internal workflow for the LLM.

This prompt-based reasoning design is important because the model’s output is used downstream as feature definitions. If the reasoning process is shallow or uncontrolled, the resulting rules may be noisy or irrelevant. The reasoning instruction is therefore a way of pushing the LLM toward more structured and task-relevant feature engineering.

# Step 1 reasoning: inferring likely relationships between features and the target using prior knowledge

In the first reasoning step, the LLM is encouraged to infer the causal relationship or general tendency between the features and the task description. Importantly, this step is performed without referring to the example demonstrations. The intention is for the model to draw on its prior knowledge and common sense about the domain, rather than overfitting to the few labeled examples.

This separation is central to the paper’s design. By asking the model to reason first without demonstrations, FeatLLM tries to activate the knowledge that the LLM already contains from pretraining. The paper suggests that this can help the model identify which features are likely to matter for the task. For example, in a medical task, the model may recognize that certain age-related or symptom-related attributes are generally associated with the target condition.

The reason this matters is that tabular datasets often contain many features, some of which are irrelevant or weakly related to the label. A first pass based on prior knowledge gives the model a way to filter the feature space before considering the examples. The paper presents this as a way of avoiding spurious correlations and focusing attention on significant variables.

# Step 2 reasoning: using examples plus prior knowledge to deduce decision rules for each class

In the second reasoning step, the LLM combines the information from the demonstrations with the knowledge inferred in Step 1 to derive explicit rules for each class. This is where the model is asked to translate its understanding into class-specific decision criteria. The goal is to produce rules that determine when a sample should be associated with a particular answer class.

The paper’s formulation treats these rules as decisive conditions. Rather than asking the model for a free-form explanation, FeatLLM wants rules that can later be applied to all samples. The examples help the model ground the rules in observed labeled instances, while the prior-knowledge step helps it interpret the examples in a more principled way. Together, the two steps produce a rule set that is both data-informed and semantically guided.

This step is where FeatLLM most clearly departs from ordinary in-context prediction. The model is not simply asked to answer the task for the given examples; it is asked to generalize those examples into reusable criteria. Those criteria become the feature engineering output of the system.

# How the two-step reasoning process helps reduce reliance on spurious correlations

The paper argues that the two-step reasoning process helps prevent the model from learning spurious correlations in irrelevant columns. This is because the first step pushes the LLM to think about the task-feature relationship before it sees the demonstrations, and the second step then uses that prior structure to interpret the examples. As a result, the model is less likely to seize on incidental patterns in the few-shot data.

This is especially important in low-shot tabular learning, where there are too few examples to reliably distinguish signal from noise. If the model were to rely only on the examples, it might generate rules based on features that happen to correlate with the label in the small training set but do not generalize. The paper presents the two-step process as a way of biasing the model toward more meaningful and robust rules.

In the experiments, the authors later show that FeatLLM is relatively resistant to added spurious columns. That result aligns with the reasoning design: by explicitly asking the model to consider the relationship between the task and features, the framework discourages rules based on irrelevant attributes. The chapter’s key point is that prompt structure is functioning as a form of algorithm design, shaping what kinds of patterns the model is likely to discover.

# Choosing a fixed number of rules per class

FeatLLM sets a fixed number of rules to be extracted for each class, namely 10. The paper explains that this choice is made to avoid two undesirable extremes: extracting too few rules, which could lead to underfitting, or extracting too many rules, which could produce an excessively large prompt. Fixing the rule count makes the process more stable and controls the size of the representation that will later become binary features.

This decision also helps standardize the rule-generation process across tasks and classes. Rather than letting the model decide freely how many rules are enough, the framework imposes a predictable budget. That is useful because the downstream model expects a manageable and consistent feature representation, and because prompt length is a practical limitation in LLM usage.

The paper later discusses the trade-off involved in choosing the number of rules. More rules can increase information but may also enlarge the input dimensionality and promote overfitting in the low-shot regime. The fixed value of 10 is therefore a design compromise that balances coverage and compactness.

# Using feature types to constrain sensible rule generation

FeatLLM does not only provide feature names and definitions; it also explicitly exposes feature types in the prompt. The paper says that the LLM is allowed to refer to the type of each feature when formulating rules in order to avoid type mismatches. This is a practical safeguard that keeps the generated rules consistent with the data schema.

This constraint matters because tabular features may be numerical, categorical, or otherwise typed in ways that affect what kinds of comparisons are meaningful. A rule that compares a categorical variable as if it were numerical, or one that applies an incompatible thresholding logic, would be difficult or impossible to use. By reminding the LLM of the feature types, the prompt reduces the chance of producing nonsensical rules.

In FeatLLM, this is part of the broader goal of making the LLM behave like an expert engineer rather than a free-form text generator. The type information narrows the search space for valid rules and improves the chances that the resulting output can be parsed and executed correctly.

# Response instruction: enforcing parseable output format

The third part of the prompt tells the LLM how to format its response. This is necessary because the rules will later be converted into program code and applied to the dataset. If the model responds in a free-form style, the parsing step becomes unreliable. The response instruction therefore serves as a contract between the LLM and the rest of the system.

The paper explains that the prompt includes instructions for the required format of the response for each answer class, as well as the structure of each rule. These format constraints make it easier to automatically extract the rules from the model output. In other words, the response instruction is not about improving reasoning directly; it is about making the generated reasoning machine-readable.

This part of the prompt reflects a key theme in the paper: prompt engineering is treated as part of system design. The model’s output must be useful not only to a human reader but also to a pipeline that will transform it into binary features. Format control is therefore a crucial part of the overall method.

# Permitted rule structures and logical operators

The response instructions also constrain the structure of individual rules. The paper notes that, without additional guidance, the LLM can combine multiple rules using logical operators such as AND or OR. Therefore, the prompt explicitly specifies the format each rule should follow. This helps ensure that the rules remain simple enough to parse and apply.

The emphasis on rule structure is tied to the way FeatLLM turns language into features. A rule must be clear enough to be translated into code that checks whether a sample satisfies it. If the model freely nests conditions or uses inconsistent syntax, the downstream parsing stage becomes more fragile. By prescribing the form of the rules, the prompt reduces ambiguity.

At the same time, the paper is careful to allow the LLM enough flexibility to express meaningful conditions. The prompt is not meant to eliminate rule diversity; it is meant to keep that diversity within a constrained format that can be operationalized. This balance between expressiveness and structure is one of the main engineering challenges in the method.

# Prompt engineering trade-offs: precision, diversity, and prompt length

The paper’s prompt design reflects several trade-offs. One is between precision and diversity. A more restrictive prompt can produce rules that are easier to parse and potentially more precise, but may also limit the variety of rules the LLM can generate. A more open prompt may encourage richer outputs, but those outputs are harder to use automatically and may include irrelevant or noisy conditions.

Another trade-off is prompt length. Because tabular datasets can have many columns, a prompt that includes too many features, too many examples, or too many rules can exceed context limits. The paper explicitly discusses this issue and uses a fixed number of rules plus ensemble methods and bagging to help manage prompt size. In the prompt itself, the structure is designed to keep the representation compact while still informative.

The most important idea is that prompt engineering in FeatLLM is not cosmetic. It is part of the algorithm that makes the framework work. The prompt must provide enough context for the LLM to use its prior knowledge, enough structure to guide reasoning, and enough constraints to make the output usable downstream. The paper’s design aims to balance these goals so that the LLM can extract predictive rules that are both meaningful and operational.

5. From Natural-Language Rules to Machine-Usable Features

# Why generated rules must be converted into executable logic

A central idea in \textsf{FeatLLM} is that the large language model does not directly produce the final class prediction for each tabular sample. Instead, it produces rules that describe conditions associated with each class. These rules are valuable because they encode the model’s inferred understanding of how features relate to the target label. But rules written in natural language are not yet something a conventional machine learning model can use directly. To be useful for prediction, they must be turned into machine-executable logic.

This conversion matters because the downstream predictor in \textsf{FeatLLM} is intentionally simple. The paper uses a low-complexity model, such as a linear model without bias, to estimate class likelihood from rule-based features. That model can only operate on numerical inputs. So the rules need to become features such as $0$ or $1$, indicating whether a given sample satisfies a rule. In this way, the system shifts from language-based reasoning to standard supervised learning over a transformed feature space.

The broader purpose of this transformation is to make the LLM’s knowledge reusable at inference time without repeatedly querying the LLM for every sample. Once the rules are extracted and transformed into binary features, the final prediction pipeline is handled by conventional machine learning. This is a major design choice in the paper: the LLM acts as a feature engineer, not as an end-to-end predictor.

# Binary rule features as indicators of rule satisfaction

The paper converts each inferred rule into a binary feature. The meaning of that feature is simple: for a given sample, does the sample satisfy the rule or not? If yes, the feature value is $1$; if no, it is $0$. These features are created separately for each class, so the model learns a class-wise rule representation rather than a single undifferentiated set of conditions.

This binary representation is important because it aligns naturally with the way the rules are generated. The LLM is prompted to produce decisive rules for distinguishing classes, and those rules are intended to be checked against the rows of the tabular dataset. A binary indicator gives a direct answer to that check. It also keeps the transformed input simple enough for a linear model to use effectively.

The paper notes that one could imagine a naive strategy where the class likelihood is measured simply by counting how many rules of a class a sample satisfies. However, \textsf{FeatLLM} does something more flexible: it learns weights for the rule features. That means some rules can matter more than others. Even though each feature is binary, the downstream model can still learn which rule indicators are informative and which are not.

# Examples of rule-based feature construction for classification

The paper frames the target problem as classification, where each class can be associated with a set of rules. For a sample $\mathbf{x}^i$, the LLM-generated rules for class $k$ are transformed into a binary vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$, where $R$ is the number of rules per class. Each entry in this vector indicates whether the sample matches one of the extracted rules.

This construction gives a compact class-specific summary of the original row. Rather than feeding all raw tabular features directly into a complex model, \textsf{FeatLLM} first asks whether the sample fits the LLM’s extracted conditions. For example, if the rules for a class describe a particular age range, a threshold on a laboratory value, or a categorical condition, then the corresponding feature becomes active whenever the sample meets that condition. The paper does not rely on a single rule per class; it extracts multiple rules, fixed at 10 per class in the experiments, so that the downstream model can aggregate several pieces of evidence.

The resulting representation is especially useful in low-shot settings. Because the rules are meant to encode decisive patterns, the binary features can capture class-relevant structure even when only a few labeled examples are available for training the linear predictor. In effect, the model is not trying to relearn the semantics of the table from scratch. It is learning how to combine already-interpreted rule indicators.

# Challenges in parsing LLM-generated text: syntactic variability and noisy formatting

Converting textual rules into executable logic is not trivial. Even when the prompt asks the LLM to produce a specific format, the output can vary in small but important ways. The paper emphasizes that the LLM may introduce noisy syntactic changes while preserving the intended meaning. For instance, a symbol like $>$ might be replaced by the phrase “greater than,” or $<$ might become “less than.” These variations make automated parsing difficult.

This is a key practical issue because the rules are generated in natural language, not in a formal programming language. Natural language is flexible, but that flexibility creates ambiguity for downstream conversion. A parser that expects a narrow syntax can easily fail when the LLM rephrases an operator, changes punctuation, or slightly alters the structure of the response.

The paper also notes that without careful prompting, the LLM can combine multiple rules using logical operators such as AND or OR in ways that are not easy to standardize. That is why the prompt design includes explicit response instructions and rule formatting instructions. These are intended to make the output more parseable, but the authors still treat the parsing problem as nontrivial because some variation remains inevitable.

# Why hand-written parsers are brittle in this setting

A straightforward response to noisy LLM output would be to build a hand-written parser with fixed rules for pattern matching. The paper explains why this is brittle. A manually engineered parser must anticipate all the possible ways the LLM might express the same rule. That becomes difficult when textual outputs vary in operator spelling, sentence structure, or formatting.

The problem is not just that the text can be different. It is that the LLM is producing semantically equivalent expressions that may not have the same surface form. A rigid parser might successfully handle one prompt output and fail on another, even when both mean the same thing. In a setting where the framework repeatedly queries the LLM across multiple trials, such brittleness would undermine the entire pipeline.

The authors therefore avoid building a complex rule parser by hand. Instead, they exploit a different property of LLMs: their ability to understand semantic meaning even when the syntax changes. This leads to the unusual but important choice of using the LLM again, this time as a code generator for parsing.

# Using the LLM as a semantic parser and code generator

Rather than writing a bespoke parser, \textsf{FeatLLM} asks the LLM to generate simple code that can evaluate the rules. The logic behind this choice is that the LLM can often infer the intended meaning of a textual rule even if the wording is not perfectly standardized. Since the model has also been trained on code data, it can produce executable Python code that operationalizes the rule semantics.

This is a distinctive design decision in the paper. The LLM first acts as a rule generator, and then it acts again as a semantic parser and code generator. The second use is not for prediction directly, but for translating natural-language descriptions into machine-readable functions. The benefit is that the system can tolerate minor syntactic variation while still converting the rule into a usable form.

The paper’s approach reflects an important practical insight: if the model already understands the rule text well enough to generate it, it may also be the best tool for bridging the gap between that text and executable logic. This avoids the need to build fragile string-processing rules and helps preserve the intended meaning of the original output.

# Prompting the LLM to produce Python functions for rule evaluation

To generate parseable code, the paper includes in the prompt several elements: a function name, input and output descriptions, and the inferred rules themselves. These details guide the LLM toward producing code that can be executed to determine whether a sample satisfies a rule. The generated code is meant to implement the rule evaluation in a structured way rather than as free-form text.

This prompt structure matters because it narrows the space of possible responses. The function name tells the LLM what role the code should play. The input-output description specifies what the function should receive and what it should return. The inferred rules supply the actual logical conditions that need to be encoded. Together, these elements help the model produce code that can be used directly in the feature-generation pipeline.

The paper presents this as a practical way to transform imperfect natural-language rules into executable form without relying on external training or custom parser development. In the end, the goal is not to create polished software automatically, but to create code that is sufficiently clear to be run on the tabular rows. That code becomes the bridge between the language output and the binary features used by the downstream classifier.

# Function signatures, input-output specifications, and inferred rules as parser inputs

The parser prompt in \textsf{FeatLLM} is not just a loose request for code. It is structured around the specific elements needed for rule evaluation. The function signature tells the LLM how the code should be organized, while the input and output specifications define what the function is expected to do. The inferred rules provide the logical content that the function must encode.

This structure helps standardize the code generation stage. If the model knows the expected inputs and outputs, it can produce a function that fits into the later execution step. In the paper, this is important because the code is not examined manually on each sample. Instead, it must be generated once and then executed automatically over the dataset.

The use of these prompt elements also reflects the paper’s broader emphasis on controllability. Since the natural-language rules can still vary, the system adds structure at the code-generation stage to reduce ambiguity. The function specification acts like a contract between the rule text and the executable parser.

# Executing generated code with Python exec()

After the LLM generates code, \textsf{FeatLLM} executes it using Python’s \texttt{exec()} function. This is the step that turns the parser output into actual data transformation. The generated code is run, and the result is used to determine whether each row of the tabular dataset satisfies the rule conditions.

This execution step is what makes the transformation operational. The rules are no longer just descriptions; they become procedures that can be applied to every row. The binary feature vector is therefore produced algorithmically, not manually. Each rule corresponds to a programmatic test, and the outcome of that test becomes a feature value.

The paper presents this as a practical method for handling noisy rule strings while maintaining an automated pipeline. The authors do not build a large parsing system around all possible textual variations. Instead, they use the LLM’s own code generation ability and rely on Python execution to complete the conversion.

# Benefits and risks of LLM-generated code execution

The benefit of using generated code is flexibility. Because the LLM can infer semantics, the system can survive small changes in wording that would defeat a rigid parser. This is especially useful when rules are repeatedly generated across multiple ensemble trials, since output variability is expected. Code generation also aligns with the paper’s larger objective of minimizing dependence on hand-crafted infrastructure.

At the same time, the method clearly depends on the quality of the generated code. If the LLM misinterprets a rule, produces incorrect logic, or generates code that does not reflect the intended condition, the resulting feature will be wrong. The paper addresses this risk indirectly through prompt design, fixed rule counts, and ensembling. Across repeated trials, incorrect rules are expected to be compensated by correct ones, and common rules across trials are more likely to be reliable.

The use of \texttt{exec()} also means that the code is not merely symbolic; it is actually executed. That makes the translation step powerful, but it also underscores the importance of careful rule formatting and prompt constraints. In \textsf{FeatLLM}, the code execution step is part of a larger system designed to extract stable signal from the LLM while filtering away some of its variability.

# Data transformation pipeline from raw tabular rows to class-wise rule vectors

The full transformation pipeline begins with raw tabular rows and ends with class-wise binary rule vectors. First, the LLM extracts a set of rules for each class based on the task description, feature descriptions, and few-shot demonstrations. Then those rules are converted into executable code. Once executed, each rule is applied to each sample in the dataset.

For a given sample, the output of this process is a binary vector for each class, $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$. This vector summarizes which class-specific rules the sample satisfies. The original tabular row is thus represented not by its raw values alone, but by its relationship to a set of LLM-discovered conditions. These transformed features are what the downstream linear model uses to estimate class probabilities.

This pipeline is the core of the paper’s feature-engineering strategy. It is how \textsf{FeatLLM} moves from language to prediction: the LLM discovers candidate decision rules, the rules are converted into code, the code produces binary indicators, and the indicators feed a conventional classifier. The result is a system that retains the knowledge of the LLM while avoiding the need to query it for each prediction.

# Interpretability of rule-based features

One important consequence of this design is interpretability. Because each feature corresponds to a specific rule, it is possible to inspect what the model is using to make a prediction. Unlike a latent embedding, a rule-based binary feature has a human-readable meaning: the sample either satisfies the condition or it does not.

The paper’s analysis of feature quality supports this interpretation. It shows that many of the generated rules are relevant to the target attribute, and that the downstream weight tuning can filter out rules with little relevance. This means the features are not only machine-usable, but also semantically meaningful enough to be evaluated in relation to the target task.

Interpretability is therefore not an accidental side effect of the pipeline; it is built into the way \textsf{FeatLLM} transforms language into features. The LLM-generated rules are meant to express the criteria underlying the prediction task, and the binary features preserve those criteria in a form that can be both learned from and inspected.

6. Learning Class Likelihoods with a Simple Downstream Model

# Why FeatLLM Uses a Simple Downstream Model Instead of Another Complex Learner

After FeatLLM uses the LLM to generate rules, it does not continue with a large or complicated predictive model. Instead, it deliberately places a simple downstream model on top of the newly created rule features. This design choice is central to the paper’s idea. The whole point of rule generation is to move the burden of reasoning into the feature construction stage. Once the LLM has identified the kinds of conditions that distinguish the classes, the prediction problem becomes much simpler: the model only needs to learn how much each rule should matter when deciding among classes.

This separation is important for two reasons emphasized in the paper. First, it reduces inference cost. If prediction were still handled by the LLM directly, then every test example would have to be sent through the LLM, which would make the method expensive and slow. By contrast, after the rules are generated, inference can be done using only the downstream model. Second, it avoids the need for training the LLM itself. FeatLLM is designed to work with API-level access, so the simple classifier is the only trainable part. In this sense, the downstream model acts as a lightweight interpreter of the rules discovered by the LLM.

The paper also suggests that a simpler model is a better match for the type of features being produced. The generated features are binary indicators of whether a sample satisfies each rule. These are not dense learned embeddings; they are explicit, human-interpretable conditions. A low-complexity model is therefore sufficient to combine them. The design reflects the paper’s broader philosophy: let the LLM handle feature engineering, and let a conventional model handle the final classification.

# Class-Wise Binary Feature Vectors Generated from Rules

Once the LLM produces rules, FeatLLM turns them into class-wise binary features. For each class, the method generates a fixed number of rules, denoted by $R$ in the paper. For a sample $\mathbf{x}^i$, the method constructs a binary vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$ for class $k$. Each entry in this vector indicates whether the sample satisfies one of the rules associated with that class.

This representation is important because it converts natural-language rules into a machine-readable form that can be used by the downstream model. If a sample satisfies a rule, the corresponding entry becomes $1$; otherwise, it becomes $0$. In effect, each rule becomes a feature, and the entire set of rules for a class becomes a compact summary of how well the sample matches that class’s inferred criteria.

These features are class-specific rather than shared across all classes. That means the method does not simply ask whether a sample matches a rule in the abstract. It asks whether the sample matches rules that the LLM has generated as relevant for a particular class. This organization aligns with the paper’s view that the LLM is extracting class-conditional criteria. The binary vectors therefore serve as a bridge between symbolic reasoning and statistical prediction.

# Defining Logits as Weighted Counts of Satisfied Rules

The most basic way to use these binary rule features would be to count how many rules a sample satisfies for each class. If many rules for class $k$ are satisfied, that class should seem more plausible. FeatLLM starts from this idea but improves upon it by learning weights for the rules instead of treating them all as equally important.

For each class $k$, the model computes a logit value $\text{logit}_k^i$ from the binary feature vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i$. The paper defines this quantity as a weighted combination of the satisfied rules. In the simplest interpretation, this is a learned weighted count: rules that are more useful contribute more, and rules that are less useful contribute less. The logit therefore becomes a score summarizing how strongly the sample matches the class-specific rule set.

This is a natural extension of naive counting. Counting assumes that every rule is equally informative, but the paper explicitly argues that this is not true. Some rules extracted by the LLM may be highly relevant, while others may be weak or redundant. By learning weights, FeatLLM allows the data to determine which rules should matter most.

# The Linear Model Without Bias

To implement this idea, the paper uses a linear model without bias. For each class $k$, there is a trainable weight vector $\mathbf{w}_k$, and the logit is computed as

$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i.
$$

This is a dot product between the non-negative version of the weight vector and the binary rule features. Because the model has no bias term, the prediction is driven entirely by the rule features themselves. This is consistent with the overall design of the framework: the LLM provides the rules, and the downstream model only measures how strongly the sample matches them.

A linear model is especially suitable here because the rule features already encode the complex reasoning that would otherwise require a larger model. The classifier does not need to discover hidden structure from raw tabular values. It only needs to combine explicit rule indicators. In that setting, adding more complexity would not be central to the method’s goal. The paper presents the linear model as a practical and interpretable way to turn rule satisfaction into class scores.

# Non-Negative Projection of Weights and Its Semantic Motivation

A notable detail in the paper is the projection of the weights into the non-negative subspace by taking $\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$. This means that negative weights are not allowed to reduce the logit contribution of a satisfied rule. The paper gives a semantic reason for this design: the rules generated by the LLM are intended to express conditions that support a class, so satisfying a rule should not actively hurt that class during learning.

This choice preserves the meaning of the rules as positive indicators. If a rule says that a certain condition is associated with a class, then meeting that condition should contribute toward the class likelihood, not subtract from it. The non-negative constraint ensures that the downstream model behaves in a way that matches this interpretation.

The paper also frames this as a way to prevent the generated features from reducing class likelihood during learning. Since the features are binary and derived from symbolic rules, it is more appropriate to treat them as evidence in favor of a class rather than as arbitrary numerical inputs. The positivity projection is therefore not just a technical choice; it is a semantic alignment between the meaning of the rules and the mechanics of the classifier.

# Softmax Conversion from Logits to Class Probabilities

Once the model computes a logit for each class, FeatLLM converts these scores into probabilities using the softmax function. If there are $c$ classes, then the vector of logits for sample $i$ is

$$
[\text{logit}_1^i, \ldots, \text{logit}_c^i],
$$

and the softmax transformation produces the class probability vector $\mathbf{p}^i$.

This step is standard in multi-class classification, but here it has a clear interpretation. Each class receives a score based on how many of its rules are satisfied and how important those rules are. The softmax then turns these scores into a normalized distribution over classes, so the model can express relative confidence. A class with a much larger logit than the others receives a higher predicted probability.

In the paper’s formulation, softmax is the final step that makes the downstream model usable for classification. The generated rules determine the evidence, the linear model aggregates the evidence, and the softmax turns the result into a probability distribution. This keeps the entire prediction pipeline simple and transparent.

# Cross-Entropy Training Objective

The trainable part of the downstream model is the weight vector $\mathbf{w}_k$ for each class. FeatLLM learns these weights from the few-shot training samples by optimizing the cross-entropy loss. This means the model is trained to assign high probability to the correct class and lower probability to the incorrect ones.

Cross-entropy is a natural objective here because the task is classification and the output is a probability distribution. During training, the model compares the predicted probabilities $\mathbf{p}^i$ with the ground-truth label $\mathbf{y}^i$ and updates the weights accordingly. The paper uses this process to learn how each rule should influence the class score.

The key point is that only a small number of labeled examples are used. This matches the few-shot setting of the paper. The classifier is not expected to learn complicated representations from scratch; it only learns how to weigh the rules that the LLM has already generated. The cross-entropy objective provides the numerical mechanism for making that adjustment.

# Interpreting Learned Weights as Rule Importance

One of the main advantages of using a linear model on rule features is interpretability. Because each rule has an associated learned weight, the model reveals which rules are more important for the final prediction. A larger weight means that satisfying that rule contributes more strongly to the class score. A smaller weight means the rule is less influential.

The paper uses this property not only for prediction but also for analysis. In its quality study, it observes that rules with little relevance to the data tend to receive low weights and can be filtered out after tuning. This shows that the learned weights are not arbitrary numbers; they encode the practical usefulness of the rules for the target task.

This interpretability is especially valuable because the features themselves are human-readable rules generated by the LLM. As a result, the entire model remains easier to inspect than a black-box predictor. One can look at the rules and their learned weights to understand why a class is favored.

# Comparison with Naive Counting of Satisfied Rules

The paper explicitly contrasts the learned weighting strategy with a naive alternative: simply summing the binary features for each class. Under naive counting, every satisfied rule contributes equally, so the logit would just be the number of matched rules. This is straightforward, but it ignores the fact that not all rules are equally useful.

FeatLLM improves on this by learning rule importance from the training data. Some generated rules may be very informative, while others may be redundant, weak, or only loosely related to the class. If all rules are counted equally, noisy rules can distort the prediction. By tuning weights, the downstream model can amplify the useful rules and reduce the effect of weaker ones.

The paper’s ablation study supports this design choice. Removing the tuning process hurts performance, showing that learned weighting is not just a cosmetic improvement. It matters because it lets the model refine the raw rule set into a better predictor. In this way, the classifier acts as a filter that converts a broad set of LLM-generated rules into a more reliable decision mechanism.

# When Rule Weighting Matters Most in Low-Shot and Higher-Shot Settings

The paper reports that the benefit of weight tuning becomes more pronounced as the number of shots increases. This is an important insight. In very low-shot settings, there may not be enough labeled data to estimate rule importance precisely, so the effect of tuning is limited by data scarcity. But as more labeled examples become available, the model can better learn which rules matter, and tuning becomes increasingly beneficial.

At the same time, the paper notes that the prior knowledge supplied by the LLM is especially valuable when the number of shots is small. In those cases, feature descriptions and reasoning instructions play a large role because they help the LLM generate better rules in the first place. So there is a division of labor: when data are extremely limited, the quality of rule generation is crucial; when more labels are available, the downstream weighting mechanism becomes more effective at refining those rules.

This observation helps explain the architecture of FeatLLM. The rule generator and the downstream classifier complement each other. The LLM provides a strong starting point, and the linear model adjusts the contribution of each rule as more evidence becomes available.

# Advantages of Separating Feature Generation from Prediction

The paper’s most important design principle is the separation between feature generation and prediction. Instead of asking the LLM to directly output a class label for each sample, FeatLLM asks it to generate rules once, then reuses those rules in a downstream model. This separation has several advantages described in the paper.

First, it reduces inference latency. Once the rules are generated and the downstream classifier is trained, predictions no longer require LLM calls. Second, it makes the method feasible with API-only access, since there is no need to fine-tune the LLM. Third, it helps with prompt size limitations because the model can use bagging and repeated rule generation to handle large feature sets without forcing all information into one giant prompt. Fourth, it improves interpretability because the final decision is based on explicit rules and their learned weights.

In short, the downstream model is not just a convenience. It is the mechanism that turns the LLM’s symbolic reasoning into an efficient, trainable, and interpretable classifier. FeatLLM’s contribution lies in showing that a simple predictive layer, when built on top of carefully generated rules, can achieve strong few-shot performance on tabular data.

7. Ensembling and Bagging for Robustness and Prompt Scalability

#
FeatLLM does not rely on a single pass of rule generation to solve a tabular task. Instead, it repeats the entire rule-extraction-and-prediction process multiple times and combines the results into an ensemble. This design is important because one set of rules generated by an LLM may be unstable, incomplete, or influenced by prompt ordering and other stochastic effects. A single rule set might capture only part of the relationship between features and labels, especially in the low-shot setting where the LLM has limited examples to work with. FeatLLM therefore treats each trial as a weak learner: each trial may produce useful but partial rules, and the final prediction is obtained by aggregating many such trials.

This ensemble strategy is not just a way to improve accuracy. It is also central to making the method practical for wide tables, where the serialized input can easily become too long for the model’s prompt window. By generating rules from different subsets of the data and features, FeatLLM avoids requiring the full table to be included in every prompt. In this way, the ensemble mechanism supports both robustness and scalability.

#
The key intuition behind repeated trials is that the LLM can discover different useful rules each time it is queried. Because rule generation is stochastic, each run may emphasize different parts of the problem. One trial might produce rules focused on one informative subset of features, while another trial might capture another useful aspect of the task. When these trials are combined, the resulting system is less dependent on any single prompt outcome.

The paper connects this behavior to the idea of weak learners in classical ensemble learning. Individual rule sets are not expected to be perfect. Instead, the goal is for the ensemble to cover more of the relevant structure than any single trial could. If one trial produces an incorrect rule or misses an important condition, other trials can compensate. This is especially important in few-shot tabular learning, where the small number of labeled examples makes overfitting and unstable reasoning more likely.

#
After rules are generated in each trial, FeatLLM converts them into binary features and trains a simple downstream model. For the final prediction, it does not choose only one trial’s output. Instead, it averages the class probabilities produced by all ensemble members. If $\mathbf{p}^i$ is the probability vector for sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ under one trial, then the final prediction is based on the average over the probabilities obtained from the set of trained weights across trials.

This averaging step matters because it smooths over trial-to-trial variation. Even if different runs produce slightly different rule sets, the final probability estimate reflects the consensus of the ensemble rather than the idiosyncrasies of one prompt. In the paper’s analysis, this ensemble approach consistently improves performance across settings. It also aligns with the interpretation that rules commonly inferred across multiple trials are more likely to be accurate.

#
FeatLLM introduces diversity across trials by setting a sufficiently high temperature during LLM inference. Higher temperature makes the model more likely to produce varied outputs rather than repeating the same response every time. This diversity is useful because if all trials were too similar, the ensemble would offer little benefit. The goal is not randomness for its own sake, but enough variation to produce multiple plausible rule sets that can be combined later.

The paper also randomizes the order of few-shot demonstrations in the prompt. This is important because the order of examples can affect the output of an LLM. By changing the demonstration order, FeatLLM encourages different reasoning paths and rule candidates across trials. This randomization helps create a richer ensemble and supports the observation that LLM outputs can depend on prompt arrangement.

#
A major part of FeatLLM’s scalability comes from feature bagging. In this setting, each trial uses only a subset of the available features. This reduces the amount of text that must be serialized into the prompt and makes it possible to work with datasets that have many columns. The paper emphasizes that this is especially valuable for tables with a large number of attributes, where prompt length would otherwise become a limiting factor.

Feature bagging also has a regularizing effect. If all features were always shown at once, the LLM might be overwhelmed by irrelevant information or may focus on spurious correlations. By sampling subsets of columns, FeatLLM encourages each trial to concentrate on a smaller, more manageable part of the problem. Across trials, the ensemble can then recover broader coverage of the full feature space.

#
FeatLLM also uses instance bagging, meaning that each trial can use only a subset of the few-shot training examples. This again serves two purposes. First, it reduces prompt length, which is crucial when the number of serialized labeled examples grows. Second, it increases diversity across trials because different subsets of examples can lead the LLM to infer different rules.

This is particularly helpful in the low-shot regime where every example may strongly influence the prompt. Using different subsets prevents the system from becoming too dependent on one small set of demonstrations. At the same time, because the ensemble pools many trials, the overall model still benefits from the information contained in the full training set across repeated runs.

#
Bagging is the mechanism that makes ensembling feasible under prompt size constraints. The paper notes that when the number of features or training samples becomes too large, directly placing everything into a single prompt may be impossible or may degrade performance. By selecting subsets of features or instances for each trial, FeatLLM keeps each prompt within a manageable size.

This is not merely a technical convenience. It is part of the method’s core strategy for applying LLMs to wide tabular data. Without bagging, the model would either exceed the context window or be forced to include a very long and unwieldy serialized table. Bagging allows the method to distribute the information across multiple smaller prompts, each of which contributes one piece of the final ensemble.

#
The ensemble helps compensate for both incorrect rules and partial rule coverage. If a particular run generates a flawed rule, that trial may still be outweighed by other runs that produce better rules. Likewise, if one trial only captures a subset of the relevant conditions, other trials may fill in the missing pieces. The final averaged prediction therefore reflects a broader and more stable picture than any single rule set.

The paper also interprets this behavior in relation to self-consistency. Rules that are repeatedly inferred across multiple trials are more likely to be correct, because they persist despite stochastic changes in prompt order, temperature, and sampled subsets. In this sense, the ensemble is not only averaging predictions; it is also acting as a filter that favors recurring patterns over isolated mistakes.

#
FeatLLM’s ensemble strategy sits at the intersection of classical ensemble learning and LLM-specific prompting behavior. From classical ensemble learning, it borrows the idea that multiple weak models can produce a stronger system when combined. From LLM prompting, it borrows stochastic rule generation, prompt sensitivity, and the use of few-shot demonstrations. The paper’s contribution is to combine these ideas in a way that turns an LLM from a per-sample predictor into a feature generator whose outputs can be aggregated efficiently.

The trade-off is that greater diversity usually requires more repeated trials, more sampled subsets, and therefore more total LLM queries during rule generation. However, the paper argues that this cost is concentrated in the feature-engineering stage, not at inference time. Since the final deployed model is only the simple downstream predictor operating on generated binary features, inference remains much cheaper than approaches that must query the LLM for every sample.

#
This distinction between training-time and inference-time cost is one of the most important practical advantages of FeatLLM’s ensemble design. In LLM-based tabular prediction methods that perform end-to-end inference, every test sample must be sent through the LLM. That makes deployment expensive and slow, especially when many samples must be predicted. FeatLLM instead spends its LLM budget upfront to generate rules, then uses a lightweight model for prediction thereafter.

As a result, the ensemble is used to build a strong feature set once, after which the system can operate without further LLM calls for each new sample. The paper reports that this leads to relatively low inference time, comparable to conventional methods, while preserving the benefits of LLM-based prior knowledge. In other words, the ensemble and bagging framework makes FeatLLM not only more robust, but also much more practical for real-world tabular learning.

8. Experimental Setup: Datasets, Baselines, and Evaluation Protocol

# Overview of the 13 classification datasets used in the paper

The empirical study in this paper evaluates \textsf{FeatLLM} on 13 tabular classification datasets. The authors use both binary and multi-class tasks, with the goal of testing whether the method works broadly across different tabular prediction problems rather than only on one narrowly defined benchmark. This is important because the paper’s central claim is that LLMs can act as feature engineers for few-shot tabular learning, so the evaluation needs to show that the idea is not restricted to a single domain or dataset type.

The datasets include Adult, Bank, Blood, Car, Communities, Credit-g, Diabetes, Heart, and Myocardial, along with four additional datasets: Cultivars, NHANES, Sequence-type, and Solution-mix. Each dataset is framed as a supervised classification problem. For example, Adult predicts whether an individual earns over \$50,000 annually, Bank predicts whether a customer will subscribe to a term deposit, Blood predicts whether donors will return for future donations, and Heart and Myocardial are healthcare-related tasks. Car and Credit-g represent other structured prediction settings, while Communities involves crime prediction. The last four datasets broaden the evaluation further: Cultivars concerns soybean yield, NHANES concerns age-group prediction, Sequence-type is a synthetic sequence classification task, and Solution-mix is a synthetic concentration-threshold task.

The paper emphasizes that each dataset comes with a clear name and descriptions for its attributes. This matters because \textsf{FeatLLM} relies on feature names and feature descriptions in its prompts. The evaluation is therefore not only a test of prediction accuracy, but also a test of whether the method can exploit the natural-language metadata that often accompanies tabular data.

# Diversity of domains: finance, healthcare, demographics, crime, agriculture, and synthetic tasks

A key design choice in the benchmark is domain diversity. The datasets span finance, healthcare, demographics, crime, agriculture, and synthetic tasks. This variety is not incidental: the paper’s method is built around an LLM that uses prior knowledge and reasoning, so the authors want to know whether that prior knowledge helps in many different contexts. For example, healthcare datasets such as Diabetes, Heart, and Myocardial may align well with knowledge that appears in pretraining corpora. Finance-related tasks such as Adult, Bank, and Credit-g test whether the method can capture patterns relevant to socioeconomic or banking data. Communities introduces a social and geographic setting, while Cultivars brings in agriculture.

By including synthetic tasks, the authors also examine situations in which the LLM cannot simply rely on memorized examples from the internet. Sequence-type and Solution-mix are especially useful for this purpose because they are synthetic and therefore should not have been memorized by the LLM API used in the study. This helps isolate whether \textsf{FeatLLM} is really extracting useful rules from the prompt and the few-shot examples rather than recalling a previously seen dataset.

# Why recently released and synthetic datasets are important for testing memorization concerns

The paper explicitly calls out memorization as a concern when evaluating LLM-based tabular methods. If a dataset was widely available during pretraining, then a language model might appear to perform well simply because it has seen similar examples before. To reduce this concern, the authors include datasets that were recently added to the UCI repository after September 2023, namely Cultivars and NHANES. They argue that these datasets would not have been part of the pretraining data for the GPT-3.5 API used in the experiments.

The synthetic datasets play a similar role. Because they were generated for the study’s evaluation context, they cannot have been memorized by the LLM. In other words, the inclusion of newly released and synthetic data makes the benchmark more credible as a test of generalization. It also strengthens the interpretation of the results: when \textsf{FeatLLM} performs well on these datasets, the performance is more plausibly due to rule extraction and feature engineering rather than dataset recall.

# Dataset characteristics such as feature counts and large-table challenges

The datasets vary substantially in size and complexity. The paper points out that some of them, such as Communities and Myocardial, have more than 100 attributes. This is a crucial detail because LLM-based tabular methods must serialize features into text prompts, and prompt length becomes a practical bottleneck as the number of columns grows. Large tables can exceed the model’s context window or degrade performance because the serialized input becomes too long and harder to process.

This dataset variability makes the benchmark especially informative. It does not just test whether \textsf{FeatLLM} can perform on small, clean tabular problems; it tests whether the method can remain effective when tables become wide and the prompt budget is constrained. The paper argues that this is one of the main settings where its feature-generation approach and bagging strategy help, because they reduce the need to send a full, long serialized table directly into the LLM for every prediction.

# Low-shot experimental settings and train-test sampling

The experiments are conducted in low-shot regimes. In this setup, only $k$ labeled samples, with $k < N$, are randomly sampled to train the model from the full dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^N$. This low-shot framing is central to the paper because \textsf{FeatLLM} is designed for few-shot tabular learning, where labels are scarce and conventional supervised methods may overfit or fail to generalize well.

The paper’s training protocol is intentionally data-efficient. It does not assume large labeled training sets, which would reduce the need for the kind of prior knowledge LLMs can provide. Instead, the method is evaluated under conditions where the model must make the most of very limited supervision. The authors also note that, for the cross-validation setup used in implementation, the value of $k$ is chosen as either 2 or 4 in order to guarantee that the training set includes at least one example of each class.

This setup reflects the intended use case of the paper: practical tabular tasks where labeling is costly, and only a small number of labeled examples are available.

# Evaluation metric: AUC and why it is used

The main performance metric reported in the paper is AUC, which is the area under the ROC curve. The authors present average AUC values, along with standard deviations, across repeated runs. AUC is a natural choice for classification benchmarks because it measures ranking quality across decision thresholds rather than relying on a single fixed threshold. In few-shot settings, this is especially useful because the model may be trained on very limited data, and threshold-sensitive metrics can be unstable.

Although the paper does not spend much time justifying AUC in abstract terms, its use is consistent with the kind of comparative evaluation needed here. Since the study includes binary and multi-class problems and compares methods across a variety of datasets, AUC offers a common quantitative scale for measuring how well the models separate classes.

# Repeating runs across random splits and reporting mean with standard deviation

To make the evaluation more robust, the authors repeat experiments three times with different randomness in the choice of training and test sets. They then report the average and standard deviation of the AUC values. This is an important methodological detail because low-shot experiments can be highly sensitive to which few examples are selected for training.

By repeating the runs, the authors reduce the chance that a single favorable or unfavorable split dominates the reported results. The standard deviation also provides a sense of stability. In few-shot tabular learning, performance can fluctuate quite a bit from split to split, so including variability is essential for interpreting whether a method is consistently strong or only occasionally successful.

# Conventional baselines: logistic regression, XGBoost, and random forest

The paper compares \textsf{FeatLLM} against several standard tabular learning baselines, beginning with logistic regression, XGBoost, and random forest. These baselines represent common choices in structured prediction problems. Logistic regression is a simple linear model, XGBoost is a powerful gradient-boosted tree ensemble, and random forest is another widely used tree-based ensemble.

Including these methods is important because they serve as the conventional reference point for tabular learning. If \textsf{FeatLLM} is to be taken seriously as a practical method, it must be compared not only against other LLM-based techniques but also against strong classical baselines. The paper treats these models as standard supervised tabular learners and tunes their parameters using grid search with $k$-fold cross-validation.

# Baselines using unlabeled data: SCARF and STUNT

The next group of baselines, SCARF and STUNT, assumes access to unlabeled data. These methods are relevant because they attempt to improve few-shot learning by exploiting additional information that may be easier to collect than labels. The paper notes, however, that obtaining large amounts of unlabeled data can be infeasible in real-world applications, so these methods may not always be practical.

SCARF and STUNT are included to show how \textsf{FeatLLM} compares with methods that use self-supervised or semi-supervised strategies. Since these baselines are designed to benefit from unlabeled examples, they represent a different philosophy from the LLM-based methods. Their inclusion makes the comparison more comprehensive: \textsf{FeatLLM} is not only being measured against standard classifiers, but also against methods that attempt to address low-shot learning through auxiliary data.

# Transfer/foundation baseline: TabPFN

TabPFN is included as a transfer or foundation-style baseline. According to the paper, TabPFN pretrains a Bayesian neural network on synthetic data generated from a mixture of different distributions. After pretraining, it performs inference by taking both training and test samples of the target task without further training. This makes it a strong few-shot baseline because it uses a learned prior over tabular tasks.

TabPFN is important in the comparison because it represents a modern approach to tabular learning that is not LLM-based but still leverages broad pretraining. In effect, it is one of the strongest references for whether \textsf{FeatLLM} adds value beyond foundation-model-style tabular inference.

# LLM-based baselines: in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM

The final group of baselines uses LLMs directly. These are in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM. In-context learning simply places few-shot training examples in the prompt and asks the model to predict without any additional tuning. TABLET extends this idea by incorporating additional hints into the prompt through an external classifier using rule sets and prototypes. TabLLM instead uses parameter-efficient tuning methods such as IA3 to adapt the LLM to the tabular task.

These baselines are especially relevant because \textsf{FeatLLM} is also an LLM-based tabular method, but it differs in a key way: it uses the LLM to generate rules and features rather than to produce every final prediction end to end. Comparing against these alternatives helps isolate whether the feature-engineering strategy is genuinely useful, rather than simply benefiting from the presence of an LLM.

The paper also notes that some of these LLM-based baselines struggle when the tabular dataset has many features. This observation reinforces the motivation for \textsf{FeatLLM}'s design, which aims to avoid repeatedly sending large serialized tables through the LLM at inference time.

# Backbone model choice for FeatLLM: GPT-3.5 via API

For the main experiments, \textsf{FeatLLM} uses GPT-3.5 as its LLM backbone. The authors stress that the framework is designed to be agnostic to the specific LLM choice, and they report additional backbone results in the appendix. Still, GPT-3.5 is the central implementation choice in the paper’s reported experiments.

This choice matters because one of the paper’s claims is that \textsf{FeatLLM} only requires API-level access to the LLM and does not require training access. That makes the method more realistic in situations where the model provider does not expose fine-tuning or internal training code. The use of GPT-3.5 aligns with that operational setting.

# Training details for the downstream linear model

After the LLM generates rules, \textsf{FeatLLM} converts them into binary features and fits a simple downstream model. In the implementation described in the paper, this model is a linear model trained with the Adam optimizer. The learning rate is set to $0.01$, and the model is trained for 200 epochs. The paper also uses $k$-fold cross-validation for selecting the optimal epoch.

The downstream model is intentionally simple. The goal is not to build a complicated classifier on top of the generated rules, but rather to test whether the rules themselves carry enough information to support good prediction. The linear model assigns non-negative weights to the binary rule features and uses a softmax over class logits to estimate class probabilities. This simplicity is part of the paper’s appeal: once the rules are extracted, inference does not require another LLM pass.

# Hyperparameters: temperature, number of rules, number of ensembles, optimizer, and epochs

The paper reports several main hyperparameters. For the LLM, the temperature is set to $0.5$ and the top-p value is kept at the API default of $1$. The number of rules extracted per class is set to 10, and the number of ensemble trials is set to 20. For the downstream linear model, the optimizer is Adam with learning rate $0.01$, trained for 200 epochs.

These settings reflect a balance between diversity, prompt length, and performance. A higher temperature encourages more variation in rule generation across ensemble trials, which is useful because the method relies on aggregating multiple rule sets. At the same time, too much randomness could reduce consistency. The number of rules per class also has to be controlled carefully: too few rules may underfit, while too many can enlarge the prompt and potentially overfit in a low-shot setting.

# Cross-validation for model selection

Cross-validation is used in two main places. First, it helps choose the optimal epoch for the downstream linear model. Second, it is used in tuning the conventional baselines such as logistic regression, XGBoost, and random forest through grid search. The paper reports that the value of $k$ is either 2 or 4, with the additional constraint that the training set must contain at least one instance of each class.

This cross-validation procedure supports a fairer and more stable comparison. In low-shot experiments, selecting hyperparameters from a very small sample is difficult, so cross-validation is a standard way to reduce dependence on a single arbitrary split. The paper’s use of cross-validation is therefore part of its effort to make the evaluation protocol consistent across methods.

# Practical issues in baseline replication and comparison

The paper also discusses practical issues that affect replication and fairness. For example, when comparing against TabLLM, the authors note that TabLLM restricts the use of LLMs to publicly available checkpoints, so GPT-3.5 cannot be used directly in that setting. Instead, T0 is used following the original TabLLM setup. For in-context learning, GPT-3.5 is used. For conventional methods, parameters are tuned with grid search and cross-validation.

These implementation details matter because baseline comparisons can otherwise become misleading. Different methods may have different access assumptions, different model families, or different training requirements. The paper tries to compare methods under their intended settings while still keeping the evaluation broadly fair. It also notes that some LLM-based baselines are not feasible on datasets with many features because of prompt-length limitations, which is one of the key challenges \textsf{FeatLLM} is designed to overcome.

Taken together, the experimental setup shows that the paper is not just benchmarking accuracy. It is carefully probing whether a rule-generating, feature-engineering use of LLMs can outperform conventional, semi-supervised, transfer-based, and direct LLM-based methods under realistic few-shot tabular constraints.

9. Main Results: Accuracy, Efficiency, and Practical Advantages

# Summary of overall performance across all datasets

The paper evaluates \textsf{FeatLLM} on 13 tabular datasets spanning binary and multi-class classification tasks, and the main message of the results is that the method is consistently strong across this diverse collection. These datasets include common benchmark problems such as Adult, Bank, Blood, Heart, and Diabetes, as well as newer or synthetic datasets such as Cultivars, NHANES, Sequence-type, and Solution-mix. The motivation for this broad evaluation is important: the paper is not claiming success on a single dataset family, but rather that the same feature-engineering framework can generalize across different domains, task types, and table shapes.

Across the reported results, \textsf{FeatLLM} performs at a high level on most datasets. The paper states that it consistently ranks either first or second among the compared methods. This is an especially meaningful outcome because the comparison includes conventional machine learning baselines, semi-supervised or pretraining-based tabular methods, and several LLM-based methods. In other words, the method is competitive not just against similar LLM systems, but also against traditional models that are strong on tabular data.

A key empirical claim is that \textsf{FeatLLM} produces high-quality rules that help the downstream classifier make accurate predictions. The paper emphasizes that the LLM is not being asked to directly output class labels at inference time; instead, it is used to generate features that encode decision rules. The experiments suggest that this transformation is effective: the generated rule-based features are informative enough that a simple downstream model can perform well, even in few-shot settings.

# Evidence that \textsf{FeatLLM} is typically the best or second-best method

The main tables report that \textsf{FeatLLM} is usually at or near the top of the leaderboard. This is a central result because it indicates that the method is not only practical but also competitive with the strongest alternatives evaluated in the paper. The authors highlight that the method is often the top performer, and when it is not, it generally remains in second place. That pattern matters because it shows robustness rather than isolated success on a few easy datasets.

The paper also notes that \textsf{FeatLLM} maintains this strong standing even though its inference procedure is conceptually simpler than end-to-end LLM prediction methods. Once the rules have been generated, prediction is handled by a downstream model with low complexity. So the strong ranking is not achieved by repeatedly querying a large language model for each sample, but by distilling LLM knowledge into reusable features. This makes the empirical performance more notable, because the gains come with a more efficient inference design.

The authors also compare performance across repeated runs with different random training/test splits and report averages and standard deviations of AUC. This matters because it indicates that the results are not a one-off outcome driven by a particular split. Instead, the method’s performance is stable enough to remain competitive under repeated low-shot sampling.

# Average improvement over alternative LLM-based methods

The paper’s abstract states that \textsf{FeatLLM} outperforms alternatives such as TabLLM and STUNT by about 10\% on average. This average advantage is one of the most important quantitative takeaways because it positions the method as a meaningful advance over prior LLM-based tabular learning approaches rather than just an incremental variant.

The improvement is especially notable because the competing methods solve the task differently. TabLLM and similar approaches still depend on the LLM for prediction, often requiring either parameter-efficient tuning or repeated inference through the language model. STUNT, by contrast, uses unlabeled data and tabular pretraining-style strategies. \textsf{FeatLLM} differs by using the LLM as a feature engineer and then relying on a simple classifier. The reported average gain suggests that this change in formulation is not merely computationally appealing; it also improves predictive quality.

The paper further suggests that the improvements come from the quality of the extracted rules and the way they are filtered by the downstream learning process. Some rules may be very informative, while others are less relevant. The linear model learns to emphasize the useful ones, which helps explain why the method can outperform end-to-end LLM baselines that must directly map serialized tables to labels.

# Performance in extremely low-shot regimes such as 4-shot learning

One of the strongest claims in the paper is that \textsf{FeatLLM} is effective even when only a very small number of labeled examples is available. The authors specifically point out that with just 4 shots, the method can achieve performance comparable to conventional tabular baselines that require 32 shots. This is an important result because it directly addresses the low-data setting that motivates the paper.

The comparison to 32-shot conventional baselines is especially informative. It suggests that \textsf{FeatLLM} can partially compensate for the scarcity of labeled examples by injecting prior knowledge from the LLM into the feature space. Rather than expecting the downstream model to discover meaningful structure from only a handful of labels, the LLM helps generate features that already encode useful class conditions. The downstream learner then only needs to estimate how much each rule matters.

This result also supports the paper’s broader thesis that the LLM should not simply be used to produce label predictions. In the low-shot setting, the LLM’s prior knowledge is most useful when it helps construct a better representation of the input data. The 4-shot results show that this representation-level assistance can be powerful enough to narrow the gap with much more data-hungry baselines.

# Comparison to conventional models requiring more labeled examples

The paper compares \textsf{FeatLLM} to standard tabular models such as logistic regression, XGBoost, and RandomForest. These are important baselines because they represent what a practitioner might reasonably try first on tabular data. The results show that \textsf{FeatLLM} is competitive with these conventional methods, especially in the low-shot setting where the labeled data are scarce.

What makes this comparison especially meaningful is that conventional models usually need substantially more labeled data to become effective. In contrast, \textsf{FeatLLM} is designed around the idea that an LLM can supply task-relevant prior knowledge through feature engineering. The reported performance indicates that this prior knowledge is useful enough to reduce the amount of labeled data needed for strong predictions.

The paper also notes that some non-LLM tabular methods, such as STUNT and TabPFN, can improve performance on certain datasets, but their overall gains over conventional machine learning are not always substantial. Against this backdrop, \textsf{FeatLLM} stands out because it combines strong results with a clearer low-shot advantage. It does not merely match conventional approaches; in many cases it surpasses them despite using very few labeled examples.

# Behavior on high-dimensional datasets where prompt length is a challenge

A major practical issue in LLM-based tabular learning is that wide tables can produce prompts that become too long for the model to handle effectively. The paper specifically discusses datasets like Communities and Myocardial, which contain over 100 attributes. These settings are difficult for methods that serialize all features into text and send them directly into the LLM for prediction.

The results show that \textsf{FeatLLM} remains effective even on such high-dimensional datasets. This is important because the method is intentionally designed to reduce dependence on long serialized prompts at inference time. By extracting a limited number of rules per class and using bagging to sample subsets of features or instances, the approach can operate under prompt size constraints more gracefully than methods that require every sample to be evaluated end-to-end by the LLM.

The paper’s evaluation suggests that the bagging and ensemble strategy is especially helpful in these wide-table settings. Instead of trying to fit all features into one large prompt, \textsf{FeatLLM} creates multiple smaller rule sets across trials. This makes the method more robust to the prompt length limitations that often undermine direct LLM-based tabular methods.

# Why some prior LLM methods fail on wide tables

The paper argues that many prior LLM tabular methods struggle when the number of features becomes large because the prompt must contain a serialized representation of the table. As more attributes are included, the prompt gets longer, and this creates two problems. First, the serialized text may exceed the practical context window or become unwieldy. Second, even if the model can process the prompt, the quality of reasoning may degrade when the input is too long or noisy.

Methods like in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM are specifically mentioned as struggling to infer on tabular datasets with many features. The reason is not that LLMs cannot reason about tabular data in principle, but that these methods still rely on direct prompt-based prediction. When the prompt becomes too large, important information may be diluted or lost, and the model may not attend reliably to the relevant attributes.

\textsf{FeatLLM} avoids this failure mode by shifting the LLM’s job from direct prediction to rule extraction. Rather than asking the model to score each sample from a large serialized table, it asks the model to identify class-related conditions and then converts those conditions into compact binary features. This lowers the burden on the prompt and makes the method more scalable to wide tables.

# Inference-time advantages of using only the downstream model after feature generation

One of the paper’s major practical advantages is that, after feature generation, inference does not require querying the LLM for each test sample. This is a substantial difference from many prior LLM-based methods, where every new input must pass through the language model. In \textsf{FeatLLM}, the LLM is used during the rule-engineering stage, but once the rules are parsed and converted into features, prediction is handled by a simple downstream model.

This design has two important consequences. First, it reduces inference latency. The downstream model is much lighter than an LLM, so predictions can be made more quickly and efficiently. Second, it makes deployment more practical, because the system does not depend on repeated API calls or repeated access to the large model at inference time. In other words, the cost of using the LLM is paid up front during rule generation, not every time a new sample is classified.

The paper emphasizes that this is not just a theoretical convenience. In the reported runtime comparison, \textsf{FeatLLM} has inference time comparable to conventional methods, while other LLM-based approaches are much slower. That makes the method especially attractive in settings where predictions need to be made repeatedly or at scale.

# Training-time costs in relation to API querying

The training side of \textsf{FeatLLM} still involves LLM API usage, but the paper argues that the cost is manageable. The implementation section reports that the method requires only 30 API queries, which the authors describe as not imposing a significant burden and as being parallelizable. This is a crucial practical point because it separates \textsf{FeatLLM} from methods that need more extensive tuning or repeated per-sample inference through an LLM.

The paper also notes that \textsf{FeatLLM} is designed to require only API-level access, not access to the LLM’s training code or parameter updates. This matters because many high-performing LLMs are only available through limited APIs. A method that depends on fine-tuning or internal model access may be difficult to use in real deployments, whereas \textsf{FeatLLM} can operate under those constraints.

The training procedure is therefore best understood as a one-time feature discovery process. The cost is concentrated in generating and parsing rules and fitting the small downstream model. Once this is done, the model can be used efficiently for inference without further LLM calls.

# Cost comparisons with in-context prediction, TABLET, and TabLLM

The paper directly compares runtime with several LLM-based baselines. It reports that \textsf{FeatLLM} has relatively low inference time, comparable to conventional methods. By contrast, in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM have much higher inference costs because they rely on LLM-based prediction for each sample.

This difference is central to the paper’s practical argument. The method is not just more accurate in many settings; it is also more economical at inference time. If a system must classify many samples, repeated LLM calls can become expensive and slow. \textsf{FeatLLM} avoids that by moving the expensive part of the computation into the rule-generation stage and then using a lightweight classifier thereafter.

The paper also remarks that the ensemble idea could in principle be adapted to existing LLM-based approaches, but doing so would double the number of per-sample inferences and therefore substantially increase computational complexity. This reinforces the claim that \textsf{FeatLLM} achieves a more favorable accuracy-efficiency tradeoff than methods that keep the LLM in the prediction loop.

# Practical implications for deployment in real applications

The empirical results support the paper’s broader practical message: \textsf{FeatLLM} is well suited to real-world tabular learning scenarios where labels are scarce, tables may be wide, and inference efficiency matters. The method is particularly relevant in domains like finance and healthcare, where collecting labels can be expensive and where prior knowledge encoded in LLMs may be useful. Because the method uses only API access and does not require training the LLM itself, it is compatible with restricted deployment settings.

The paper’s broader implication is that LLMs can be used more effectively as feature engineers than as direct predictors for tabular data. This opens the door to systems that combine the prior knowledge of an LLM with the efficiency of a conventional downstream model. The resulting pipeline is more scalable than direct prompt-based prediction and more data-efficient than traditional supervised learning alone.

At the same time, the results also suggest a realistic boundary: the method is intended for low-shot learning, and the authors explicitly identify expansion to larger training sets and broader feature types as future work. Within its target regime, however, the experiments show that \textsf{FeatLLM} offers a strong combination of predictive accuracy, lower inference cost, and better handling of prompt-size constraints than prior LLM-based tabular methods.

10. Ablations and Mechanistic Analysis of FeatLLM

# Purpose of ablation studies in validating system design

Ablation studies are used in the paper to answer a very specific question: which parts of \textsf{FeatLLM} are actually responsible for its performance, and which parts are merely helpful refinements? Because \textsf{FeatLLM} is a multi-stage system that combines prompt design, rule generation, feature parsing, a simple predictive model, and ensembling, it is not enough to report final accuracy alone. The authors want to know whether the model works because of the LLM’s prior knowledge, because of the downstream linear model, because of the ensemble, or because of the prompt structure that guides the LLM toward useful rules. Ablation studies isolate these components one by one.

This type of analysis is especially important for a method like \textsf{FeatLLM}, because its central claim is not that the LLM is used directly for prediction, but that it can be repurposed as a feature engineer. If the performance gain came only from the final linear model or only from averaging multiple trials, then the broader design principle would be weaker. By selectively removing components, the paper evaluates whether each piece contributes in a meaningful way to the system’s ability to learn from a few labeled tabular examples.

# Ablation of weight tuning versus simple rule counting

One major ablation removes the tuning stage in the linear model, denoted \textsf{-Tuning}. In the full method, each generated rule becomes a binary feature, and the model learns non-negative weights over these features so that more informative rules contribute more strongly to the class logit. In the ablated version, this learned weighting is replaced by a much simpler strategy: the binary features are summed directly to compute the logit, so every rule contributes equally.

The paper shows that this simplification consistently hurts performance. The interpretation is straightforward: not all LLM-generated rules are equally useful, even when they are syntactically valid and semantically plausible. Some rules are more closely aligned with the target label than others, and some may be noisy or only weakly relevant. Learned weights allow the model to calibrate the contribution of each rule based on the small training set. By contrast, simple counting assumes uniform importance, which cannot distinguish strong rules from weak ones.

An interesting observation in the paper is that the benefit of weight tuning becomes larger as the number of shots increases. This makes sense within the authors’ framework. When more labeled examples are available, the linear model has more evidence for estimating which rule features are truly predictive. In very low-shot settings, tuning still helps, but the advantage becomes more pronounced as the training data becomes less sparse and the model can better identify the importance of individual rules.

# Ablation of ensemble averaging

The second major ablation removes the ensemble stage, denoted \textsf{-Ensemble}. In the full method, \textsf{FeatLLM} repeats the whole process multiple times: it generates rules with the LLM, parses them into features, trains a linear model, and then averages predicted probabilities across trials. This repeated process is designed to exploit diversity in rule generation and to reduce the effect of any one poor prompt outcome.

When the ensemble is removed, performance drops across settings. The paper’s interpretation is that the ensemble stabilizes the system against variability in LLM outputs. Since rule generation is performed through prompting rather than explicit training, the exact rules produced can differ across trials. Some trials may produce high-quality rules, while others may generate partially incorrect or incomplete ones. Averaging across many trials lets the correct patterns reinforce each other and reduces the impact of mistakes.

The ensemble also plays a practical role beyond robustness. It helps address the prompt-size limitation of tabular inputs. By using bagging, each trial can operate on a subset of features or instances, which keeps the prompt manageable when the table is large. Thus, the ensemble is not only a predictive trick; it is also part of the method’s strategy for scaling to wider tables. The ablation shows that removing this mechanism weakens both the robustness and the scalability of the framework.

# Ablation of feature descriptions in prompts

The paper also studies what happens when the feature descriptions are removed from the prompt, denoted \textsf{-Description}. In the full prompt design, the LLM is given not only serialized examples and task descriptions, but also natural language descriptions of the features, including their value types and, when available, definitions. These descriptions are meant to help the LLM understand what each column means, especially in tabular domains where raw column names can be ambiguous.

The ablation shows that removing these feature descriptions degrades performance. This indicates that the LLM is using the descriptions to connect its prior knowledge with the structure of the task. In tabular learning, the raw values alone can be hard to interpret because many features are not self-explanatory. When descriptions are available, the LLM can reason about whether a feature is relevant to the target and can formulate rules that are more aligned with the actual semantics of the domain.

The paper emphasizes that feature descriptions matter most in the low-shot regime. This is consistent with the general goal of \textsf{FeatLLM}: when labeled examples are scarce, the model must rely more heavily on prior knowledge. The descriptions help the LLM bring that knowledge to bear in a structured way. Without them, the model has less context for deciding which conditions are meaningful, and rule extraction becomes less effective.

# Ablation of the first reasoning step based on prior knowledge

Another key ablation removes the first step of the reasoning instruction, denoted \textsf{-Reasoning}. In the full method, the LLM is guided through a two-step reasoning process. First, it is asked to infer the general causal relationship or tendency between features and the task using prior knowledge and common sense, without looking at the example demonstrations. Then, in the second step, it uses the demonstrations and the information from the first step to derive class-specific rules.

Removing the first step causes performance to fall, which shows that this step is not just rhetorical decoration. It serves an important filtering function. By letting the LLM first think about the task at a conceptual level, the prompt encourages it to focus on features that plausibly matter for the target rather than latching onto superficial correlations in the few-shot examples. The authors explicitly connect this to reducing the chance that irrelevant columns will dominate the rule generation process.

This ablation reveals an important mechanism in \textsf{FeatLLM}: the model is not only extracting rules from examples, but also using prior knowledge to shape which rules are worth considering. The first reasoning step helps the LLM separate general task-relevant structure from accidental patterns in the small labeled set. When this step is removed, the extracted rules become less reliable, especially when the data are sparse.

# How component importance changes with the number of shots

One of the paper’s most informative findings is that the importance of different components changes depending on how many labeled examples are available. The ablation results show a clear pattern. Feature descriptions and reasoning instructions are especially valuable when the number of shots is small. In contrast, the value of weight tuning becomes more apparent when the number of shots increases.

This pattern fits the role of each component in the pipeline. When there are only a few labeled examples, the system depends heavily on the LLM’s prior knowledge to infer useful rules in the first place. In that regime, prompt design matters enormously because the examples alone are too sparse to reliably convey the task. As a result, feature descriptions and the structured reasoning prompt have a large effect.

As more labeled examples become available, the linear model has a better basis for learning which generated rules are actually predictive. That is why tuning becomes more impactful with more shots. The system shifts from mainly relying on LLM prior knowledge to increasingly adapting those generated features to the observed data. The paper presents this as an important strength of \textsf{FeatLLM}: it can use prior knowledge when data are scarce, but it can also refine that knowledge with training signals as more examples appear.

# Feature-quality analysis using AUROC of generated rule features

Beyond final prediction accuracy, the paper evaluates the quality of the generated rule features themselves. To do this, it measures the average AUROC between each newly generated binary feature and the target labels, and then computes the ratio of useful features, defined as those with AUROC greater than $0.5$. This analysis asks whether the rules produced by the LLM are actually informative before the linear model even learns to weight them.

The results indicate that many of the generated rules are relevant to the target attribute. This is important because it shows that \textsf{FeatLLM} is not merely discovering arbitrary binary patterns; it is producing candidate features that often have predictive value on their own. The paper interprets this as evidence that the LLM is successfully combining its prior knowledge with the few-shot examples to generate meaningful rules.

This feature-quality analysis also helps explain why the downstream linear model works so well. If the generated features were mostly noise, then even a well-tuned classifier would struggle. Instead, the rules are often already aligned with the label structure, and the linear model’s role is to select and combine them effectively. In that sense, the AUROC analysis provides direct support for the central feature-engineering claim of the paper.

# Ratio of useful rules before tuning

The paper separates the feature-quality analysis into a view before tuning and after tuning. Before tuning, the question is simply how many generated rules are useful in the sense of having AUROC above $0.5$. The reported results show that the majority of the created rules are informative. This matters because it suggests that the LLM’s rule generation is already doing substantial work before any supervised learning step is applied.

This finding is central to the paper’s interpretation of \textsf{FeatLLM}. The LLM is not just generating random symbolic conditions; it is often generating rules that reflect genuine relationships between feature values and the target class. In other words, the LLM’s prior knowledge plus the prompt structure are sufficient to produce a strong initial pool of candidate features.

At the same time, “majority useful” does not mean “all equally important.” Some rules are still weaker or less relevant than others. That is why the downstream tuning step remains necessary. The pre-tuning analysis establishes that the system starts from a promising set of candidate features, which then can be refined by the linear model.

# Filtering weak or irrelevant rules after tuning through learned weights

After tuning, the paper observes that rules with little relevance to the data are automatically filtered out by the learned weights. In other words, the linear model does more than just assign importance scores; it effectively suppresses weak features. The authors note that rules with learned weights below a threshold are treated as negligible, and they use a threshold of $0.1$ in their analysis, based on the overall weight histogram.

This filtering behavior is an important part of the mechanism by which \textsf{FeatLLM} adapts to the data. The LLM may generate a broad set of plausible rules, but not all of them will match the specifics of the current dataset. The learned weights allow the model to keep the helpful rules while down-weighting those that do not contribute much to prediction. This is how the system combines broad prior knowledge with task-specific adaptation.

The threshold analysis also gives a practical interpretation of what the linear model is doing. The downstream learner is not building a complex nonlinear decision boundary; it is pruning the rule set by identifying which extracted conditions deserve attention. In this way, the model acts as a gatekeeper between LLM-generated hypotheses and final predictions.

# Interpretation of weight thresholds and feature pruning

The discussion of the $0.1$ threshold helps clarify how feature pruning functions in \textsf{FeatLLM}. The paper does not treat this threshold as a universal theoretical constant, but as an empirical choice informed by the weight distribution. Its purpose is to separate features that have meaningful contribution from those that are effectively unused after training.

This kind of pruning is consistent with the paper’s broader design philosophy. Since the LLM may generate many rules across trials, some redundancy is expected. The downstream model does not need to preserve every rule equally; instead, it can focus on the subset that best supports classification. The threshold thus gives a concrete lens for understanding how the system compresses a larger symbolic rule space into a smaller set of useful predictors.

Importantly, the threshold-based view shows that \textsf{FeatLLM} is not just extracting rules, but also learning which rules matter. That distinction is central to the method’s mechanism. The LLM supplies candidate structure, while the linear model and its weights adapt that structure to the observed labels.

# What these studies reveal about LLM prior knowledge and data adaptation

Taken together, the ablation and feature-quality studies reveal a clear division of labor inside \textsf{FeatLLM}. The LLM contributes prior knowledge and reasoning ability, which are used to generate semantically meaningful rules from the prompt. The downstream linear model contributes data adaptation, learning which of those rules matter for the target task. The ensemble contributes robustness and handles prompt-size constraints by aggregating multiple partial views of the problem.

The paper’s analyses show that neither part alone is enough. If the prompt lacks feature descriptions or the first reasoning step, the LLM’s prior knowledge is not used effectively. If tuning is removed, the model cannot adapt the rules to the small labeled set. If ensembling is removed, the system becomes more fragile to variability in generated rules. The method works because these components complement one another.

The spurious-correlation experiment reinforces this interpretation. By attaching irrelevant columns, the authors show that \textsf{FeatLLM} is relatively resistant to noise because its rule extraction process is guided by the task and the feature semantics rather than by blind pattern matching. At the same time, the paper also notes a limitation: if unrelated columns come from closely related data, the model can still learn spurious correlations and misunderstand causal relations. So the mechanism is strong, but not perfect.

# Mechanistic understanding of why FeatLLM works

The mechanistic picture emerging from the paper is that \textsf{FeatLLM} transforms an LLM from an end-to-end predictor into a rule generator. It first uses prompt design to encourage a reasoning path that combines prior knowledge with examples, then turns the generated rules into binary features, and finally learns a simple weighted combination of those features. The resulting system is interpretable at the level of extracted rules and efficient at inference time because it no longer requires an LLM call per sample.

The analyses suggest that the method succeeds because the LLM is used where it is strongest: identifying likely task-relevant conditions from limited text-like context. The linear model is used where classical machine learning is strong: estimating the relative importance of features from labeled data. Ensemble averaging then stabilizes this pipeline by reducing dependence on any single generated rule set.

In short, the ablation studies and mechanistic analyses support the paper’s core claim that \textsf{FeatLLM} works by merging prior knowledge with data-driven selection. The LLM proposes candidate explanations in the form of rules, and the downstream learner retains the ones that genuinely help prediction. This division of labor is what lets the method perform well in few-shot tabular learning while remaining practical under prompt-size and inference-cost constraints.

11. Robustness to Spurious Correlations and Hyperparameter Choices

# Why spurious correlations are especially dangerous in few-shot learning

A central concern in few-shot tabular learning is that the model has very little labeled data from which to learn what truly matters. When examples are scarce, it becomes easier for a method to latch onto accidental patterns that happen to appear in the tiny training set but do not reflect the real relationship between features and labels. The paper treats this as a particularly important risk because tabular data often contains many columns, and in a low-shot regime there may not be enough evidence to distinguish genuinely useful signals from irrelevant or misleading ones.

This problem is not just theoretical. The paper’s analysis emphasizes that, with few labeled samples, even strong models may overfit to spurious correlations, especially when unrelated features are present. In such cases, a method can appear to perform well on the training data while actually learning the wrong associations. The consequence is poor robustness: once the data distribution changes or noisy features are introduced, predictions can degrade sharply.

# Experimental design for injecting unrelated columns into the Heart dataset

To directly test robustness against spurious correlations, the paper constructs a controlled experiment using the Heart dataset as the target task. Random columns from the Adult dataset are attached to the Heart data, even though those columns are irrelevant to the Heart prediction problem. The purpose of this design is to simulate the presence of noisy, unrelated attributes and measure how different methods respond as the amount of irrelevant information increases.

The experiment progressively increases the number of unrelated columns added to the Heart dataset. This allows the authors to observe performance under a growing burden of distraction. For fairness, the comparison uses the same $8$ labeled shots and does not include methods that rely on unlabeled data, such as SCARF or STUNT. That choice keeps the focus on how methods behave in a low-shot supervised setting when the feature space becomes noisier.

# Comparing degradation of FeatLLM and baselines under added noise features

The results show that \textsf{FeatLLM} suffers the least performance degradation as more unrelated columns are added. In the paper’s comparison, baseline methods experience stronger drops in performance, while \textsf{FeatLLM} remains relatively stable. This suggests that the framework is better at resisting distraction from irrelevant attributes.

The paper interprets this outcome as evidence that \textsf{FeatLLM} is not merely fitting directly to serialized features. Instead, it first asks the LLM to reason about the relationship between the task and the features, and then to extract rules based on that reasoning. This extra step appears to filter out many irrelevant columns before they are converted into binary features. As a result, the downstream learner is exposed to a more distilled representation, which helps preserve robustness when noise is introduced.

# Why prior-knowledge-guided reasoning helps avoid irrelevant columns

The paper argues that the key reason \textsf{FeatLLM} is more robust is its prior-knowledge-guided rule extraction process. In the prompt design, the model is first encouraged to infer the causal relationship or tendency between features and the task before it sees the example demonstrations. Only after this does it derive class-specific rules. This staged reasoning is meant to imitate how an expert would approach a tabular prediction problem.

By forcing the LLM to consider the broader task-feature relationship first, the framework reduces the chance that it will focus on irrelevant columns just because they are present in the serialized input. The paper explicitly states that this helps prevent the generation of rules based on unrelated columns. In the noisy-column experiment, this design seems to matter: the framework is able to filter out most spurious attributes rather than elevating them into features that influence prediction.

# Observed percentage of rules based on noisy columns

The paper reports that rules based on noisy columns accounted for only $1$--$2\%$ of the total. This is an important quantitative indicator of robustness. It means that, even after unrelated columns are attached, very few extracted rules are actually driven by those irrelevant features.

This result does not mean that noisy columns are never used, but it does show that they are rare among the extracted rules. The low percentage supports the idea that the prompt structure and rule-generation process help the LLM concentrate on meaningful feature relations. In other words, most of the generated rule set remains aligned with the target task rather than being dominated by injected noise.

# Limits of robustness: when related but misleading columns can still cause errors

The paper also makes clear that this robustness has limits. It notes that \textsf{FeatLLM} can still learn spurious correlations and misunderstand causal relationships if the attached columns come from closely related data. That is, when misleading columns are not obviously unrelated but instead resemble the target task in some way, the framework can still be fooled.

This is an important caveat. The method is better at ignoring blatantly irrelevant attributes than at perfectly distinguishing subtle causal structure from correlated but misleading information. So while the experiment demonstrates strong resistance to obvious noise, it does not claim immunity to all forms of spurious correlation. The paper’s discussion therefore presents robustness as improved, not absolute.

# Main hyperparameters of FeatLLM: number of ensembles and rules per class

The paper identifies two main hyperparameters for \textsf{FeatLLM}. The first is the number of ensembles, meaning how many times the rule-generation, parsing, feature creation, and downstream training process is repeated and then combined. The second is the number of rules per class that are extracted during each LLM query.

These two quantities directly affect both performance and practicality. More ensembles can improve stability by averaging over multiple rule sets, while more rules per class can increase the richness of the generated feature representation. At the same time, both choices influence prompt size and computational cost, so they require careful balancing.

# Empirical effect of increasing the number of ensembles

The empirical analysis shows that performance improves as the number of ensembles increases. This makes sense in the context of the paper’s bagging-style design: repeated trials introduce diversity in rule generation, and averaging across trials helps correct mistakes made in individual runs. The paper links this effect to the idea that correct rules from some trials can compensate for incorrect ones in others.

However, the gains do not increase indefinitely. The paper reports that performance begins to converge beyond a certain point, around $20$ ensembles. This indicates diminishing returns: once enough diverse trials have been aggregated, adding more provides only marginal improvement. In practice, this means that very large numbers of ensembles are not necessary to capture most of the benefit.

# Diminishing returns and convergence around the chosen ensemble size

The convergence around $20$ ensembles is presented as a practical compromise. The paper observes that, while performance keeps improving up to that point, the curve flattens afterward. So the chosen ensemble size is not arbitrary; it reflects the point at which additional trials no longer justify their extra cost as strongly.

This also fits the paper’s broader design goal of keeping inference efficient. Because \textsf{FeatLLM} aims to avoid repeated LLM calls at test time, the ensemble is used as a one-time or limited repeated generation strategy rather than as an expensive per-sample inference mechanism. The empirical convergence supports the idea that a moderate ensemble size can deliver robustness without unnecessary overhead.

# Trade-off in choosing the number of rules: information versus overfitting and prompt length

The paper also studies how many rules per class should be extracted. The results do not show a statistically significant difference across values, but the authors select $10$ rules per class as the most appropriate choice. The reason is a trade-off among three factors: prompt length, available information, and overfitting risk.

More rules can encode more information and potentially capture a broader set of useful conditions. But they also enlarge the input dimensionality of the downstream model, which may increase overfitting in low-shot settings. At the same time, too many rules can make the prompt longer and more difficult to manage, which is especially problematic when tabular datasets already have many features. The chosen value of $10$ is therefore a balance between expressiveness and practical constraints.

# Practical recommendations for configuring FeatLLM

The paper’s analysis suggests a simple practical lesson: \textsf{FeatLLM} works best when its configuration preserves diversity while avoiding unnecessary complexity. A moderate ensemble size, around $20$, is sufficient to capture most of the robustness benefits. Likewise, extracting about $10$ rules per class offers a reasonable balance between information content and prompt length.

More broadly, the hyperparameter study reinforces the paper’s main message about reliability in low-data regimes. Robustness comes not from making the system larger in an unrestricted way, but from structuring the reasoning process, filtering irrelevant features, and combining multiple weak but diverse rule sets. In that sense, the method’s stability is tied to both its design and its careful tuning.

12. Broader Significance, Limitations, and Future Directions

# Core contributions of FeatLLM to few-shot tabular learning

The main contribution of \textsf{FeatLLM} is to reframe how large language models can be used for tabular prediction. Instead of asking the model to directly predict the class of each sample end to end, the paper treats the LLM as a feature engineer that extracts rules from a few labeled examples and from task descriptions. These rules are then converted into binary features, and a simple downstream model learns how important each rule is for predicting each class. In this way, the LLM supplies task-specific knowledge, while the statistical model handles prediction.

The paper argues that this approach is especially valuable in few-shot settings. When only a small number of labeled examples is available, conventional tabular learners can overfit or fail to learn useful patterns. \textsf{FeatLLM} aims to address that problem by using the LLM’s prior knowledge to propose criteria that matter for the task. Empirically, the paper reports that \textsf{FeatLLM} performs strongly across 13 tabular datasets and often outperforms or matches other methods in low-shot regimes. The authors also emphasize that the model is robust across diverse domains and remains effective even on datasets with many features.

Another important contribution is practical. The method is designed to work with API-only access to LLMs, does not require fine-tuning, and reduces inference cost because the LLM is not queried for every test sample. Once the rules are extracted and the simple model is trained, prediction can proceed without passing each sample through the LLM. This makes \textsf{FeatLLM} more feasible than many earlier language-interfaced tabular approaches.

# Why using LLMs as feature engineers is a meaningful shift in perspective

A central insight of the paper is that LLMs may be more useful for tabular learning if they are used to generate criteria rather than final predictions. This is a meaningful change in perspective because it leverages what the model appears to do well: connect task descriptions, feature meanings, and few-shot examples to infer useful relationships. In the paper’s framing, the LLM is not just a classifier but a source of human-like reasoning that can propose rules such as which feature conditions correspond to a class.

This shift matters because it separates knowledge extraction from prediction. The LLM contributes knowledge in the form of symbolic or rule-like statements, and a conventional learning algorithm uses those rules to infer class likelihoods. That division of labor has two benefits. First, it reduces dependence on the LLM at inference time. Second, it creates a representation that can be handled by standard machine learning methods, which are easier to train, more efficient at inference, and more transparent than direct LLM-based prediction.

The paper also suggests that this feature-engineering view helps the model focus on more meaningful information. The prompt is structured to first encourage the LLM to infer relationships between features and the task, and then to generate rules for each class. According to the authors, this two-step reasoning can reduce attention to irrelevant columns and limit spurious correlations. So the conceptual shift is not only computational; it is also about how to organize reasoning so that the LLM’s prior knowledge becomes usable for tabular learning.

# Strengths of the framework: API-only access, no fine-tuning, lower inference cost, and scalability to wider tables

One of the most practical strengths of \textsf{FeatLLM} is that it requires only API-level access to an LLM. The paper explicitly contrasts this with methods that depend on fine-tuning or parameter-efficient adaptation such as LoRA or IA3. That distinction matters because many strong modern LLMs are accessible only through inference APIs, with no training codebase or tuning interface. \textsf{FeatLLM} is designed to operate in that setting, which broadens its potential usability.

A second strength is that the method does not require the LLM to be invoked for each test example. The LLM is used during the feature-engineering stage to generate rules, and after that, the downstream classifier makes predictions from the derived binary features. This substantially lowers inference cost compared with end-to-end language-interfaced tabular methods, where every sample must be serialized and sent through the LLM. The paper reports that the inference time of \textsf{FeatLLM} is relatively low and comparable to conventional methods, while other LLM-based baselines have much higher inference time.

The method also addresses prompt-length limitations. The authors note that tabular datasets may contain many columns, which can make direct serialization into prompts difficult or infeasible for ordinary LLM-based prediction. \textsf{FeatLLM} mitigates this through bagging and ensembling. By selecting subsets of features or instances in different trials, the method reduces prompt size and still aggregates multiple rule sets into a final prediction. This makes it more scalable to datasets with a large number of features, which is important because the paper shows that some datasets have over 100 attributes.

# Limitations of the current method: focus on low-shot settings and rule-based feature types

The authors are clear that \textsf{FeatLLM} is designed for low-shot learning. It is evaluated primarily in settings where only a small number of labeled samples are available, and the method is positioned as a solution for that regime. That means its performance and design are optimized for scenarios with limited labels, not for large-data tabular learning. The conclusion explicitly states that future work should expand the approach to datasets with a larger number of samples.

Another limitation is the current dependence on rule-based features. The method transforms LLM-generated rules into binary indicators that represent whether a sample satisfies each rule. This design is elegant and interpretable, but it is also constrained. The paper suggests that future work should explore richer feature types beyond rules. That implies the current framework may not fully exploit all forms of information the LLM could provide, especially when a task might benefit from features that are not naturally expressed as simple binary conditions.

The paper also notes that the method is still fundamentally tied to the quality of rule extraction. If the LLM produces imperfect rules, the downstream model can only work with what it receives. Bagging and ensembling are used to improve robustness, but they do not eliminate the possibility of poor rule generation. In other words, the method reduces reliance on a single rule set, but it does not guarantee that every extracted criterion is correct.

# Potential risks of relying on LLM prior knowledge

A major theme in the broader impact discussion is that LLM prior knowledge can be both useful and risky. \textsf{FeatLLM} intentionally relies on that prior knowledge to guide rule generation and improve performance in low-shot settings. However, because the prior knowledge comes from pretraining on broad text corpora, it may contain bias, misinformation, or outdated assumptions. The paper warns that this prior knowledge can directly influence predictions and may even dominate the limited labeled data available in a low-shot regime.

This creates a particular risk when the LLM’s background knowledge conflicts with the actual data. In \textsf{FeatLLM}, the generated rules are used to construct the features that drive prediction, so an incorrect assumption can enter the system early and affect the entire decision pipeline. The paper’s analysis on spurious correlations shows that the framework is relatively robust against irrelevant columns, but it also acknowledges that if closely related but misleading columns are introduced, the model can still learn spurious relationships and misunderstand causality.

Thus, the use of LLM-derived prior knowledge should not be treated as neutral. It is a source of inductive bias, and that bias may help or hurt depending on the task and the reliability of the pretrained model’s world knowledge. The method is powerful precisely because it exploits prior knowledge, but that same feature also introduces the possibility of systematic error.

# Societal bias and misinformation embedded in pretrained language models

The paper explicitly identifies societal bias and misinformation as important concerns for future work. Since \textsf{FeatLLM} uses the LLM’s prior knowledge to shape predictions, any embedded bias in that knowledge can propagate into the extracted rules and, ultimately, into the model’s output. This is not a secondary issue; it is central to the method, because the prior knowledge is not merely a background resource but part of the predictive mechanism itself.

The concern becomes stronger because the framework uses rules as a bridge between language and prediction. If the language model has learned biased associations from its pretraining data, those associations may appear as apparently reasonable decision criteria. Because the downstream model trains on these rules, biased or misleading criteria may receive weight and influence the final class likelihood. In this sense, the method can amplify problems already present in the pretrained model.

The authors suggest that analyzing these effects is important for widespread adoption. That implies a need for future work that can detect, measure, and mitigate when LLM-derived rules encode harmful assumptions. The paper does not propose a complete solution, but it makes clear that responsible use of \textsf{FeatLLM} requires awareness of these risks.

# Implications for high-stakes domains such as healthcare and finance

The paper highlights healthcare and finance as examples of real-world domains where \textsf{FeatLLM} could be particularly valuable. These are settings where labeling can be expensive or difficult, and where domain knowledge is often important for making sense of tabular data. The authors suggest that LLMs may already contain relevant background knowledge from pretraining on financial or medical text, which could be useful for few-shot tabular tasks in these domains.

At the same time, those are precisely the domains where errors and bias can be especially consequential. A system that uses LLM prior knowledge to generate predictive rules may be helpful when data are scarce, but it also requires careful scrutiny because a wrong rule or biased prior can affect decisions about health or financial risk. The paper’s broader impact section therefore treats these domains as both promising and sensitive.

This duality is important for interpreting the method. \textsf{FeatLLM} is not just a technical improvement over tabular baselines; it is a practical proposal for situations where human labeling is costly. But in high-stakes settings, the source of the model’s knowledge matters as much as its accuracy. The framework invites careful consideration of whether the LLM’s internalized knowledge is appropriate for the task and population at hand.

# Future work suggested by the authors: more data regimes, richer feature types, and improved interpretability

The paper points to several directions for future work. First, the authors want to extend \textsf{FeatLLM} beyond low-shot settings to data regimes with more samples. That would require adapting the current feature-generation strategy so that it remains useful when more training data are available and the structure of the task may be learned more directly from data.

Second, they suggest exploring feature types beyond rules. The present method converts LLM-generated criteria into binary features, which is effective for the tasks studied, but it may not be the most expressive form of feature engineering. The paper implies that richer or more varied forms of features could allow the LLM to contribute more flexibly to tabular learning.

Third, the authors emphasize interpretability as a future goal. Since the framework already produces rule-like outputs, it has a natural interpretive structure, but the paper suggests there is more to do in making the resulting features and decisions easier to understand. Improved interpretability would also help users inspect whether the rules are sensible, which would be valuable in high-stakes settings.

# Open research questions at the intersection of LLM reasoning and tabular machine learning

The paper raises several broader research questions, even if it does not answer them directly. One question is how best to guide LLM reasoning so that it extracts useful tabular criteria rather than superficial correlations. \textsf{FeatLLM} uses a structured two-step prompt to encourage causal or tendency-based reasoning before rule generation, but it is still an open problem how to make this process consistently reliable across different tasks and data distributions.

Another question concerns the balance between symbolic and statistical components. \textsf{FeatLLM} uses symbolic rules as features and then relies on a statistical model to learn their importance. This suggests a hybrid paradigm in which language models propose structure and traditional learners estimate weights. Future work may ask when such hybridization is superior to direct prediction, and how much structure should be imposed by the prompt versus learned from data.

A third question is robustness. The paper studies prompt size, rule diversity, spurious correlations, and ensemble behavior, but there remains a broader issue of how stable the extracted rules are across different LLM backbones, prompt formulations, and data regimes. Because the method depends on reasoning through an LLM, understanding variability is crucial for both scientific and practical reasons.

# Final synthesis: what FeatLLM teaches us about combining symbolic criteria with statistical learning

The most important lesson of \textsf{FeatLLM} is that tabular learning need not choose between language-model reasoning and conventional statistical prediction. The paper shows a way to combine them: let the LLM infer rules that capture task-relevant criteria, turn those rules into features, and use a lightweight model to learn how they should be weighted. This combines symbolic structure with statistical estimation in a way that is efficient, flexible, and practical in low-shot settings.

The framework also demonstrates that LLMs can be valuable even when they are not used as direct predictors. Their prior knowledge can serve as a source of feature engineering, helping to create representations that are more suitable for small-data tabular learning. At the same time, the paper makes clear that this strategy comes with limitations: the method is designed for low-shot regimes, depends on rule-based features, and inherits the risks of pretrained prior knowledge.

In that sense, \textsf{FeatLLM} is both a technical proposal and a conceptual one. It suggests that the most productive role for an LLM may not always be to answer directly, but to help define the criteria by which answers should be judged. For tabular machine learning, that is a powerful idea: use language models to propose meaningful structure, and use statistical learning to turn that structure into predictions.
