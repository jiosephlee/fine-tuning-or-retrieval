\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 2: 2. Background: Existing Approaches to Few-Shot and LLM-Based Tabular Learning

# Classical supervised methods for tabular data: logistic regression, random forests, and gradient boosting

Tabular learning traditionally relies on standard supervised models that directly map input features to labels. In the paper, the representative classical baselines include logistic regression, XGBoost, and random forests. These methods are widely used because they can work well when enough labeled data are available and when the data distribution is reasonably captured by the chosen model class. Logistic regression is a simple linear classifier that estimates class likelihood from a weighted combination of input features. Random forests aggregate many decision trees, while XGBoost builds boosted trees sequentially to improve predictive accuracy. In the setting of tabular data, these models provide strong, established reference points because they require no special language processing or external pretraining.

The paper treats these methods as the conventional starting point for tabular prediction. They operate directly on the structured features and do not require the data to be translated into text. This makes them computationally simpler than LLM-based methods at inference time, and they are often effective when enough labeled examples are available. However, the paper’s central motivation is that these conventional models become less reliable when labels are scarce. That low-shot regime is precisely where the need for additional prior knowledge becomes most important.

# Why conventional tabular models struggle in low-shot settings

The paper emphasizes that learning from only a few labeled examples creates a substantial risk of capturing spurious correlations rather than the true relationships between features and labels. When the number of training examples is small, even a flexible model may fit accidental patterns that do not generalize. This is a general challenge in low-shot learning, and the paper frames it as a major obstacle for tabular tasks.

Another issue is that tabular data are heterogeneous. Different datasets may contain numerical, categorical, and mixed-type variables, and the importance of features can vary widely from task to task. In low-shot conditions, conventional supervised learners do not have enough evidence to reliably determine which signals are truly meaningful. As a result, even well-established methods such as logistic regression, random forests, and gradient boosting can underperform when the labeled set is very small. This is why the paper turns to methods that can incorporate additional information beyond the few labeled samples themselves.

# Semi-supervised and self-supervised tabular learning methods

To address the limitations of low-shot supervised learning, earlier work has explored methods that use unlabeled data or auxiliary learning objectives. The paper describes semi-supervised approaches that exploit large quantities of unlabeled tabular data together with augmentation or other mechanisms to improve learning. The basic idea is to use unlabeled examples to provide structure that the labeled samples alone cannot supply. This can be especially appealing in tabular domains, where labels may be costly but raw records may be easier to collect.

The paper also discusses self-supervised methods that train models on pretext tasks rather than directly on labels. These approaches aim to learn representations that transfer better to downstream classification tasks. The text mentions masking or corrupting part of the data and then reconstructing it, as well as contrastive learning with data augmentation. Such methods are intended to force the model to learn useful patterns in the input structure rather than memorizing labels. However, the paper argues that tabular data are difficult for these strategies because the heterogeneous nature of tables makes it hard to define universally effective augmentations. As a result, the paper notes that these strategies have not been notably effective in few-shot settings.

# Representation learning for tabular data: masking, reconstruction, and contrastive learning

A major line of tabular representation learning attempts to improve downstream performance by learning useful internal representations before supervised training. In the paper’s account, this includes objectives based on masking or corrupting portions of the input and reconstructing them. The intuition is that if a model can recover missing information, it must learn latent structure in the data. Another family of methods uses contrastive learning, where the model is trained to distinguish between related and unrelated examples under data augmentations. In both cases, the goal is to acquire representations that are more transferable and more robust than those learned from scarce labels alone.

The paper places methods such as TabNet-style reconstruction and SCARF-style contrastive learning in this category. These methods can be helpful when large amounts of unlabeled data are available, but the paper stresses that they depend on design choices that may not generalize well across tables. Because tabular datasets differ so much in feature types and semantics, an augmentation that helps one dataset may be unhelpful or even harmful for another. This limits the reliability of these techniques in the low-shot regime the paper focuses on.

# Transfer learning across tabular datasets

Another strategy reviewed in the paper is transfer learning across multiple tabular datasets. Rather than learning only from the target dataset, these methods train on a broader collection of tabular tasks and then adapt to the new task. The motivation is that tabular domains may share useful patterns at a higher level, so a model exposed to diverse source tasks can develop generalizable knowledge that improves performance on a new target table.

The paper cites methods such as TransTab and related approaches in this category. TransTab, for instance, learns semantic relationships between columns using column names, not just values. The paper’s discussion suggests that this broader exposure can support zero-shot or few-shot inference on new tasks. In the same spirit, transfer learning methods seek to reduce dependence on large labeled sets in the target domain. Still, these approaches usually require a pretraining stage over many tabular datasets, which is a different practical setting from directly using a general-purpose LLM through an API.

# TabPFN and synthetic pretraining for few-shot tabular inference

The paper also discusses TabPFN as a notable few-shot tabular method. TabPFN uses synthetic data generation with diverse distributions to pretrain a Bayesian neural network. After pretraining, inference is carried out without further training by providing the model with both training and test samples from the target task. The point of this design is to bake strong inductive bias into the model ahead of time, so that it can perform well when only a few labeled examples are available.

In the paper’s framing, TabPFN is an example of how prior knowledge can improve few-shot tabular performance without relying on the usual supervised learning pipeline from scratch. By training on many synthetic distributions, the method aims to become broadly useful across tasks. The paper notes that this prior knowledge helped TabPFN outperform traditional tree-based methods and become beneficial in few-shot settings. Even so, it is conceptually different from the approach taken by FeatLLM, which does not pretrain a tabular predictor but instead uses an LLM to engineer features from the few-shot examples.

# Language-interfaced tabular learning: serializing rows and prompting LLMs

A newer direction, which the paper calls language-interfaced tabular learning, uses large language models as the basis for prediction. The central idea is to convert structured table rows into natural language text and then provide that text as part of an LLM prompt. The prompt typically includes the feature names, feature descriptions, task description, and a few labeled examples. The table is serialized into a textual form so that the LLM can process it in the same way it handles other language tasks.

The paper points out that this design lets the LLM infer relationships between features and labels from the prompt itself. Because LLMs are pretrained on very large and diverse text corpora, they may bring in external prior knowledge relevant to the task. For tabular learning, that prior knowledge can be especially valuable when labels are scarce. The paper therefore presents language-interfaced tabular learning as a promising way to inject broad knowledge into structured prediction problems.

# In-context learning versus parameter-efficient fine-tuning for tabular tasks

The paper distinguishes two main ways of using LLMs for tabular tasks. The first is in-context learning, in which the prompt contains a task description and a few example rows, but the model itself is not updated. The second is parameter-efficient fine-tuning, where the LLM is adapted using techniques such as LoRA or IA3. In-context learning uses the examples to steer the model’s behavior at inference time, while parameter-efficient tuning changes the model weights in a limited way to better fit the task.

The paper presents both as part of the recent trend in LLM-based tabular learning. In-context learning is attractive because it avoids training, while tuning can improve accuracy by adapting the model to the task. Yet the paper also highlights a key tradeoff: methods that rely on tuning are not usable when the LLM is accessible only through an inference API, and methods that rely only on in-context learning still require the LLM to be queried for each sample. These distinctions become central to understanding why FeatLLM is designed the way it is.

# Representative methods: TabLLM, TABLET, and related approaches

The paper identifies TabLLM, TABLET, and related systems as representative language-based tabular learning methods. TabLLM uses parameter-efficient tuning to adapt an LLM to tabular prediction. TABLET, by contrast, augments the prompt with additional hints from an external classifier, including rule sets and prototypes, to improve inference quality. Other approaches use in-context learning by adding few-shot demonstrations directly to the prompt without further training.

Across these methods, the common pattern is that the table is translated into language and then fed through the LLM to produce the prediction. The paper views this as a black-box style of end-to-end inference. The LLM is responsible for producing the final answer for each sample, rather than just extracting reusable structure from the data. This design has been useful, but the paper argues that it also creates practical limitations that become especially severe in real-world deployment.

# Limitations of prior LLM-based methods: per-sample inference cost, fine-tuning dependence, and prompt-length constraints

The paper is explicit about three main limitations of prior LLM-based tabular methods. First, they require at least one LLM inference per sample at test time. That makes inference computationally expensive, particularly when many samples must be scored. Second, many methods depend on fine-tuning or parameter-efficient training to obtain high performance, which is not always possible when only API-level access to an LLM is available. This matters because some of the strongest recent LLMs do not expose training code or tuning interfaces. Third, these methods are vulnerable to prompt-length limitations. As the number of features in a table grows, the serialized text can become too long for the available context window, or performance may degrade even before the limit is reached.

The paper argues that these constraints are not minor engineering inconveniences but fundamental barriers to practical use. If every prediction must pass through the LLM, inference cost remains high. If tuning is required, the method cannot be used with API-only models. If the prompt becomes too long, then large tables become difficult or impossible to handle. These limitations motivate the paper’s shift away from using the LLM as the end-to-end predictor.

# How FeatLLM differs conceptually from end-to-end LLM prediction

The paper’s central conceptual move is to stop treating the LLM as the final predictor and instead use it as a feature engineer. Rather than asking the LLM to output the class directly for every sample, FeatLLM asks it to infer the rules or criteria that distinguish classes. Those rules are then converted into binary features that can be reused by a simpler downstream model. In this way, the LLM contributes its prior knowledge and reasoning ability once during feature generation, while the downstream learner handles prediction.

This is a significant departure from end-to-end language-based tabular prediction. The paper’s approach reduces inference cost because the LLM does not need to be queried for every new sample. It also avoids the need for fine-tuning, since the framework relies on in-context learning to extract rules. Finally, by using bagging and repeated rule generation, the method can better handle long tables and prompt-size limitations. The background literature therefore sets up the paper’s main claim: that LLMs are not only useful as predictors, but can also act as automatic feature engineers for few-shot tabular learning.
