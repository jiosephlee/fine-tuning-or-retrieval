\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Is FeatLLM really learning causal rules, or only plausible correlational heuristics?
Question:
Several parts of the paper talk about the LLM inferring "causal relationship or tendency" between features and the task, and the examples make it sound like the model is discovering meaningful criteria such as medical risk factors.

But the actual training signal seems purely predictive: a few labeled examples, then rules, then a linear classifier. There is no intervention data or explicit causal identification procedure.

So should these extracted rules be interpreted as causal statements, or just as semantically plausible heuristics that often correlate with the label? I worry that using words like "causal relationship" could be misleading, especially in domains like healthcare.

How should I read this claim carefully?
Answer:
You should read that claim as **heuristic, not causal in the formal statistical sense**.

Nothing in FeatLLM performs causal identification: there are no interventions, no counterfactuals, no structural causal model, and no attempt to estimate a treatment effect or isolate a causal mechanism. The method is still entirely supervised and predictive. The LLM is prompted to produce rules from a few labeled examples, and then those rules are turned into binary features for a downstream linear model trained with cross-entropy. So the actual learning objective is “find features that help predict the label,” not “discover the true cause of the label.”

The paper’s wording about the LLM inferring a “causal relationship or tendency” is best understood as a **prompting heuristic**. In context, they are asking the LLM, in the first reasoning step, to use its prior knowledge and common sense to focus on features that are plausibly related to the task and to avoid obviously irrelevant columns. This is reinforced by their own discussion: they say the reasoning instruction is meant to “prevent the model from identifying spurious correlations in irrelevant columns,” and in the spurious-correlation experiment they explicitly note that FeatLLM can still “learn spurious correlations and misunderstand causal relationships” when closely related but irrelevant columns are added. That is a very important caveat: the authors themselves are not claiming causal discovery in the rigorous sense.

So the safest interpretation is:

The extracted rules are **semantically plausible, predictive heuristics** that may coincide with real-world causal factors, especially in domains where the LLM’s prior knowledge is strong, but they should not be treated as validated causal statements unless you independently verify them with causal methods. In healthcare, for example, a rule like “older age implies higher risk” may reflect a genuine risk factor and be useful for prediction, but FeatLLM does not establish that age is causal in the target data distribution; it merely exploits a prior association that helps classification.

In other words, the paper is about **feature engineering for prediction using LLM priors**, not causal inference. The term “causal” in the prompt is doing pragmatic work: it nudges the model toward more meaningful rules. It should not be overread as a guarantee that the extracted rules are causally correct.
