\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Why is FeatLLM strong on synthetic datasets if its main advantage is supposed to be prior knowledge?
Question:
The paper emphasizes that LLM prior knowledge helps feature engineering, especially on real-world domains like healthcare and finance. But in the experiments they also test on synthetic datasets such as sequence-type and solution-mix, which by construction should not be memorized from pretraining.

If FeatLLM still performs well there, what is the source of the gain? Is it because the LLM contributes general reasoning ability rather than domain knowledge? For example, maybe it can infer arithmetic or compositional patterns from the prompt even without prior exposure to the exact dataset.

I'm asking because this seems important for understanding what the method is actually exploiting: memorized world knowledge, abstract reasoning, or just a useful way to generate nonlinear feature crosses from a few examples.
Answer:
Yes — in this paper, strong performance on the synthetic datasets is not evidence of memorized dataset-specific knowledge. The paper is actually quite explicit that \textsf{FeatLLM} uses the LLM in two different ways: first, to bring in prior knowledge or common-sense causal intuition, and second, to reason over the few-shot examples and extract rules. On synthetic tasks, the first source is mostly irrelevant, but the second one can still be very useful.

The key point is that \textsf{FeatLLM} is not making direct predictions from memorized facts. It is using the LLM as a rule generator. In Section 4, the authors say the prompt is split into two reasoning steps: Step 1 asks the model to infer the general relationship between features and the task “without referring to example demonstrations,” relying on “general knowledge or common sense,” and Step 2 uses the few examples to deduce class-specific rules. For synthetic datasets like sequence-type and solution-mix, there may be little or no prior world knowledge to exploit, but the model can still do the second step: it can infer the pattern from the demonstrations and translate it into a rule.

So the gain on synthetic data is best understood as coming from the LLM’s in-context reasoning and pattern extraction, not from memorized facts. The synthetic tasks are still structured enough that a language model can often infer their compositional or arithmetic form from a small number of examples, then turn that into candidate features such as “sequence is arithmetic” or “mixture concentration exceeds $0.5$.” Once those binary rule features are created, the downstream linear model only has to learn how to weight them. That is a much easier learning problem than learning the original tabular decision boundary from scratch.

This also fits the ablation results in the paper. The authors report that removing the reasoning instruction hurts performance, especially in the low-shot setting, which suggests that the useful signal is not just domain priors but the way the LLM focuses on relevant features and extracts rules. Likewise, the ensemble and bagging help by making the rule extraction more robust, so even if some generated rules are wrong, the final model can average over multiple weak rule sets.

So the short answer is: on synthetic datasets, \textsf{FeatLLM} is benefiting mainly from the LLM’s abstract reasoning and rule-induction ability, together with the fact that the downstream classifier is learning over engineered binary features. The “prior knowledge” story is important for real-world domains like healthcare and finance, but it is not the only mechanism behind the method’s success. In that sense, the synthetic experiments are actually useful because they show the method is not merely memorizing domain facts; it can also extract useful nonlinear structure from a few examples.
