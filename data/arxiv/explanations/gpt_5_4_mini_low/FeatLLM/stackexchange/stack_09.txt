\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### What exactly is being optimized during training if the rule features are binary and fixed?
Question:
After rule extraction and parsing, the paper says the downstream model is just a simple linear model over the generated binary features, trained with cross-entropy.

I want to make sure I understand the training dynamics correctly. Since the binary rule features are fixed after the LLM step, the only trainable parameters are the weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ for each class, right?

If that is the case, then most of the expressive power must come from the LLM-generated feature map rather than the learned classifier. Is it fair to think of FeatLLM as doing representation learning through prompting, followed by a shallow supervised learner? Also, since the features are binary and few-shot data are scarce, why train for 200 epochs instead of using a closed-form or convex solver with regularization?
Answer:
Yes — once the rules have been generated and parsed, the downstream learner is just a shallow supervised model. In the paper’s notation, the LLM produces the binary feature vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$ for class $k$, and the only trainable parameters are the weight vectors $\mathbf{w}_k$ used in the linear score
$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i.
$$
So during training, FeatLLM is not learning new features at this stage; it is learning how to weight the extracted rule-features so as to minimize cross-entropy. The projection $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$ is important because it enforces non-negative contributions, which matches the paper’s interpretation that a rule should not reduce the likelihood of a class.

So your intuition is right: most of the expressive power comes from the LLM-generated feature map, not from the classifier. The learned linear layer is mainly doing two things: reweighting rules by importance and filtering out weak or noisy rules. This is also consistent with the ablation in the paper: removing weight tuning, i.e. replacing learned weights by simply summing the binary features, hurts performance. That suggests the rule generation step provides the main semantic lift, while supervised tuning still matters for selecting which rules actually help on the few labeled examples.

I would phrase FeatLLM as a two-stage pipeline: first, representation construction by prompting the LLM to engineer rule-based features; second, lightweight supervised calibration with a linear model. It is not representation learning in the usual gradient-based sense, because the feature map itself is fixed after the LLM step. It is more like LLM-assisted feature engineering followed by convex-like linear classification.

As for why train for 200 epochs instead of using a closed-form solver: there is no closed-form solution here. A closed form exists for ordinary least squares, but not for multiclass cross-entropy with softmax, and certainly not with the non-negativity constraint $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$. So an iterative optimizer such as Adam is the standard choice. The paper also says they use $k$-fold cross-validation for optimal epoch selection, so the 200 epochs is just the training budget, not necessarily the number of epochs used in every run.

In short: the LLM does the hard part by producing useful binary rules; the learned model only estimates their importance under cross-entropy, which is why FeatLLM is best thought of as prompt-based feature engineering plus a shallow supervised learner.
