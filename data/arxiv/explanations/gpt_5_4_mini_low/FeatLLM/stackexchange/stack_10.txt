\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Why do the authors measure rule quality using AUROC between each generated feature and the target label?
Question:
In the analysis section, the paper evaluates the "informativeness" of generated rule features by computing the average AUROC between each individual binary feature and the target label, and then counting how many features have AUROC above $0.5$.

I am not sure this is the right way to assess rule quality.

A rule could be useful only in combination with other rules, yet have weak marginal AUROC on its own. Also, AUROC above $0.5$ just means better than random ranking, which seems like a low bar.

Why is this considered evidence that the features are high quality? Wouldn't mutual information, calibration, sparsity, or contribution to the final classifier be more direct measures?

I'm trying to understand how much I should conclude from Table 5.
Answer:
The short answer is that the paper is not using AUROC as a full theory of “rule quality”; it is using it as a simple sanity check for whether the generated binary rules carry *any* label signal on their own.

That choice makes sense in the context of their method. Each generated rule is turned into a binary feature, and the downstream model is a linear model over those features. So before any tuning, a rule with AUROC above $0.5$ is one whose positive/negative outcomes are correlated with the target label in the right direction. Since AUROC is threshold-free, it is a convenient way to ask, “If I look at this rule alone, does it tend to fire more often on positive examples than on negative ones?” For a binary feature, that is essentially what AUROC measures: $0.5$ means no ranking power, above $0.5$ means some positive association, below $0.5$ means the rule is inversely related to the label.

You are right that this is not sufficient to prove a rule is useful in combination with others. The paper is not claiming otherwise. In fact, their actual prediction pipeline already addresses that issue by learning weights for the rules in a linear model and then ensembling across bagged trials. So a rule can be weak marginally but still get a useful weight once the model sees it alongside other features. The AUROC analysis is only meant to show that, on average, the LLM is not generating random rules; many of them have some direct alignment with the target. Then the “After tuning” column shows that the downstream model further filters out rules that do not help prediction.

So Table 5 should be read as evidence for *informativeness*, not completeness. It supports the claim that the generated rules are often better than random and that the learned linear layer can suppress weak ones. It does not establish that every rule is individually strong, nor that AUROC is the best possible metric for semantic rule quality. Metrics like mutual information or contribution to the final classifier would indeed be more direct in some senses, but they are also less simple to interpret and would still not fully capture interaction effects between rules. Because the paper’s goal is to show that the LLM is producing a useful feature set for a downstream learner, a marginal ranking measure is a reasonable first diagnostic.

So the right conclusion from Table 5 is modest: the generated rules usually contain real signal, and the model’s tuning step removes some of the weaker ones. It is evidence that the rule generation stage is not arbitrary, but not a definitive measure of rule quality in a broader sense.
