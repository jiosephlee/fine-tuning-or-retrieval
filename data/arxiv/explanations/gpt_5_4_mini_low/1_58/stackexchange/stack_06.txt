\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why does the paper say post-training quantization is sub-optimal compared to training BitNet b1.58 from scratch?
Question:
The introduction says post-training quantization is widely used but sub-optimal, while BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with ternary weights. I understand that quantizing after training can hurt performance, but I want to pin down what is fundamentally different here.

Is the main advantage that the network learns to organize its internal representations around the quantization constraint during optimization? Or is there a more specific reason related to transformers, such as activation statistics, outlier channels, or the inability of a pretrained FP16 optimum to map well into $\{-1,0,1\}$? In other words, why should we expect a model trained natively with ternary weights to match FP16, while a post-training quantized FP16 model often does not?
Answer:
The short version is that the paper is not claiming that a pretrained FP16 model can simply be “rounded down” to ternary weights and keep the same quality. Rather, it is arguing that if you want a model whose weights are truly $\{-1,0,1\}$, then the model has to be optimized under that constraint from the start, so the whole network learns a parameterization that is naturally representable in that low-precision space.

That distinction matters because post-training quantization and native quantization solve different optimization problems. A full-precision LLM is trained to find a good optimum in a continuous parameter space. If you later project those weights onto $\{-1,0,1\}$, you are taking a solution that was never optimized for that discrete geometry and snapping it to a very coarse grid. Even if the FP16 model is excellent, it may sit in a region where many weights are “important” in the sense that small changes matter; after rounding, those small changes can become large functional errors. So the issue is not just that quantization injects noise, but that it changes the hypothesis class after training.

In BitNet b1.58, by contrast, the quantization is part of the forward computation during training, with an absmean scaling and rounding to $\{-1,0,1\}$. That means the optimizer sees the quantized model, not the FP16 proxy. The weights can therefore adapt to the limited alphabet, and the rest of the network can reorganize around those constraints. This is the key intuition behind why training from scratch can work much better than post-training quantization: the model learns distributed representations that are “easy” to express with ternary weights rather than trying to compress an already-learned FP16 representation.

There is also a more specific point in the paper: the authors say that adding the zero value gives “explicit support for feature filtering.” That is, ternary weights are not just a crude approximation to binary weights; the zero weight lets the model suppress some connections entirely, which increases modeling capacity relative to pure $\{-1,1\}$. So the ternary constraint is not merely a compression trick after the fact, but part of the inductive bias of the architecture.

For transformers, this is especially relevant because the layers are highly coupled: if one projection matrix changes scale or sign patterns, it affects attention and feed-forward blocks downstream. Post-training quantization has to preserve those learned interactions without letting the model re-balance itself. Training natively with low-bit weights lets the whole system co-adapt, including the activation statistics. The paper also keeps activations at 8 bits, which helps preserve enough dynamic range so that the difficult part is mainly the weights, not both weights and activations being aggressively quantized at once.

So the fundamental reason is optimization mismatch, not just a hardware trick. A pretrained FP16 optimum is not generally close, in function space, to an optimum constrained to ternary weights. BitNet b1.58 works because it is trained directly in that constrained space, letting the model learn a representation that is compatible with $\{-1,0,1\}$ weights from the beginning. The paper’s claim that it can match FP16 is therefore an empirical statement about this training recipe, not a guarantee that arbitrary FP16 models can be post-quantized to the same level.
