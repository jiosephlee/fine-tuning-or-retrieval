\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why should BitNet b1.58 become relatively more efficient as model size increases?
Question:
The paper repeatedly says that the speedup, memory savings, and energy benefits of BitNet b1.58 increase as the model size scales. I would like help understanding the mechanism.

Is the idea that as transformers get larger, the fraction of total computation and memory due to linear layers becomes larger relative to other components like embeddings, normalization, and attention bookkeeping, so compressing the linear layers pays off more? Or is there something special about the 2-bit kernel and GPU utilization that makes larger matrices disproportionately better for the ternary model?
Answer:
The paper’s explanation is mostly the first one: the benefit grows because the parts of a Transformer that BitNet accelerates and compresses, especially the \emph{nn.Linear} layers, take up a larger share of the total cost as the model gets bigger.

In a Transformer LLM, most of the parameter count and most of the arithmetic in the forward pass live in the linear projections inside attention and MLP blocks. BitNet b1.58 replaces those FP16/BF16 linear layers with ternary weights and an integer-friendly kernel, so the operations that dominate the model become much cheaper. As the model size increases, these linear layers scale up strongly, so the absolute savings from replacing them also scale up. That is why the paper says the speedup increases with model size and explicitly states, “This is because the time cost for \emph{nn.Linear} grows with the model size.”

Your intuition about the “fixed overhead” components is also correct and is part of the same story. Things like embeddings, normalization, and other non-linear bookkeeping do not shrink in absolute cost, but they become relatively less important in larger models. So even if BitNet does not accelerate those parts much, their contribution to the end-to-end runtime or energy becomes a smaller fraction of the total as the model grows. The paper makes exactly this point for energy: “the percentage of \emph{nn.Linear} grows with the model size, while the cost from other components is smaller for larger models.”

For memory, the same logic applies. The embedding layer remains full precision, so its relative share of the total memory footprint is larger in smaller models and smaller in larger ones. Once the model grows, more of the memory is tied up in the weight matrices that BitNet compresses, so the reduction in memory footprint becomes more pronounced in absolute terms.

The paper does \emph{not} claim that the scaling advantage is mainly because the ternary kernel becomes magically more efficient only at large matrix sizes, although better hardware utilization for large GEMMs is certainly relevant in practice. What the paper actually says is more modest: the results were measured with a 2-bit kernel, and “there is still room for optimization.” So the observed scaling trend is primarily attributed to the changing composition of the model, not to some special property of the kernel that only appears at large size.

So the short answer is: yes, the key mechanism is that larger Transformers spend proportionally more of their compute, memory, and energy in linear layers, and BitNet b1.58 is designed to make those layers much cheaper. The larger the model, the more of the total bill comes from the part BitNet optimizes, and the more impressive the end-to-end savings become.
