\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### If BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit activations, why does the paper still describe it as a '1-bit LLM'?
Question:
The paper repeatedly frames BitNet b1.58 as part of the era of "1-bit LLMs," but in the architecture section it says the model is trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations.

This makes me wonder what part of the system is actually 1-bit-like. Since matrix multiplication depends on both weights and activations, wouldn't 8-bit activations still be a significant source of compute and memory cost? Is the idea that the weights dominate the storage and much of the inference bottleneck, so quantizing them is enough to justify the label? Or is there some special arithmetic trick where ternary weights make the multiplication almost disappear even when activations are 8-bit integers?
Answer:
The paper is using “1-bit LLM” to refer primarily to the **weights**, not to every number in the system. In BitNet b1.58, “every single parameter (or weight) of the LLM is ternary $\{-1,0,1\}$,” which is why the authors call it a 1-bit-family model even though the activations are kept at $8$ bits. The “1.58 bits” name comes from the ternary weight alphabet: three states can be encoded with about $\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits per weight.

So the key point is that the model is not “1-bit” in the sense that *all* arithmetic is 1-bit. It is “1-bit” in the sense that the **model parameters, which dominate storage and bandwidth, are extremely low precision**. That is also why the paper emphasizes memory footprint and weight transfer from DRAM to SRAM as major inference costs. Since the weights are ternary, they occupy far less memory than FP16 weights, and loading them is much cheaper.

The $8$-bit activations do still matter, especially for the KV cache and for some remaining runtime cost, and the paper is explicit about that. In fact, in the discussion the authors say that reducing activations from $16$ bits to $8$ bits helps with long-sequence inference by allowing the context length to be doubled under the same resources. So the model is not claiming that activations are negligible; rather, it is claiming that **the biggest and most expensive part of the model, the weights, has been pushed into a ternary regime**.

As for computation, the special trick is exactly the ternary weight set. If a weight is $-1$, $0$, or $1$, then the matrix multiplication no longer needs a full general multiply for that term: multiplying by $1$ is copying, by $-1$ is sign flip, and by $0$ is skipping the term entirely. That is why the paper says the matrix multiplication “only involves integer addition” and “requires almost no multiplication operations.” The activations can still be $8$-bit integers, but the expensive multiply is largely removed because the weights have such a restricted form.

So the short answer is: **the “1-bit LLM” label refers to the ternary weights and the resulting multiplication-free-ish linear layers, not to the activations.** The activations are still quantized to $8$ bits for efficiency and sequence-length reasons, but the paper’s main claim is that the dominant model cost comes from weights, and those are what have been driven into the 1-bit/$1.58$-bit regime.
