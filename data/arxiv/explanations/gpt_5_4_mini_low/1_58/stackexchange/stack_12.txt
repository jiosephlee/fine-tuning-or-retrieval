\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What is the paper really claiming by saying BitNet b1.58 defines a 'new scaling law'?
Question:
One of the strongest claims in the abstract is that the 1.58-bit LLM defines a new scaling law and recipe for training future generations of LLMs. But in the body, what I mostly see are empirical comparisons of perplexity, latency, memory, and energy across model sizes.

Is the term "scaling law" being used informally here to mean a new tradeoff curve between quality and inference cost? Or are the authors claiming something closer to classical neural scaling laws, where loss follows a predictable power-law with model size, data, or compute?

I want to understand whether this is a rigorous scaling-law claim or more of a systems-performance framing.
Answer:
In this paper, “new scaling law” is being used much more loosely than in the classical neural scaling-law literature.

A classical scaling law usually means an empirical relation of the form “loss decreases predictably as a power law in model size, data size, or compute,” often written as something like $L(N) \approx aN^{-\alpha} + b$, with exponents fit from experiments. That kind of claim is about how quality scales smoothly and predictably with scale, and it is usually backed by fitted curves over a range of sizes.

That is not really what the paper establishes. What the authors actually show is that BitNet b1.58 changes the cost-performance tradeoff: for a given level of perplexity or zero-shot accuracy, it uses less latency, memory, throughput, and energy than an FP16 baseline. Their “scaling law” language is tied to the observation that as model size increases, the efficiency advantage of the ternary model becomes larger, and they summarize this with equivalences like “13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 3B FP16,” “30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16,” and so on. So the claim is essentially that there is a new Pareto frontier between model capability and inference cost.

The strongest evidence in the paper is therefore systems-oriented and empirical, not a formal scaling-law fit. They compare perplexity, downstream accuracy, latency, memory, throughput, and energy across model sizes, and they argue that BitNet b1.58 can “match” FP16 performance starting around 3B while being much cheaper to run. That is a useful and potentially important result, but it is not the same as proving a new universal scaling law in the classical sense.

So the short answer is: yes, they are using “scaling law” somewhat informally, to mean a better scaling relationship between performance and inference cost, not a rigorously fitted law of loss versus parameters/data/compute. The paper’s actual contribution is a new efficiency frontier, plus an empirical recipe showing that ternary-weight LLMs can stay competitive at scale.
