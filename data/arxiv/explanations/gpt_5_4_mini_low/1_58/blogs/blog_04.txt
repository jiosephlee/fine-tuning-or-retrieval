\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# Inside the Design of BitNet b1.58: How You Train a Ternary Transformer

Large language models are usually built from two expensive ingredients: big matrices of 16-bit floating-point weights, and lots of matrix multiplication. BitNet b1.58 takes a very different path. Instead of training a standard Transformer and then compressing it later, it trains the model from scratch with ternary weights: each weight is constrained to one of three values, $-1$, $0$, or $1$.

That design choice is what makes BitNet b1.58 interesting. It is not just a smaller version of a normal LLM. It is a new model family built to be efficient from the start, while still aiming to match the performance of full-precision baselines.

## What “1.58-bit” actually means

At first glance, “1-bit LLM” sounds like a model whose parameters are simply either on or off. BitNet b1.58 adds one more state, $0$, so each weight can be negative, zero, or positive. Because ternary values carry more information than binary ones, the paper refers to this as 1.58 bits per weight.

That extra zero matters. It gives the model an explicit way to filter features, not just flip them positive or negative. According to the paper, this improves modeling capability compared with the original 1-bit BitNet design.

The key point is that the model is trained directly with these low-bit weights. This is different from post-training quantization, where a full-precision model is trained first and only compressed afterward. The paper argues that post-training quantization is still useful, but it is ultimately sub-optimal compared with a model architecture designed for low-bit operation from the ground up.

## The core trick: ternary weight quantization

BitNet b1.58 uses a quantization rule called absmean quantization. The idea is simple:

1. Compute the average absolute value of the weight matrix.
2. Use that scale to normalize the weights.
3. Round each normalized value to the nearest value in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

In the paper, this is written as:

$$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
$$

where

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
$$

The function $\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b)$ rounds $x$ and then clamps it to the interval $[a, b]$.

You can think of this as a way of keeping the weights centered around a learned scale, while forcing the final values into a tiny discrete set. That makes the model much easier to execute efficiently, because the weight matrix no longer needs full floating-point multiply-accumulate operations.

## 8-bit activations, not 1-bit activations

The weights are ternary, but the activations are not. BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit activations.

This is an important design choice. Making weights extremely low precision is already a big win for memory and compute, but keeping activations at 8 bits avoids some of the instability and accuracy loss that would come from making everything too aggressive too early.

The paper also makes one practical simplification: activations are scaled to the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token, rather than using a zero-point quantization scheme. That removes extra implementation complexity and makes system-level optimization easier, while having negligible effect on performance in the authors’ experiments.

So the recipe is not “everything is as low-bit as possible.” It is more balanced than that: ternary weights for efficiency, 8-bit activations for practicality.

## Why this is not just another quantized LLM

The distinction between BitNet b1.58 and post-training quantization is worth emphasizing.

Post-training quantization usually starts with a model trained in FP16 or BF16. After training, its weights and sometimes activations are compressed to lower precision. This can help with inference cost, but the model was never optimized for that low-bit regime during training.

BitNet b1.58 is different. It is trained from scratch as a low-bit model. That means the architecture, quantization function, and training process are all aligned with the final representation. The paper’s claim is that this leads to better performance and better efficiency than simply compressing a standard model after the fact.

## A familiar Transformer backbone, but with BitLinear layers

To make the model easier to adopt, the authors keep the architecture close to what the open-source community already uses. Specifically, BitNet b1.58 follows a LLaMA-like design.

It uses:

- RMSNorm
- SwiGLU
- rotary embeddings
- no biases

The biggest structural change is that it replaces standard $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ layers with $\mathrm{BitLinear}$ layers.

This is a practical choice. LLaMA-style components are already well supported in libraries and inference stacks such as Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp. By sticking close to that ecosystem, BitNet b1.58 is easier to integrate into existing tooling rather than requiring a completely new software stack.

## Why the design matters for inference

The benefit of ternary weights is not just smaller storage. It also changes how inference is computed.

In a standard Transformer, matrix multiplication is dominated by floating-point multiplications and additions. In BitNet-style models, the paper emphasizes that matrix multiplication can be implemented with almost no multiplication operations, relying heavily on integer addition. That is a major energy and latency advantage, especially at scale.

The paper also points out a second bottleneck in inference: moving weights from DRAM into on-chip memory. Since 1.58-bit weights take much less space, the memory footprint and bandwidth demand are much lower. That can improve throughput and reduce latency, even before you consider the arithmetic savings.

## Why the LLaMA-style choice is important for adoption

A model can be technically clever and still hard to use. The authors clearly try to avoid that problem.

By matching the overall design pattern of LLaMA, BitNet b1.58 can fit more naturally into existing open-source infrastructure. That matters because deployment is often the real barrier to adoption, not model quality alone.

The paper’s goal is not to invent an exotic architecture that only works in a custom research environment. It is to define a low-bit model that can plug into the tools people already use.

## What the paper shows in practice

The results are a central part of the story. The authors compare BitNet b1.58 against a reproduced FP16 LLaMA-style baseline, using the same training setup and 100 billion training tokens on RedPajama.

Across perplexity and zero-shot benchmarks like ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ, BitNet b1.58 narrows the gap as model size increases. The paper reports that it can match the full-precision baseline starting at 3B parameters.

At the same time, it is significantly cheaper to run. For example, the 3B BitNet b1.58 model is reported to be 2.71 times faster and use 3.55 times less GPU memory than the FP16 baseline. Larger models show even stronger efficiency gains.

## The bigger idea: design for low-bit from the start

The main lesson from BitNet b1.58 is not just that ternary weights are possible. It is that model architecture, quantization, and system design need to be aligned.

If you want the efficiency benefits of low-bit inference, it is often better to train a model to live in that regime than to compress a full-precision model afterward. BitNet b1.58 is a concrete example of that philosophy.

It keeps the Transformer familiar, uses a ternary weight representation, keeps activations at 8 bits, and leans on LLaMA-like components so it can fit into today’s open-source ecosystem. That combination is what makes the model both technically interesting and practically relevant.

## Where this points next

The paper also suggests where this direction could go next: mixture-of-experts models, longer context lengths, edge and mobile deployment, and specialized hardware for 1-bit LLMs.

For now, the most important takeaway is simple. BitNet b1.58 shows that low-bit LLMs do not have to be a post-processing trick. They can be an architectural choice, trained end to end, with performance that starts to look competitive with standard FP16 models.
