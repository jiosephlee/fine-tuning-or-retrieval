\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# What Is a 1.58-Bit LLM? A Plain-English Guide to BitNet b1.58

Large language models are usually described in terms of parameters, tokens, and benchmark scores. But there is another important axis: how many bits it takes to store and compute those parameters.

In the paper *The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits*, the authors introduce **BitNet b1.58**, a language model whose weights are restricted to just three values: $-1$, $0$, and $1$. That sounds almost too simple to work. But the paper shows that, at the 3B scale and above, this ternary setup can match a full-precision FP16 Transformer on perplexity and end-task performance, while being much cheaper to run.

## Why “1.58 bits”?

The name comes from information content. A model with three possible values per weight needs $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits to represent each weight. That is why the model is called **1.58-bit** rather than 1-bit.

This is also the key difference from earlier 1-bit-style approaches. A pure binary model only has two choices, typically $-1$ and $1$. BitNet b1.58 adds a third option: $0$.

That extra value matters more than it might seem.

## Why the extra zero helps

The paper argues that the $0$ weight gives the model **feature filtering** ability. In other words, the model can choose not to pass some signals through at all. This makes the representation more expressive than a simple binary weight system.

That extra flexibility is one reason BitNet b1.58 can close the gap with full-precision models. According to the paper, once the model reaches 3B parameters, it can match an FP16 baseline on both perplexity and downstream task performance, using the same training setup.

So this is not just compression for the sake of compression. The ternary representation is designed to preserve model quality while reducing the cost of inference.

## How the model is built

BitNet b1.58 is based on the BitNet architecture, which replaces standard linear layers with **BitLinear** layers. The model is trained from scratch, not quantized after the fact.

That distinction is important. The paper positions BitNet b1.58 as a **training-time architecture**, not a post-training quantization trick. The authors also use:

- 8-bit activations
- LLaMA-style components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and no biases

This makes the model easier to integrate into existing open-source tooling such as Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp.

## How the weights are quantized

The weights are constrained with an **absmean** quantization function. In simple terms, the model scales the weight matrix by its average absolute value, then rounds each entry to the nearest value in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

The paper defines this as:

$$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)
$$

where

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|
$$

This keeps the weights small and discrete while still allowing the model to train effectively.

For activations, the authors keep the 8-bit setup from BitNet, but simplify the implementation by scaling activations to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token, rather than using a zero-point scheme. They report negligible impact on performance from this choice.

## Why this could be much cheaper to run

The big appeal of 1.58-bit LLMs is not just smaller storage. It is also cheaper computation.

In standard FP16 or BF16 LLMs, most of the work happens in matrix multiplication, which relies on floating-point multiplication and addition. In BitNet-style models, matrix multiplication is designed to use mostly integer addition, greatly reducing arithmetic cost.

The paper highlights three main efficiency gains:

- **Lower latency**: fewer expensive operations during inference
- **Lower memory use**: much smaller weights to store and load
- **Higher throughput**: more tokens or larger batch sizes on the same hardware

This matters because LLM inference is often limited by memory bandwidth and power, not just raw compute.

## What the results show

The paper compares BitNet b1.58 against a reproduced FP16 LLaMA-like baseline across several model sizes. The models are pretrained on RedPajama for 100 billion tokens and evaluated on common zero-shot benchmarks such as ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ.

The headline result is that **BitNet b1.58 starts to match the FP16 baseline at 3B parameters**.

At that point, it is also substantially cheaper:

- **2.71$\times$ faster**
- **3.55$\times$ less GPU memory**

The 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model goes further: it outperforms the 3B FP16 baseline while still using less memory and having lower latency.

The same pattern appears in end-task accuracy. As model size increases, the performance gap narrows, and the 3B ternary model reaches parity with the FP16 baseline.

## The scaling story

One of the most interesting claims in the paper is that BitNet b1.58 suggests a new scaling law for LLMs: not just scaling model size and data, but scaling under a much cheaper numerical format.

The paper gives examples of equivalences in efficiency:

- **13B BitNet b1.58** is more efficient than **3B FP16**
- **30B BitNet b1.58** is more efficient than **7B FP16**
- **70B BitNet b1.58** is more efficient than **13B FP16**

The key point is that the performance-cost tradeoff shifts. A larger ternary model can be more practical than a much smaller full-precision model.

## Energy, throughput, and memory at scale

The advantages become even clearer at larger model sizes.

The paper reports that on 7nm chips, BitNet b1.58 saves **71.4$\times$ arithmetic energy** for matrix multiplication compared with FP16 LLaMA. It also shows that the speedup increases as the model gets larger. For example, the 70B BitNet b1.58 model is reported to be **4.1$\times$ faster** than the FP16 baseline.

Throughput improves too. On two 80GB A100 GPUs, BitNet b1.58 70B supports up to **11$\times$ the batch size** of the FP16 baseline, resulting in **8.9$\times$ higher throughput**.

These are not just academic wins. They point to a model family that could be much more practical to deploy.

## Training on more tokens still works

The paper also tests whether the approach scales with training data. It trains a BitNet b1.58 model on **2T tokens**, following the data recipe of StableLM-3B, and evaluates it on a benchmark set including Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC-Easy.

The result: BitNet b1.58 performs better than the StableLM-3B reference on all tasks in that comparison. That suggests the ternary approach is not just a small-model trick; it can generalize well when trained at scale.

## Why this matters

If these results hold up broadly, 1.58-bit LLMs could change how we think about model deployment.

Instead of asking, “How do we squeeze a 16-bit model down to 4 bits after training?” the better question may become, “How do we train natively low-bit models that are efficient from the start?”

That shift has implications for:

- **Data center inference**, where memory bandwidth and energy are major costs
- **Mixture-of-Experts models**, where lower weight storage could reduce deployment overhead
- **Long-context inference**, where smaller activations can help manage KV cache pressure
- **Edge and mobile devices**, where CPU-friendly low-bit models may be far easier to run

The paper also argues that 1-bit-style computation opens the door to specialized hardware designed around these new arithmetic patterns.

## The bottom line

BitNet b1.58 is a ternary LLM with weights in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, which works out to about **1.58 bits per weight**. The extra zero is not a minor detail; it improves expressiveness and helps the model match FP16 baselines at the 3B scale and beyond.

The bigger message is that LLMs may not need to live in 16-bit floating point to be useful. If low-bit models can preserve quality while dramatically cutting memory, latency, and energy, then the next generation of LLMs may be built very differently from today’s systems.
