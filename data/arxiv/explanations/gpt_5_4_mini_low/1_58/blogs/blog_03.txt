\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# Can Tiny-Precision Models Really Match Full-Precision AI?

For a long time, the default assumption in large language models has been simple: if you want better quality, you usually pay for it with more parameters, more memory, and more expensive math. Lower precision has mostly been treated as a deployment trick. It helps you run a model cheaper after training, but it is not usually expected to preserve full model quality.

The paper *The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits* challenges that assumption directly. Its headline result is striking: a ternary model called **BitNet b1.58**, where every weight is constrained to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, can match a full-precision FP16 baseline in both perplexity and zero-shot downstream task performance starting at around the **3B parameter** scale, when trained under the same setup.

That is the core idea of the paper: tiny-precision does not have to mean tiny performance.

## What is BitNet b1.58?

BitNet b1.58 is a Transformer-based model that replaces standard linear layers with a custom **BitLinear** layer. Instead of storing weights as FP16 or BF16 values, the weights are ternary: $-1$, $0$, or $1$. The paper refers to this as **1.58 bits**, because three discrete states require a little more than one bit to encode.

The model still uses 8-bit activations, and it is trained from scratch rather than quantized after the fact. That distinction matters. The paper is not claiming that you can take any trained LLM and compress it into ternary weights with no loss. Instead, it argues that if you train the model natively in this format, it can learn to perform competitively with much higher precision baselines.

The architecture is also designed to look familiar to the open-source LLM community. It uses LLaMA-like choices such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and no biases. That makes it easier to integrate into existing software stacks.

## Why the extra zero matters

The original BitNet used a 1-bit-style design, but BitNet b1.58 adds a third state: zero. This is not just a cosmetic change.

According to the paper, the inclusion of $0$ improves the model’s ability to filter features. In other words, the weights can now explicitly suppress some inputs rather than only flipping sign. That extra flexibility is one of the reasons the authors argue that BitNet b1.58 has stronger modeling capability than the earlier 1-bit variant.

This is a useful reminder that precision is not only about compression. The choice of quantization scheme changes what the model can represent.

## The surprising result: matching FP16 at 3B

The strongest claim in the paper is about performance parity. The authors compare BitNet b1.58 against their reproduced FP16 LLaMA-style baseline across multiple model sizes, using the same training recipe and 100 billion pretraining tokens from RedPajama.

They evaluate two main things:

- **Validation perplexity** on WikiText2 and C4.
- **Zero-shot accuracy** on tasks including ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ.

Their conclusion is that **BitNet b1.58 starts to match the FP16 baseline at 3B parameters** on both perplexity and downstream tasks.

That does not mean every metric is identical in every setting. Rather, the paper’s claim is that, at that scale, the gap closes enough that the ternary model is effectively competitive with the full-precision baseline under the same training budget.

In practical terms, that is the key result: the lower-precision model is no longer just cheaper. It is also good.

## What “matching performance” means here

It is worth being precise about what the paper is and is not saying.

“Matching performance” in this context means that, under the same model size and training token budget, BitNet b1.58 reaches results comparable to the FP16 baseline on the reported perplexity and zero-shot benchmarks. The paper also highlights that the gap shrinks as model size increases, and that the 3.9B BitNet b1.58 can even outperform the 3B FP16 baseline while using less memory and lower latency.

This is not a universal claim that ternary models always beat full-precision models. It is a result under a particular training setup, architecture choice, and evaluation protocol. The paper is careful to ground the comparison in a fair setup: both models are trained on the same data recipe and token count.

That said, even this narrower claim is important. It suggests that the old intuition “lower precision must hurt quality” is too simplistic when the model is designed and trained for that precision from the start.

## The efficiency gains are not just about storage

The performance result would already be interesting on its own, but the paper’s broader point is that BitNet b1.58 changes the compute story too.

Because the weights are ternary, matrix multiplication can be implemented with almost no multiplication operations. The paper argues that this leads to major gains in:

- **Latency**
- **Memory usage**
- **Throughput**
- **Energy consumption**

On the reported GPU measurements, the paper finds that BitNet b1.58 becomes increasingly advantageous as model size grows. For example, the 70B BitNet b1.58 model is reported to be **4.1 times faster** than the FP16 baseline. On a throughput test with 70B parameters, it supports up to **11 times the batch size** and achieves **8.9 times higher throughput**.

The energy estimate is also dramatic. For matrix multiplication on 7nm chips, the paper reports **71.4 times less arithmetic energy consumption** compared to the FP16 baseline.

The main message is that tiny precision is not just a compression trick. It changes the economics of inference.

## Why this matters for deployment

This paper is especially interesting because it connects model quality with systems-level benefits. A lot of quantization work focuses on making existing models cheaper to run after training. BitNet b1.58 takes a different path: it suggests a native low-precision training recipe that may be easier to optimize in hardware and more efficient in production.

That opens up several practical implications.

First, the reduced memory footprint makes it easier to deploy larger models on limited hardware. Second, lower memory bandwidth pressure helps inference speed. Third, the paper argues that this new computation pattern could motivate dedicated hardware for 1-bit LLMs, similar to how specialized accelerators have emerged for other workloads.

The authors also point to potential benefits for long-context inference, Mixture-of-Experts systems, and edge or mobile deployment, although those are presented as future directions rather than demonstrated results.

## The bigger takeaway

The most important lesson from this paper is not that precision no longer matters. It clearly does. But precision is not just a knob for squeezing a trained model into less memory. It can also be part of the model design itself.

BitNet b1.58 shows that if you train a model natively with ternary weights, you can preserve much of the quality people expect from FP16 Transformers while dramatically improving efficiency. That makes the common tradeoff look less absolute than it used to be.

For practitioners, this is a useful shift in perspective. Instead of asking only how to quantize a model after training, we may also need to ask: what if the model was built for low precision from the beginning?

That is the real challenge—and the real promise—of 1.58-bit LLMs.
