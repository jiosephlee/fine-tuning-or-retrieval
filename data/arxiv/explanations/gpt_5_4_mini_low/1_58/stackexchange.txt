\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why does a ternary weight set $\{-1,0,1\}$ correspond to $1.58$ bits instead of $2$ bits?
Question:
I'm reading the paper "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits", and I am confused by the naming. The model uses ternary weights $\{-1,0,1\}$, which are three possible states per weight. The paper calls this "1.58-bit" because of the binary-system representation, but I don't fully understand the exact reasoning.

Is this just because $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$, meaning each weight carries that much information in principle? Or does the implementation literally pack weights into memory at $1.58$ bits on average? If actual hardware stores them in 2-bit containers, then in what sense is the model really $1.58$-bit rather than 2-bit?
Answer:
The short answer is that $1.58$ bits comes from the information-theoretic cost of representing three symbols, not from a literal hardware storage format.

If a weight can take one of $3$ values, here $\{-1,0,1\}$, then the minimum number of binary bits needed to uniquely encode it is $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$. That is the sense in which the paper calls the model “1.58-bit”: each weight has three possible states, so its idealized per-weight information content is $1.58$ bits rather than $2$ bits. This is exactly what the paper means when it says they “added an additional value of $0$ to the original 1-bit BitNet, resulting in 1.58 bits in the binary system.”

But that does not mean the weights are necessarily stored in memory at $1.58$ bits each in a literal, physical sense. Real hardware memory is discrete, so a naïve implementation would typically use a $2$-bit container per ternary weight, because $2$ bits are enough to encode up to $4$ states. In practice, the paper even notes that their runtime experiments used a “$2$-bit kernel,” which strongly suggests that the implementation is not packing weights into exactly $1.585$ bits on average at the storage level.

So there are really two different notions here. The first is the representational alphabet size: ternary weights have $3$ possible values, and the information-theoretic lower bound is $\log_2(3)$ bits per weight. The second is physical storage: actual implementations may still use $2$ bits per weight, or some compressed packing scheme, depending on the system. The paper’s headline “1.58 bits” refers to the former, not necessarily the latter.

This is similar to why people say “1-bit” binary networks even when the implementation uses extra metadata or packing overhead. The naming is about the number of distinct quantized states per parameter, not a promise that every parameter is stored in exactly that many raw hardware bits. In other words, “1.58-bit” is a quantization-level description, while memory usage is a separate engineering question.

So your intuition is right: $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$ is the reason. The model is “really” $1.58$-bit in the information-theoretic sense, but not necessarily in the literal memory-layout sense.

### What exactly is the role of the scaling factor $\gamma$ in the BitNet b1.58 weight quantization formula?
Question:
The paper defines ternary weight quantization as

$$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right), \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
$$

I understand that $\widetilde{W}$ is constrained to $\{-1,0,1\}$, but I am not clear on why they divide by the average absolute value $\gamma$ before rounding.

What intuition should I have for this normalization? Does it mainly control the fraction of entries that become $0$ versus $\pm 1$? Also, after quantization, is there any rescaling applied during the forward pass, or do they literally use only the ternary matrix $\widetilde{W}$? I feel like I am missing how the magnitude information of the original full-precision matrix is preserved.
Answer:
In the paper, $\gamma$ is best understood as a **per-layer normalization constant** that makes the ternarization rule depend on the *shape* of the weight distribution rather than its raw scale.

If you divide each weight by the layer’s average absolute weight, then a typical weight has magnitude around $1$ before rounding. That means the thresholds for producing $-1$, $0$, or $1$ are set relative to the layer itself, not by some fixed global cutoff. In other words, $\gamma$ makes the quantizer **scale-adaptive**: if the whole matrix $W$ is multiplied by a constant, $\gamma$ scales with it too, so the ternary pattern is largely unchanged. This is the intuition behind using the absmean normalization.

Your interpretation about sparsity is also essentially right. Since
\[
\widetilde W_{ij}=\mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right),
\]
the fraction of entries that become $0$ is controlled by how many weights satisfy $|W_{ij}| < \gamma/2$ (roughly, because rounding to the nearest integer sends small normalized values to $0$). So $\gamma$ indirectly sets the balance between zeros and $\pm 1$ by measuring the typical weight magnitude of that layer. It is not an arbitrary sparsity knob, but it does determine the effective threshold.

As for the forward pass, the paper is describing a genuinely ternary weight matrix $\widetilde W \in \{-1,0,1\}^{n\times m}$. It does not mention a separate post-quantization rescaling of the weight matrix during multiplication. So the matrix multiplication in BitNet b1.58 is performed with the ternary weights themselves; the magnitude information from the original full-precision matrix is not preserved exactly in the weights. Instead, the model learns to represent useful information through the ternary pattern, while surrounding components such as RMSNorm and the activation scaling help keep the signal well-conditioned. In practice this is a standard quantization-aware-training idea: the full-precision weights are the latent parameters being updated, but the forward path uses the quantized version.

So the short answer is: $\gamma$ normalizes each layer so that ternarization is relative to that layer’s typical weight size, which controls how many entries become $0$ versus $\pm 1$, and the forward computation uses the ternary matrix $\widetilde W$ rather than a separately rescaled version. The scale information is not stored explicitly in $\widetilde W$; it is absorbed by the training dynamics and the rest of the network.

### Why does adding the value $0$ to 1-bit weights improve modeling capacity so much?
Question:
The paper argues that moving from binary weights to ternary weights $\{-1,0,1\}$ gives stronger modeling capability because the value $0$ supports "feature filtering." I think the idea is that a binary model with weights $\{-1,1\}$ can only flip signs or keep contributions, while a ternary model can also ignore some contributions entirely.

But I am not sure whether this is the right interpretation. In a large matrix product, why is the ability to set many weights to exactly $0$ especially important? Is this mostly about sparsity, regularization, representational power, optimization stability, or some combination of these? I would appreciate an explanation at the level of linear layers in transformers.
Answer:
The paper’s “feature filtering” claim is best understood at the level of a linear layer, not as generic sparsity regularization.

In a transformer block, a linear map computes something like $y = Wx$, where each output coordinate is a weighted sum of input features. If the weights are constrained to $\{-1,1\}$, then every input feature must contribute to every output feature with the same magnitude after scaling; the only thing the model can decide is whether a contribution is added or subtracted. That means a binary layer can change the sign of a feature, but it cannot truly *turn it off*. Every input channel is always “in the mix,” which makes the layer relatively rigid.

Once you add $0$, the weight matrix can choose among three behaviors for each connection: pass it positively, pass it negatively, or remove it entirely. That third option is what the paper calls feature filtering. It is important because many useful linear transformations in a transformer are not “use everything equally,” but rather “use these few signals, suppress the rest.” In attention projections, MLP projections, and output heads, it is often beneficial for each neuron to ignore irrelevant or noisy coordinates. With ternary weights, the model can represent that kind of selective routing directly.

So the gain is not mainly about sparsity as a compression trick or a regularizer, although sparsity is a side effect. The main point is representational power. Compared with $\{-1,1\}$, the ternary alphabet makes the set of possible weight matrices much richer: it greatly increases the number of distinct linear maps you can realize at a fixed parameter count. This is especially helpful in low-bit settings, because when the weight magnitudes are heavily quantized, losing the ability to zero out entries is a big restriction. The model then has to encode selection indirectly through cancellations between positive and negative weights, which is much less flexible and often much less stable.

Intuitively, think of a binary layer as forcing every neuron to listen to every input channel, while a ternary layer can decide, per connection, “listen,” “listen with opposite sign,” or “do not listen.” That extra “do not listen” option is what lets BitNet b1.58 behave much more like a real transformer layer and less like a coarse sign pattern. The paper’s empirical result—that this is enough to match FP16 baselines from around 3B parameters onward—suggests that this extra selectivity matters a lot in practice.

### If BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit activations, why does the paper still describe it as a '1-bit LLM'?
Question:
The paper repeatedly frames BitNet b1.58 as part of the era of "1-bit LLMs," but in the architecture section it says the model is trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations.

This makes me wonder what part of the system is actually 1-bit-like. Since matrix multiplication depends on both weights and activations, wouldn't 8-bit activations still be a significant source of compute and memory cost? Is the idea that the weights dominate the storage and much of the inference bottleneck, so quantizing them is enough to justify the label? Or is there some special arithmetic trick where ternary weights make the multiplication almost disappear even when activations are 8-bit integers?
Answer:
The paper is using “1-bit LLM” to refer primarily to the **weights**, not to every number in the system. In BitNet b1.58, “every single parameter (or weight) of the LLM is ternary $\{-1,0,1\}$,” which is why the authors call it a 1-bit-family model even though the activations are kept at $8$ bits. The “1.58 bits” name comes from the ternary weight alphabet: three states can be encoded with about $\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits per weight.

So the key point is that the model is not “1-bit” in the sense that *all* arithmetic is 1-bit. It is “1-bit” in the sense that the **model parameters, which dominate storage and bandwidth, are extremely low precision**. That is also why the paper emphasizes memory footprint and weight transfer from DRAM to SRAM as major inference costs. Since the weights are ternary, they occupy far less memory than FP16 weights, and loading them is much cheaper.

The $8$-bit activations do still matter, especially for the KV cache and for some remaining runtime cost, and the paper is explicit about that. In fact, in the discussion the authors say that reducing activations from $16$ bits to $8$ bits helps with long-sequence inference by allowing the context length to be doubled under the same resources. So the model is not claiming that activations are negligible; rather, it is claiming that **the biggest and most expensive part of the model, the weights, has been pushed into a ternary regime**.

As for computation, the special trick is exactly the ternary weight set. If a weight is $-1$, $0$, or $1$, then the matrix multiplication no longer needs a full general multiply for that term: multiplying by $1$ is copying, by $-1$ is sign flip, and by $0$ is skipping the term entirely. That is why the paper says the matrix multiplication “only involves integer addition” and “requires almost no multiplication operations.” The activations can still be $8$-bit integers, but the expensive multiply is largely removed because the weights have such a restricted form.

So the short answer is: **the “1-bit LLM” label refers to the ternary weights and the resulting multiplication-free-ish linear layers, not to the activations.** The activations are still quantized to $8$ bits for efficiency and sequence-length reasons, but the paper’s main claim is that the dominant model cost comes from weights, and those are what have been driven into the 1-bit/$1.58$-bit regime.

### How can matrix multiplication 'require almost no multiplication operations' when activations are still INT8?
Question:
The paper says that BitNet b1.58 enables a new computation paradigm and that matrix multiplication requires almost no multiplication operations, with the arithmetic being mostly INT8 additions. This is where I got lost.

If a weight is in $\{-1,0,1\}$ and an activation is an 8-bit integer $a$, then multiplying gives either $-a$, $0$, or $a$. I can see that this avoids general-purpose floating-point multiplication, but doesn't the hardware still need some kind of sign handling and conditional logic? In what precise sense is the multiplication removed?

Is the intended implementation closer to selecting, negating, or skipping activations and then accumulating them? I am trying to understand whether this is just a theoretical simplification or a genuine low-level computational advantage on real accelerators.
Answer:
In the paper’s sense, “almost no multiplication operations” does not mean that the model output is produced without any arithmetic at all. It means that the expensive general-purpose multiply-accumulate pattern of standard matrix multiplication is replaced by something much cheaper: conditional add, subtract, or skip.

If a weight is $w \in \{-1,0,1\}$ and the activation is an 8-bit integer $a$, then the product is indeed just $-a$, $0$, or $a$. So the kernel does not need a full integer multiplier for the weight side. For each weight value, the implementation can simply do

$$
w = 1 \Rightarrow \text{accumulate } a,\qquad
w = -1 \Rightarrow \text{accumulate } -a,\qquad
w = 0 \Rightarrow \text{do nothing}.
$$

That is exactly what the paper means by “matrix multiplication only involves integer addition” and by “almost no multiplication operations.” The multiplication has been turned into a sign decision plus an addition or subtraction.

You are right that there is still some low-level logic involved. The hardware has to determine whether each ternary weight is $-1$, $0$, or $1$, and it must negate or skip the activation accordingly. But that logic is much cheaper than a conventional multiplication unit. In particular, there is no general-purpose multiply datapath doing products of two multi-bit numbers. From a systems point of view, that is the real gain.

This is not just a theoretical simplification. The paper’s efficiency claims are explicitly about inference cost on real accelerators. It reports that the matrix multiplication energy is dominated by INT8 additions in BitNet b1.58, whereas standard LLaMA uses FP16 additions and multiplications. They also mention using a specialized 2-bit kernel from Ladder in their runtime experiments, which is evidence that the intended implementation is a low-level optimized kernel, not merely a mathematical abstraction.

So the precise answer is: the multiplication is removed on the weight side, because multiplying by $-1$, $0$, or $1$ can be implemented as sign flip, zeroing, or passthrough. The remaining cost is mostly integer addition and some control logic. That is why the paper describes a new computation paradigm: not “no arithmetic,” but “no general multiplication,” which is the expensive part on conventional hardware.

### Why does the paper say post-training quantization is sub-optimal compared to training BitNet b1.58 from scratch?
Question:
The introduction says post-training quantization is widely used but sub-optimal, while BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with ternary weights. I understand that quantizing after training can hurt performance, but I want to pin down what is fundamentally different here.

Is the main advantage that the network learns to organize its internal representations around the quantization constraint during optimization? Or is there a more specific reason related to transformers, such as activation statistics, outlier channels, or the inability of a pretrained FP16 optimum to map well into $\{-1,0,1\}$? In other words, why should we expect a model trained natively with ternary weights to match FP16, while a post-training quantized FP16 model often does not?
Answer:
The short version is that the paper is not claiming that a pretrained FP16 model can simply be “rounded down” to ternary weights and keep the same quality. Rather, it is arguing that if you want a model whose weights are truly $\{-1,0,1\}$, then the model has to be optimized under that constraint from the start, so the whole network learns a parameterization that is naturally representable in that low-precision space.

That distinction matters because post-training quantization and native quantization solve different optimization problems. A full-precision LLM is trained to find a good optimum in a continuous parameter space. If you later project those weights onto $\{-1,0,1\}$, you are taking a solution that was never optimized for that discrete geometry and snapping it to a very coarse grid. Even if the FP16 model is excellent, it may sit in a region where many weights are “important” in the sense that small changes matter; after rounding, those small changes can become large functional errors. So the issue is not just that quantization injects noise, but that it changes the hypothesis class after training.

In BitNet b1.58, by contrast, the quantization is part of the forward computation during training, with an absmean scaling and rounding to $\{-1,0,1\}$. That means the optimizer sees the quantized model, not the FP16 proxy. The weights can therefore adapt to the limited alphabet, and the rest of the network can reorganize around those constraints. This is the key intuition behind why training from scratch can work much better than post-training quantization: the model learns distributed representations that are “easy” to express with ternary weights rather than trying to compress an already-learned FP16 representation.

There is also a more specific point in the paper: the authors say that adding the zero value gives “explicit support for feature filtering.” That is, ternary weights are not just a crude approximation to binary weights; the zero weight lets the model suppress some connections entirely, which increases modeling capacity relative to pure $\{-1,1\}$. So the ternary constraint is not merely a compression trick after the fact, but part of the inductive bias of the architecture.

For transformers, this is especially relevant because the layers are highly coupled: if one projection matrix changes scale or sign patterns, it affects attention and feed-forward blocks downstream. Post-training quantization has to preserve those learned interactions without letting the model re-balance itself. Training natively with low-bit weights lets the whole system co-adapt, including the activation statistics. The paper also keeps activations at 8 bits, which helps preserve enough dynamic range so that the difficult part is mainly the weights, not both weights and activations being aggressively quantized at once.

So the fundamental reason is optimization mismatch, not just a hardware trick. A pretrained FP16 optimum is not generally close, in function space, to an optimum constrained to ternary weights. BitNet b1.58 works because it is trained directly in that constrained space, letting the model learn a representation that is compatible with $\{-1,0,1\}$ weights from the beginning. The paper’s claim that it can match FP16 is therefore an empirical statement about this training recipe, not a guarantee that arbitrary FP16 models can be post-quantized to the same level.

### What does it mean that BitNet b1.58 'matches' FP16 starting from 3B parameters?
Question:
The paper says BitNet b1.58 matches the FP16 LLaMA-like baseline starting from the 3B size, both in perplexity and end-task performance. I am unsure how strong this claim is.

Do they mean the results are statistically indistinguishable, or just very close numerically? Also, since both models are trained for 100B tokens, should I interpret this as evidence that ternary models need a minimum scale before the quantization constraint stops hurting? If so, what changes around 3B parameters that makes the gap narrow so much compared to smaller models?
Answer:
In this paper, “matches” should be read as an empirical claim about the reported benchmark numbers, not as a formal statement of statistical indistinguishability. The authors do not report confidence intervals, repeated runs, or significance tests. What they show is that, on their chosen evaluation suite and training recipe, the ternary BitNet b1.58 curves become very close to the FP16 LLaMA-like baseline and, from the $3$B scale onward, the gap is small enough that they describe it as “matching” both perplexity and end-task performance. So the strongest defensible interpretation is “roughly on par within the resolution of the reported experiments,” not “provably equal up to noise.”

The important nuance is that this is a scaling observation, not a theorem about ternary networks in general. The paper is careful to fix the training budget at $100$B tokens and compare models of the same size under the same recipe. Under that setup, the gap between BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 baseline narrows as model size increases, and they explicitly call out $3$B as the point where it starts to match. That does support your intuition that quantization constraints hurt smaller models more than larger ones, but the paper does not establish a universal minimum scale. It is an empirical result for this architecture, this quantization scheme, and this training regime.

Intuitively, the reason larger models tolerate ternary weights better is that they have more capacity to absorb the representational loss introduced by quantization. A $1$-bit-ish model is not just a “smaller-precision version” of FP16; it is a much more constrained parameterization. At small sizes, those constraints can be too severe, because every weight has to carry more of the burden of representation. As the model gets bigger, the redundancy in the parameterization increases, so the network can distribute information across more parameters and compensate for the coarse weight values. The paper’s own framing is that the inclusion of the zero value gives “feature filtering,” which strengthens modeling capability relative to the original binary BitNet. That helps explain why the ternary model closes the gap more successfully at larger scale.

What changes around $3$B? The paper does not identify a special architectural threshold or a new training phenomenon that suddenly appears there. The safer reading is simply that, in their scaling sweep, $3$B is the first size where the remaining quantization error is no longer large enough to noticeably degrade the benchmark results relative to FP16. In other words, the transition is gradual, not magical. The gap narrows because model scaling improves the signal-to-noise balance of the learned representations, and because the ternary parameterization is expressive enough at that scale to approximate the FP16 baseline well enough for the tasks tested.

So your interpretation is broadly right, with one caveat: the paper’s evidence supports “larger ternary models can recover FP16-like performance under this training setup,” but it does not prove a general minimum scale or a statistical equivalence. It is a strong empirical scaling result, not a formal equivalence claim.

### Is it fair to compare a 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model against a 3B FP16 model and call it a Pareto improvement?
Question:
The results section emphasizes that BitNet b1.58 with 3.9B parameters is faster and uses less memory than a 3B FP16 LLaMA-like model while performing better, and this is described as a Pareto improvement.

I am not sure how to think about this comparison. On one hand, the quantized model has more parameters, so it is not a like-for-like architecture comparison. On the other hand, the total deployment cost is still lower.

In model compression and systems papers, is this considered a fair comparison standard? Should the key comparison axis here be quality at fixed latency/memory budget rather than quality at fixed parameter count?
Answer:
Yes, it is fair in the sense that the paper is explicitly comparing the *deployment trade-off* rather than the raw parameter count.

The key point is that a BitNet b1.58 model and an FP16 LLaMA-like model do not live on the same cost axis. A 3.9B BitNet model stores its weights in ternary form, so each parameter is vastly cheaper in memory and arithmetic than an FP16 parameter. Because of that, “same parameter count” is not the right fairness criterion if the question is “which model gives better quality for a given inference budget?” In fact, the paper’s whole argument is that the meaningful comparison is quality versus latency, memory, throughput, and energy, not quality versus parameter count alone.

That is why the authors are comfortable calling the 3.9B BitNet b1.58 versus 3B FP16 result a Pareto improvement. In multi-objective terms, a point is Pareto-dominating another if it is no worse on all objectives and strictly better on at least one. Here the paper claims that 3.9B BitNet b1.58 has better task performance while also using less GPU memory and being faster than the 3B FP16 baseline. Under those measured deployment metrics, that is exactly the kind of claim “Pareto improvement” refers to.

What would be less fair is to compare only parameter count and say the 3.9B BitNet is “larger,” as if that alone should make it worse. That would ignore that BitNet changes the cost per parameter dramatically. The paper even frames this as a new scaling law: for example, it states that 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient in latency, memory, and energy than 3B FP16, and similarly for larger sizes. So the relevant axis is not just model size in the abstract, but model size translated into actual system cost.

So, in model compression and systems papers, the standard is usually to compare at fixed deployment budget, or to report the Pareto frontier over quality versus cost. Fixed parameter count is useful when you want an apples-to-apples architectural comparison, and the paper does that too by saying BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision baselines “with the same model size and training tokens.” But once the question becomes “is this better to deploy?”, then comparing a 3.9B ternary model to a 3B FP16 model is reasonable, because the number of stored parameters is not the resource that matters most anymore.

In short, the comparison is fair if the claim is about *end-to-end efficiency*: better quality at lower latency and memory. It would be misleading only if the authors were presenting it as a pure parameter-matched architectural comparison.

### How can BitNet b1.58 have lower memory usage if the embedding remains full precision?
Question:
The paper states that latency and memory improve with scale, and mentions that the embedding remains full precision, but its memory proportion becomes smaller for larger models.

I think I understand this qualitatively, but I would like a more concrete explanation. Is the claim simply that the linear layers dominate parameter count as the model gets larger, so compressing them overwhelms the cost of keeping embeddings in FP16? If so, how much does the full-precision embedding limit the theoretical memory savings, especially for smaller models?
Answer:
Yes — the paper’s claim is essentially that once the model is large enough, the quantized linear layers dominate the parameter and runtime/memory cost, so leaving the embedding matrix in full precision becomes a relatively small fixed overhead.

The key point is that “the embedding remains full precision” does not mean “most of the model remains full precision.” In a LLaMA-like Transformer, almost all parameters live in the repeated linear projections inside each block: the attention projections and the MLP weights. Those are exactly the parts BitNet b1.58 turns into ternary weights. The embedding matrix, by contrast, is only one matrix at the input side, so its parameter count grows much more slowly than the total model size. As the model gets wider/deeper, the embedding becomes a smaller and smaller fraction of the whole.

A simple way to see the limit is to write the memory for the weights as

$$
M \approx 16P_{\text{emb}} + 1.58\,P_{\text{rest}},
$$

measured in bits, where $P_{\text{emb}}$ is the number of embedding parameters and $P_{\text{rest}}$ is everything else. Compared to an FP16 model with the same parameter count,

$$
M_{\text{FP16}} = 16(P_{\text{emb}} + P_{\text{rest}}).
$$

So the ideal weight-memory reduction factor is

$$
\frac{M_{\text{FP16}}}{M}
=
\frac{16(P_{\text{emb}}+P_{\text{rest}})}{16P_{\text{emb}} + 1.58P_{\text{rest}}}.
$$

If $P_{\text{emb}}$ is tiny relative to $P_{\text{rest}}$, this tends to

$$
\frac{16}{1.58} \approx 10.1\times,
$$

which is the asymptotic ceiling from quantizing the weights while keeping embeddings full precision.

If the embedding is a nontrivial fraction of parameters, it reduces that ideal speedup. For example, if embeddings were $10\%$ of all parameters, then the best possible weight-memory reduction would be

$$
\frac{16}{0.1\cdot 16 + 0.9\cdot 1.58} \approx 6.4\times.
$$

If embeddings were $20\%$, it drops to about $4.6\times$. So yes, for smaller models the full-precision embedding can materially limit the theoretical savings.

That said, the paper’s reported numbers are not pure “parameter-bit ratio” numbers; they are measured GPU inference memory, which also includes activations and other runtime buffers. The paper explicitly says that at larger scales the embedding’s memory proportion is smaller, which is why the observed memory reduction improves with scale. That is consistent with the reported trend: BitNet b1.58 uses about $3.55\times$ less GPU memory at $3$B and about $3.32\times$ less at $3.9$B, while the gains become more compelling at larger sizes. The exact factor is not just determined by the embedding, but the embedding does set a floor on the achievable compression.

So the intuition is: the embedding is a fixed full-precision “tax,” but for large Transformers the many ternary linear layers dominate so strongly that this tax becomes negligible. For small models, it matters more and prevents you from reaching the full $\approx 10\times$ theoretical weight-only compression ceiling.

### What assumptions go into the claim of $71.4\times$ lower arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication?
Question:
The paper claims that BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4\times$ arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips, based on prior energy models. I find this impressive, but I am not sure how to interpret it.

Is this number only counting primitive arithmetic operations like FP16 multiplication/addition versus INT8 addition, while ignoring data movement, control overhead, and memory hierarchy effects? If so, how much should we expect the end-to-end energy savings to differ from the arithmetic-only estimate?

I am basically trying to understand whether this $71.4\times$ figure is a realistic deployment number or mainly a lower-level proxy metric.
Answer:
Yes — in the paper, the $71.4\times$ number is an **arithmetic-operation energy estimate**, not a full system-level deployment measurement.

The authors explicitly say they “estimate the arithmetic operations energy consumption” and that they “focus mainly on the calculation for matrix multiplication,” because that is the dominant cost in LLM inference. So the claim is based on a prior **7nm-chip energy model** from the cited works, where different primitive operations are assigned different energy costs. Under that model, the relevant comparison is essentially: FP16 matrix multiplication involves FP16 multiplication plus FP16 addition, whereas BitNet b1.58’s matrix multiplication is mostly INT8 addition. Because integer addition is much cheaper than floating-point multiply/add on that model, the arithmetic energy drops by a large factor.

So your interpretation is right: this is **not** counting the full inference stack. It does **not** directly include data movement, cache/SRAM/DRAM traffic, control overhead, kernel launch overhead, or the details of a particular accelerator’s memory hierarchy. The paper separates those concerns: latency, memory, throughput, and an “end-to-end energy cost” are reported elsewhere, while the $71.4\times$ figure is specifically about the arithmetic part of matrix multiplication.

That distinction matters because, in modern accelerators, **data movement can dominate energy** once arithmetic becomes cheap. So you should not read $71.4\times$ as “the whole model will use $71.4\times$ less energy in practice.” It is better understood as a **lower-level proxy metric** showing that the compute itself is much cheaper. The actual end-to-end savings should usually be **smaller than or at best comparable to** that arithmetic-only number, depending on how much of the workload is compute-bound versus memory-bound.

The paper does make the broader point that BitNet b1.58 also reduces memory footprint and bandwidth pressure, so some of the arithmetic advantage can translate into system-level gains. But the exact end-to-end factor is architecture- and implementation-dependent; the paper does not claim that $71.4\times$ is a deployment guarantee. In short, it is a **theoretical/analytical energy proxy**, not a full hardware benchmark.

### Why does quantizing activations to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ remove the need for a zero-point?
Question:
The paper says that unlike the original BitNet, activations are scaled per token to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ "to get rid of the zero-point quantization." I have only a partial understanding of affine versus symmetric quantization, so this statement confused me.

My current guess is that if the quantized range is centered at zero, then integer zero corresponds exactly to real zero, so no offset term is needed. Is that the whole idea? Also, why is this especially convenient for implementation and system-level optimization in this model?
Answer:
Yes — that is essentially the idea, and in this paper that is exactly what “getting rid of the zero-point quantization” means.

In affine quantization, a real value is represented as
$$
x \approx s\,(q - z),
$$
where $s$ is the scale, $q$ is the integer quantized value, and $z$ is the zero-point. The zero-point is needed when the quantized integer range is not centered on real zero. It ensures that the real value $0$ is represented exactly, which matters when the range is asymmetric, such as $[0, Q_b]$.

If you instead quantize to a symmetric signed range like $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, then the natural mapping is centered at zero, so real $0$ maps to integer $0$. In that case the zero-point is just $z=0$, so there is no offset term to carry around. That is the main mathematical reason.

Why does that help in practice? Because zero-points complicate low-bit inference kernels. With affine quantization, matrix multiplication is no longer just a scaled integer dot product; you have to account for terms involving the zero-points, which introduces extra additions and bookkeeping. In contrast, symmetric signed quantization lets you use a much cleaner computation path:
$$
x \approx s\,q
$$
with $q$ signed, so the core operation becomes ordinary integer arithmetic without offset correction. This is especially attractive for BitNet b1.58 because the whole point of the architecture is to make inference easy to implement efficiently on current systems and, eventually, on hardware specialized for low-bit computation.

So your intuition is right: centering the range at zero makes zero map to zero, and that removes the need for a zero-point. The paper’s practical point is that this simplification makes the implementation and system-level optimization easier, because the kernels become simpler and more hardware-friendly. The authors also say this change has negligible effect on performance in their experiments, so they prefer the cleaner symmetric form.

### What is the paper really claiming by saying BitNet b1.58 defines a 'new scaling law'?
Question:
One of the strongest claims in the abstract is that the 1.58-bit LLM defines a new scaling law and recipe for training future generations of LLMs. But in the body, what I mostly see are empirical comparisons of perplexity, latency, memory, and energy across model sizes.

Is the term "scaling law" being used informally here to mean a new tradeoff curve between quality and inference cost? Or are the authors claiming something closer to classical neural scaling laws, where loss follows a predictable power-law with model size, data, or compute?

I want to understand whether this is a rigorous scaling-law claim or more of a systems-performance framing.
Answer:
In this paper, “new scaling law” is being used much more loosely than in the classical neural scaling-law literature.

A classical scaling law usually means an empirical relation of the form “loss decreases predictably as a power law in model size, data size, or compute,” often written as something like $L(N) \approx aN^{-\alpha} + b$, with exponents fit from experiments. That kind of claim is about how quality scales smoothly and predictably with scale, and it is usually backed by fitted curves over a range of sizes.

That is not really what the paper establishes. What the authors actually show is that BitNet b1.58 changes the cost-performance tradeoff: for a given level of perplexity or zero-shot accuracy, it uses less latency, memory, throughput, and energy than an FP16 baseline. Their “scaling law” language is tied to the observation that as model size increases, the efficiency advantage of the ternary model becomes larger, and they summarize this with equivalences like “13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 3B FP16,” “30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16,” and so on. So the claim is essentially that there is a new Pareto frontier between model capability and inference cost.

The strongest evidence in the paper is therefore systems-oriented and empirical, not a formal scaling-law fit. They compare perplexity, downstream accuracy, latency, memory, throughput, and energy across model sizes, and they argue that BitNet b1.58 can “match” FP16 performance starting around 3B while being much cheaper to run. That is a useful and potentially important result, but it is not the same as proving a new universal scaling law in the classical sense.

So the short answer is: yes, they are using “scaling law” somewhat informally, to mean a better scaling relationship between performance and inference cost, not a rigorously fitted law of loss versus parameters/data/compute. The paper’s actual contribution is a new efficiency frontier, plus an empirical recipe showing that ternary-weight LLMs can stay competitive at scale.

### Why should BitNet b1.58 become relatively more efficient as model size increases?
Question:
The paper repeatedly says that the speedup, memory savings, and energy benefits of BitNet b1.58 increase as the model size scales. I would like help understanding the mechanism.

Is the idea that as transformers get larger, the fraction of total computation and memory due to linear layers becomes larger relative to other components like embeddings, normalization, and attention bookkeeping, so compressing the linear layers pays off more? Or is there something special about the 2-bit kernel and GPU utilization that makes larger matrices disproportionately better for the ternary model?
Answer:
The paper’s explanation is mostly the first one: the benefit grows because the parts of a Transformer that BitNet accelerates and compresses, especially the \emph{nn.Linear} layers, take up a larger share of the total cost as the model gets bigger.

In a Transformer LLM, most of the parameter count and most of the arithmetic in the forward pass live in the linear projections inside attention and MLP blocks. BitNet b1.58 replaces those FP16/BF16 linear layers with ternary weights and an integer-friendly kernel, so the operations that dominate the model become much cheaper. As the model size increases, these linear layers scale up strongly, so the absolute savings from replacing them also scale up. That is why the paper says the speedup increases with model size and explicitly states, “This is because the time cost for \emph{nn.Linear} grows with the model size.”

Your intuition about the “fixed overhead” components is also correct and is part of the same story. Things like embeddings, normalization, and other non-linear bookkeeping do not shrink in absolute cost, but they become relatively less important in larger models. So even if BitNet does not accelerate those parts much, their contribution to the end-to-end runtime or energy becomes a smaller fraction of the total as the model grows. The paper makes exactly this point for energy: “the percentage of \emph{nn.Linear} grows with the model size, while the cost from other components is smaller for larger models.”

For memory, the same logic applies. The embedding layer remains full precision, so its relative share of the total memory footprint is larger in smaller models and smaller in larger ones. Once the model grows, more of the memory is tied up in the weight matrices that BitNet compresses, so the reduction in memory footprint becomes more pronounced in absolute terms.

The paper does \emph{not} claim that the scaling advantage is mainly because the ternary kernel becomes magically more efficient only at large matrix sizes, although better hardware utilization for large GEMMs is certainly relevant in practice. What the paper actually says is more modest: the results were measured with a 2-bit kernel, and “there is still room for optimization.” So the observed scaling trend is primarily attributed to the changing composition of the model, not to some special property of the kernel that only appears at large size.

So the short answer is: yes, the key mechanism is that larger Transformers spend proportionally more of their compute, memory, and energy in linear layers, and BitNet b1.58 is designed to make those layers much cheaper. The larger the model, the more of the total bill comes from the part BitNet optimizes, and the more impressive the end-to-end savings become.

### How does BitNet b1.58 interact with the KV cache if the paper only quantizes weights and activations?
Question:
In the discussion section, the paper says BitNet b1.58 is a significant step toward native support for long sequences because it reduces activations from 16 bits to 8 bits, allowing context length to double under the same resources. But I thought the main long-context bottleneck during autoregressive inference was the KV cache.

Are the stored keys and values also 8-bit in this setup, or is the paper speaking more generally about the activation precision throughout the network? If the KV cache is not explicitly quantized, I am struggling to see why the context length would simply double.
Answer:
The paper is using “activations” in a broad systems sense, and that is the key to reading that discussion section. In autoregressive inference, the KV cache is indeed the main activation-like memory resident across decoding steps, so if the cache were still stored in FP16/BF16, then the claim that context length can simply double would not follow. The paper’s wording suggests that they are thinking of the whole activation footprint during inference, which includes the KV cache, not just the transient hidden states inside the layers.

That said, the paper does not spell out a separate KV-cache quantization scheme in the main text. It says BitNet b1.58 is trained with “8-bit activations,” and then, in the discussion, it says this “reduces the activations from 16 bits to 8 bits, allowing the context length to be doubled given the same resources.” So the intended interpretation is that the activation precision used in inference, including the cached states, is lower than in a standard FP16/BF16 Transformer. Under that assumption, halving the number of bytes per cached key/value vector roughly doubles the number of tokens you can store for the same memory budget.

The paper also hints that this is not the end of the story: it explicitly says the KV-cache-related memory could be “further losslessly compressed to 4 bits or even lower for 1.58-bit LLMs, which we leave as future work.” That is an important clue that the authors see KV-cache compression as a separate optimization avenue, not something fully solved in the current work.

So the short answer is: the paper is not claiming that BitNet b1.58 magically removes the KV-cache bottleneck. Rather, it is arguing that because the model already uses 8-bit activations, the cache footprint is reduced relative to a 16-bit baseline, which in turn can approximately double the context length under the same memory budget. If you keep the KV cache at 16 bits, that conclusion would not hold as stated; the discussion is implicitly assuming the activation memory used for caching is also reduced.

### Does the success at 2T tokens mean ternary models follow the same data-scaling behavior as FP16 models?
Question:
The paper reports that a 3B BitNet b1.58 model trained on 2T tokens outperforms StableLM-3B on several zero-shot tasks, and concludes that 1.58-bit LLMs have strong generalization capability. I am unsure how far that conclusion should be taken.

Does this result suggest that ternary models benefit from additional data in roughly the same way as full-precision models, or could there still be differences in the data-scaling curve that are not visible from a single 2T-token comparison? In other words, what can we really infer about token scaling from this experiment alone?
Answer:
Not really. The $2T$-token result shows that a ternary model can continue to improve with substantially more data and, at least in this setup, can outperform a strong $\mathrm{FP16}$ baseline of similar size. But it does **not** by itself establish that ternary models follow the *same* token-scaling law as $\mathrm{FP16}$ models.

The key point is that a single comparison at one data budget tells you only one point on the curve, not the curve itself. From the paper’s experiment, you can infer that BitNet b1.58 does not “run out of steam” at $2T$ tokens and that it can make effective use of large-scale training data. That is consistent with the authors’ claim that $1.58$-bit LLMs have strong generalization capability. But it does **not** tell you whether the data efficiency is the same, better, or worse than $\mathrm{FP16}$ as you move from, say, $100B$ to $500B$ to $2T$ to even larger token counts.

In other words, the experiment supports a statement like: “At $3B$ scale and $2T$ tokens, ternary weights do not prevent strong downstream performance.” It does **not** support a stronger statement like: “Ternary models have the same scaling exponent or the same asymptotic data-scaling behavior as $\mathrm{FP16}$ models.” To claim that, you would want several matched runs across multiple token budgets and ideally multiple model sizes, so you could compare the entire scaling trend rather than a single endpoint.

There is also a practical caveat in how to read the result. The paper compares BitNet b1.58 trained on $2T$ tokens with StableLM-$3B$ results taken from its technical report. That is a useful benchmark, but it is still one comparison between two training recipes and two model families. So the result is evidence that ternary training can be highly competitive under a large-token regime, not proof that the underlying scaling law is identical.

So the safest inference is: the paper provides evidence that $1.58$-bit LLMs can benefit from additional data in a meaningful way and can generalize well at large scale. It does **not** let us conclude, from this experiment alone, that their data-scaling curve matches $\mathrm{FP16}$ models point-for-point.

### Why are biases removed in the BitNet b1.58 LLaMA-like architecture, and is that related to quantization?
Question:
The paper says BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and removes all biases. I know many recent LLMs omit biases anyway, but I am not sure whether this design choice has any special importance here.

Is removing biases mainly done for compatibility with the LLaMA family, or does it also make ternary quantization easier or more stable? For example, could biases become disproportionately important or hard to represent when the main weight matrices are constrained to $\{-1,0,1\}$?
Answer:
In the paper, the stated reason is mainly architectural and practical, not a special quantization trick. They say BitNet b1.58 “adopts the LLaMA-alike components” and “removes all biases” so that it can be integrated into the standard open-source LLaMA ecosystem “with minimal efforts.” In other words, this is largely a design choice for consistency with LLaMA-style Transformers and for implementation convenience.

That said, removing biases does fit the quantized setting quite naturally. In a ternary-weight model, the expensive part is the linear map $Wx$. If you also had biases, they would be extra nonzero parameters added after the matrix multiplication, and they would not benefit from the ternary structure in the same way. So removing them keeps the model cleaner and more aligned with the paper’s goal that the model’s parameters/weights are ternary and the core computation is almost entirely integer-friendly. But the paper does not present bias removal as a separate quantization innovation, nor does it argue that biases are uniquely hard to represent in $\{-1,0,1\}$.

So the best reading is: the bias-free design is inherited from the LLaMA family and helps preserve the simple, hardware-friendly, low-bit computation pipeline. It is more about architectural alignment and avoiding unnecessary full-precision extras than about a deep theoretical requirement of ternary quantization.

If you want the intuition: quantizing the weight matrices gives you the big win because they dominate compute and memory. Bias terms are usually small by comparison, so they are not the main bottleneck. Removing them is therefore a sensible simplification, but the paper’s main quantization story is about the matrices, not the biases.
