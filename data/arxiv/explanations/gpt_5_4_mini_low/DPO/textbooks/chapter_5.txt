\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 5: 5. From KL-Constrained RL to Closed-Form Optimal Policies

# Statement of the KL-Constrained Optimization Problem

The starting point for the paper’s argument is the standard RLHF fine-tuning objective. After a language model has been trained by supervised fine-tuning, the goal in the final stage is to adjust the policy so that it produces responses that receive high reward under a learned reward function, while still staying close to the original model. In the paper, this is written as maximizing expected reward minus a KL-divergence penalty:
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr].
\]
Here, $x$ is a prompt, $y$ is a completion, $r_{\phi}(x,y)$ is the learned reward model, and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the reference policy, typically the supervised fine-tuned model. The scalar $\beta$ controls how much the policy is allowed to move away from the reference.

This objective captures the core tension in RLHF. On one hand, the model should improve according to human preferences encoded in the reward. On the other hand, if the model is allowed to change too freely, it may drift into regions where the reward model is unreliable or collapse onto a narrow set of high-reward outputs. The KL penalty is what formalizes this caution. It makes the optimization problem not just about maximizing reward, but about doing so conservatively relative to a baseline model.

# The Role of the Reference Policy in Regularized Optimization

The reference policy plays a central conceptual role in the paper. It is not just an implementation detail; it defines the center of gravity of the optimization problem. The paper takes the reference policy to be the initial supervised fine-tuned model, $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$, and uses it to regulate how much the final policy can differ from the model before preference optimization begins.

The reason this matters is that reward models are only accurate on the kind of data they were trained on. If the policy moves too far from the reference policy, it may enter regions of the response space where the reward model makes poor predictions. The KL term therefore serves two purposes in the paper’s discussion. First, it keeps the learned policy near the distribution on which the reward model is valid. Second, it preserves diversity and prevents mode collapse toward a single response that looks good under the reward model but is not broadly useful.

This reference policy is also what makes the later DPO reparameterization possible. The paper’s key idea is that the reward can be expressed relative to the reference policy, rather than as an absolute score. That relative form is what allows the reward model to be eliminated and the preference optimization problem to be rewritten directly in terms of the policy.

# Deriving the Optimal Policy as an Exponentiated Reward Reweighting of the Reference Policy

A central result in the paper is that the solution to the KL-constrained objective has a closed form. Under a general reward function $r$, the optimal policy is
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
\]
This says that the optimal policy is obtained by reweighting the reference policy according to the exponential of the reward. Responses with larger reward are assigned larger probability, but only after being scaled by $\beta$. The smaller $\beta$ is, the more strongly reward influences the policy; the larger $\beta$ is, the more the policy remains close to the reference.

This formula is the paper’s foundational bridge between reinforcement learning and preference optimization. It shows that the KL-constrained RL problem does not merely encourage the policy to improve in some vague sense; it specifies exactly how the policy should transform the reference distribution. The reference policy provides the base distribution, and the reward exponentially tilts that distribution toward preferred responses.

The paper emphasizes that this result is not new in itself; it is a known consequence of KL-regularized reward maximization. What is new is how the authors use this form. By treating the optimal policy expression as a change of variables, they can rewrite preference learning in terms of the policy directly. That move is what ultimately leads to DPO.

# The Partition Function and Normalization Over Responses

The closed-form optimal policy includes a normalization constant:
\[
Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
\]
This partition function ensures that the resulting policy is a valid probability distribution over all possible responses $y$ for a fixed prompt $x$. Without it, the exponentiated reward reweighting would not sum to one.

Conceptually, $Z(x)$ collects the total mass of all reward-weighted responses under the reference policy. Responses with high reward contribute more to the sum, and responses with low reward contribute less. The normalization then divides by this total so that the policy remains properly normalized. The paper later observes that this same term can be viewed as a soft value function of the reference policy.

The partition function is also the source of a major practical obstacle. Although the optimal policy has a closed-form expression, the normalization term depends on a sum over all possible completions. For language models, that sum is enormous. So even though the solution is elegant on paper, directly using it in training is not straightforward.

# Interpreting the Optimal Policy as Reward-Tilted Sampling

The exponentiated form of the optimal policy gives a useful intuition: the policy is a reward-tilted version of the reference model. If the reference model assigns some response a moderate probability and the reward function rates it highly, then the optimal policy increases its probability. If the reward is low, the probability decreases. In this sense, the reward does not replace the language model; it reshapes the model’s distribution.

This interpretation is especially helpful because it keeps the role of the reference policy visible. The model does not generate responses from scratch based only on reward. Instead, it starts from the original language model distribution and then tilts that distribution toward better-aligned completions. The KL regularization makes this tilt smooth rather than extreme. So the final policy remains a language model in the same general family as the base model, but one that is biased toward responses with greater reward.

The paper’s DPO formulation later uses this exact idea in reverse. Rather than learning a reward and then converting it into a policy, DPO parameterizes the reward in terms of the policy. The paper’s slogan that the language model is “secretly a reward model” comes from this equivalence: the policy itself can encode the reward through its deviation from the reference policy.

# Why This Closed-Form Solution Is Theoretically Elegant but Practically Incomplete

The closed-form optimal policy is theoretically elegant because it solves the RLHF objective exactly. It shows that the optimization problem has a precise optimum, and it identifies the structure of that optimum. In principle, this means the task of preference optimization could be reduced to fitting a model that matches the optimal policy implied by human preferences.

But the paper explains why this exact solution is still not enough by itself. The main issue is that the reward function is not known. In RLHF, we usually estimate it from human preference comparisons using a Bradley-Terry or similar model. So before we can even talk about the optimal policy, we must fit a reward model from data. Then, to use the closed-form policy formula, we would need to evaluate the partition function $Z(x)$, which is itself intractable for language generation.

This means that the closed-form solution is more of a theoretical destination than a usable training algorithm. It reveals the structure of the answer, but not yet a practical route to computing it. The paper’s main contribution is to turn this theoretical result into a direct optimization method that avoids both explicit reward fitting and reinforcement learning.

# The Computational Difficulty of the Partition Function

The partition function is the main reason the closed-form optimum cannot be used naively in practice. It requires summing over all possible responses $y$, weighted by the reference policy and exponentiated reward. For language models, the response space is astronomically large, so this sum cannot be computed exactly.

The paper notes that even if we had an accurate estimate of the reward function, evaluating $Z(x)$ would still be expensive. This makes the straightforward use of the optimal policy formula impractical. One could imagine approximate methods, but the paper is motivated precisely by the desire to avoid a complicated RL loop and avoid sampling-heavy optimization procedures. So the partition function becomes the obstacle that turns a neat mathematical identity into an implementation challenge.

This difficulty is also why the standard RLHF pipeline remains in use. The policy is not directly optimized from the closed-form solution because the exact normalizer is unavailable. Instead, the usual workflow fits a reward model and then uses an RL algorithm such as PPO, which estimates gradients from sampled completions rather than requiring an explicit global sum over all responses.

# Why the Standard Reward-First, RL-Second Pipeline Persists Despite the Closed-Form Optimum

The standard RLHF pipeline persists because it offers a practical workaround for the fact that the exact optimum is analytically known but computationally hard to exploit. The procedure is: first fit a reward model from preference data, and then use reinforcement learning to adjust the policy so that it maximizes reward while staying close to the reference policy. This two-stage setup avoids the need to compute the full partition function directly.

In the paper’s view, however, this pipeline is unnecessarily indirect. It introduces complexity in the form of multiple models, sampling during training, and reinforcement learning instability. The reward model must be trained separately, then held fixed or updated carefully, and the policy optimization must be done through an RL method like PPO. The paper argues that this machinery is a consequence of the way the problem is usually framed, not a fundamental necessity.

The closed-form optimum is therefore the conceptual pivot of the paper. Once one recognizes that the RL-constrained optimum can be written as an exponentiated reweighting of the reference policy, the next question becomes whether the preference-learning objective itself can be rewritten in terms of the policy. DPO answers yes. It takes the mathematical structure of the optimal policy and uses it to bypass the reward-first, RL-second pipeline entirely, replacing it with a direct classification-style loss.
