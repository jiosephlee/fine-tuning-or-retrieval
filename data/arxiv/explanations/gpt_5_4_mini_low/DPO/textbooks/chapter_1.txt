\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 1: 1. Why Preference Alignment Matters for Language Models

# Capabilities and limitations of large unsupervised language models

Large unsupervised language models learn by training on massive text datasets, and the paper emphasizes that this training can produce surprisingly broad capabilities. These models acquire world knowledge and some reasoning skills simply from exposure to large-scale data, and they can often complete tasks zero-shot or with only a few examples in the prompt. In other words, pretraining makes the model generally capable in a wide range of settings without being explicitly taught each task one by one.

At the same time, the paper stresses an important limitation: the pretraining objective is completely unsupervised, so the model is not directly trained to follow human preferences or to behave in a carefully controlled way. The model learns from the full diversity of the training corpus, which contains many different goals, skill levels, and styles of writing. As a result, the model may absorb not only the useful patterns we want it to use, but also patterns that are undesirable when the model is deployed as an assistant.

This creates a gap between what the model can do and what we want it to do. A pretrained model may know many facts, understand common errors, or imitate many styles of text, yet still produce outputs that are not aligned with the user’s goals. The paper’s central motivation is that acquiring capabilities is not enough; we also need a way to select the right behavior from among all the behaviors the model has learned.

# The difference between knowledge/ability and desired behavior

A key idea in the paper is that a language model’s knowledge and abilities are broader than the behavior we want from it. The model may have learned many useful patterns from human text, but those patterns are not automatically organized around helpfulness, safety, or fidelity to user intent. The paper explains that selecting the model’s desired responses and behavior from its wide knowledge base is crucial for building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable.

This distinction matters because a model can be aware of something without being a good generator of it in context. For example, a coding assistant might need to recognize common mistakes in order to help correct them, but that does not mean it should reproduce those mistakes when generating code. Likewise, a model may have learned that a misconception is widespread, but the correct behavior is not to repeat that misconception as though it were true. The challenge is therefore not simply to make the model knowledgeable, but to make it behave in a way that reflects the task and the user’s needs.

The paper frames alignment as the process of steering the model toward those desired responses. This is not a change in the model’s basic ability to represent language, but a change in how those abilities are used at generation time. In that sense, alignment is about selection and control, not just learning more text patterns.

# Examples of misalignment: coding mistakes, misconceptions, undesirable behaviors

The paper gives concrete examples to show why raw pretraining is not enough. One example is a coding assistant. Even if the model has seen many code examples and can understand common programming mistakes, we still want it to bias its outputs toward high-quality code. The important point is that the model should use its knowledge of mistakes to avoid them, not to imitate them.

Another example concerns misconceptions. A language model may know that a false belief is common among people, perhaps because it appears frequently in text. But if users ask whether the belief is true, the model should not answer that it is true just because many people believe it. The paper highlights this as a basic mismatch between distributional learning and desired behavior: frequency in the training data is not the same as correctness or usefulness in deployment.

These examples show that a pretrained model can be simultaneously competent and unreliable. It may contain the relevant knowledge internally, but without further training it may not consistently produce the responses humans would want. This is why the paper treats preference alignment as a separate and necessary stage after pretraining.

# Why behavior steering is critical for safety, usability, and controllability

The paper argues that controlling model behavior is essential for building useful AI systems. A model that is powerful but poorly controlled is hard to deploy safely, because it may produce responses that are unhelpful, unsafe, or inconsistent with user intent. In contrast, a model whose behavior is shaped by human preferences can be directed toward helpfulness and harmlessness more reliably.

Usability is also a major concern. Even when a model has the capability to solve tasks, it is much more useful if it can present those capabilities in the style and format people want. The paper emphasizes that aligning the model with human preferences improves its practical value because it becomes more responsive to the kinds of outputs humans judge to be high quality.

Controllability is the third reason. If users or developers want a model to behave in a particular way, they need a training method that can shift the model’s outputs in a targeted direction. The paper’s examples make clear that broad pretraining alone does not provide that control. Preference alignment is presented as the mechanism for narrowing the model’s behavior toward the subset of responses that humans prefer.

# Human demonstrations vs. human preference judgments

The paper distinguishes between two ways of teaching a model after pretraining. One is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations: the model is trained on examples of high-quality responses, and it learns to imitate those responses directly. This approach can be effective and is used in instruction tuning, where the model is trained on instructions paired with human-written completions.

The other approach is learning from human preferences. Rather than asking people to provide a single ideal answer, the training process asks them to compare two model outputs and choose the better one. The paper notes that relative judgments of response quality are often easier to collect than expert demonstrations, which makes preference data a practical and scalable supervision signal. Preference judgments can be used in many domains, including translation, summarization, story generation, and instruction following.

The importance of this distinction is that preferences can be collected even when producing an ideal demonstration is difficult. A human judge may not want to write a full answer, but may still be able to say which of two candidate outputs is better. That makes preference data attractive for large-scale alignment, especially when the goal is to refine an already capable language model rather than teach it from scratch.

# The promise and practical burden of RLHF

The paper identifies reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, as the most successful class of preference-based alignment methods. In the standard RLHF pipeline, a reward model is first trained to predict which responses humans prefer. Then a language model is optimized with reinforcement learning to maximize that learned reward while staying close to the original model. This two-stage procedure is designed to make the model more aligned with human judgments.

RLHF has produced impressive models, and the paper acknowledges that it has been effective in conversational and coding settings. However, it also emphasizes that the pipeline is complicated. It usually involves training multiple language models, learning a reward model, and then running reinforcement learning with the policy sampled inside the training loop. This makes the process computationally expensive and practically unstable compared with straightforward supervised learning.

The paper’s motivation is therefore not to reject preference learning, but to simplify it. The challenge is to keep the benefits of RLHF while removing the complexity of fitting a reward model and then running a separate RL procedure. This is what leads to the paper’s central proposal, Direct Preference Optimization.

# The paper’s central claim: RLHF-style optimization can be reduced to a simple classification objective

The central claim of the paper is that the RLHF objective used by prior methods can be optimized exactly using a simple binary classification loss. Instead of training a reward model and then using reinforcement learning to optimize a policy against that reward, the paper shows that the same preference-learning problem can be reparameterized so that the policy itself directly represents the implicit reward structure.

This is the key conceptual shift. In the paper’s formulation, the model is not first trained to predict a separate reward function and then optimized in a second stage. Rather, the policy is trained directly on preference comparisons using a binary cross-entropy objective. The resulting method is called Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO. It keeps the same underlying preference model as RLHF, but it changes the parameterization so that the optimal policy can be expressed in closed form.

The significance of this claim is practical as well as theoretical. If the preference-learning objective can be written as a classification problem over chosen and rejected responses, then the training pipeline becomes far simpler. The paper argues that this approach is stable, lightweight, and effective, while still matching or outperforming more complicated RLHF methods. In the broader context of the chapter, this means that preference alignment need not require a costly reinforcement learning loop; it can be achieved by directly optimizing the model’s preferences over outputs.
