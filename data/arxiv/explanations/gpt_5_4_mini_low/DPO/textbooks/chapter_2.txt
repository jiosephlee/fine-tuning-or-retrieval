\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 2: 2. Background: From Instruction Tuning to RLHF

# Instruction tuning and its role in improving downstream behavior

Large unsupervised language models are trained on massive text corpora and can learn broad world knowledge as well as some reasoning and completion abilities. However, a pretrained model is not yet well aligned with what users actually want it to do. The paper emphasizes an important distinction: a model may be able to *understand* many patterns in its training data, but that does not mean it will reliably produce the *desired response* in a given setting. For example, a model may know about common coding mistakes, but when used as a coding assistant we want it to produce high-quality code rather than imitate mediocre code that appears frequently in the data. Likewise, the model may know a common misconception, but that is not the same as wanting it to confidently repeat the misconception in an answer.

One way to improve this behavior is instruction tuning. In this procedure, a pretrained language model is fine-tuned on datasets of instructions paired with human-written completions. The goal is to teach the model to follow tasks more effectively and to generalize beyond the exact examples seen during fine-tuning. The paper notes that this approach has been successful at making language models more useful, especially because instruction-tuned models can often respond to instructions that were not explicitly included in the instruction-tuning set. In the broader alignment story described by the paper, instruction tuning is an important intermediate step between raw pretraining and preference-based alignment methods.

The role of instruction tuning in the paper is mainly historical and practical. It explains why an LM can already be somewhat capable before RLHF begins: the model is first brought into a helpful, task-oriented regime by supervised learning on good examples. That said, instruction tuning alone does not solve the more difficult problem of selecting the right behavior from the model’s broad range of capabilities. The paper’s central motivation is that we still need a way to bias the model toward the behaviors humans prefer, not merely toward the behaviors humans happened to demonstrate.

# Why relative preferences are often easier to collect than expert demonstrations

The paper highlights that, although supervised fine-tuning on expert demonstrations is straightforward, it is often easier to gather *relative judgments* about quality than to collect perfect demonstrations. Rather than asking a human annotator to write an ideal answer from scratch, it can be simpler to show two candidate responses and ask which one is better. This is especially useful in open-ended language tasks where there may be many acceptable completions, and where ranking candidates may be easier than authoring a complete response.

This preference-collection setup is central to the paper’s framing of alignment. Human feedback is not treated as an absolute scalar reward provided directly by the annotator; instead, the data usually consists of comparisons of the form “response $y_w$ is preferred to response $y_l$ given prompt $x$.” The paper argues that this type of data is attractive because it can be collected at scale and because it captures human judgments about quality, helpfulness, safety, or other desired attributes in a relatively lightweight form.

The paper also notes that preference datasets are often sampled from a model such as the supervised fine-tuned model, so the comparisons reflect responses the model is actually likely to produce. This matters because the preferences are meant to guide the model toward better behavior within the model’s own response distribution, not just in some abstract space of possible answers. In this sense, relative preferences are not merely a cheaper labeling format; they are a natural fit for aligning a generative model whose outputs are themselves sampled strings.

# The standard RLHF pipeline in language modeling

The paper reviews the standard RLHF pipeline as it is commonly used in language modeling. This pipeline usually has three phases. First is supervised fine-tuning, where a pretrained language model is trained on a set of high-quality task-specific examples, yielding a model denoted $\pi^\text{SFT}$. This gives the model a better starting point for later alignment training.

The second phase is reward modeling. Here, the supervised fine-tuned model is used to generate pairs of candidate responses for each prompt. Human labelers then indicate which response they prefer. These pairwise judgments are assumed to arise from an underlying latent reward function $r^*(x,y)$ that reflects the true desirability of a response $y$ to a prompt $x$. A reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ is trained to predict these preferences. The paper uses the Bradley-Terry model as the standard example: the probability that one response is preferred over another is modeled as a logistic function of the difference in their rewards.

The third phase is reinforcement learning fine-tuning. Once a reward model has been learned, the language model policy is optimized to generate responses with high estimated reward, while remaining close to the reference policy, usually the initial supervised fine-tuned model. The paper writes this as a KL-regularized objective, where the model maximizes expected reward but is penalized for drifting too far from the reference distribution. This KL constraint is important because it helps prevent the policy from exploiting weaknesses in the reward model and helps preserve output diversity.

In the standard RLHF approach described in the paper, the policy optimization step is typically carried out with reinforcement learning algorithms such as PPO. The reward used in practice combines the learned reward model with a KL penalty against the reference model. This is the conventional two-stage logic of RLHF: first learn what humans prefer, then use RL to push the language model toward those preferences.

# Applications of preference-based fine-tuning: summarization, dialogue, translation, storytelling

The paper situates preference-based fine-tuning as a broadly useful technique for tasks where output quality is subjective or difficult to specify with a single supervised target. One major example is summarization. In summarization, there may be many summaries that capture the same document differently, and human preference data can reflect which summary is more faithful, concise, or useful. The paper cites work showing that preference-based fine-tuning improves summarization quality, and its own experiments use the Reddit TL;DR dataset as a key benchmark.

Dialogue is another central application. In open-ended conversation, a response may need to be helpful, engaging, safe, and contextually appropriate all at once. Preference data allows annotators to express which of two candidate responses is better overall, even if there is no uniquely correct answer. The paper uses the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset as an example of this kind of preference supervision.

The related literature also includes translation and storytelling. The paper notes that preference-based fine-tuning has improved proficiency in translation and story generation, again because these domains often admit multiple acceptable outputs but humans can still reliably rank them. The common theme is that relative judgments are often more natural than exact target outputs for open-ended generation problems.

This broad applicability matters for the paper’s argument because DPO is not presented as a narrow trick for one benchmark. Instead, it is framed as a general method for learning from preference data in the same family of tasks where RLHF has already been shown to work: summarization, dialogue, and other open-ended text generation settings.

# Synthetic feedback and constitutional approaches

The paper also discusses a related line of work that uses synthetic feedback rather than fully human-labeled preferences. In these approaches, a language model already trained with human feedback is used to generate additional preference data. This can be done for targeted attributes such as safety or harmlessness. The human role in this setting may be reduced to providing a rubric or textual guidance that steers the model’s annotations, rather than labeling every comparison directly.

This family of methods is associated in the paper with constitutional approaches. The key idea is that instead of collecting large quantities of fine-grained human preference labels, one can use a model to produce judgments according to a written policy or constitution. The paper treats this as part of a larger convergence between language-model training and general preference learning: humans may specify the principles, and models may help scale the annotation process.

The reason this matters in the background section is that it shows the flexibility of the preference-learning paradigm. Human feedback does not have to be limited to direct pairwise annotation. Instead, the broader RLHF ecosystem includes both human preference data and model-assisted feedback systems. DPO is presented against this backdrop as a way to optimize directly from preference data, regardless of whether those preferences come from humans or are generated through a related feedback process.

# Relevant reinforcement learning methods: REINFORCE, PPO, and variants

The standard RLHF pipeline requires an optimization method for updating the language model according to the reward model. The paper mentions REINFORCE, PPO, and variants as the most common choices. These methods treat language generation as a reinforcement learning problem, where each generated response receives a reward and the policy is adjusted to increase the expected reward.

REINFORCE is the classic policy-gradient method. PPO is the more widely used modern algorithm in RLHF because it is designed to make policy updates more stable by limiting how far each update can move the policy. The paper emphasizes PPO in particular because it is the most common choice in language-model RLHF pipelines. When RLHF methods are described in practice, PPO is often used to maximize a reward signal from the learned reward model while keeping the policy near the original supervised fine-tuned policy.

The paper’s discussion is careful to present these algorithms as part of the existing RLHF toolchain rather than as the main subject of study. Their role is to show how the conventional approach solves the preference-alignment problem indirectly: a reward model is trained first, and then RL is used to optimize the policy. DPO will later be introduced as a way to bypass this two-stage optimization while still capturing the same underlying objective.

# Connections to preference-based reinforcement learning and contextual dueling bandits

The paper places RLHF in a wider research context by connecting it to preference-based reinforcement learning and contextual dueling bandits. In both of these areas, learning is based on comparisons rather than absolute reward labels. The core idea is that an agent can learn what is better by observing pairwise or ranked outcomes, even if it never observes a numeric reward directly.

In contextual dueling bandits, the learner chooses among actions in a context and receives preference feedback comparing actions rather than scalar rewards. The paper notes that theoretical analysis in this setting often introduces the notion of a von Neumann winner, which is a policy that achieves at least a $50\%$ win rate against any other policy. This is a useful concept in the bandit literature, though the paper stresses that contextual dueling bandits typically operate online, whereas language-model preference learning usually happens offline from a fixed preference dataset.

Preference-based RL is another related line of work in which preferences arise from some hidden scoring function. Existing algorithms in that setting often estimate the latent score or reward first and then optimize it. The paper’s key point is that DPO instead uses a single-stage policy-learning approach that directly optimizes the policy from preferences, without explicitly learning a separate reward function as an intermediate object.

These connections help clarify that RLHF is not an isolated invention but part of a broader family of methods for learning from comparisons. DPO is motivated by this literature, but the paper’s contribution is to show how the RLHF objective itself can be rewritten in a way that avoids the standard reward-model-plus-RL pipeline.

# What makes RLHF difficult in practice: multiple models, sampling-in-the-loop, instability, compute cost

A major reason the paper proposes DPO is that standard RLHF is practically difficult. The conventional pipeline involves training multiple models: the original supervised fine-tuned policy, a reward model, and then a policy optimized by reinforcement learning. Each stage adds complexity, and the overall workflow is more elaborate than ordinary supervised learning.

Another difficulty is that RLHF training often requires sampling from the current language model during optimization. This means the model is not simply trained on a fixed dataset in a single pass. Instead, generation is part of the training loop, which makes the process computationally heavier and harder to manage. The paper describes this as a major practical cost of the RLHF pipeline.

Instability is also a serious issue. Because language generation is discrete, the RL objective is not directly differentiable with respect to sampled outputs, so one typically relies on policy-gradient methods and additional variance-reduction or stabilization tricks. The paper notes that this can make training brittle. Even with a learned reward model, the policy can drift too far from the distribution on which the reward model is accurate, or it can collapse toward a small set of high-reward responses if not properly constrained.

The KL penalty is intended to reduce these problems, but it introduces additional tuning and optimization challenges. The paper argues that the standard RLHF setup often requires significant hyperparameter tuning and careful engineering. In contrast, DPO is introduced as a simpler alternative: it avoids explicit reward modeling and RL training loops, while still targeting the same KL-constrained preference objective. This reduction in complexity is one of the central motivations for the method developed in the rest of the paper.
