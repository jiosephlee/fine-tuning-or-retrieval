\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 12: 12. Results on Controlled Sentiment Generation

#
The controlled sentiment setting is the cleanest experiment in the paper because the reward signal is not hidden inside human judgments or a learned reward model. Instead, the authors use a pre-trained sentiment classifier as the ground-truth reward function. This makes the task especially useful for understanding whether an algorithm is actually optimizing the intended RLHF objective, rather than merely benefiting from imperfections in reward estimation or evaluation.

The main idea is to isolate the tradeoff that RLHF is supposed to manage: the policy should move toward higher-reward outputs, but not by drifting too far from the reference model. In this experiment, that tradeoff can be measured directly, since the sentiment classifier supplies the reward and the divergence from the reference policy can be computed explicitly. The result is a setting where methods can be compared on a shared, interpretable reward–KL frontier.

#
The task begins with prompts $x$ drawn from prefixes of movie reviews in the IMDb dataset. The language model must continue each prefix with a response $y$ that expresses positive sentiment. To build a preference dataset for training, the authors do not rely on human annotations. Instead, they generate pairs of continuations and label them using the sentiment classifier: if one continuation has a higher probability of being positive than the other, it is treated as the preferred completion.

This setup creates a controlled preference-learning problem that still resembles the structure of RLHF. There is a prompt, two competing completions, and an ordering between them, but the ordering is derived from a known reward function. That choice allows the authors to test whether DPO and competing methods can recover the same kind of policy improvement that RLHF seeks, while keeping the evaluation fully grounded in the true reward.

#
For this experiment, the authors fine-tune GPT-2-large on IMDb reviews to obtain the supervised fine-tuning model, or SFT model. This SFT model serves as the starting point for all subsequent preference-learning methods in the controlled sentiment setting. In the language of the paper, it is both the reference model and the base policy from which preference optimization proceeds.

The use of GPT-2-large matters because it gives the experiment a realistic language model baseline while still keeping the task manageable. The model has enough capacity to exhibit meaningful changes in sentiment and style, and the controlled setting makes it possible to see how those changes affect reward and divergence. Because the experiment is small enough to study carefully, it becomes a strong test of whether a training method truly optimizes the RLHF objective efficiently.

#
The preference pairs in this experiment are constructed by sampling completions from the model and comparing them with a sentiment classifier. For each prompt, the pair $(y_w, y_l)$ is defined so that the preferred completion $y_w$ is the one with higher predicted positive sentiment, meaning $p(\text{positive}\mid x, y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x, y_l)$. In this way, the reward signal is not noisy human preference but a direct, known ordering based on sentiment.

This construction is important because it removes ambiguity about what the target behavior is. The model is not being asked to approximate a vague or subjective notion of quality; it is being pushed toward a clearly specified objective. That makes the setting ideal for comparing algorithms that claim to optimize a reward while preserving closeness to the reference model.

#
The main evaluation tool in this section is the reward–KL frontier. This means the authors do not look only at reward, and they do not look only at KL divergence from the reference policy. Instead, they examine the set of outcomes each method can achieve across different degrees of conservativeness. A better method is one that achieves higher reward for the same KL, or equivalently lower KL for the same reward.

This is the right metric for the RLHF objective used throughout the paper because the objective is explicitly a balance between reward maximization and a penalty for drifting away from the reference policy. A method that increases reward by a large amount but only by moving very far from the reference model is not necessarily preferable. The frontier captures this balance directly and therefore gives a more faithful picture of optimization quality than reward alone.

#
To trace out the reward–KL frontier, the authors run multiple training sweeps for each method. For PPO, they vary the target KL in $\{3,6,9,12\}$. For DPO, they sweep $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$. For Unlikelihood, they sweep $\alpha \in \{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$. For Preferred-FT, they use different random seeds. Altogether, this gives 22 runs.

After every 100 training steps until convergence, each policy is evaluated on test prompts. The authors compute the average reward under the true sentiment classifier and the average sequence-level KL divergence from the reference policy. This systematic sweep is crucial because it lets the paper compare methods at several operating points rather than at just one hand-picked hyperparameter setting. It also shows whether a method is robust or whether it needs careful tuning to work well.

#
The KL divergence used here is sequence-level KL, defined as the sum of the per-timestep KL divergences. In other words, the comparison is not made token by token in isolation, but over the full generated continuation. This is appropriate because the behavior of interest is the quality of the whole completion, and the RLHF objective itself is about controlling the overall deviation of the policy from the reference distribution.

This quantity plays a central role in the interpretation of the results. A method that keeps KL low while increasing reward is more efficient than a method that only gets high reward by moving far away from the reference model. Since DPO is designed to optimize the same KL-constrained reward objective as PPO-based RLHF, sequence-level KL is part of the fundamental comparison.

#
The main result of the controlled sentiment experiment is that DPO produces the most efficient frontier. Among all the methods tested, it achieves the highest reward while still maintaining low KL divergence from the reference policy. In the paper’s phrasing, DPO’s reward/KL tradeoff strictly dominates PPO’s.

This is a strong result because DPO and PPO are meant to optimize the same underlying objective. The fact that DPO does so more efficiently suggests that the direct preference optimization formulation is not merely a simpler implementation of a different goal; it is a better way to reach the same goal. The frontier shows that DPO can extract more reward improvement per unit of deviation from the base model.

#
The comparison to PPO trained with learned rewards is important because it reflects the standard RLHF pipeline. In that pipeline, a reward model is fit to preference data and then PPO is used to optimize the policy against that learned reward. DPO outperforms this version of PPO in the controlled sentiment task, showing that the direct objective can be more effective than the conventional two-stage procedure.

The authors also compare DPO to PPO-GT, which is an oracle variant that uses the ground-truth reward function from the sentiment classifier. This comparison is especially informative because it removes the reward-modeling error that usually burdens PPO. Even in this idealized case, DPO still achieves a better reward–KL frontier. That means DPO is not merely benefiting from a better reward estimate; it is outperforming an algorithm that already has access to the correct reward.

#
The fact that DPO outperforms PPO-GT is especially notable because it is a harder comparison than the usual one. Normally, PPO-based RLHF is limited by the quality of the learned reward model, so a method that avoids reward-model errors might be expected to do better simply because it has a cleaner signal. But here PPO-GT already has the true reward, so the comparison isolates the optimization procedure itself.

In that sense, the result suggests that the advantage of DPO is not only about simplifying the pipeline. It also appears to be a better optimizer for the KL-constrained preference objective. This is one of the strongest pieces of evidence in the paper that the direct preference formulation is not just convenient, but genuinely effective.

#
The paper’s interpretation is that DPO is more efficient at translating preference data into useful policy updates. Because it directly optimizes a classification-style objective derived from the preference model, it avoids the complications of sampling-based RL. The controlled sentiment results indicate that this simplicity does not come at the cost of performance. On the contrary, the method improves both reward and KL behavior.

This result has a broader implication for the paper’s thesis. If DPO can dominate PPO even when PPO receives ground-truth reward, then the main bottleneck in RLHF may not be reward inference alone. The optimization procedure itself matters, and a direct objective over preferences can be a better way to align a model with the desired behavior. In this controlled setting, DPO therefore serves as a clean demonstration that the paper’s central idea works in practice.
