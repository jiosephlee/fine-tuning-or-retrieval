\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 7: 7. Understanding the DPO Update Rule

# Gradient of the DPO objective

To understand Direct Preference Optimization at a mechanistic level, it helps to look directly at the gradient of its loss. DPO is not just a clever reparameterization of RLHF; it defines a very specific update rule that moves the language model in response to each preference example. The central object is the DPO loss

\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\]

The paper shows that when you differentiate this objective, the update has a particularly intuitive structure. The gradient increases the probability of the preferred completion $y_w$ and decreases the probability of the dispreferred completion $y_l$. But it does not do so uniformly across all examples. Instead, each example is scaled by a factor determined by the model’s current implicit reward ordering of the two completions.

This matters because DPO is not merely “make winners more likely and losers less likely.” It is a preference-aware learning rule that checks how well the current policy already ranks the pair. If the policy already gets the ordering right, the update is smaller; if it gets the ordering wrong, the update is larger. That structure is visible in the gradient form given in the paper, where the loss is weighted by a sigmoid term involving the difference between the implicit rewards of $y_l$ and $y_w$.

# Increasing the likelihood of preferred completions

The most basic effect of the DPO update is that it pushes up the log probability of the preferred completion. In the gradient expression, this appears as a positive contribution involving $\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_w \mid x)$. Intuitively, whenever the model sees a prompt $x$ and a human-preferred answer $y_w$, DPO makes the model more likely to produce that answer in the future.

This is the part of DPO that resembles ordinary supervised learning. If we ignored everything else, the algorithm would look like a direct attempt to imitate the preferred response. But DPO is more careful than that. It does not simply copy preferred outputs indiscriminately, because the update is always mediated by the comparison with a dispreferred completion and by the reference policy. So although the preferred answer is reinforced, that reinforcement is controlled and contextualized.

The paper emphasizes that this is done in a way that is directly tied to the policy itself. There is no separately trained reward model whose output is later consumed by reinforcement learning. Instead, the policy parameters are adjusted so that the policy’s own implied reward representation becomes more consistent with human preferences.

# Decreasing the likelihood of dispreferred completions

DPO also pushes down the probability of the dispreferred completion $y_l$. This is the other side of the same update. The gradient contains a term involving $-\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_l \mid x)$, which means the model is trained not only to favor the winner but also to suppress the loser.

This winner-loser asymmetry is important. Preference data is inherently comparative: a human did not label $y_w$ as absolutely good in isolation, but rather preferred it over $y_l$ for the same prompt $x$. DPO therefore uses that comparison directly. The algorithm learns a relative ranking, not an absolute target score.

The paper’s interpretation is that DPO behaves like a classification loss over pairs. The model is encouraged to assign higher relative probability to the chosen completion than to the rejected one. This pairwise nature is what makes DPO aligned with the structure of preference data, which is why the authors frame it as a form of stable preference-aware classification.

# The implicit reward: beta times the policy/reference log-ratio

A key insight in the paper is that DPO can be understood through an implicit reward function defined by the policy relative to the reference model. This reward is

\[
\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}.
\]

This quantity is not trained separately as a reward model. Rather, it is induced by the policy itself. The reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$ plays the role of an anchor, so the reward is not just about making a response likely in absolute terms. It is about making it more likely than the reference model would have made it.

This is exactly what gives DPO its connection to RLHF. The paper shows that the KL-constrained RL objective has an optimal policy of the form

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\]

and by rearranging this relation, the reward can be written in terms of the policy and reference policy. DPO uses that relationship directly. So the policy network is doing double duty: it is the language model, but it also implicitly defines the reward signal used for preference learning.

# Dynamic importance weighting by preference misranking

The paper highlights that DPO does not simply apply the same push to every preference pair. Instead, it includes a dynamic weighting factor that depends on whether the current implicit reward model is ranking the pair correctly. In the gradient, that weight is a sigmoid of the difference between the implicit rewards of the loser and winner.

This means that if the model currently thinks the dispreferred completion is better than the preferred one, the update becomes large. If the model already places the preferred completion above the dispreferred one by a comfortable margin, the update becomes smaller. This is a very natural learning rule: the model spends more effort on its mistakes and less effort on examples it already understands.

The paper describes this as a per-example importance weight. That terminology is important because it explains why DPO is more stable than a naive pairwise update. The algorithm does not blindly reinforce every winner and punish every loser equally. It adapts the size of the update to the model’s current error on that example.

# Why examples with more incorrect reward ordering receive larger updates

The dynamic weighting can be understood as focusing learning where the policy’s implicit reward ordering is most wrong. Suppose the model assigns a higher implicit reward to the loser than to the winner. In that case, the sigmoid factor in the gradient is large, so the update is strong. This causes DPO to correct misranked examples aggressively.

By contrast, if the model already assigns the winner a much higher implicit reward than the loser, then the sigmoid factor becomes small. That means the example contributes little to further learning. The update is therefore self-regulating: it is strongest precisely when the current policy most badly disagrees with the preference data.

This behavior is one of the paper’s central explanations for DPO’s stability. A naive objective that simply increases the probability of winners and decreases the probability of losers would not distinguish between easy and hard examples. DPO does distinguish them, and that distinction prevents the model from being pushed too hard in directions that are already saturated or that could destabilize training.

# The role of beta as a conservativeness or KL-control parameter

The parameter $\beta$ plays a dual role in the paper’s exposition. It appears in the implicit reward definition and in the DPO objective, and it controls how strongly the policy is allowed to deviate from the reference model. In the original RLHF formulation, $\beta$ is the coefficient on the KL penalty against the reference policy. That means it regulates conservativeness: larger values correspond to stronger pressure to stay close to the reference model.

This interpretation carries over directly to DPO. Because DPO is derived from the KL-constrained RLHF objective, $\beta$ determines how aggressively the policy can move away from $\pi_\text{ref}$. The gradient weighting also depends on $\beta$, so it affects not only the overall scale of the implicit reward, but also how strongly each misranking drives an update.

The paper emphasizes that this connection to KL control is important for stability. A method that ignores the reference policy could overfit the preference data by moving too far from the original model distribution. DPO keeps the reference in the objective, so the policy learns preferences while remaining tethered to the base model.

# Comparison to naive probability-ratio or unlikelihood-style objectives

The paper explicitly compares DPO to simpler approaches that might seem reasonable at first glance. One such idea is a naive probability-ratio objective that merely increases the ratio $\pi(y_w \mid x)/\pi(y_l \mid x)$. Another is an unlikelihood-style objective that maximizes the preferred completion and minimizes the dispreferred one, possibly with a coefficient on the unlikelihood term.

The authors argue that these simpler methods can be unstable or degenerate because they lack DPO’s dynamic weighting and its proper normalization by the reference policy. DPO is not just a winner/loser classifier on raw probabilities. It is a classifier built from the ratio of the current policy to a fixed reference model, with a weighting that depends on how wrong the current implicit reward ordering is.

This distinction is not cosmetic. The paper notes that a naive version of the method without the weighting coefficient can cause degeneration. In other words, if the update is too simplistic, the language model can drift into poor behavior rather than improving reliably. DPO avoids that by preserving the structure inherited from the KL-constrained RLHF objective.

# Why naive winner/loser updates can cause degeneration

The failure mode of naive winner/loser updates is that they treat all preference pairs as equally informative, regardless of how the model currently behaves on them. If the model is repeatedly pushed to increase winners and decrease losers without checking the current ordering, the updates can become overly aggressive. This can cause the language model to collapse or degenerate in the paper’s terminology.

The reference policy is what keeps DPO grounded. Because the loss is built from the log-ratio $\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$, the model is not just learning to assign high absolute probability to whatever is preferred. It is learning relative changes from a stable baseline. That is much harder to game than a raw maximize-winner-minimize-loser rule.

The paper also connects this stability to the fact that DPO can be seen as optimizing a reparameterized Bradley-Terry preference model. That reparameterization preserves the class of representable rewards, but it chooses a form that makes the optimal policy analytically tractable. The result is a stable update rule that avoids the practical difficulties of sampling-based RL optimization.

# Interpretation of DPO as stable preference-aware classification

The cleanest way to think about DPO, according to the paper, is as a stable classification objective over preference pairs. For each prompt $x$ and pair of completions $(y_w, y_l)$, DPO asks the policy to assign higher relative likelihood to the winner than to the loser, but it does so through the policy/reference log-ratio and the implicit reward induced by that ratio.

This makes DPO feel like supervised learning, but with an important twist: the labels are preference comparisons, and the model is trained relative to a reference policy. That is why the paper says DPO can solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple binary cross-entropy objective. It is classification, but classification that is carefully aligned with the KL-constrained policy optimization problem.

The main takeaway from the update rule is that DPO learns from preferences in a way that is both direct and controlled. It increases the chance of preferred completions, decreases the chance of dispreferred ones, concentrates learning on the examples the model gets wrong, and uses $\beta$ and the reference policy to keep the optimization conservative. This combination is what makes DPO both effective and stable.
