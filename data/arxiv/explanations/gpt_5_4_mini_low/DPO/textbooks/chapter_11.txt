\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 11: 11. Experimental Design and Evaluation Methodology

# Overview of the paper’s three experimental domains

The paper evaluates Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO, in three distinct text-generation settings: controlled sentiment generation, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. These three domains are chosen to cover both a setting where the reward function is known and can be measured directly, and settings where the true notion of quality is only available through human preference data. That split is important because it lets the authors test two different questions at once: whether DPO can optimize the same constrained objective as RLHF methods, and whether it remains effective on realistic preference-learning tasks where human judgment is the only practical benchmark.

The controlled sentiment task is the most diagnostic experiment. Because the reward is synthetic and known, the authors can evaluate not just how good the generated text is, but how well each algorithm trades off reward against deviation from the reference model. Summarization and dialogue are closer to real RLHF use cases. In those settings, the paper cannot compute a true reward directly, so it relies on preference-based evaluation, using GPT-4 as a proxy judge and validating that choice with a human study. Together, these experiments are designed to show both theoretical fidelity and practical usefulness.

# Controlled sentiment generation on IMDb

In the controlled sentiment experiment, the input $x$ is a prefix of a movie review from the IMDb dataset, and the model must generate a continuation $y$ with positive sentiment. The key point is that the task is constructed to be controllable: the model should not merely continue the review in any plausible way, but should steer toward positive sentiment in its output. This makes it a useful testbed for alignment-style training, because the desired behavior is easy to describe and can be judged automatically.

To build the supervised fine-tuning model for this setting, the paper fine-tunes GPT-2-large on reviews from the IMDb training split until convergence. This SFT model then serves as the starting point for the preference-learning methods. Because the experiment is synthetic, the authors can generate preference pairs themselves rather than relying on human raters. That makes the task especially useful for measuring how faithfully DPO and competing methods optimize the intended objective.

The sentiment task is also the one place where the paper can plot an actual frontier of reward versus KL divergence from the reference model. That is crucial because RLHF-style methods are supposed to improve reward while staying close to the base policy. A method that gets slightly more reward by drifting much farther away is not necessarily better. The controlled sentiment setup therefore acts as a clean laboratory for comparing optimization behavior rather than just final text quality.

# Summarization on Reddit TL;DR

The summarization task uses forum posts from Reddit as inputs and asks the model to produce summaries capturing the main points. The paper follows prior work and uses the Reddit TL;DR dataset together with human preference annotations collected by earlier authors. In this setting, the task is not about sentiment control but about generating summaries that humans judge to be better, more useful, and more faithful to the source post.

For summarization, the paper uses an SFT model already fine-tuned on human-written forum post summaries. This is an important difference from some other tasks because a suitable starting model is available, so DPO and PPO can both begin from the same reference policy. The human preference dataset was gathered on samples from a similarly trained SFT model, which means the preference annotations are well matched to the type of model being optimized.

Summarization is also where the paper emphasizes that standard automatic metrics are not enough. The authors point out that ROUGE can be poorly correlated with human preferences, so they do not treat it as the primary evaluation criterion. Instead, they judge systems by win rate against reference summaries, using GPT-4 as the evaluator and then validating that choice with human judgments. This is one of the clearest examples in the paper of why preference learning requires evaluation methods that reflect human choice rather than surface overlap.

# Single-turn dialogue on Anthropic Helpful and Harmless

The dialogue task uses the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dataset, where each example consists of a human query and two candidate responses produced by a large language model, together with a preference label indicating the human-preferred response. The task is single-turn: the model receives one user input and must produce a helpful, engaging answer. The range of queries is broad, spanning everything from factual questions to interpersonal advice.

This task is particularly valuable because it reflects the kind of general assistant behavior that RLHF methods are meant to improve. The quality target is not just linguistic fluency but helpfulness as judged by humans. The paper uses a subset of the test split with one interaction step and evaluates different methods against the preferred responses in that test set. Because dialogue is open-ended, the evaluation must be preference-based rather than based on a direct reward signal.

A notable design detail is that no pre-trained SFT model is available for this task in the way it is for summarization. To address that, the authors start with an off-the-shelf language model and fine-tune it only on the preferred completions to form a reference model. That reference then becomes the starting point for DPO. This makes the dialogue experiment especially informative about how DPO behaves when the reference model has to be constructed from preference data rather than inherited from an existing task-specific SFT model.

# How synthetic preferences are generated in the sentiment task

The sentiment experiment does not use human annotators. Instead, the paper generates preference labels with a pre-trained sentiment classifier. For each prompt prefix, candidate completions are assigned preferences according to whether the classifier judges one completion to have higher positive sentiment than the other. In other words, a pair $(y_w, y_l)$ is created by comparing the classifier’s scores, with the preferred completion being the one that better satisfies the sentiment objective.

This synthetic setup is valuable because it removes ambiguity in evaluation. Since the ground-truth reward is known by construction, the authors can directly assess the methods’ ability to maximize it while respecting the KL constraint to the reference policy. That is much more informative than merely checking whether a generated sample “looks better,” because it allows precise measurement of reward maximization and policy drift.

The paper also uses this task to test whether the methods can be compared under multiple levels of conservativeness. Different values of the RL or DPO control parameters are swept, and the resulting reward-KL tradeoff curves are compared. Because the labels are synthetic, the authors can repeatedly evaluate each trained policy on the true reward function, making this experiment the most controlled and the most suitable for studying optimization dynamics.

# How human preference datasets are used in summarization and dialogue

In summarization and dialogue, preference data comes from human annotations rather than synthetic scoring. The paper uses offline datasets of pairwise comparisons, where each example provides a prompt and two responses, along with a label indicating which response humans preferred. These datasets are exactly the kind of data DPO is designed to train from directly.

For summarization, the preference data was collected by earlier work on Reddit TL;DR summaries. For dialogue, the paper uses the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dataset, which provides paired responses and preference labels. In both cases, the training process does not require explicit reward labels; it only needs comparisons. That matches the theoretical framing of DPO, where preference information is converted into a policy optimization objective without fitting a separate reward model.

This offline preference setting is important because it reflects the practical reality of human feedback collection. Preference pairs are much easier to gather than full demonstrations of ideal answers, and they are also easier to annotate consistently. The paper’s experimental design makes clear that DPO is intended for exactly this kind of data: static datasets of comparisons collected once and then reused to train a policy.

# Available and unavailable SFT models across tasks

The paper treats the availability of a task-specific SFT model differently across experiments. In controlled sentiment generation and summarization, a suitable SFT model is available and can be used directly as the reference policy. For sentiment, GPT-2-large is fine-tuned on IMDb reviews. For summarization, the paper uses a GPT-J-based SFT model trained on human-written forum post summaries. These references are task-aligned starting points.

In the dialogue setting, however, the paper does not have an existing task-specific SFT model in the same way. Instead, it starts from a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model and uses Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the chosen completions, so that the resulting policy is at least roughly within the distribution of the data. This matters because DPO relies on a reference policy, and the quality of that reference can affect training when the original SFT model is unavailable.

The paper also notes that when a pre-trained SFT model is not available, the reference model can be initialized by maximizing likelihood on the preferred completions $(x, y_w)$. This is not treated as a separate main method but as a practical way to reduce distribution shift between the unavailable true reference and the reference actually used in DPO training. The experimental design therefore adapts to whether a natural reference model exists for the task.

# Baselines: zero-shot prompting, SFT, Preferred-FT, Unlikelihood, PPO, PPO-GT, Best-of-N

The paper compares DPO against a broad set of baselines. Some of these are simple prompting baselines, while others are learning-based methods that use either supervised or reinforcement learning objectives. In the dialogue task, the authors try zero-shot or few-shot prompting with GPT-J or Pythia-2.8B, depending on the setting. They also evaluate the raw SFT model, which provides the unaligned starting point for preference tuning.

Preferred-FT is a supervised baseline that trains on the chosen response $y_w$ only. It is meant to ask how far one can get by simply imitating the preferred completions rather than learning a relative preference objective. Unlikelihood is another pseudo-supervised method that explicitly raises the probability of preferred completions and lowers the probability of dispreferred ones, optionally with a coefficient on the unlikelihood term. This baseline is especially relevant because it resembles a simplified preference objective, but without DPO’s derived weighting structure.

The paper also evaluates PPO, the standard RLHF optimization method, using a learned reward model. In the controlled sentiment setting, it additionally includes PPO-GT, which uses the true reward function directly. Finally, Best-of-$N$ is used as a strong but expensive baseline: the model samples $N$ responses and returns the one with the highest reward-model score. This gives a sense of what can be achieved when search is pushed harder at inference time rather than by policy optimization alone.

# Why PPO-GT is an oracle baseline in sentiment control

PPO-GT is possible only in the controlled sentiment experiment because there the ground-truth reward is known. Instead of learning a reward model from preferences, the method is given the true sentiment-based reward directly. This makes PPO-GT an oracle baseline: it removes one major source of error, namely reward-model misspecification, and tests only how well PPO itself can optimize the objective.

This baseline is important because it helps separate two questions that are otherwise confounded in standard RLHF. If PPO underperforms, is that because the reward model is poor or because the RL optimization is unstable? By giving PPO the real reward signal, PPO-GT checks the best-case performance of the RL optimization stage. The paper’s finding that DPO still outperforms PPO-GT in the reward-KL frontier is therefore especially striking.

The paper also notes that two PPO-GT implementations are used in the sentiment experiments: an off-the-shelf version and a modified version with reward normalization and additional hyperparameter tuning. This makes the comparison more robust, because it shows that the observed behavior is not just an artifact of a weak PPO implementation. Even with these advantages, PPO-GT does not match DPO’s efficiency on the frontier.

# Ground-truth reward/KL frontiers for controlled evaluation

The controlled sentiment experiment is evaluated by plotting the frontier of achieved reward against KL divergence from the reference policy. This is the most direct way to test the RLHF objective used throughout the paper, because the objective is inherently a tradeoff between making the reward higher and staying close to the original model. A method is not judged only by how much reward it gets, but by how much reward it gets for a given amount of deviation.

To generate these frontiers, the paper runs multiple training configurations for each algorithm, varying their conservativeness settings such as target KL for PPO, $\beta$ for DPO, and $\alpha$ for unlikelihood. Each trained policy is then evaluated on test prompts after regular intervals during training. The authors compute average reward under the true sentiment classifier and average sequence-level KL from the reference policy. Sequence-level KL is defined as the sum of per-timestep KL divergences.

This frontier-based evaluation is especially appropriate for controlled sentiment because it reveals whether one algorithm dominates another across the entire tradeoff curve, not just at one arbitrary operating point. The paper reports that DPO produces the most efficient frontier, achieving the highest reward while maintaining low KL. That is a stronger result than simply saying it gets good reward, because it shows DPO is better at the exact constrained optimization problem RLHF is meant to solve.

# Win-rate evaluation using GPT-4 as a proxy judge

For summarization and dialogue, the paper cannot use a ground-truth reward function, so it instead evaluates models by win rate against a baseline response. In summarization, the baseline is a reference summary from the test set; in dialogue, it is the preferred response from the test set. The comparison is judged by GPT-4, which is used as a proxy for human preference.

This evaluation choice is central to the paper’s experimental design. Since the true quality of a summary or assistant response is not directly measurable, the authors ask which output is better according to a strong model judge. Win rate is a natural metric in that setting because the training data itself is pairwise preference data. It measures how often a method’s output is preferred over the baseline output.

The authors are careful, however, not to rely on GPT-4 blindly. They conduct a human study to check whether GPT-4’s judgments match human judgments at a reasonable level. They find that GPT-4’s agreement with humans is typically similar to or higher than inter-human agreement, which supports its use as a practical evaluation proxy in the main experiments. This is a necessary part of the methodology because the main results depend on automated comparison rather than direct human annotation for every sample.

# Sampling temperature as an important evaluation variable

The paper treats sampling temperature as an important part of evaluation, not just generation. For all methods in summarization and dialogue, outputs are sampled at different temperatures, and performance can change substantially with the sampling setting. This is especially important because some methods may produce stronger outputs when sampled conservatively, while others degrade more sharply as temperature rises.

In summarization, the paper reports win rates across temperatures ranging from $0.0$ to $1.0$. The best-performing temperature for DPO is compared against the best temperature for PPO, Preferred-FT, and other methods. In dialogue, the same principle applies: the authors compare methods at the temperatures that give them their strongest observed performance. This makes the comparison more fair and reveals how robust each method is to decoding choices.

Temperature sensitivity is also useful for understanding the practical usability of a trained policy. A method that performs well only at one narrow temperature may be less robust than a method that maintains quality across a range of decoding settings. The paper finds that DPO is often more robust than PPO in this regard, especially in summarization, where PPO’s performance can fall back toward the base model at higher temperatures.

# Why ROUGE is insufficient for preference-sensitive summarization evaluation

The paper explicitly argues that ROUGE is not a sufficient evaluation metric for the summarization experiments. This is because ROUGE measures lexical overlap with a reference summary, but the paper is interested in human preference, which does not necessarily track surface overlap. A summary can be a good one even if it uses different words or reorganizes information, and a summary can score well on overlap while still being less helpful or less concise than a human would prefer.

Because of this mismatch, the authors use preference-based evaluation instead. They compare generated summaries against reference summaries in the test set and ask GPT-4 to judge which is better. They then validate that procedure with human raters. This methodology is more aligned with the problem the paper actually cares about: whether a trained policy produces outputs that humans prefer.

The choice to move away from ROUGE is not a rejection of automatic metrics in general, but a recognition that the goal of RLHF-style training is not n-gram matching. The paper’s evaluation strategy is therefore designed to match the learning objective. Since DPO is trained from preference data, it makes sense to evaluate it with preference-sensitive judgments rather than overlap-based scores.
