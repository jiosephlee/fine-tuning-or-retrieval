\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 9: 9. Why DPO Can Be More Stable than PPO-Based RLHF

# Review of Actor-Critic Optimization in RLHF

The RLHF pipeline described in the paper has two distinct optimization stages after supervised fine-tuning: first, a reward model is learned from preference data, and then a language model policy is optimized to produce outputs that score highly under that reward model. In the standard formulation, this second stage is an instance of reinforcement learning with a constraint that keeps the policy close to a reference policy, typically the supervised fine-tuned model. The paper writes this objective as maximizing the expected learned reward while subtracting a KL-divergence penalty from the reference policy.

This setup is commonly handled with actor-critic style reinforcement learning methods such as PPO. In this context, the policy is the actor, and a value function or reward-related baseline plays the role of the critic. The overall aim is not simply to maximize reward, but to do so without moving too far from the policy on which the reward model is reliable. The paper emphasizes that this is important because the reward model is only accurate in the region of behavior represented by the data it was trained on.

The challenge, as the paper argues, is that this RL stage is difficult to optimize reliably for language models. The objective is defined over discrete sequences, so direct differentiation is not possible, and the optimization must proceed through sampling-based RL methods. This makes the procedure more complex than ordinary supervised learning, and it introduces instability from both the RL algorithm itself and the need to estimate auxiliary quantities such as value functions or baselines.

# Minimizing Divergence to the Optimal Policy Under a Learned Reward

A central perspective in the paper is that the RLHF objective can be viewed as searching for a policy that balances reward maximization against deviation from a reference policy. The KL term is not just a regularizer in a loose sense; it explicitly constrains how far the learned policy can move from the base language model. This keeps the model from collapsing onto a narrow set of high-reward outputs and helps preserve diversity.

The paper shows that, under the KL-regularized reward maximization objective, the optimal policy has a closed-form expression involving the reference policy and the reward function. This is a key step in the argument. It means that the solution to the constrained RL problem can be written as a reweighted version of the reference model, where the reweighting depends exponentially on the reward scaled by $\beta$. In the paper’s notation, this optimal policy is

$$
\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right).
$$

This form is important because it shows that the optimal policy is not arbitrary: it is exactly the reference model adjusted by the reward, with normalization by $Z(x)$ to make it a valid distribution. The paper then uses this relationship to derive DPO. But from the standpoint of stability, the key point is that PPO is trying to approximate the optimization of this constrained objective using gradient-based RL, which can be sensitive to the quality of the reward estimate and to the variance in the policy updates.

# Control-as-Inference Perspective on KL-Regularized RL

The paper connects RLHF to the control-as-inference framework. In this view, policy optimization under a KL constraint can be interpreted as inference over trajectories or actions that are "preferred" under the reward function, while remaining anchored to the reference policy. This viewpoint is useful because it reveals that the constrained RL problem has a structured probabilistic form rather than being an unconstrained search for high reward.

Under this perspective, the reward-regularized objective can be re-expressed in a way that makes clear what the optimal policy must look like. The reward function and the KL term interact so that the policy optimization problem is equivalent to fitting a distribution that balances two pressures: matching high-reward outputs and staying close to the base model. The paper emphasizes that this is the same objective optimized by prior RLHF methods, including PPO-based systems.

What matters for stability is that the reference policy is not merely an initialization. It is part of the objective itself. The policy is penalized for drifting too far from the reference distribution because the reward model is only trustworthy near that region. This interpretation helps explain why a method that directly encodes the optimal policy form, rather than estimating the reward and then solving an RL problem numerically, can be more stable.

# The Soft Value or Normalization Term in the RL Objective

The closed-form optimal policy contains a normalization term, $Z(x)$, which is the partition function

$$
Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right).
$$

The paper interprets the logarithm of this term as a kind of soft value function for the reference policy. This term does not change which policy is optimal, but it is mathematically necessary to ensure the distribution is properly normalized. In the rewritten objective, it appears as the term

$$
\beta \log\sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right).
$$

This normalization term is central to the stability discussion because it is exactly the quantity that becomes difficult to handle in practice. Even if a reward model is learned accurately, the partition function may still be expensive to estimate. In policy-gradient methods, the effect of this term must somehow be accounted for, either implicitly or through an auxiliary estimate. The paper points out that prior work often treats this normalization as something to be approximated rather than exactly computed.

The importance of the term is subtle. It does not affect the identity of the optimal solution, but it affects how noisy and unstable the learning process can be. If the normalization is poorly estimated or omitted, the gradient can have high variance. That makes the optimization sensitive to the sampling process and to the quality of any baseline or critic used to compensate for it.

# Why Missing or Poorly Estimated Baselines Create High-Variance Gradients

The paper explains that the normalization term in the reparameterized reward can be thought of as a baseline. In policy-gradient methods, baselines are used to reduce variance, and when they are missing or poorly estimated, the gradient estimates can become unstable. This is a major practical issue in RLHF because the policy is being optimized over long, discrete sequences, where reward information is sparse and expensive to estimate.

In the formulation used by prior RLHF systems, the reward passed to PPO is often modified to include the KL penalty directly. But even then, the effective optimization still depends on a normalized reward structure. If the baseline term is not handled carefully, the resulting gradient updates can fluctuate substantially from batch to batch. The paper argues that this contributes to the instability commonly associated with actor-critic optimization in language model RL.

The issue is especially acute because the reward model itself is only an approximation. If the model is inaccurate, then the estimated baseline will also be inaccurate. This means that the policy may receive misleading gradient signals, especially when the updates are large or when the sampled outputs lie in regions where the reward model was not well trained. DPO avoids this source of variance by reformulating the problem so that the normalization is absorbed analytically rather than estimated numerically.

# Difficulties of Learning Value Functions in Large Language Model RL

The paper notes that while one could in principle handle the normalization term with a learned value function, this can itself be difficult to optimize. In large language model settings, the state space is enormous and the outputs are long sequences, so learning an accurate value function is not trivial. The value function must somehow predict the expected future reward under the current policy, which is a hard regression problem in a high-dimensional, nonstationary setting.

This difficulty compounds the instability of the RLHF pipeline. The actor is changing during training, so the critic or value function must continually track a moving target. If the critic lags behind or is poorly calibrated, the policy updates can be noisy or biased. The paper’s discussion of PPO-based RLHF makes clear that these complications are not incidental; they are part of why the standard pipeline is hard to tune and reproduce.

In addition, the paper emphasizes that the RLHF objective requires sampling from the language model during training. This means the critic must operate on data generated by the current policy, rather than on a fixed supervised dataset. That makes the learning problem even more delicate, because the distribution of training inputs changes as the policy updates. DPO eliminates this separate critic-learning problem entirely.

# Reward Normalization Tricks in Prior RLHF Work

Prior RLHF methods have tried to manage instability by normalizing rewards. The paper specifically mentions that previous work has used a human completion baseline, which acts like a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate of the normalization term. This is a practical trick meant to reduce variance in the reward signal and stabilize policy optimization.

The idea is that instead of estimating the full partition function or learning a separate value function, one uses a baseline derived from human-completion comparisons or other normalization heuristics. This can help in practice, but it remains an approximation. The paper’s point is not that such tricks are useless, but that they reveal how much engineering is required to make the RLHF pipeline work reliably.

These reward normalization methods are evidence that the underlying objective is hard to optimize directly. If the reward model and its normalization were easy to handle, such approximations would not be necessary. The paper uses this observation to motivate DPO: if the normalization is the main source of complexity, then a formulation that analytically incorporates it should be more stable and simpler to train.

# How DPO Implicitly Absorbs the Normalization Term

DPO is derived by reparameterizing the reward in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy. In the paper’s theory section, this reparameterization makes the normalization term part of the structure of the reward itself, rather than something that must be separately estimated during training. The reward becomes

$$
r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)},
$$

up to an additive function of $x$ corresponding to the normalization. Because preference models such as Bradley-Terry depend only on differences between rewards, that additive term cancels out. This is the crucial point: DPO can fit the policy directly using preference data while never having to explicitly represent or optimize the partition function in the way PPO does.

The paper interprets this as selecting a particular member of each reward equivalence class, namely the one for which the policy is properly normalized. In other words, DPO uses the mathematical structure of the preference model to absorb the soft value term into the policy parameterization itself. That removes the need to estimate a separate baseline or value function for the RL stage.

This is a major source of stability. Since the normalization is handled analytically, the optimization reduces to a classification-style objective over pairs of preferred and dispreferred outputs. The model does not need to learn a critic, estimate an advantage function, or repeatedly approximate the soft value through rollouts.

# No Rollout Sampling During Training

A major practical difference between DPO and PPO-based RLHF is that DPO does not require rollout sampling from the language model during training. In standard RLHF, the policy must generate responses, those responses must be scored by the reward model, and the RL algorithm then updates the policy based on those sampled trajectories. This creates a feedback loop in which training depends on online generation.

DPO instead trains from a fixed offline dataset of preferences. The policy is optimized by maximum likelihood on preference pairs, using the objective

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
$$

Because this objective is trained entirely on a static dataset, it avoids the instability introduced by repeated on-policy sampling. There is no need to generate new completions at each update step or to interleave generation, reward evaluation, and policy improvement in a reinforcement learning loop.

This matters for robustness and reproducibility. Offline training is generally easier to control than online RL because the data distribution is fixed. The paper’s experiments reinforce this advantage by showing that DPO achieves strong results without the extensive tuning often needed for PPO.

# No Separate Reward Model and No Critic to Tune

Perhaps the biggest simplification in DPO is that it eliminates both the explicit reward model and the critic. In the standard RLHF pipeline, the reward model must be learned first, and then a separate RL algorithm must use that model to update the policy. The critic or value function may also need to be trained to reduce variance in the policy gradient. Each of these components introduces its own optimization challenges.

DPO collapses this pipeline into a single objective. The policy itself implicitly defines the reward through its ratio to the reference policy. As a result, there is no standalone reward network that needs to be trained and then frozen for the RL phase. There is also no separate value function that needs to estimate the normalization term. The same model parameters govern the behavior of the language model and the implicit reward structure.

This is why the paper repeatedly emphasizes that DPO is computationally lightweight and straightforward to train. By removing the extra moving parts, it reduces the number of failure modes in the alignment pipeline. There is no need to coordinate the training of multiple networks, no need to tune an RL critic, and no need to manage a complex loop of reward estimation and policy optimization.

# Practical Implications for Robustness, Simplicity, and Reproducibility

The paper’s empirical message is that these theoretical simplifications matter in practice. DPO is not merely an elegant reparameterization; it is presented as a method that is stable, performant, and easy to implement. The authors report that it performs as well as or better than PPO-based RLHF on tasks such as sentiment control, summarization, and dialogue, while being substantially simpler.

From a robustness standpoint, the key benefit is that DPO avoids several sources of instability at once. It does not require online rollouts, does not depend on a learned critic, and does not rely on separately estimating a reward model during the final policy optimization stage. Because its loss is a simple binary cross-entropy objective over preference pairs, training is closer to standard supervised learning than to a full RL pipeline.

From a reproducibility standpoint, this is important because RLHF systems can be fragile and highly sensitive to implementation details. The paper argues that DPO reduces the need for hyperparameter tuning and makes it easier to train aligned language models. In that sense, its main contribution is not just a new loss function, but a way of turning a difficult RL optimization problem into a direct and reliable preference-learning procedure.
