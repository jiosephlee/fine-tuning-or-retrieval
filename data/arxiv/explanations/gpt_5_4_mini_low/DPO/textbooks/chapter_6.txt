\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 6: 6. Direct Preference Optimization: Core Derivation

# Rewriting reward in terms of policy and reference-policy log ratios

The key mathematical move in Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO, begins with the standard RLHF objective: maximize expected reward while staying close to a reference policy using a KL penalty. For a general reward function $r(x,y)$, the paper shows that the optimal policy under this KL-constrained problem has a closed form. Specifically, the optimal policy is

$$
\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right),
$$

where $Z(x)$ is the partition function. This formula says that the optimal policy is just the reference policy reweighted by an exponential transform of the reward. The stronger the reward for a completion $y$, the more the optimal policy favors it relative to the reference model.

The paper then reverses this expression. Taking the logarithm of both sides and rearranging gives a reward representation in terms of the policy and the reference policy:

$$
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
$$

This is the central reparameterization. It shows that reward can be expressed as a log-ratio between the optimal policy and the reference policy, up to an additive term $\beta \log Z(x)$ that depends only on the prompt $x$. Since that extra term does not depend on the completion $y$, it will vanish whenever we compare two completions for the same prompt.

# The change-of-variables idea behind DPO

DPO is built on a change of variables. Instead of first learning a reward model and then using reinforcement learning to find a policy that maximizes that reward, DPO directly works with the policy parameterization implied by the optimal solution of the KL-regularized RLHF problem.

The intuition is that the policy itself can be viewed as carrying the reward information implicitly. If the optimal policy satisfies the closed-form relation above, then learning the policy can stand in for learning the reward. The paper emphasizes that this does not reduce expressiveness: the reparameterization can represent the same reward classes that arise in the Bradley-Terry and Plackett-Luce preference frameworks.

This is what makes DPO different from standard RLHF. Rather than fitting an explicit reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ and then optimizing it with RL, DPO rewrites the preference-learning problem so that the policy $\pi_\theta$ appears directly inside the preference model. The reward model is therefore implicit, not separate.

# Cancellation of the partition function inside reward differences

The Bradley-Terry preference model depends only on differences in reward between two completions. If a preferred completion is $y_1$ and a dispreferred one is $y_2$, then

$$
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\!\left(r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2)\right).
$$

This difference is crucial because it causes the partition function terms to cancel. Using the reward reparameterization,

$$
r^*(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x),
$$

both rewards contain the same additive term $\beta \log Z(x)$ for a fixed prompt $x$. When we subtract one reward from the other, that term disappears. As a result, the preference probability can be written entirely in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy.

This cancellation is what makes DPO practical. The partition function $Z(x)$ is generally hard to compute, and the paper explicitly notes that estimating it is expensive even if we have a good reward model. But because reward differences eliminate it, the preference model becomes directly expressible through policy ratios alone.

# Expressing preference probabilities directly in terms of policy ratios

After substituting the reward reparameterization into the Bradley-Terry model, the preference probability becomes

$$
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)
=
\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(
\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2 \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2 \mid x)}
-
\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1 \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1 \mid x)}
\right)}.
$$

This expression is the policy-side version of Bradley-Terry. It says that the probability humans prefer $y_1$ over $y_2$ can be modeled by comparing how much more the optimal policy favors $y_1$ than $y_2$, relative to the reference policy. The log-ratio structure is important because it measures preference not in absolute terms, but relative to the base model.

The resulting model is not a new preference theory. It is the same Bradley-Terry model, just expressed using the policy rather than an explicit reward network. That is why DPO can be viewed as a reparameterized preference model.

# The policy-side Bradley-Terry model

The paper’s main reformulation is that the usual preference probability can be written as a Bradley-Terry model whose score is the policy ratio term

$$
\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}.
$$

So instead of a reward network producing a scalar score for each completion, the language model itself induces that score through its log probability relative to the reference model. In other words, the policy is also acting as an implicit reward model.

This is an important conceptual shift. In standard RLHF, the reward model and the policy are separate objects trained in separate stages. In DPO, the policy parameterization is chosen so that its induced score can stand in for the reward model inside the Bradley-Terry framework. The paper argues that this does not lose generality under mild assumptions, because every reward class relevant to the preference model can be represented in this way.

# Derivation of the DPO loss

Once the preference probability is written directly in terms of the policy, learning from a dataset of comparisons becomes a straightforward maximum likelihood problem. For a dataset

$$
\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N,
$$

where $y_w$ is the preferred completion and $y_l$ is the dispreferred one, DPO minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the Bradley-Terry probability:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{\text{ref}})
=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\left[
\log \sigma\left(
\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)}
-
\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}
\right)
\right].
$$

This is the DPO loss. It is a classification-style objective over pairwise preferences, but it is derived from the same KL-regularized RLHF objective used by prior methods. The paper’s point is not that the objective is merely similar to RLHF, but that it is mathematically equivalent to optimizing the RLHF objective under the preference model, once the reward is reparameterized in terms of the policy.

# Binary cross-entropy interpretation of DPO

The DPO objective is exactly a binary cross-entropy loss on preference comparisons. The model predicts that the preferred completion should beat the dispreferred one, and the target label is always that $y_w$ is preferred over $y_l$. The probability of that event is given by a logistic function of the policy log-ratio difference.

This binary classification view is important because it shows why DPO is simple to train. Instead of a reinforcement learning loop with sampled rollouts, advantage estimates, and policy updates, DPO can be optimized with standard supervised learning machinery. The paper emphasizes that this avoids the complexity and instability of actor-critic methods like PPO while still targeting the same preference-alignment goal.

# Why DPO fits an implicit reward model without explicitly training one

DPO does not eliminate the reward concept; it internalizes it. The paper defines an implicit reward

$$
\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}.
$$

This quantity behaves like a reward score, but it is not produced by a separate reward network. Instead, it is induced by the policy itself. The DPO gradient makes this clear: updates increase the likelihood of the preferred response and decrease the likelihood of the dispreferred one, weighted by how much the current implicit reward seems to mis-rank them.

The paper emphasizes that this weighting matters. A naive version of the update, without the dynamic per-example weighting, can lead to degeneration. DPO avoids that problem because the logistic form of the loss automatically downweights examples that are already correctly ranked and upweights examples where the model’s implicit reward is wrong.

# How DPO preserves the same RLHF objective as KL-regularized reward maximization

A central claim of the paper is that DPO is not a different objective from RLHF in spirit; it is a different parameterization of the same one. The original RLHF problem is to maximize expected reward while penalizing deviation from a reference policy:

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(y \mid x)}
\left[
r_\phi(x,y)
\right]
-
\beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\left[\pi_\theta(y \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\right].
$$

The paper shows that the optimal policy for this problem has the exponential form above, and that the DPO reparameterization captures exactly the reward equivalence class that matters for this optimization. In that sense, DPO preserves the original RLHF objective while removing the need to separately estimate a reward function and then solve an RL problem.

The theoretical discussion also clarifies why this is valid. Reward functions that differ by an $x$-dependent constant induce the same preference distribution and the same optimal policy under the constrained RL problem. DPO chooses a particular representative from each equivalence class, namely the one representable as a log-ratio of policy and reference policy. This is enough to recover the same optimal policy without loss of generality.

# Extension from Bradley-Terry to Plackett-Luce style ranking models

Although the derivation in the paper is presented using Bradley-Terry pairwise comparisons, the same logic extends to more general ranking models such as Plackett-Luce. The key reason is that these models also depend on relative comparisons among completions rather than absolute reward values. Therefore, the same reparameterization trick applies: reward can be expressed through policy ratios, and the partition-function term can be eliminated when only relative preferences matter.

The paper notes that the corresponding DPO-style objective can be generalized beyond pairwise comparisons when ranked lists of responses are available. The Bradley-Terry case is the simplest and most direct instance, but it is not the only one compatible with the framework. This shows that DPO is not tied to one specific preference model; it is a more general way of turning preference data into a direct policy objective.
