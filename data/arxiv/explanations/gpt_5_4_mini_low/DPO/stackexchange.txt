\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does the partition function $Z(x)$ disappear in the DPO derivation?
Question:
I'm trying to follow the step from the optimal policy form

$$
\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)
$$

to the DPO preference probability, where the paper says the partition function cancels when using the Bradley-Terry model.

I understand that after rearranging,

$$
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x),
$$

but I don't see concretely why substituting this into

$$
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2))
$$

removes all dependence on $Z(x)$. Is this only because $Z(x)$ depends on $x$ but not on $y$? If so, is that the same reason reward functions that differ by an additive $f(x)$ are considered equivalent in the paper?
Answer:
Yes — the cancellation happens for exactly that reason, and it is the same invariance principle behind the paper’s notion of equivalent reward functions.

Start from the reparameterization in the paper,
$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta \log Z(x).
$$
Now plug this into the Bradley-Terry preference model
$$
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)=\sigma\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\bigr).
$$
If you write out the difference explicitly, you get
$$
r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)
=
\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}
-\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}
+\beta \log Z(x)-\beta \log Z(x).
$$
The two partition terms are identical because $Z(x)$ depends only on the prompt $x$, not on which completion $y$ you choose. So they subtract out exactly. That is why the DPO derivation can express preference probabilities purely in terms of log-ratios of the policy and the reference policy, with no explicit partition function.

Intuitively, $Z(x)$ is just the normalization constant needed to make $\pi_r(\cdot\mid x)$ a valid distribution. But Bradley-Terry only cares about *relative* utility between two completions under the same prompt. Any quantity shared by both candidates is invisible to the comparison. This is the same reason that adding an arbitrary baseline $f(x)$ to a reward does not change preferences: if
$$
r'(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x),
$$
then
$$
r'(x,y_1)-r'(x,y_2)=r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2).
$$
So the preference model is unchanged. The paper makes exactly this point in its equivalence relation: rewards differing by a function of $x$ belong to the same class because they induce the same preference distribution and the same optimal policy.

So, in short, the partition function disappears because it is a prompt-dependent constant, and Bradley-Terry uses only reward differences for two responses to the same prompt. That is precisely the same invariance that makes reward functions equal up to an additive $f(x)$ indistinguishable in the paper.

### What exactly is being optimized in DPO if there is no explicit reward model?
Question:
The paper says that DPO avoids fitting a separate reward model and instead treats the policy as an implicit reward model using

$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$

I am confused about what this means operationally. In standard RLHF, I can picture two objects: a reward model $r_\phi$ and a policy $\pi_\theta$. In DPO, are we still conceptually learning a reward function, just not storing it as a separate network? Or is the reward merely a mathematical reinterpretation of the policy logits?

I would appreciate an explanation of what it means to say "your language model is secretly a reward model" without relying only on the slogan.
Answer:
In DPO, what is being optimized is still a policy, not a separate reward network. The key point in the paper is that the usual RLHF objective can be rewritten so that the reward function is no longer an independent object you have to learn and then optimize. Instead, the reward is represented implicitly by the current policy relative to a fixed reference policy.

Concretely, the paper starts from the standard KL-regularized RLHF objective
$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim \pi_\theta}[r(x,y)] - \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)).
$$
For this problem, the optimal policy has the closed form
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$
Rearranging gives
$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta \log Z(x),
$$
where the extra term $\beta \log Z(x)$ depends only on the prompt $x$. That term does not matter for pairwise preferences, because preferences only depend on differences like $r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)$, and the $Z(x)$ term cancels.

This is why the paper says the policy is an “implicit reward model.” It is not claiming that the model literally has a reward head hidden inside it, nor that the logits are themselves rewards in some absolute sense. Rather, it is saying that once you fix a reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, every candidate policy $\pi_\theta$ defines an equivalent reward function up to a prompt-dependent constant:
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
This quantity is a relative score: it says how much more the current policy favors $y$ than the reference policy does. A completion gets a higher implicit reward if the model has moved probability mass toward it relative to the reference model.

Operationally, DPO then fits $\pi_\theta$ so that preferred completions $y_w$ get higher implicit reward than dispreferred ones $y_l$, using the Bradley-Terry preference likelihood:
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=
-\mathbb{E}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
$$
So the optimization target is a classification-style loss over preference pairs, but the thing being adjusted is still the policy parameters $\theta$. There is no separately trained $r_\phi$ and no RL loop.

The cleanest intuition is this: in standard RLHF, you first learn “what humans like” in a reward model, then use RL to make the policy produce more of that. In DPO, you skip the middle step and directly adjust the policy so that its relative preference ordering matches the data. The “reward” language is a mathematical reinterpretation of this relative ordering, not an additional learned module. The slogan “your language model is secretly a reward model” means that, under the KL-regularized RLHF formulation, the policy itself determines an equivalent reward up to an irrelevant constant, so optimizing the policy directly is enough.

### Why is DPO called 'RL-free' if it optimizes the same KL-constrained RLHF objective?
Question:
The paper repeatedly says DPO solves the same problem as RLHF but without reinforcement learning. This confuses me because the starting point is still the KL-regularized objective

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta} \; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x) \|\| \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\right).
$$

If the objective is an RL objective, in what precise sense is DPO not RL? Is it just that the optimum can be rewritten as a supervised classification loss? Or is there a deeper distinction between "solving an RL-derived objective" and "running an RL algorithm"?
Answer:
DPO is called “RL-free” not because it changes the underlying preference objective, but because it avoids the *reinforcement learning procedure* usually used to optimize that objective.

The paper is quite explicit about this distinction. In RLHF, one first fits a reward model from comparisons and then solves the KL-regularized control problem
\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\right),
\]
typically with PPO or a related policy-gradient method. That is “RL” in the usual practical sense: you need on-policy sampling from the current policy, reward computation, and a reinforcement-learning optimization loop.

DPO’s key observation is that for this KL-regularized objective, the optimal policy has a closed form,
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
\]
and under the Bradley-Terry preference model the partition function cancels when you write preference probabilities in terms of the optimal policy. That lets the authors reparameterize the reward in terms of the policy itself and turn the problem into a simple binary cross-entropy loss over preference pairs:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=
-\mathbb{E}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\]

So the deeper distinction is this: DPO is not “doing RL” because it does not solve the KL-constrained objective by sampling trajectories and applying a policy-gradient or actor-critic algorithm. Instead, it directly fits the policy with a supervised classification loss derived from the same objective. The paper even says this in a very literal way: DPO is “a simple RL-free algorithm,” and “the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective.”

So your summary is basically right: the objective is RL-derived, but the optimization is not RL. “RL-free” here means “no explicit reward model, no rollout-based policy optimization, no PPO loop,” not “unrelated to reinforcement learning in theory.” In fact, the paper’s main point is that DPO solves the same preference-aligned control problem while bypassing the machinery of RLHF.

### How should I interpret the role of $\beta$ in DPO compared with $\beta$ in RLHF?
Question:
In the paper, $\beta$ appears in both the KL-constrained RLHF objective and in the DPO loss

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\mathbb{E}\left[\log \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
$$

I initially thought larger $\beta$ would mean the model is allowed to move farther from the reference policy, but in the RL objective it multiplies the KL penalty, so that sounds backwards.

Can someone explain intuitively what increasing or decreasing $\beta$ does in DPO training? Also, does the exact same interpretation carry over from the RLHF objective, or does the reparameterization change the practical meaning of $\beta$?
Answer:
In the paper, $\beta$ has essentially the same *conceptual* role in RLHF and DPO: it sets how strongly you want to stay near the reference policy while still moving toward preferred outputs. The important subtlety is that it appears in the two formulations in different algebraic places, so it is easy to misread its effect.

In the RLHF objective
$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}[r_\phi(x,y)] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}),
$$
a larger $\beta$ means the KL penalty is weighted more heavily, so the optimizer is pushed harder to remain close to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. In other words, increasing $\beta$ makes the policy more conservative and decreasing $\beta$ lets it move farther away from the reference. The paper says this explicitly: $\beta$ is “a parameter controlling the deviation from the base reference policy.”

DPO uses the same underlying RLHF problem, but after reparameterizing the reward in terms of the optimal policy. That is why the DPO loss contains terms like
$$
\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
This does **not** mean the interpretation flips. It is still the same tradeoff parameter, but now it is embedded inside the implicit reward definition
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
Because the optimal-policy relation is
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
$$
you can see the inverse dependence directly: for a fixed reward difference, larger $\beta$ makes the exponential tilt weaker, so the optimal policy stays closer to the reference. That is the same meaning as in RLHF.

So the clean intuition is: in both RLHF and DPO, larger $\beta$ means “more reference-anchored, less aggressive preference optimization,” while smaller $\beta$ means “more willing to depart from the reference to satisfy preferences.” The reparameterization in DPO changes the *form* of the objective, but not the practical interpretation of $\beta$.

The paper also notes a mechanistic effect in the gradient: the examples are weighted by how wrong the implicit reward ordering is, “scaled by $\beta$,” so $\beta$ also affects the strength of the update. But that is still the same tradeoff in different clothing: larger $\beta$ changes both the sharpness of the preference fit and the implied KL pressure toward the reference.

### Why isn't DPO just the same as maximizing the probability of preferred responses and minimizing dispreferred ones?
Question:
The gradient discussion says DPO increases the likelihood of the preferred completion $y_w$ and decreases the likelihood of the dispreferred completion $y_l$, with a weighting term

$$
\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x,y_w)).
$$

This makes it sound very similar to a simple pairwise supervised objective or unlikelihood training. But the paper claims the weighting is important and that the naive version can lead to degeneration.

Could someone explain, preferably with a toy example, what this weighting is doing that plain preferred-vs-dispreferred log-likelihood training is not? Why does this weighting arise naturally from the derivation rather than being just a heuristic?
Answer:
No — the DPO update is **not** just “increase $\pi(y_w \mid x)$ and decrease $\pi(y_l \mid x)$” in the raw, unweighted sense. The key difference is that DPO is optimizing a **logistic preference loss** over the *difference* of implicit rewards, so the update is automatically **self-modulated by confidence**. That weighting is not a heuristic add-on; it is exactly what you get when you take the gradient of the DPO objective.

If we write the DPO loss for one preference pair as

$$
\ell(\theta) = -\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}\right),
$$

then defining the implicit reward
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
$$
the gradient becomes proportional to
$$
\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr)\Bigl[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)-\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)\Bigr].
$$

That sigmoid factor is the important part. It is large when the model currently believes the wrong thing, namely when $\hat r_\theta(x,y_l) > \hat r_\theta(x,y_w)$, and small when the model already ranks the preferred completion above the dispreferred one. So DPO does **not** keep pushing every example with equal force. It focuses its learning on pairs that are still misordered or only weakly separated.

This is exactly the same intuition as ordinary logistic regression: examples that are already classified correctly with high confidence contribute little gradient, while hard or misclassified examples contribute more. DPO inherits that property because it is, in effect, a logistic classification problem over preference pairs.

A toy example makes the difference clear. Suppose for a given prompt $x$ you have two comparisons. For one pair, the model already assigns much higher implicit reward to the preferred response, say $\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)=10$. Then
$$
\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))=\sigma(-10)\approx 0,
$$
so the gradient is tiny. DPO basically says, “this pair is already solved; don’t waste capacity pushing it further.” For another pair, the model gets it backwards, say $\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)=-2$. Then
$$
\sigma(2)\approx 0.88,
$$
so the gradient is strong. That example gets a big corrective update. This is precisely what prevents the model from being driven aggressively on examples it already handles well.

By contrast, a naive objective like “maximize $\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and minimize $\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$” applies pressure more uniformly. In its simplest form it does not know whether the model is already making the right preference distinction or not. So the same pair can keep contributing a large update even after the model has already put $y_w$ above $y_l$. Over many steps, that kind of unweighted pressure can over-sharpen the policy, distort probabilities too much, and lead to degeneration, which is what the paper reports for the unlikelihood-style baseline.

The deeper reason the weighting arises naturally is that DPO is not invented as a heuristic pairwise ranking loss. The paper starts from the standard KL-constrained RLHF objective and uses the Bradley-Terry preference model. After reparameterizing the reward in terms of the policy ratio $\log \pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{ref}}$, the preference likelihood becomes a binary cross-entropy objective over the policy itself. The sigmoid weight is then just the derivative of that likelihood. In other words, DPO is doing maximum likelihood under the same preference model as reward-model training, but directly in policy space. The weighting is therefore part of the probabilistic model, not an extra trick.

So the short answer is: plain preferred-vs-dispreferred training says “always push,” while DPO says “push in proportion to how wrong or uncertain you currently are.” That adaptive weighting is exactly what makes DPO stable and why the paper warns that removing it can cause degeneration.

### What assumptions are hidden in the statement that DPO recovers the optimal RLHF policy?
Question:
The paper says DPO can recover the optimal policy corresponding to the standard RLHF problem, but I am not sure under what conditions this is actually true.

For example:

1. Does this require the human preferences to exactly follow a Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce model?
2. Does it assume infinite data or a realizable model class?
3. Does it assume the preference pairs were sampled from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ specifically?
4. What breaks if the preference labels are noisy or systematically inconsistent?

I'm trying to separate the exact mathematical equivalence in the idealized setting from the practical approximation in real preference datasets.
Answer:
The “DPO recovers the optimal RLHF policy” statement is true only in the paper’s idealized setting, and the hidden assumptions are exactly the ones needed to make the algebraic equivalence go through.

The key point is that DPO is not claiming to solve arbitrary RLHF from arbitrary preference data. It is claiming that if the data really come from the same preference model used in the derivation, then the DPO objective is just a reparameterization of the standard KL-regularized RLHF objective. In the paper’s notation, the RLHF objective is

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal D,\; y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta \, \mathbb D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)],
$$

whose optimal policy has the closed form

$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$

Then, under Bradley-Terry, the preference probability depends only on reward differences, so the unknown normalizer $Z(x)$ cancels. This is what lets the paper rewrite the preference likelihood directly in terms of $\pi_\theta$.

So to your specific questions:

1. Yes, the exact equivalence is derived under a Bradley-Terry style pairwise preference model, or more generally a Plackett-Luce ranking model when you have ranked lists rather than pairs. The paper explicitly says the Bradley-Terry case is the main one, and that the same style of derivation works for Plackett-Luce. If human preferences do not behave like a latent reward passed through such a model, then DPO is no longer exactly “recovering the RLHF optimum”; it is just optimizing a useful surrogate.

2. Yes, the recovery claim is an asymptotic, well-specified statement. The paper phrases its theory as holding “under mild assumptions” and notes consistency “under suitable assumption[s] of the preference data distribution.” In practice, exact recovery requires the model class to be rich enough to represent the optimal policy, and enough data for the maximum-likelihood estimate to converge. If the policy class is misspecified or the data are finite, you only get an approximation, not exact equality with the true RLHF optimum.

3. The derivation is tied to a particular reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, because the RLHF objective itself is KL-regularized toward that reference. In the paper, the offline preference pairs are usually generated by sampling completions from $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$, and DPO ideally uses that same model as $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. However, the paper also explicitly says that when the original SFT model is unavailable, they initialize $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ by fitting preferred completions to reduce distribution shift. So the exact mathematical equivalence is with the reference policy appearing in the RLHF objective; if your dataset was sampled from a different distribution, the equivalence becomes approximate.

4. Noisy or systematically inconsistent labels break the idealized “optimal policy recovery” interpretation. The paper assumes the comparison labels are sampled from a latent preference distribution $p^*$ that is compatible with Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce. If labels are noisy but the noise still matches that model, then the MLE interpretation still makes sense: you are fitting the best model under that stochastic preference process. But if labels are systematically inconsistent with any latent reward function, then there may be no reward $r^*$ whose Bradley-Terry probabilities explain the data. In that case, DPO still minimizes its cross-entropy loss, but the solution is only the best fit to the misspecified preference model, not the RLHF-optimal policy in the paper’s sense.

The cleanest way to summarize the paper is this: DPO exactly recovers the RLHF optimum only in a realizable, well-specified, infinite-data setting where preferences come from a Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce model induced by some latent reward, the reference policy is the one appearing in the KL-regularized RLHF objective, and the policy class is expressive enough to contain the optimum. Outside that regime, DPO is still a practical approximation, and the paper’s experiments are precisely about how well that approximation works.

### Why are reward functions that differ by $f(x)$ considered equivalent?
Question:
The paper defines two reward functions $r(x,y)$ and $r'(x,y)$ as equivalent if

$$
r(x,y) - r'(x,y) = f(x)
$$

for some function of the prompt only.

I can see that such a shift would cancel in pairwise differences like $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$, so the preference model would be unchanged. But the paper also says these reward functions induce the same optimal policy under the KL-constrained RL objective.

I do not immediately see why adding a prompt-dependent constant to all completions leaves the optimal policy unchanged. Is this because it only shifts the expected reward by a constant for each $x$? If so, can someone show that carefully using the objective in the paper?
Answer:
Yes — the invariance comes from the fact that, for a fixed prompt $x$, adding $f(x)$ to every completion shifts the objective by a constant that does not depend on the policy.

The RLHF objective in the paper is, for each prompt $x$,
$$
\max_{\pi(\cdot \mid x)} \; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta \, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi(\cdot \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\right).
$$
Now suppose we replace $r$ by
$$
r'(x,y)=r(x,y)-f(x),
$$
where $f(x)$ depends only on the prompt. Then the objective becomes
$$
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}[r'(x,y)] - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
= \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}[r(x,y)-f(x)] - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).
$$
Since $f(x)$ does not depend on $y$, it comes out of the expectation:
$$
= \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}[r(x,y)] - f(x) - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).
$$
For a given prompt $x$, the term $-f(x)$ is just a constant with respect to the policy $\pi(\cdot \mid x)$. So it cannot change which policy maximizes the expression. In other words, the argmax over $\pi(\cdot \mid x)$ is identical for $r$ and $r'$.

Intuitively, the policy only cares about *relative* utility among completions for the same prompt. A prompt-dependent baseline shifts all completions equally, so it never changes which completion is better than another, nor the tradeoff between reward and KL regularization. This is exactly analogous to the standard RL fact that adding a state-dependent baseline to a reward function does not change the optimal policy.

You can also see this directly from the closed-form optimizer given in the paper:
$$
\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right).
$$
If you replace $r$ by $r'(x,y)=r(x,y)-f(x)$, then
$$
\pi_{r'}(y \mid x) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}(r(x,y)-f(x))\right)
= e^{-f(x)/\beta}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$
The factor $e^{-f(x)/\beta}$ is the same for all $y$ at that $x$, so it gets absorbed into the normalization constant $Z(x)$ and disappears. Therefore $\pi_{r'}(\cdot \mid x)=\pi_r(\cdot \mid x)$.

So the paper’s equivalence relation is saying: reward functions that differ only by a prompt-dependent offset represent the same “preference geometry” and induce the same optimal KL-constrained policy. The offset matters neither to pairwise comparisons nor to the optimal policy, only to the absolute scale/level of the reward.

### What does Theorem 1 really mean when it says DPO does not lose reward-model generality?
Question:
The theorem says that under mild assumptions, all reward classes consistent with Plackett-Luce can be represented as

$$
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
$$

for some model $\pi$.

I am having trouble understanding the significance of this. At first glance, this looks like a very restrictive form for the reward, because it is tied to a particular reference policy and a normalized conditional distribution.

So when the paper says there is "no loss of generality," is it only true modulo the equivalence relation $r \sim r + f(x)$? If yes, does that mean DPO can represent every preference distribution but not every raw reward function uniquely? I think I am mixing up representational equivalence and identifiability.
Answer:
Yes — the key point is that Theorem 1 is about the *reward class* that matters for preferences and the KL-constrained RL objective, not about recovering a unique scalar reward function.

In the paper’s setup, two reward functions are considered equivalent if they differ by a prompt-dependent offset, $r(x,y) \sim r'(x,y)$ when $r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)$. This is exactly the right equivalence relation, because both the Bradley-Terry/Plackett-Luce preference model and the KL-regularized RL optimum depend only on reward *differences across completions for the same prompt*. Adding $f(x)$ to every completion for a fixed $x$ changes neither $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$ nor the optimal policy. So the reward is not identifiable up to an absolute additive baseline, only up to this equivalence class.

That is what Theorem 1 is really saying: for every such reward class, there exists some policy $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$ such that a representative of the class can be written as
$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
More precisely, if you start from any reward function $r$, the paper defines a normalized version
$$
r'(x,y)=r(x,y)-\beta \log \sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
$$
and this differs from $r$ only by a function of $x$. That normalization term is the log partition function, and it is exactly what makes the corresponding $\pi(y\mid x)$ sum to $1$. So the theorem is not claiming that every raw reward function literally has that form without modification; it is claiming that every *equivalence class* of rewards consistent with the preference model has a representative of that form.

So your intuition is right: DPO is not uniquely identifying every raw reward function. It is representing the preference-relevant part of the reward, i.e. the class modulo $r \sim r+f(x)$. But that is not a weakness; it is the same notion of identifiability already present in Bradley-Terry/Plackett-Luce. In fact, the paper explicitly points out that this under-specification is a standard issue in reward modeling. DPO simply chooses one convenient representative from each class, namely the one tied to a normalized policy ratio with respect to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.

So when the paper says “no loss of generality,” it means “no loss relative to the preference model and the RL objective,” not “we can recover an arbitrary absolute reward function uniquely.” DPO can represent every preference distribution in the model class, but only up to the natural additive ambiguity in reward.

### Why does PPO need a value-function-like normalization term, but DPO does not?
Question:
In the theory section, the paper rewrites the actor-critic objective and highlights a normalization term

$$
\beta \log \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y)\right),
$$

saying this can be viewed as a soft value function of the reference policy and that omitting it can make policy gradients high variance.

I do not understand this connection. Why is this term analogous to a value baseline, and why does its absence destabilize PPO? Also, if DPO effectively bakes this normalization into the parameterization, is that the main reason it is more stable, or is the stability claim coming from something else?
Answer:
The normalization term is “value-like” for essentially the same reason a baseline is value-like in policy gradients: it depends only on the prompt $x$, not on which completion $y$ was sampled, so it shifts all actions for that prompt by the same amount.

In the paper, the KL-constrained RLHF objective has optimal policy

$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
$$

with

$$
Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$

Taking logs gives

$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta \log Z(x).
$$

The key point is that $\beta \log Z(x)$ is a function of $x$ only. So for a fixed prompt, it is the same constant added to every candidate completion. That is exactly the role played by a baseline in RL: baselines do not change the expected gradient, but they reduce variance by removing a large prompt-specific offset from the reward signal. In this paper’s language, the normalization term is the “soft value function of the reference policy” because it is the log-partition term that summarizes how good the prompt is under the current reward landscape, analogous to a state value in standard actor-critic methods.

Why does omitting it make PPO unstable? Because PPO is optimizing a sampled reward signal, and if that reward has a large prompt-dependent offset, the policy gradient has to learn from noisy differences between very different prompts. The paper notes that prior RLHF systems often normalize rewards, either with a learned value function or with a human-completion baseline, precisely to reduce variance. Without such centering, the updates can be dominated by reward scale and offset rather than by the relative preference between completions. That is one reason the paper says the gradient can become high variance and learning unstable. In other words, the issue is not that the optimum changes, but that the path to the optimum becomes much noisier.

For DPO, the stability story is slightly different and stronger than just “it has the normalization built in.” DPO does not literally estimate the partition function during training. Instead, it rewrites the preference model so that the partition term cancels in pairwise comparisons. That gives the loss

$$
-\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right),
$$

which directly compares preferred and dispreferred completions. So DPO avoids the unstable ingredients of PPO-based RLHF altogether: no separate reward model to fit and normalize, no sampling-based RL loop, and no critic/value function to train. The paper is explicit that this is a major source of the improved stability and simplicity.

So the short answer is: the normalization term is baseline-like because it is prompt-dependent but completion-independent, hence it centers the reward without changing the optimal policy. PPO needs something like that to keep gradients low-variance. DPO is more stable mainly because it absorbs the RLHF objective into a pairwise classification loss where the problematic partition/baseline term cancels analytically; the paper also emphasizes that its gradient includes a dynamic weighting factor, which helps prevent degeneration.

### How can DPO work with an offline preference dataset if the original reference policy is unavailable?
Question:
The paper says DPO ideally uses a reference policy equal to the model that generated the comparison data, but in practice that model may not be available. In that case they train a proxy reference model by maximizing likelihood on the preferred completions only.

I am confused about why this is a reasonable substitute. If the original pairs $(y_w, y_l)$ were sampled from some unknown policy, wouldn't fitting only on $y_w$ bias the estimated reference toward the winners and distort the ratios

$$
\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}?
$$

How sensitive is DPO to mismatch between the true sampling policy and the chosen $\pi_{\text{ref}}$? I'm trying to understand whether this is just a pragmatic approximation or something more principled.
Answer:
The key point is that in DPO the reference policy is not trying to be a statistically faithful estimate of the behavior policy that generated the data; it is part of the parameterization of the objective. In the paper’s derivation, the reference policy appears in the reparameterization

$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x),
$$

and the preference loss is then written entirely in terms of ratios against that chosen $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. So when the original sampling policy is unavailable, the goal is not to recover it exactly, but to choose a reasonable baseline policy that keeps the learned model in the same region of response space as the data.

That is why the paper uses maximum likelihood on the preferred completions only as a proxy reference. It is admittedly an approximation, but a principled one in the sense that it tries to place $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ near the “good” part of the data distribution. If you have offline preference pairs sampled from some unknown policy, fitting only on $y_w$ will indeed bias the proxy toward better responses rather than the original generator. But that is actually what you want for DPO’s role as a reference: the reference is there to define a KL-style anchor and to make the log-ratios numerically and semantically sensible. A proxy trained on winners is less likely to assign tiny probability to good completions, which would otherwise make the ratios unstable and increase mismatch between the data and the model.

So the paper’s answer to your concern is essentially: yes, it is a pragmatic approximation, but not an arbitrary one. They explicitly say that initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ this way “helps mitigate the distribution shift between the true reference distribution which is unavailable, and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ used by DPO.” In the dialogue experiment, for example, they do exactly this because no SFT model is available.

How sensitive is DPO to mismatch? The paper does not give a formal robustness theorem for reference mismatch. What it does say is that DPO is consistent under suitable assumptions on the preference data distribution, and that the reference choice defines which representative of the reward-equivalence class you are effectively learning. In other words, different $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ induce different implicit rewards, but the same preference ordering can still be represented as long as the reference is reasonable and has support where the relevant completions live. If the proxy reference is badly mismatched, then the ratios $\log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ no longer correspond to the intended KL-regularized update, and training can become less stable or push the model in a different direction. The paper’s claim is therefore not that the proxy is exact, but that it is a useful way to reuse offline preference datasets when the original reference model is unavailable.

So the short version is: this is mainly a principled approximation to the unavailable baseline, not an attempt to estimate the hidden sampling policy. DPO depends on a reference model, but that reference is a modeling choice inside the objective, and the paper’s preferred-completion MLE proxy is meant to preserve the geometry of the preference data rather than reconstruct the true generator.

### Why does DPO not need on-policy sampling during training if the RLHF objective is an expectation over $y \sim \pi_\theta$?
Question:
One thing I find surprising is that standard RLHF usually samples from the current policy during optimization, but DPO trains only on a fixed dataset of preference pairs.

Since the original RL objective is

$$
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta \|\| \pi_{\text{ref}}),
$$

I would have expected the optimization to depend on the model's current generations. But DPO turns it into a binary classification objective over offline pairs.

What is the exact reason this offline objective is enough? Is it because the Bradley-Terry likelihood already contains all the information needed about the optimal policy ratios, or is there still some hidden distribution-shift issue that is being ignored?
Answer:
The key point is that DPO is not directly optimizing the original expectation over $y \sim \pi_\theta$ in the way PPO does. Instead, the paper shows that under the Bradley-Terry preference model and the KL-regularized RLHF objective, you can reparameterize the reward in terms of the optimal policy itself, and then the policy optimization problem becomes equivalent to fitting a pairwise preference classifier on an offline dataset.

Concretely, the RLHF objective is

$$
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}}),
$$

and its optimizer has the closed form

$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$

That means you can invert the relationship and write the reward as

$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
$$

Now the crucial observation in the paper is that Bradley-Terry preferences depend only on reward differences, $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$. So when you substitute this expression into the Bradley-Terry likelihood, the partition term $\beta \log Z(x)$ cancels out. The result is a preference probability written entirely in terms of the policy ratio $\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and maximizing the likelihood of the observed preference pairs gives the DPO loss

$$
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal D}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
$$

So the offline dataset is enough because DPO is not trying to estimate an expectation by sampling from the current policy. It is instead doing maximum likelihood on a reparameterized preference model whose optimal policy is analytically linked to the reward-maximizing RLHF solution. In that sense, the dataset already contains the supervision signal needed to identify which completions should be relatively more probable than others.

This does not mean distribution shift disappears entirely. The paper is explicit that the reference model matters, because the learned reward is only valid relative to that reference distribution. That is why they sample preference data from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ in the standard setup, and when an SFT model is unavailable they initialize $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by maximizing likelihood on the preferred completions to reduce mismatch. The paper also notes that the reward class is only identifiable up to an additive function of $x$, which is fine because such terms do not affect the optimal policy or the preference probabilities.

So the short answer is: yes, the Bradley-Terry form is doing the heavy lifting, but more precisely the paper exploits the fact that the KL-regularized RLHF optimum can be written as a policy ratio against $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and that the pairwise preference likelihood depends only on differences. That is why an offline pairwise dataset is sufficient for DPO. What is being “ignored” is not the preference structure, but the need for online rollouts during training; the paper argues that this is unnecessary once you make the right reparameterization.

### Why does DPO sometimes outperform PPO even though the paper says they optimize the same objective?
Question:
A central claim of the paper is that DPO and PPO are optimizing the same KL-regularized reward maximization objective, yet the experiments show DPO can produce a strictly better reward-vs-KL frontier and even beat PPO with access to ground-truth rewards.

If the objective is the same, shouldn't an ideal PPO implementation eventually reach the same optimum? Is the gap entirely due to optimization issues like variance, reward normalization, clipping, and hyperparameter sensitivity? Or is there some subtle mismatch between the practical PPO objective and the theoretical one used in the DPO derivation?

I'm trying to understand whether the empirical advantage of DPO is fundamentally about optimization, estimation, or objective mismatch.
Answer:
The key point is that the paper’s “same objective” claim is theoretical, not a claim that the practical training procedures are identical.

In the derivation, DPO and KL-regularized RLHF are connected through an exact change of variables: if the reward satisfies the Bradley-Terry preference model, then the optimal policy for

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}[r(x,y)] - \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
$$

can be written in closed form as

$$
\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$

DPO then rewrites the preference likelihood directly in terms of $\pi_\theta$ instead of learning $r_\phi$ and running RL. So at the level of the idealized model, both methods are targeting the same KL-regularized optimum.

But PPO in practice is not “idealized objective optimization”; it is an approximate actor-critic method applied to a learned reward model. The paper explicitly points out several sources of mismatch and instability. First, PPO usually optimizes a reward of the form

$$
r(x,y)=r_\phi(x,y)-\beta\bigl(\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)-\log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr),
$$

and the normalization term that arises from the exact reparameterization is not naturally available. The authors note that this term acts like a soft value function; omitting it or estimating it crudely increases variance and makes policy gradients unstable. Second, the reward itself is learned from preference data, so there is estimation error before PPO even starts. Third, PPO’s clipped surrogate objective, value-function learning, advantage estimation, reward normalization, and target-KL tuning are all extra approximations and hyperparameters that can matter a lot.

That is why DPO can beat PPO even when PPO has access to ground-truth rewards in the controlled experiment. DPO is not necessarily “better at solving the exact same optimization problem” in an abstract sense; rather, it avoids the brittle parts of the standard RLHF pipeline. It turns the problem into a single supervised-style binary classification loss, with no reward-model training loop, no on-policy sampling during optimization, and no critic/value-function machinery. In the paper’s own language, DPO “bypasses” the instability of actor-critic methods and uses a reparameterization that removes the need for baselines.

So the short answer is: the gap is mainly about optimization and estimation in the practical PPO pipeline, plus some objective mismatch between the exact theoretical KL-regularized problem and the surrogate objective actually optimized by PPO. DPO’s advantage is not that it changes the target; it is that it reaches that target more directly and with fewer sources of variance and tuning sensitivity.
