\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How should I interpret the role of $\beta$ in DPO compared with $\beta$ in RLHF?
Question:
In the paper, $\beta$ appears in both the KL-constrained RLHF objective and in the DPO loss

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\mathbb{E}\left[\log \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
$$

I initially thought larger $\beta$ would mean the model is allowed to move farther from the reference policy, but in the RL objective it multiplies the KL penalty, so that sounds backwards.

Can someone explain intuitively what increasing or decreasing $\beta$ does in DPO training? Also, does the exact same interpretation carry over from the RLHF objective, or does the reparameterization change the practical meaning of $\beta$?
Answer:
In the paper, $\beta$ has essentially the same *conceptual* role in RLHF and DPO: it sets how strongly you want to stay near the reference policy while still moving toward preferred outputs. The important subtlety is that it appears in the two formulations in different algebraic places, so it is easy to misread its effect.

In the RLHF objective
$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}[r_\phi(x,y)] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}),
$$
a larger $\beta$ means the KL penalty is weighted more heavily, so the optimizer is pushed harder to remain close to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. In other words, increasing $\beta$ makes the policy more conservative and decreasing $\beta$ lets it move farther away from the reference. The paper says this explicitly: $\beta$ is “a parameter controlling the deviation from the base reference policy.”

DPO uses the same underlying RLHF problem, but after reparameterizing the reward in terms of the optimal policy. That is why the DPO loss contains terms like
$$
\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
This does **not** mean the interpretation flips. It is still the same tradeoff parameter, but now it is embedded inside the implicit reward definition
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
Because the optimal-policy relation is
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
$$
you can see the inverse dependence directly: for a fixed reward difference, larger $\beta$ makes the exponential tilt weaker, so the optimal policy stays closer to the reference. That is the same meaning as in RLHF.

So the clean intuition is: in both RLHF and DPO, larger $\beta$ means “more reference-anchored, less aggressive preference optimization,” while smaller $\beta$ means “more willing to depart from the reference to satisfy preferences.” The reparameterization in DPO changes the *form* of the objective, but not the practical interpretation of $\beta$.

The paper also notes a mechanistic effect in the gradient: the examples are weighted by how wrong the implicit reward ordering is, “scaled by $\beta$,” so $\beta$ also affects the strength of the update. But that is still the same tradeoff in different clothing: larger $\beta$ changes both the sharpness of the preference fit and the implied KL pressure toward the reference.
