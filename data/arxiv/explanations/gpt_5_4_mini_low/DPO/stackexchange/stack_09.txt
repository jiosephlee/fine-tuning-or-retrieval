\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does PPO need a value-function-like normalization term, but DPO does not?
Question:
In the theory section, the paper rewrites the actor-critic objective and highlights a normalization term

$$
\beta \log \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y)\right),
$$

saying this can be viewed as a soft value function of the reference policy and that omitting it can make policy gradients high variance.

I do not understand this connection. Why is this term analogous to a value baseline, and why does its absence destabilize PPO? Also, if DPO effectively bakes this normalization into the parameterization, is that the main reason it is more stable, or is the stability claim coming from something else?
Answer:
The normalization term is “value-like” for essentially the same reason a baseline is value-like in policy gradients: it depends only on the prompt $x$, not on which completion $y$ was sampled, so it shifts all actions for that prompt by the same amount.

In the paper, the KL-constrained RLHF objective has optimal policy

$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
$$

with

$$
Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$

Taking logs gives

$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta \log Z(x).
$$

The key point is that $\beta \log Z(x)$ is a function of $x$ only. So for a fixed prompt, it is the same constant added to every candidate completion. That is exactly the role played by a baseline in RL: baselines do not change the expected gradient, but they reduce variance by removing a large prompt-specific offset from the reward signal. In this paper’s language, the normalization term is the “soft value function of the reference policy” because it is the log-partition term that summarizes how good the prompt is under the current reward landscape, analogous to a state value in standard actor-critic methods.

Why does omitting it make PPO unstable? Because PPO is optimizing a sampled reward signal, and if that reward has a large prompt-dependent offset, the policy gradient has to learn from noisy differences between very different prompts. The paper notes that prior RLHF systems often normalize rewards, either with a learned value function or with a human-completion baseline, precisely to reduce variance. Without such centering, the updates can be dominated by reward scale and offset rather than by the relative preference between completions. That is one reason the paper says the gradient can become high variance and learning unstable. In other words, the issue is not that the optimum changes, but that the path to the optimum becomes much noisier.

For DPO, the stability story is slightly different and stronger than just “it has the normalization built in.” DPO does not literally estimate the partition function during training. Instead, it rewrites the preference model so that the partition term cancels in pairwise comparisons. That gives the loss

$$
-\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right),
$$

which directly compares preferred and dispreferred completions. So DPO avoids the unstable ingredients of PPO-based RLHF altogether: no separate reward model to fit and normalize, no sampling-based RL loop, and no critic/value function to train. The paper is explicit that this is a major source of the improved stability and simplicity.

So the short answer is: the normalization term is baseline-like because it is prompt-dependent but completion-independent, hence it centers the reward without changing the optimal policy. PPO needs something like that to keep gradients low-variance. DPO is more stable mainly because it absorbs the RLHF objective into a pairwise classification loss where the problematic partition/baseline term cancels analytically; the paper also emphasizes that its gradient includes a dynamic weighting factor, which helps prevent degeneration.
