\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What assumptions are hidden in the statement that DPO recovers the optimal RLHF policy?
Question:
The paper says DPO can recover the optimal policy corresponding to the standard RLHF problem, but I am not sure under what conditions this is actually true.

For example:

1. Does this require the human preferences to exactly follow a Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce model?
2. Does it assume infinite data or a realizable model class?
3. Does it assume the preference pairs were sampled from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ specifically?
4. What breaks if the preference labels are noisy or systematically inconsistent?

I'm trying to separate the exact mathematical equivalence in the idealized setting from the practical approximation in real preference datasets.
Answer:
The “DPO recovers the optimal RLHF policy” statement is true only in the paper’s idealized setting, and the hidden assumptions are exactly the ones needed to make the algebraic equivalence go through.

The key point is that DPO is not claiming to solve arbitrary RLHF from arbitrary preference data. It is claiming that if the data really come from the same preference model used in the derivation, then the DPO objective is just a reparameterization of the standard KL-regularized RLHF objective. In the paper’s notation, the RLHF objective is

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal D,\; y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta \, \mathbb D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)],
$$

whose optimal policy has the closed form

$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$

Then, under Bradley-Terry, the preference probability depends only on reward differences, so the unknown normalizer $Z(x)$ cancels. This is what lets the paper rewrite the preference likelihood directly in terms of $\pi_\theta$.

So to your specific questions:

1. Yes, the exact equivalence is derived under a Bradley-Terry style pairwise preference model, or more generally a Plackett-Luce ranking model when you have ranked lists rather than pairs. The paper explicitly says the Bradley-Terry case is the main one, and that the same style of derivation works for Plackett-Luce. If human preferences do not behave like a latent reward passed through such a model, then DPO is no longer exactly “recovering the RLHF optimum”; it is just optimizing a useful surrogate.

2. Yes, the recovery claim is an asymptotic, well-specified statement. The paper phrases its theory as holding “under mild assumptions” and notes consistency “under suitable assumption[s] of the preference data distribution.” In practice, exact recovery requires the model class to be rich enough to represent the optimal policy, and enough data for the maximum-likelihood estimate to converge. If the policy class is misspecified or the data are finite, you only get an approximation, not exact equality with the true RLHF optimum.

3. The derivation is tied to a particular reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, because the RLHF objective itself is KL-regularized toward that reference. In the paper, the offline preference pairs are usually generated by sampling completions from $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$, and DPO ideally uses that same model as $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. However, the paper also explicitly says that when the original SFT model is unavailable, they initialize $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ by fitting preferred completions to reduce distribution shift. So the exact mathematical equivalence is with the reference policy appearing in the RLHF objective; if your dataset was sampled from a different distribution, the equivalence becomes approximate.

4. Noisy or systematically inconsistent labels break the idealized “optimal policy recovery” interpretation. The paper assumes the comparison labels are sampled from a latent preference distribution $p^*$ that is compatible with Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce. If labels are noisy but the noise still matches that model, then the MLE interpretation still makes sense: you are fitting the best model under that stochastic preference process. But if labels are systematically inconsistent with any latent reward function, then there may be no reward $r^*$ whose Bradley-Terry probabilities explain the data. In that case, DPO still minimizes its cross-entropy loss, but the solution is only the best fit to the misspecified preference model, not the RLHF-optimal policy in the paper’s sense.

The cleanest way to summarize the paper is this: DPO exactly recovers the RLHF optimum only in a realizable, well-specified, infinite-data setting where preferences come from a Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce model induced by some latent reward, the reference policy is the one appearing in the KL-regularized RLHF objective, and the policy class is expressive enough to contain the optimum. Outside that regime, DPO is still a practical approximation, and the paper’s experiments are precisely about how well that approximation works.
