\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why isn't DPO just the same as maximizing the probability of preferred responses and minimizing dispreferred ones?
Question:
The gradient discussion says DPO increases the likelihood of the preferred completion $y_w$ and decreases the likelihood of the dispreferred completion $y_l$, with a weighting term

$$
\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x,y_w)).
$$

This makes it sound very similar to a simple pairwise supervised objective or unlikelihood training. But the paper claims the weighting is important and that the naive version can lead to degeneration.

Could someone explain, preferably with a toy example, what this weighting is doing that plain preferred-vs-dispreferred log-likelihood training is not? Why does this weighting arise naturally from the derivation rather than being just a heuristic?
Answer:
No — the DPO update is **not** just “increase $\pi(y_w \mid x)$ and decrease $\pi(y_l \mid x)$” in the raw, unweighted sense. The key difference is that DPO is optimizing a **logistic preference loss** over the *difference* of implicit rewards, so the update is automatically **self-modulated by confidence**. That weighting is not a heuristic add-on; it is exactly what you get when you take the gradient of the DPO objective.

If we write the DPO loss for one preference pair as

$$
\ell(\theta) = -\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}\right),
$$

then defining the implicit reward
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
$$
the gradient becomes proportional to
$$
\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr)\Bigl[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)-\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)\Bigr].
$$

That sigmoid factor is the important part. It is large when the model currently believes the wrong thing, namely when $\hat r_\theta(x,y_l) > \hat r_\theta(x,y_w)$, and small when the model already ranks the preferred completion above the dispreferred one. So DPO does **not** keep pushing every example with equal force. It focuses its learning on pairs that are still misordered or only weakly separated.

This is exactly the same intuition as ordinary logistic regression: examples that are already classified correctly with high confidence contribute little gradient, while hard or misclassified examples contribute more. DPO inherits that property because it is, in effect, a logistic classification problem over preference pairs.

A toy example makes the difference clear. Suppose for a given prompt $x$ you have two comparisons. For one pair, the model already assigns much higher implicit reward to the preferred response, say $\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)=10$. Then
$$
\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))=\sigma(-10)\approx 0,
$$
so the gradient is tiny. DPO basically says, “this pair is already solved; don’t waste capacity pushing it further.” For another pair, the model gets it backwards, say $\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)=-2$. Then
$$
\sigma(2)\approx 0.88,
$$
so the gradient is strong. That example gets a big corrective update. This is precisely what prevents the model from being driven aggressively on examples it already handles well.

By contrast, a naive objective like “maximize $\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and minimize $\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$” applies pressure more uniformly. In its simplest form it does not know whether the model is already making the right preference distinction or not. So the same pair can keep contributing a large update even after the model has already put $y_w$ above $y_l$. Over many steps, that kind of unweighted pressure can over-sharpen the policy, distort probabilities too much, and lead to degeneration, which is what the paper reports for the unlikelihood-style baseline.

The deeper reason the weighting arises naturally is that DPO is not invented as a heuristic pairwise ranking loss. The paper starts from the standard KL-constrained RLHF objective and uses the Bradley-Terry preference model. After reparameterizing the reward in terms of the policy ratio $\log \pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{ref}}$, the preference likelihood becomes a binary cross-entropy objective over the policy itself. The sigmoid weight is then just the derivative of that likelihood. In other words, DPO is doing maximum likelihood under the same preference model as reward-model training, but directly in policy space. The weighting is therefore part of the probabilistic model, not an extra trick.

So the short answer is: plain preferred-vs-dispreferred training says “always push,” while DPO says “push in proportion to how wrong or uncertain you currently are.” That adaptive weighting is exactly what makes DPO stable and why the paper warns that removing it can cause degeneration.
