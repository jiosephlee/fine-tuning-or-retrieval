\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What does Theorem 1 really mean when it says DPO does not lose reward-model generality?
Question:
The theorem says that under mild assumptions, all reward classes consistent with Plackett-Luce can be represented as

$$
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
$$

for some model $\pi$.

I am having trouble understanding the significance of this. At first glance, this looks like a very restrictive form for the reward, because it is tied to a particular reference policy and a normalized conditional distribution.

So when the paper says there is "no loss of generality," is it only true modulo the equivalence relation $r \sim r + f(x)$? If yes, does that mean DPO can represent every preference distribution but not every raw reward function uniquely? I think I am mixing up representational equivalence and identifiability.
Answer:
Yes — the key point is that Theorem 1 is about the *reward class* that matters for preferences and the KL-constrained RL objective, not about recovering a unique scalar reward function.

In the paper’s setup, two reward functions are considered equivalent if they differ by a prompt-dependent offset, $r(x,y) \sim r'(x,y)$ when $r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)$. This is exactly the right equivalence relation, because both the Bradley-Terry/Plackett-Luce preference model and the KL-regularized RL optimum depend only on reward *differences across completions for the same prompt*. Adding $f(x)$ to every completion for a fixed $x$ changes neither $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$ nor the optimal policy. So the reward is not identifiable up to an absolute additive baseline, only up to this equivalence class.

That is what Theorem 1 is really saying: for every such reward class, there exists some policy $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$ such that a representative of the class can be written as
$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
More precisely, if you start from any reward function $r$, the paper defines a normalized version
$$
r'(x,y)=r(x,y)-\beta \log \sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
$$
and this differs from $r$ only by a function of $x$. That normalization term is the log partition function, and it is exactly what makes the corresponding $\pi(y\mid x)$ sum to $1$. So the theorem is not claiming that every raw reward function literally has that form without modification; it is claiming that every *equivalence class* of rewards consistent with the preference model has a representative of that form.

So your intuition is right: DPO is not uniquely identifying every raw reward function. It is representing the preference-relevant part of the reward, i.e. the class modulo $r \sim r+f(x)$. But that is not a weakness; it is the same notion of identifiability already present in Bradley-Terry/Plackett-Luce. In fact, the paper explicitly points out that this under-specification is a standard issue in reward modeling. DPO simply chooses one convenient representative from each class, namely the one tied to a normalized policy ratio with respect to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.

So when the paper says “no loss of generality,” it means “no loss relative to the preference model and the RL objective,” not “we can recover an arbitrary absolute reward function uniquely.” DPO can represent every preference distribution in the model class, but only up to the natural additive ambiguity in reward.
