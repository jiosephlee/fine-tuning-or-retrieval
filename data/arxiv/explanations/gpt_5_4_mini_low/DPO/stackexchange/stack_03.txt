\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why is DPO called 'RL-free' if it optimizes the same KL-constrained RLHF objective?
Question:
The paper repeatedly says DPO solves the same problem as RLHF but without reinforcement learning. This confuses me because the starting point is still the KL-regularized objective

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta} \; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x) \|\| \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\right).
$$

If the objective is an RL objective, in what precise sense is DPO not RL? Is it just that the optimum can be rewritten as a supervised classification loss? Or is there a deeper distinction between "solving an RL-derived objective" and "running an RL algorithm"?
Answer:
DPO is called “RL-free” not because it changes the underlying preference objective, but because it avoids the *reinforcement learning procedure* usually used to optimize that objective.

The paper is quite explicit about this distinction. In RLHF, one first fits a reward model from comparisons and then solves the KL-regularized control problem
\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\right),
\]
typically with PPO or a related policy-gradient method. That is “RL” in the usual practical sense: you need on-policy sampling from the current policy, reward computation, and a reinforcement-learning optimization loop.

DPO’s key observation is that for this KL-regularized objective, the optimal policy has a closed form,
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
\]
and under the Bradley-Terry preference model the partition function cancels when you write preference probabilities in terms of the optimal policy. That lets the authors reparameterize the reward in terms of the policy itself and turn the problem into a simple binary cross-entropy loss over preference pairs:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=
-\mathbb{E}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\]

So the deeper distinction is this: DPO is not “doing RL” because it does not solve the KL-constrained objective by sampling trajectories and applying a policy-gradient or actor-critic algorithm. Instead, it directly fits the policy with a supervised classification loss derived from the same objective. The paper even says this in a very literal way: DPO is “a simple RL-free algorithm,” and “the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective.”

So your summary is basically right: the objective is RL-derived, but the optimization is not RL. “RL-free” here means “no explicit reward model, no rollout-based policy optimization, no PPO loop,” not “unrelated to reinforcement learning in theory.” In fact, the paper’s main point is that DPO solves the same preference-aligned control problem while bypassing the machinery of RLHF.
