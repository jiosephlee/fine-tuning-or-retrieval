\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

# Patches, Not Tokens: How BLT Learns to Spend Compute Where It Matters

Large language models usually rely on tokenization: text is broken into a fixed vocabulary of subword pieces, and the model processes one token at a time. That works well, but it also forces every token to get the same amount of compute, even when some parts of the text are easy to predict and others are genuinely hard.

The Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, takes a different approach. Instead of starting from a fixed vocabulary, it works directly on raw bytes and groups them into dynamic patches. The key idea is simple: predictable text should be compressed into larger patches, while uncertain text should be split into smaller ones. That lets BLT spend more of its compute where the next-byte prediction is harder.

## Why fixed tokens waste compute

Tokenization is a convenient heuristic, but it is not always aligned with prediction difficulty. A tokenizer may compress common words into single tokens and rare or messy text into smaller pieces, but the result is still a fixed segmentation scheme. The model then applies roughly the same amount of processing to every token.

BLT argues that this is not the best use of compute. Consider two examples. Completing the word “Mozart” after “Who composed the Magic Flute?” is relatively easy once the model sees the beginning of the answer. By contrast, deciding the first byte of the answer is much harder. These two situations have very different uncertainty, but a token-based model gives them the same basic treatment.

BLT’s solution is to make patch boundaries depend on the data itself.

## Dynamic patching with next-byte entropy

BLT creates patches using a small byte-level language model that estimates the entropy of the next byte at each position. In simple terms, entropy here measures uncertainty: high entropy means the model is less sure what byte comes next, while low entropy means the next byte is easy to guess.

BLT uses these entropy estimates to decide where a new patch should begin. The intuition is that patch boundaries should appear around harder-to-predict regions. When the next-byte distribution is uncertain, BLT cuts the sequence into a new patch. When the text is predictable, it keeps bytes together in a longer patch.

This creates a form of adaptive compression. Easy stretches of text become fewer, larger patches, which means fewer expensive steps in the main transformer. Harder regions are broken up more finely, so the model can devote more capacity to them.

## A smarter way to allocate compute

The architecture behind BLT is built around this idea of variable compute. It has three main pieces: a small local encoder, a large global latent transformer, and a small local decoder.

The local encoder reads bytes and turns them into patch representations. The global transformer operates on those patches and does most of the heavy lifting. The local decoder then turns the patch-level outputs back into bytes. Since the global transformer is the expensive part, running it on fewer patches saves compute. That is the whole point of dynamic patching.

The nice part is that BLT does not just choose a single patch size for the whole model. It can use longer patches where text is predictable and shorter patches where it is not. In other words, it allocates more transformer steps only when the input actually deserves them.

## Why entropy-based patching matters

The paper compares several patching strategies, including fixed-size patches and whitespace-based patches. Those are useful baselines, but entropy-based patching is the most flexible because it is driven by the actual difficulty of prediction rather than a hand-written rule.

Whitespace patching already gives a hint of the idea: words often make a natural unit, and the beginning of a word is often harder to predict than the rest. Entropy patching generalizes that intuition. It does not assume that word boundaries are always the right place to split. Instead, it uses a learned estimate of uncertainty to decide where compute should be spent.

That matters because text is not uniform. Code, math, multilingual text, and noisy inputs all have different structure. A fixed segmentation scheme cannot adapt to those differences, but entropy-based patching can.

## The result: longer patches when text is easy

The paper shows that BLT can scale with average patch sizes larger than the average byte count per token used by tokenizer-based models. In the experiments, larger patches reduce inference flops because the global transformer runs less often. Those saved flops can be used to grow the model itself, which creates a new scaling tradeoff.

This is the core insight behind “patches scale better than tokens.” Tokens are constrained by a fixed vocabulary, so increasing token size quickly runs into tradeoffs in the embedding and output layers. BLT avoids that bottleneck because patches are not drawn from a fixed vocabulary. Their size can vary dynamically, and the model can keep getting more efficient as patch size grows.

## Why this helps in practice

The paper reports two broad benefits from direct byte modeling with dynamic patches.

First, BLT is more robust to noisy inputs. Because it sees raw bytes, it is less brittle when text includes unusual capitalization, dropped characters, or other perturbations that can confuse tokenizers.

Second, BLT shows stronger character-level understanding. That includes spelling-sensitive tasks, grapheme-to-phoneme conversion, and low-resource machine translation. These are all areas where byte-level information matters more than a tokenizer’s predefined units.

So the advantage is not just theoretical efficiency. Better patching also seems to improve how the model handles the long tail of text.

## The big picture

BLT’s main innovation is not simply “remove tokenization.” It is to replace fixed token boundaries with dynamic patch boundaries chosen from next-byte entropy. That allows the model to compress easy regions aggressively and spend extra compute on difficult ones.

This is a more adaptive way to run a language model. Instead of treating every token as equally important, BLT asks a better question: where is the text hard enough to justify more computation?

That question leads to patches, not tokens.
