\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

1. Why Move Beyond Tokens?

# How Tokenization Works in Modern LLMs

Modern large language models are usually not trained directly on raw text characters or bytes. Instead, they first apply tokenization, a preprocessing step that converts the byte stream into a sequence of discrete symbols drawn from a fixed vocabulary. In the paper, this is described as a heuristic that groups bytes into a static set of tokens before the model sees the data. The resulting tokens are then the units processed by the Transformer.

This design has a major practical advantage: it shortens the sequence length. A model trained on tokens processes fewer units than one trained on individual bytes, so the expensive parts of training and inference are applied less often. That is one reason tokenization became standard in modern LLMs. The paper frames tokenization as deeply embedded in current end-to-end language model pipelines, even though it is not itself learned as part of the model.

At the same time, tokenization imposes a specific, fixed way of compressing the data. Because the vocabulary is fixed in advance, the model does not directly decide how to group bytes on the fly. Instead, the tokenizer determines the segmentation before the model begins computation. The paper’s central claim is that this design may not be the best way to allocate compute, because the boundaries chosen by a tokenizer do not always line up with where prediction is actually easy or hard.

# Strengths and Weaknesses of Fixed-Vocabulary Tokenization

The main strength of fixed-vocabulary tokenization is efficiency. By turning long byte sequences into shorter token sequences, tokenization reduces the number of steps the main Transformer must run. That matters because the paper emphasizes that, at scale, the dominant cost is not attention alone but also the feed-forward layers that run on every step. Reducing sequence length can therefore reduce both training and inference cost.

Tokenization also provides a convenient and standardized interface for language modeling. Since the vocabulary is fixed, the model’s input and output spaces are well defined in advance. This makes large-scale training practical and has enabled the success of token-based LLMs such as Llama 3, which the paper uses as the main baseline.

However, the paper argues that this efficiency comes with a tradeoff: the tokenizer imposes a heuristic compression scheme that is not always aligned with the underlying complexity of the prediction problem. Tokenization allocates the same amount of model compute to each token, but not all tokens are equally hard to predict. Some byte sequences are simple continuations of a word, while others begin a new word or a new phrase and are much less certain. A fixed token boundary can therefore waste computation on easy predictions and under-allocate computation where uncertainty is high.

The paper also notes that tokenization-based approaches make it difficult to vary token size substantially without changing the vocabulary size and the cost of the output projection layer. This means tokenization-based models have limited flexibility in changing the average unit size they operate on. In contrast, BLT is designed to vary patch size dynamically without relying on a fixed vocabulary.

# Failure Modes of Tokenization: Noise Sensitivity, Orthographic Blind Spots, Multilingual Inequity, Domain Sensitivity

The paper identifies several ways in which tokenization can be brittle. One problem is sensitivity to input noise. Because tokenization depends on the exact surface form of the byte sequence, small changes in casing, spacing, character repetition, or character deletion can change how the input is segmented into tokens. The paper evaluates this directly with noisy versions of benchmark tasks and finds that tokenizer-based models degrade more than BLT.

Another failure mode is a lack of orthographic knowledge. Since tokens are treated as atomic units, a tokenizer-based model may know that a token has a spelling, but it does not directly operate on the internal character structure of that spelling. The paper argues that this creates blind spots in tasks that require manipulating or reasoning about sub-word and character-level structure, such as spelling-related tasks and other character-level benchmarks.

The paper also points to multilingual inequity. A fixed vocabulary may segment languages unevenly, giving some languages or scripts less favorable compression than others. Because tokenization is driven by a predetermined vocabulary and heuristic segmentation, it can treat different writing systems in uneven ways. This is especially relevant when modeling low-resource languages or languages with varying script properties.

Finally, the paper discusses domain and modality sensitivity. Since tokenization reflects a particular compression strategy, it may fit some domains better than others. The same tokenizer can behave differently on code, natural language, math, or other structured data. The paper’s argument is not that tokenization fails everywhere, but that its fixed segmentation can be misaligned with the diversity of real data encountered by large language models.

# Why Byte-Level Modeling Is Appealing

Byte-level modeling removes the tokenizer entirely and trains directly on the raw byte stream. The paper presents this as appealing for several reasons. First, it avoids the heuristic preprocessing step of tokenization altogether. This means the model no longer depends on a fixed vocabulary chosen in advance.

Second, byte-level modeling gives the model direct access to the underlying byte structure of text. This is important because many of the failure modes of tokenization arise precisely from hiding that structure. With bytes, the model can learn about spelling, character composition, and subtle patterns in the raw input rather than only seeing compressed subword units.

Third, byte-level modeling is naturally more robust to surface-form variation. Since bytes are the underlying representation, the model can process noisy inputs, unusual casing, and nonstandard character sequences without depending on whether a tokenizer recognizes them cleanly. The paper argues that this provides improved robustness and better long-tail generalization.

Finally, byte-level modeling avoids multilingual inequity introduced by a fixed subword vocabulary. Because bytes are universal across scripts and languages, byte-level models can in principle treat all languages more uniformly at the input level. The paper reports that BLT improves on low-resource machine translation tasks and character-level tasks that are difficult for token-based systems.

# Why Byte-Level Modeling Has Historically Been Too Expensive

Despite these advantages, direct byte-level modeling has historically been too costly at scale. The fundamental reason is sequence length. If a model works on bytes rather than tokens, it must process many more units for the same text. That increases the number of computation steps, especially for attention-based architectures.

The paper explains that previous work tried to mitigate this with more efficient self-attention methods or with attention-free architectures. These ideas can help, but the paper argues they mainly help smaller models. At larger scale, the total cost is dominated not just by attention but by the feed-forward layers that are executed on every byte. So simply making attention cheaper does not fully solve the scaling problem.

This is why earlier byte-level systems struggled to match token-based LLMs at large scale. The paper cites prior byte-level and character-level work that showed promise, often with robustness gains, but notes that the compute burden remained a serious obstacle. In particular, operating on every byte with a full Transformer remains expensive unless the model can somehow avoid spending full computation uniformly on every position.

# The Main Idea of BLT: Dynamic Grouping of Bytes into Patches

BLT’s central idea is to group bytes into dynamically sized patches and make patches the main unit of computation. Instead of applying the largest Transformer to every byte, BLT first segments the byte stream into patches and then runs its large global Transformer over those patches. The local models handle the byte-level details, while the latent Transformer handles the heavier global computation.

The crucial point is that the patching is dynamic and learnable rather than fixed. BLT does not use a fixed vocabulary for patches. Instead, arbitrary groups of bytes are mapped into latent patch representations by lightweight encoder and decoder modules. This means the model can choose longer patches where the data is predictable and shorter patches where the data is more complex.

The paper emphasizes that patching is driven by entropy, specifically the entropy of the next-byte prediction under a small byte-level language model. Intuitively, bytes that are easy to predict can be grouped together into longer patches, while bytes that are harder to predict can trigger new patch boundaries. This lets BLT allocate more compute where uncertainty is higher.

This is also why the architecture is organized into three parts: two small local models and one large global latent Transformer. The local encoder compresses bytes into patch representations, the global Transformer processes those patch representations, and the local decoder expands the patch outputs back into bytes. In this way, BLT aims to preserve byte-level flexibility while recovering much of the efficiency that tokenization traditionally provided.

# Overview of BLT’s Claimed Contributions: Scaling Parity, Inference Efficiency, Robustness, and New Scaling Dimensions

The paper presents BLT as more than just a new byte-level architecture. Its first major claim is scaling parity: for the first time, a byte-level model matches tokenization-based LLM performance at scale. The paper reports flop-controlled scaling studies up to $8$B parameters and $4$T training bytes, showing that BLT can match the training flop-controlled performance of Llama 3.

A second contribution is inference efficiency. Because BLT can use longer patches when the data is predictable, it can reduce the number of times the expensive global Transformer is invoked. The paper reports that this can yield up to $50\%$ fewer inference flops while keeping evaluation performance competitive, depending on the patch-size tradeoff chosen.

A third contribution is robustness. The paper shows that BLT performs better than token-based models on noisy inputs, character-level tasks, orthographic reasoning, phonology, and low-resource machine translation. These results are presented as evidence that direct byte modeling captures information that token-based systems tend to miss.

The fourth contribution is a new scaling dimension. In token-based systems, changing average unit size is tightly constrained by vocabulary design. In BLT, patch size can be increased while keeping inference cost fixed, and the saved compute can be reallocated to a larger global model. The paper argues that this makes it possible to simultaneously grow both patch size and model size under a fixed inference budget, leading to better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures.

In short, the paper’s motivation is not simply that bytes are more fundamental than tokens. Rather, the argument is that a model should allocate compute according to prediction difficulty, and dynamic patching is the mechanism BLT uses to do that.

2. Foundations: Bytes, Tokens, Patches, and Compute

# Byte sequences as the raw representation of text

The starting point for Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, is the idea that text can be represented directly as a sequence of bytes. In this formulation, the model does not begin with words or subword units. Instead, it receives raw byte data and learns from that directly. This is important because bytes are the most basic representation used to encode text, and they avoid the need for a predefined vocabulary.

The paper emphasizes that this byte-level perspective changes what the model can observe. Because bytes are not pre-grouped into wordpieces, the model has direct access to low-level character information and can potentially capture orthographic structure, noise, and long-tail patterns that token-based systems may miss. At the same time, the challenge is that byte sequences are often longer than token sequences, which makes naive byte-level modeling expensive. The entire BLT architecture is built around solving this problem without abandoning bytes as the input representation.

# Tokens versus patches: fixed vocabulary vs dynamic grouping

The paper draws a sharp distinction between tokens and patches. Tokens are byte-groups drawn from a fixed vocabulary determined before training. This is the standard setup in modern large language models, where a tokenizer decides how a string is broken into pieces. Because the vocabulary is fixed, the model processes the text as a sequence of discrete token IDs.

Patches, in contrast, are dynamically grouped sequences of bytes with no fixed vocabulary. BLT does not rely on a predefined list of byte groups. Instead, it segments the byte stream into variable-length patches based on context. These patches are then mapped into latent representations and processed by the main Transformer. The crucial difference is that tokenization imposes a static compression scheme, while patching is a learnable and context-dependent way of grouping bytes.

This distinction matters because tokenization and patching solve different problems. Tokenization tries to compress text into a manageable sequence length using a fixed heuristic, but that heuristic is not always aligned with prediction difficulty. Patching is designed to group bytes in a way that better matches where computation is needed. In BLT, patches are not a replacement for text itself, but a mechanism for deciding how to organize computation over byte sequences.

# How sequence length affects Transformer cost

A central motivation for patching is that Transformer cost grows with sequence length. The paper notes that, for token-based and patch-based models alike, the computational cost is primarily determined by the number of steps executed by the main Transformer. In BLT, that means the number of patches rather than the number of raw bytes. Fewer patches mean fewer expensive Transformer evaluations.

This is especially important at scale. The paper points out that, for large models, the cost is dominated not just by attention but by large feed-forward layers that are applied at each step. So even if attention itself is made more efficient, processing each byte individually remains costly because the heavy Transformer machinery still runs too often. Patching reduces the number of times that machinery must be invoked.

The basic intuition is that a long byte sequence is not automatically expensive if it can be represented by fewer patches. What matters is not the raw length of the input alone, but how many latent steps the model must take to process it. This is why patch size becomes the key variable for compute efficiency.

# Why the number of latent steps matters more than raw input length

BLT’s architecture is organized around a large global Transformer that operates on patch representations. The paper argues that what ultimately determines cost is how many latent steps this global model takes. If a sequence of bytes can be compressed into larger patches, then the global Transformer is called fewer times, and the model uses less compute.

This helps explain why BLT separates byte processing into local and global components. The local encoder and decoder handle the byte-level details, while the latent Transformer performs the expensive global reasoning over patch representations. The model is therefore not trying to eliminate byte-level processing; rather, it is trying to make the expensive part of processing happen only when necessary.

In this view, the number of latent steps is more informative than the number of bytes because the latent steps determine how often the main model pays its computational cost. A byte sequence of a given length may be cheap or expensive depending on how it is patched. That is why patching is not just a data preprocessing trick in BLT; it is the core control mechanism for compute allocation.

# Average patch size as a compute-control knob

Because BLT’s cost depends on the number of patches, the average patch size becomes a direct control knob for compute. If patches are larger on average, then fewer patches are needed to cover the same number of bytes, and the global Transformer runs fewer times. If patches are smaller, the model spends more compute and has more opportunities to refine difficult parts of the input.

The paper treats patch size as a central quantity in both training and inference. It explicitly states that the average size of a patch is the main factor determining the cost of processing data for a given patching function. This makes patch size comparable to a budget parameter: by selecting how large patches tend to be, one can control the flops spent on a sequence.

What is especially novel in the paper is that patch size is not fixed once and for all. BLT can use dynamically sized patches, which means compute can vary across contexts and data regions. In practice, this allows the model to trade off efficiency and accuracy in a way that tokenization-based systems cannot. Tokenizers choose a vocabulary once, and that largely fixes the average token size. BLT instead can vary patch size to fit the data.

# How patching changes training and inference efficiency

The paper argues that patching improves both training and inference efficiency. During training, fewer patches mean fewer expensive global Transformer steps. During inference, larger patches reduce the number of latent computations required to generate output, which can translate into significant flop savings. The paper reports that BLT can achieve up to $50\%$ fewer inference flops in some settings.

This efficiency gain comes from dynamically selecting longer patches when the data is predictable. If the current region of text is relatively easy to forecast, the model can group more bytes into one patch and spend less compute there. Conversely, if the region is hard, the model can allocate more steps by using smaller patches. This gives BLT a way to match computation to complexity rather than treating every part of the sequence equally.

The paper also stresses that patching is useful because it helps avoid the rigid trade-offs of fixed-vocabulary tokenization. In token-based models, increasing average token size often requires a larger vocabulary, which increases the size of the output projection layer. BLT avoids this by using dynamic patches with no fixed vocabulary, so it can vary patch size without paying the same vocabulary-cost penalty. That is a major reason the architecture can explore larger effective patch sizes than tokenizers typically can.

# The idea of allocating more compute to harder parts of the input

The guiding principle behind BLT’s patching scheme is that not all bytes are equally hard to predict. Some parts of a sequence are naturally easy, while others contain more uncertainty and require more modeling power. The paper gives a simple intuition: finishing a common word is often easier than predicting the first word of a new sentence. Similarly, the ending of a word may be comparatively predictable, while the beginning of a new word or a more information-dense region may require more computation.

BLT is designed around this principle of adaptive compute allocation. Instead of using the same amount of compute for every token-like unit, it segments bytes into patches in a way that reflects local prediction difficulty. The paper describes entropy-based patching as a data-driven method for finding high-uncertainty positions by using next-byte entropy estimates from a small byte-level language model. This means patch boundaries are chosen so that regions with relatively uniform information density are grouped together.

This idea is what makes BLT more than simply a byte-level model. It is a byte-level model with a mechanism for concentrating computation where it matters most. That is the conceptual foundation for the rest of the architecture: bytes provide the raw input, patches organize computation, and the model’s latent Transformer is invoked selectively based on how difficult the data appears to be.

3. Patching Strategies: From Static Rules to Entropy-Based Segmentation

# Formal Definition of a Patching Function

A central idea in Byte Latent Transformer ({\textsc BLT}) is that raw bytes should not all be processed with the same amount of compute. Instead, bytes are grouped into larger units called patches, and the model spends one expensive global Transformer step per patch rather than per byte. This makes the way bytes are segmented into patches a crucial design choice, because the average patch size directly determines how much computation the model uses.

Formally, a patching function $f_p$ takes a byte sequence $\pmb{x}=\{x_i \mid i=1,\ldots,n\}$ of length $n$ and maps it to a sequence of patches $\pmb{p}=\{p_j \mid j=1,\ldots,m\}$ where $m<n$. The paper describes this as mapping each byte $x_i$ to either $0$ or $1$, where $1$ indicates the start of a new patch. In other words, the patching function identifies boundaries in the byte stream. If many boundaries are created, then there are many patches and the model does more work. If boundaries are sparse, patches are longer and the model does less work.

This definition matters because BLT treats patching as more than a preprocessing trick. Patches are the primary units of computation for the global Transformer, so choosing how to segment bytes is equivalent to deciding where the model should spend its capacity. The key question is therefore not just how to compress a byte sequence, but how to compress it in a way that aligns compute with prediction difficulty.

# Strided Patching Every $K$ Bytes

The simplest patching strategy in the paper is strided patching, where bytes are grouped into fixed-size blocks of $k$ bytes. This approach is straightforward: after every $k$ bytes, a new patch begins. The paper notes that this method was used in MegaByte and that it is easy to implement for both training and inference. It also makes the average patch size easy to control, which means the compute cost is easy to tune.

The advantage of this simplicity is that it gives a direct handle on the flop budget. Since the global Transformer is invoked once per patch, increasing $k$ reduces the number of patches and therefore reduces the number of expensive Transformer steps. This makes strided patching a convenient baseline for studying compute tradeoffs.

However, the paper emphasizes that fixed striding does not adapt to the structure of the data. A fixed rule cannot tell whether the model is currently predicting something easy or difficult. As a result, the same compute is spent on all parts of the sequence, even when some regions are highly predictable and others are information-dense. The paper gives examples where this is inefficient, such as spending a Transformer step just to predict whitespace in code, or failing to allocate enough compute to bytes containing dense information such as math.

Another drawback is that strided patching can split similar byte sequences inconsistently. The same word may be broken into different patch boundaries depending on its position in the sequence, which makes the segmentation less contextual and less natural. This inconsistency is one reason the paper looks beyond fixed-size grouping.

# Benefits and Limitations of Fixed-Size Patching

Fixed-size patching has one major virtue: it is easy to reason about. Because every patch has the same length, the model’s compute pattern is regular and predictable. This makes it a useful starting point for designing byte-level architectures and for measuring how patch size affects flops.

But the paper argues that this regularity is also the main weakness. The cost of prediction is not uniform across a byte sequence. Some bytes are easy to guess from nearby context, while others are much more uncertain. A patching method that treats all positions equally cannot exploit this variation. In BLT’s framing, fixed-size patching allocates compute uniformly, while the actual information content of the sequence is not uniform.

This mismatch is especially important because BLT’s efficiency gains come from spending the expensive global modeling capacity only where it is needed. If the patching rule does not reflect the difficulty of the underlying data, then the architecture cannot fully benefit from its dynamic compute allocation design. Fixed-size patching therefore serves as a baseline, but not as the final answer.

# Space Patching and Linguistic Boundary Heuristics

The paper next discusses space patching, following the idea proposed by SpaceByte. In this scheme, a new patch is created after any space-like byte. The paper defines space-like bytes as any byte that is not a Latin character, digit, or UTF-8 continuation byte, and also requires that each patch contain at least one non-space-like byte. This heuristic uses a simple linguistic observation: spaces often mark boundaries between words or other meaningful units.

Space patching is appealing because it aligns the patch structure with natural boundaries in text. Words tend to be the right granularity for many language phenomena, and creating patches around spaces makes the segmentation more stable across different contexts. Unlike striding, space patching ensures that the same word is usually patched in the same way wherever it appears. This gives a more consistent segmentation than fixed-length grouping.

The paper also notes that space patching tends to allocate compute where predictions are harder. The first byte after a space is often difficult to predict because it begins a new word, and the number of plausible continuations is large. By contrast, the remaining bytes in a word are often easier. Thus, placing patch boundaries at spaces gives more compute to the beginning of words and less to their interior, which better matches prediction difficulty than uniform striding.

# Why Whitespace-Based Patching Helps Compared with Striding

The main reason whitespace-based patching improves on striding is that it partially aligns compute with uncertainty. A fixed stride ignores the fact that language is organized into words, whereas a space boundary often signals a transition to a new and harder prediction. The paper’s example illustrates this clearly: predicting the first byte of the answer to “Who composed the Magic Flute?” is much harder than predicting the remaining bytes after the first letter has already been seen. Once the model knows the word begins with “M,” the rest of “Mozart” becomes much easier.

Space patching captures this asymmetry better than striding. It concentrates more computation on word starts, which are typically the points of highest uncertainty in ordinary text. At the same time, it preserves consistency across sequences by using the same natural boundary rule everywhere.

Still, the paper stresses that space patching is limited. It cannot handle all languages and domains gracefully, because not all data has clean space-delimited structure. It also cannot vary patch size in a data-driven way. In BLT’s larger design, this is a serious limitation, because the whole point is to let the model spend more compute where the data is complex and less where it is predictable. Space patching is a step in that direction, but it remains a rule-based heuristic rather than a truly dynamic segmentation method.

# Entropy Patching Using a Small Next-Byte Language Model

To move beyond hand-crafted rules like whitespace, the paper introduces entropy patching. Instead of using a fixed boundary rule, entropy patching uses a small byte-level autoregressive language model to estimate where the next byte is uncertain. These uncertainty estimates then determine patch boundaries.

The intuition is that patch boundaries should appear where prediction difficulty changes. If a sequence of bytes is easy to continue, that region can be compressed into a larger patch. If the next byte becomes more uncertain, a new patch should begin so that the global Transformer can devote more compute to the difficult region. In this sense, entropy patching turns patching into a data-driven segmentation problem.

The paper describes this entropy model as a small byte-level LM trained on the same training distribution as the full BLT model. Its role is not to replace BLT, but to guide segmentation. Because the entropy model is lightweight, it can be used during preprocessing to decide where boundaries should go without making the full model expensive to run at every byte.

# Computing Next-Byte Entropy over the Byte Vocabulary

The segmentation rule is based on the entropy of the next-byte distribution. Given the current prefix $\pmb{x}_{<i}$, the entropy model defines a probability distribution $p_e$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ for the next byte $x_i$. The paper computes the next-byte entropy as

\[
H(x_i)=\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i=v \mid \pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i=v \mid \pmb{x}_{<i}).
\]

Although the expression is written in this form in the paper, the important interpretation is that entropy measures how uncertain the model is about the next byte. When the distribution over possible bytes is broad, entropy is high. When the model is confident and places most of the probability mass on a few options, entropy is low.

This makes entropy a practical proxy for local information density. Regions with low entropy are relatively predictable and can be packed into longer patches. Regions with high entropy are more uncertain and may deserve shorter patches, so that the global Transformer intervenes more often. The paper’s core claim here is that entropy gives a better signal than a fixed rule like whitespace because it responds to the actual structure of the data.

# Global Threshold Boundary Detection

The first entropy-based boundary method in the paper uses a global threshold. A new patch boundary is placed whenever the entropy at position $t$ exceeds a threshold $\theta_g$:

\[
H(x_t) > \theta_g.
\]

This rule is simple and interpretable. If the entropy model says that the next byte is sufficiently uncertain, then the model starts a new patch. In effect, the threshold divides the sequence into regions of relatively predictable and relatively unpredictable bytes.

This approach has the advantage of being easy to understand and easy to tune. By adjusting $\theta_g$, the user can control the average patch size. A lower threshold creates more boundaries and therefore shorter patches, while a higher threshold creates fewer boundaries and longer patches. This makes the method suitable for controlling the compute budget while still letting the segmentation depend on the content of the sequence.

The paper uses this thresholding idea as one of the main entropy-patching strategies. It is a direct way of turning uncertainty estimates into segmentation decisions, and it provides a clear bridge between language modeling confidence and compute allocation.

# Approximate Monotonicity Boundary Detection

The second entropy-based method looks not at the absolute entropy level, but at how entropy changes from one byte to the next. The paper defines an approximate monotonicity constraint:

\[
H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r.
\]

The idea is that within a patch, entropy often decreases or remains roughly stable as more context accumulates. A new boundary is introduced when this pattern is broken strongly enough. In other words, the method detects points where the local trend in uncertainty changes.

This can be interpreted as finding places where the sequence stops being smoothly predictable and becomes harder again. Rather than asking only whether a byte is globally uncertain, this rule asks whether it is unusually uncertain relative to the immediately preceding context. The paper suggests that this approach can be useful because it identifies points that break the approximate monotonic decrease of entropy within a patch.

The paper reports that for the large-scale BLT-Entropy run with patch size $4.5$, it resets the entropy context with new lines and uses the approximate monotonicity constraint, because this form suffers less from “entropy drift” caused by changes in context length. That detail reinforces the idea that entropy patching is sensitive to the local context in which entropy is estimated.

# Choosing Patch Boundaries During Preprocessing

A notable aspect of entropy patching in the paper is that patch boundaries are identified during a lightweight preprocessing step executed during dataloading. This means the segmentation is computed before the main model processes the data, rather than being learned end-to-end inside the architecture. The entropy model provides the boundary signal, and the resulting patch structure is then fed into BLT.

This design keeps patching efficient. The model does not need to run an expensive boundary predictor jointly with every training step. Instead, the segmentation is prepared ahead of time using a smaller model. The paper explicitly contrasts this with prior work where a classifier is trained to predict entropy-based boundaries. BLT’s approach is simpler: use a small byte LM to estimate entropy, then turn those estimates into patch boundaries as part of preprocessing.

This choice also helps separate the question of segmentation from the question of full language modeling. The entropy model acts as a practical guide for grouping bytes, while the main BLT architecture focuses on learning representations and making predictions at scale.

# Why Entropy Can Reflect Local Information Density

The conceptual reason entropy patching works is that entropy reflects how much uncertainty remains at a given position. If a byte is easy to predict from context, it carries relatively little new information and can be grouped with nearby bytes into a longer patch. If the byte is hard to predict, that is a signal that the region contains more information density, so the model should allocate more compute there.

This is exactly the kind of adaptive allocation BLT is designed to exploit. The paper emphasizes that tokenization-based models already implicitly use compression heuristics, but those heuristics are not always aligned with prediction difficulty. Entropy patching uses a learned signal instead of a fixed linguistic heuristic, which makes the segmentation sensitive to the actual data distribution.

The paper’s examples reinforce this point. In structured content such as multiple-choice tasks, entropy patching can create progressively larger patches because the repeated text is highly predictable. That behavior would be difficult to achieve with a fixed rule. Entropy therefore provides a principled way to vary patch size according to local predictability.

# Comparing Rule-Based and Learned Patching Approaches

The paper’s patching discussion ultimately compares two broad philosophies. Rule-based patching, exemplified by striding and whitespace segmentation, uses a fixed heuristic to determine boundaries. These methods are easy to implement and interpret, but they only approximate the structure of the data. Learned or data-driven patching, exemplified by entropy patching, uses a small language model to estimate where uncertainty changes and then derives boundaries from those estimates.

The main advantage of the learned approach is adaptability. It can assign shorter patches to difficult regions and longer patches to easy ones, which better matches BLT’s goal of dynamic compute allocation. It also generalizes beyond simple whitespace structure, which matters for multilingual text, code, math, and other domains where spaces are not a reliable guide.

At the same time, the paper does not present learned patching as completely replacing simpler heuristics in every setting. Instead, it shows that the best patching strategy depends on the tradeoff between performance and complexity. Space patching is a strong baseline and often competitive, while entropy patching provides the strongest evidence that dynamic segmentation can improve scaling and downstream performance. Together, these results support the paper’s larger thesis: patches scale better than tokens because they let the model choose where compute is spent, rather than forcing every unit of input to receive the same treatment.

4. Incremental Patching and the Difference Between Patches and BPE Tokens

# Review of Byte Pair Encoding (BPE)

Many modern large language models, including the Llama 3 baseline discussed in the paper, use a subword tokenizer such as byte pair encoding, or BPE. In the paper’s framing, BPE is not just a convenient preprocessing step; it is a mechanism for converting raw bytes into a sequence of units that the model processes as its basic inputs and outputs. These units are often called tokens. A token may correspond to a whole word, part of a word, or a short byte sequence, depending on what the tokenizer has learned during vocabulary construction.

The key idea behind BPE is that frequently occurring byte sequences are merged into larger units before training. This can shorten the sequence length the model sees, because several bytes may be replaced by a single token. In that sense, BPE performs a kind of compression. The paper emphasizes, however, that this compression is heuristic: it is based on a fixed vocabulary created before training, rather than being decided dynamically by the model from the current input context.

This matters because the paper’s central argument is that the way bytes are grouped should depend on the difficulty of prediction in the current context, not only on a fixed dictionary of common patterns. BPE groups bytes according to a static vocabulary, whereas BLT groups bytes into patches dynamically. That contrast becomes important when we ask how much computation the model should spend on different parts of the sequence.

# Finite Token Vocabularies and Their Constraints

A defining feature of token-based models in the paper is that they use a finite vocabulary determined before training. The model does not invent new tokens on the fly. Instead, it maps each input sequence into a sequence of items drawn from that fixed set. The paper contrasts this directly with patches, which are dynamically grouped sequences of bytes without a fixed vocabulary.

This fixed vocabulary creates several constraints. First, the model’s segmentation choices are limited to the token inventory it already knows. Second, the vocabulary size affects the model architecture itself, especially the final projection layer. The paper notes that increasing the vocabulary tends to produce larger tokens on average, which reduces the number of steps the model needs, but it also increases the size of the embedding table and output projection. In other words, a token-based model cannot freely choose larger or smaller units without paying a cost elsewhere in the model.

The paper gives a concrete example from Llama 2 and Llama 3. Llama 3 increases the average token size from $3.7$ to $4.4$ bytes, but does so at the cost of increasing the embedding table size $4\times$ compared to Llama 2. This illustrates the bottleneck the authors are pointing to: tokenization-based systems have limited flexibility in how they trade off vocabulary size, sequence length, and compute.

# Why Token-Based Models Lose Direct Access to Byte-Level Structure

The paper makes a strong distinction between working with tokens and working with bytes. When a model uses tokens, it no longer has direct access to the byte structure underneath those tokens. That means the model sees the result of a preprocessing decision, not the raw sequence itself. According to the paper, this can obscure important information.

This loss of direct access is part of why the authors describe tokenization as introducing bias into how strings are compressed. A fixed tokenizer may be sensitive to domain, modality, or language in ways that are unrelated to the actual predictive difficulty of the sequence. The paper argues that this can lead to shortcomings such as sensitivity to input noise, weak orthographic knowledge, and multilingual inequity. The essential point is that tokenization does not merely shorten sequences; it also changes what information the model can easily exploit.

BLT is designed to avoid this limitation by operating on raw bytes and learning how to group them into patches dynamically. Because the underlying bytes remain available to the model, the architecture can preserve sub-word and character-level information that token-based systems may blur away. This is one of the reasons the paper reports improved robustness and stronger character-level understanding.

# Vocabulary-Size vs Compute Tradeoffs in Token-Based LLMs

A major point in the paper is that token-based models are constrained by a tradeoff between vocabulary size and compute. Increasing the vocabulary size gives the tokenizer more freedom to create larger units, which can reduce the number of sequence positions the model must process. But a larger vocabulary also increases the dimensionality of the output projection layer and the size of the embedding table.

The paper argues that this tradeoff leaves little room for tokenization-based methods to achieve major changes in token size and inference cost. The model can only compress the input so far before the vocabulary becomes too large to be practical. This is why, in the authors’ view, fixed-vocabulary tokenization cannot fully solve the problem of allocating compute where it is most needed.

This tradeoff is important because the paper’s goal is not simply to reduce sequence length. Instead, it wants to reduce computation in a way that tracks the complexity of prediction. Tokenization-based models allocate the same amount of model computation to each token. That means the model spends equal compute on easy and hard parts of the input, even though the paper argues that these parts do not deserve equal treatment. BLT is presented as a way to escape this limitation.

# The Concept of Incremental Patching

The paper introduces the idea of incremental patching to define what a valid patching scheme must do during autoregressive generation. When BLT generates text, it must decide whether the current position in the byte sequence is a patch boundary, because that decision determines whether the latent transformer is invoked. This decision cannot depend on future bytes that have not yet been generated.

That requirement motivates the notion of incremental patching. A patching scheme is incremental if the patching of a prefix is consistent with the prefix of the patching of the full sequence. In other words, if you patch an already generated prefix, you should get the same patch boundaries that appear in that prefix when the whole sequence is eventually patched. This is essential for online generation, where the model cannot look ahead.

The paper treats this as a defining difference between patches and tokens. Patches are meant to support generation in a way that respects causality at the level of segmentation itself, not just at the level of prediction. This is why patching is not merely a preprocessing step in BLT; it is part of the model’s autoregressive machinery.

# Formal Condition for Incremental Patching

The paper gives a formal condition for incremental patching. If $f_p$ is a patching scheme, then it satisfies incremental patching when

$$
f_p(\pmb{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\pmb{x})_{<i}.
$$

This equation says that if we take the prefix of a byte sequence up to position $i$ and patch it, the result should be the same as taking the patching of the full sequence and then restricting it to the prefix. The two operations should commute.

The intuition behind this condition is straightforward. A valid patching scheme must make boundary decisions based only on information available so far. If the segmentation of a prefix could change after seeing future bytes, then the model could not reliably generate patches during inference. It would be forced to revise earlier segmentation decisions, which is incompatible with autoregressive decoding.

The paper emphasizes that this condition is not automatically satisfied by tokenization schemes like BPE. That is one of the key reasons BLT does not treat tokenization as a drop-in replacement for patching.

# Why BPE Is Not Incremental During Autoregressive Generation

The paper states directly that BPE is not an incremental patching scheme, because the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on the continuation sequence. In other words, the boundary decisions made by a BPE tokenizer are not guaranteed to be stable when new bytes are appended. This violates the incremental condition above.

This is a subtle but crucial point. If token boundaries can change when future context changes, then the segmentation of an already generated prefix is not fixed. That is acceptable for offline preprocessing, where the entire sequence is available in advance, but it is not suitable as a segmentation rule that must operate step by step during generation.

The paper notes one workaround: using a special delimiter token to mark patch boundaries can turn BPE into an incremental patching scheme, but this increases the byte-sequence length. The authors include this remark to show that incremental behavior can be engineered, but not without altering the sequence in a way that introduces extra cost. This reinforces the broader claim that tokenization is not naturally aligned with the requirements of BLT-style generation.

# Why Incremental Patching Matters for BLT Inference

Incremental patching is important for BLT because the model must decide, at inference time, when to invoke the expensive latent transformer. The global transformer is the main consumer of flops, so its invocation frequency is what determines much of the inference cost. If patch boundaries could not be determined incrementally, BLT would not be able to generate text efficiently in an autoregressive setting.

This is why the paper treats patching as a dynamic mechanism tied to inference cost control. The model needs a segmentation rule that can be applied as bytes arrive, without depending on future bytes. That allows BLT to spend more compute on more difficult regions and less compute on predictable regions, while still operating causally.

The incremental requirement also fits the paper’s broader philosophy. BLT is built to allocate compute based on context and complexity, but that allocation must happen in a way that is consistent with generation. Incremental patching provides exactly that: a causal way to decide where a new patch begins and where the latent transformer should run.

# How BLT Avoids the Token Vocabulary Bottleneck

BLT avoids the token vocabulary bottleneck by not using a fixed vocabulary for its main units of computation. Instead of mapping bytes into a predetermined set of tokens, it groups bytes into dynamically sized patches and maps those patches into latent representations using lightweight learned encoder and decoder modules. These patches are not drawn from a vocabulary; they are contextual groupings of raw bytes.

This design frees BLT from the rigid tokenization tradeoffs described earlier. Because patch size is not tied to a finite vocabulary, the model can choose larger patches when the data are predictable and smaller patches when the data are complex. That means compute can be reallocated dynamically, rather than being fixed by vocabulary design. The paper argues that this is a more efficient allocation of compute than tokenization-based models provide.

The broader consequence is that BLT can scale in ways token-based models cannot. Longer patches save compute, and the saved compute can be invested in a larger global latent transformer. This is part of the paper’s central claim that patches scale better than tokens: by separating grouping from vocabulary, BLT gains a new axis for controlling inference cost and scaling model capacity.

5. The BLT Architecture: A Hierarchical Byte-to-Patch Language Model

# Overall Architecture: Local Encoder, Global Latent Transformer, Local Decoder

The Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, is built around a simple but powerful idea: instead of processing every byte with the same expensive Transformer computation, it first groups bytes into patches and then performs the main language modeling work over those patches. The architecture has three main parts. A local encoder reads raw bytes and turns them into patch representations. A large global latent Transformer processes those patch representations autoregressively. A local decoder then turns the resulting patch-level outputs back into byte-level predictions.

This division of labor is what makes BLT different from standard token-based language models. Traditional language models operate on a fixed vocabulary of tokens, and the same sort of compute is applied to every token. BLT instead works directly from bytes, but it does not treat every byte independently at the highest level of the model. The local encoder and decoder are lightweight byte-level modules, while the global Transformer is the main capacity-bearing component that reasons over patches. In this way, BLT can preserve byte-level information while still controlling computation through patching.

The paper emphasizes that the global model consumes the bulk of the FLOPs during both pre-training and inference. That means the key efficiency question is not just how to process bytes, but how often the expensive global Transformer needs to be invoked. BLT answers this by letting the patch size vary with the data, so that the number of global steps changes depending on how predictable or complex the current portion of the sequence is.

# Separation of Local and Global Computation

BLT separates computation into local and global layers because not all parts of a byte sequence require the same amount of modeling capacity. The local models handle byte-level detail efficiently, while the global model focuses on longer-range abstraction at the patch level. This separation reflects the paper’s central intuition that some predictions are easy and low-entropy, while others are hard and require more compute.

The local encoder and local decoder are deliberately much smaller than the global Transformer. In the notation of the paper, the local encoder has $l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers, and the local decoder has $l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers. The global model is therefore the dominant source of representational power, but it is not used at every byte. Instead, it is invoked once per patch. This is the mechanism by which BLT allocates compute more selectively than tokenization-based models.

The paper argues that this separation is especially important at scale. Earlier byte-level models often reduced sequence length or attention cost, but at large model sizes the dominant cost is not just attention over long byte sequences. It is also the feed-forward computation that occurs throughout the Transformer. BLT’s patch-based design directly reduces how often the largest Transformer block must run, which is why it can achieve better inference efficiency as patch sizes grow.

# Why BLT Uses Small Byte-Level Modules and a Large Patch-Level Module

BLT uses small byte-level modules for a practical reason: bytes contain very fine-grained information, but not every byte requires the full representational machinery of a large Transformer. The local encoder and decoder are responsible for converting between the raw byte stream and the latent patch stream. Because these modules are lightweight, they can process bytes without making the overall model prohibitively expensive.

The large patch-level module exists because the actual hard reasoning in language modeling often happens at a more abstract level than individual bytes. A patch representation can summarize a group of bytes that share similar information density. Once that summary is available, the global Transformer can focus its capacity on modeling relationships between those summaries rather than on the low-level details of every byte. In the paper’s view, this is a better use of compute than applying the same large model step to every byte, regardless of complexity.

The architecture is therefore hierarchical in a very literal sense. Local modules work close to the data and preserve byte-level detail. The global module works over compressed patch representations and handles the main autoregressive modeling task. This hierarchy allows BLT to keep raw byte inputs while still gaining efficiency from dynamic grouping.

# How BLT Mixes Byte Information with Patch Representations

A central technical challenge in BLT is how to move information from bytes into patches and then back again without losing the detail that makes byte modeling useful. BLT solves this with learned cross-attention modules in both the encoder and the decoder. These cross-attention blocks are what connect the byte-level and patch-level parts of the system.

In the local encoder, byte embeddings are first created from the input bytes. These embeddings can also be augmented with hash $n$-gram embeddings, which give the model access to short byte sequences beyond the current byte alone. Then, after alternating transformer and cross-attention layers, the byte information is pooled into patch representations. The cross-attention block uses patch queries and byte keys and values, so each patch representation can selectively collect information from the bytes that belong to it.

The decoder uses the inverse direction. There, the patch representations from the global Transformer become the keys and values, while byte representations are the queries. This allows the decoder to reconstruct byte-level outputs from the patch-level latent states. The paper’s architecture therefore does not simply compress bytes and discard detail; it learns a reversible flow of information in which patches are latent summaries and the decoder restores byte predictions from them.

An important point is that the patch representations are not fixed tokens from a vocabulary. They are dynamically formed from groups of bytes. This means that the model is not restricted to a predetermined set of symbols. Instead, the representations depend on the actual byte content and the patching rule used for the current input.

# Autoregressive Modeling Over Patches and Bytes

BLT remains an autoregressive language model, but the autoregressive structure is spread across two levels. The global Transformer is autoregressive over patches, and the local decoder is autoregressive over bytes. This arrangement preserves the standard language modeling objective while changing where the expensive modeling occurs.

The global Transformer, denoted $\mathcal{G}$ in the paper, maps a sequence of latent input patch representations $p_j$ to output patch representations $o_j$. It uses a block-causal attention mask, so each patch can attend only to itself and earlier patches within the same document. This means the patch-level sequence is modeled in left-to-right order, just as in ordinary autoregressive language modeling.

The decoder then converts those patch outputs into byte predictions. Since the decoder receives the output patch representations and the previously decoded bytes, it can generate byte-level outputs in sequence. In other words, BLT does not abandon byte-level autoregression; it reorganizes it. The global Transformer handles the main sequence modeling at patch granularity, and the decoder recovers the byte sequence one step at a time.

This design matters because it allows BLT to preserve the fine-grained byte-level objective while avoiding the cost of running the full global model on every byte. The patch representation acts as an intermediate latent form that supports efficient autoregressive modeling.

# Where Most Compute Is Spent in BLT

The paper is explicit that the global latent Transformer is the main consumer of FLOPs. The local encoder and decoder are lightweight by comparison, and the cross-attention blocks are designed to be efficient bridges rather than the primary source of computation. As a result, the number of patches, rather than the number of bytes, largely determines how much expensive global computation is performed.

This is why patch size is such an important quantity in BLT. If patches are large, then fewer global Transformer steps are needed, which reduces inference cost. If patches are small, then the global model is called more often, which increases cost but may improve modeling of more complex regions. BLT therefore exposes a trade-off between efficiency and expressiveness through the patching mechanism.

The paper’s scaling experiments are built around this compute structure. Because the global Transformer is run less often when patches are longer, the compute saved there can be used to grow the global model itself. This is a key reason the authors argue that patches scale better than tokens: patch-based models can increase both patch size and model size under a fixed inference budget, something fixed-vocabulary tokenization does not allow in the same way.

# How Dynamic Patching Changes the Frequency of Global Transformer Calls

Dynamic patching is the mechanism that lets BLT vary how often the global Transformer is invoked. Rather than splitting the byte stream into fixed-length chunks, BLT uses entropy-based patching to decide where patches should begin. The idea is that bytes with high next-byte entropy are harder to predict and should receive more modeling attention, while low-entropy regions can be grouped into longer patches and processed with fewer global steps.

This changes the frequency of global Transformer calls in a context-sensitive way. When the data is predictable, BLT forms larger patches, so the global model is called less frequently. When the data is complex, patches become smaller, which means the global Transformer is invoked more often and more compute is allocated to those difficult regions. The paper presents this as a form of dynamic compute allocation: the model spends more on what is hard and less on what is easy.

This design is central to the claim that BLT is more efficient than tokenization-based architectures. Tokenizers impose a fixed segmentation, so the compute per token is uniform even when the underlying byte sequence varies in complexity. BLT instead makes patch size a variable of the input itself. As a result, the number of expensive latent Transformer steps adapts to the structure of the data, which is the mechanism that underlies BLT’s efficiency gains.

6. The Global Latent Transformer

# Input and Output Patch Representations

At the center of Byte Latent Transformer, or \textsc{BLT}, is a large autoregressive Transformer that does not operate directly on bytes or on tokenizer-produced tokens. Instead, it works over \emph{patch representations}. These patch representations are latent summaries of groups of bytes, and they are the units that the global model consumes and produces.

This design is important because the patch representation is where \textsc{BLT} separates the costly part of language modeling from the raw byte stream. The local encoder first converts bytes into expressive patch representations, and the global latent Transformer then processes those representations as its input sequence. In the paper’s notation, the global model $\mathcal{G}$ maps a sequence of latent input patch representations $p_j$ to a sequence of output patch representations $o_j$. The subscript $j$ is used for patches, while $i$ is reserved for bytes. This distinction matters because the global model is explicitly meant to reason over a shorter, higher-level sequence than a byte-level model would.

The paper emphasizes that these patch representations are not fixed vocabulary symbols. Unlike tokenizer-based models, where each position corresponds to a token from a predetermined set, patches are dynamically formed groups of bytes. The local encoder produces these patches from the byte stream, and the global model consumes them as latent units. In that sense, the global Transformer is not learning over words or subwords, but over learned compressed summaries whose length can vary with the data.

# Autoregressive Modeling at the Patch Level

The latent global Transformer is autoregressive. That means it predicts the next patch representation based only on earlier patch representations, just as a standard language model predicts future symbols from past symbols. The difference is that the prediction happens at the patch level rather than at the token or byte level.

This patch-level autoregression is the mechanism that gives \textsc{BLT} its compute flexibility. Since the global model is expensive, \textsc{BLT} does not invoke it for every byte. Instead, it invokes it once per patch. If a stretch of bytes is predictable, the patch can be longer and the global model can be called less often. If a stretch of bytes is information-dense or difficult to predict, patches can be shorter and the model can spend more of its compute there. The global Transformer therefore acts as the main reasoning engine over a variable-rate representation of the sequence.

The paper describes this global model as consuming the bulk of the flops during pre-training and inference. That is not an incidental implementation detail; it is the key reason patching matters. Because the global model is the dominant cost, deciding how often it runs is equivalent to deciding where most of the compute budget goes. The patch count is therefore directly tied to the amount of computation spent on a sequence.

# Block-Causal Attention Masking

The latent global Transformer uses a block-causal attention mask. This means that, when processing a patch, the model may attend only to patches up to and including the current patch within the same document. The mask enforces the autoregressive structure at the patch level by preventing information from later patches from leaking into earlier predictions.

The intuition is the same as in ordinary causal language modeling, but applied to patch units instead of individual bytes or tokens. If the model is predicting a representation for patch $j$, it may use all previous patch representations, as well as the current patch context, but nothing from future patches. This ensures that the model remains a proper generative model over sequences while still benefiting from the compressed patch sequence.

The paper notes that this block-causal masking is used within the current document. That detail is important because document boundaries matter for preventing one example from contaminating another during training. The global model is not allowed to carry information across documents through its attention pattern; its attention is restricted to the patches belonging to the present document.

# Document Boundary Handling

Document boundaries play a special role in \textsc{BLT} because the patching process itself is incremental and context-sensitive, but the model must still respect the segmentation of the training data into documents. The paper states that the block-causal mask restricts attention to patches within the current document. This means that a patch in one document cannot attend to patches in a different document, even if those documents are adjacent in a batch.

This handling is part of the general design philosophy of \textsc{BLT}: make the patching dynamic, but keep the generation process properly causal and document-aware. The local encoder and decoder operate on byte sequences, but the global model’s reasoning is confined to the patch sequence inside each document. In practice, this makes the latent Transformer a document-local reasoning module over compressed units.

The document boundary restriction also fits the way the model is trained and evaluated. Since the paper carefully controls context length and batch composition, document boundaries are part of the mechanism that keeps the comparison with tokenizer-based models fair. The model gains efficiency from patching, not from illicitly using more context.

# Why the Latent Transformer Dominates Total Flops

A central claim in the paper is that, at scale, the Transformer’s feed-forward layers dominate computation, not the attention mechanism. This is one of the key reasons \textsc{BLT} focuses on reducing the number of times the global Transformer must be applied. For small models, attention efficiency improvements can matter a lot. But at large scale, the expensive part is the repeated application of the Transformer block itself, especially the feed-forward network, across many positions.

Because the global latent Transformer is large, it accounts for most of the cost during both training and inference. The local encoder and local decoder are intentionally lightweight, with far fewer layers than the global model. Their role is to bridge between bytes and patches, while the global Transformer does the heavy computation. This structure lets \textsc{BLT} spend most of its capacity where the paper argues it is most useful: on the latent patch sequence.

The paper’s flop accounting makes this division of labor explicit. In the formula for flops per byte, the global Transformer is one of the major terms, and its cost is normalized by the number of patches. This reflects the basic idea that the global model’s cost is incurred once per patch, not once per byte. Consequently, any increase in average patch size reduces how often the global model runs and lowers inference cost.

# How Patch Count Determines Global-Model Cost

In \textsc{BLT}, the number of patches is the main driver of global-model cost. If a sequence of bytes is segmented into fewer, longer patches, the global Transformer is invoked fewer times. If the sequence is broken into many short patches, the global Transformer runs more often. The paper treats the average patch size as the most important factor in determining the cost of processing data.

This relationship is simple but powerful. Suppose a sequence contains a fixed number of bytes. If those bytes are represented by many patches, then the global model must process a long patch sequence. If the same bytes are represented by fewer patches, the global sequence is shorter, so the model performs fewer expensive steps. That is why patching is not just a preprocessing trick; it is the mechanism by which compute is dynamically allocated.

The paper uses this property in both training and inference. By changing patch size, the authors can trade off between compute and performance. Larger patches generally save flops, and those savings can be reallocated to a larger global Transformer. This is one of the most important ideas in the paper: patch size is a scaling dimension. Unlike a fixed tokenizer vocabulary, patch size can be adjusted to control the compute budget without changing the basic structure of the model.

# Interpreting Latent Patches as Variable-Length Units of Reasoning and Compression

The latent patches in \textsc{BLT} can be understood as variable-length units that sit between raw bytes and full-sequence reasoning. They are not tokens in the usual tokenizer sense, because they do not come from a fixed vocabulary. Instead, they are learned groups of bytes whose lengths vary according to the information content of the data. This makes them both compressed and contextual.

The paper’s intuition is that not all parts of text require the same amount of computation. Predicting the ending of a common word is usually easier than predicting the first word of a new sentence, and some regions of text are much more information-dense than others. Patches allow \textsc{BLT} to reflect this difference in the representation itself. Easier regions can be grouped into longer patches, while harder regions can be split into shorter ones. The global Transformer then reasons over these variable-length chunks.

This is why the authors describe patches as a way of dynamically allocating compute where it is needed. A patch is not just a compressed byte group; it is a unit over which the model decides to spend a Transformer step. In that sense, patches function as variable-length units of reasoning. Longer patches mean that the model can compress predictable spans and move on quickly. Shorter patches mean that the model can slow down and dedicate more representational capacity to complex or uncertain parts of the sequence.

Seen this way, the latent Transformer is not simply a replacement for token-based language modeling. It is the component that makes the patch-based design meaningful. By operating over latent patch representations, it turns the patch sequence into the model’s main reasoning space. The result is a model that can adapt its computational effort to the structure of the input, rather than treating every byte or token as equally expensive.

7. The Local Encoder: Turning Bytes into Patch Representations

# Byte embedding lookup

In BLT, the local encoder begins with raw bytes, not tokens. Each byte is first mapped through a learned embedding lookup table of shape $\mathbb{R}^{256 \times h_{\mathcal{E}}}$. This table assigns a vector to each possible byte value, so the model can start from a numerical representation of the input before any deeper processing occurs. Because the input alphabet is fixed and small, this embedding step is treated as an efficient lookup rather than a dense matrix multiplication.

This initial byte embedding is the encoder’s first opportunity to turn discrete raw data into a form the transformer can process. At this stage, the model has not yet compressed the sequence into patches. Instead, it represents each byte individually, preserving the full byte-level detail that BLT is designed to exploit. The local encoder then enriches these byte embeddings with additional information before producing patch representations.

# The role of the local encoder in BLT

The local encoder, denoted by $\mathcal{E}$, is one of the three main components of BLT. Its job is to convert a sequence of bytes into a sequence of patch representations that can be consumed by the global latent transformer. In the paper’s terminology, the local encoder is lightweight relative to the global model: it has $l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers, where $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ is the number of layers in the global transformer.

This design reflects one of the central ideas behind BLT: not every part of the input deserves the same amount of compute. The local encoder performs the byte-level processing needed to prepare rich patch representations, but it does so in a constrained, efficient way. It works at byte resolution, so it can capture fine-grained details, but it only uses a small transformer stack and a local attention window. The heavy global reasoning is reserved for the latent transformer, which operates on the patches produced by the encoder.

# Local block-causal attention over bytes

Inside the encoder, the byte sequence is processed by transformer layers that use a local block-causal attention mask. This means that each byte can attend only to a fixed window of preceding bytes, rather than to the entire sequence. The paper denotes this window size by $w_{\mathcal{E}}$. The attention is causal, so the model never looks ahead, and it is local, so the receptive field remains bounded.

This local attention structure is important for two reasons. First, it keeps the encoder efficient, since attention cost does not grow with the full sequence length. Second, it focuses the encoder on modeling nearby byte patterns, which are exactly the kinds of patterns that are useful for building patch representations. The local model is allowed to cross dynamic patch boundaries when attending, but it cannot cross document boundaries. So it has some flexibility within a document while still respecting the sequence structure that BLT uses for training and inference.

The encoder alternates transformer layers with cross-attention blocks, so the local byte representations are gradually refined and then pooled into patch-level representations. In this sense, the encoder is not just a shallow byte transformer; it is a mechanism for building compressed latent units from byte sequences in a context-sensitive way.

# Windowed byte-level context in the encoder

The fixed byte window $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ determines how much local context the encoder can use around each byte. The paper emphasizes that the local encoder uses a fixed window of preceding bytes, which can span across dynamic patch boundaries but not across documents. This design allows the encoder to gather useful short-range context without paying the cost of full-sequence attention.

The intuition is that many local byte-level decisions depend on only a limited amount of prior context. For example, the bytes that form a word, a code fragment, or a local punctuation pattern often need only nearby information to be represented well. By restricting the encoder to a fixed local window, BLT uses the byte-level transformer where it is most useful, while still leaving the larger-scale modeling problem to the global latent transformer.

This local context also complements the patching scheme. Because patch boundaries are dynamic, the model should not assume that all meaningful structure aligns with a fixed segmentation. The local encoder can gather nearby bytes regardless of whether they happen to fall inside the same patch, which helps it form patch representations that better reflect the actual information density of the input.

# Encoder hash n-gram embeddings

A major addition to the local encoder is the use of hash $n$-gram embeddings. The paper introduces these embeddings to make the byte representations more expressive and more robust. The key idea is to represent each byte not only as an isolated symbol, but also as part of the short byte sequences that precede it.

For each position $i$, BLT constructs byte-grams
$$
g_{i,n}=\{b_{i-n+1},\ldots,b_i\}
$$
for $n$ ranging from $3$ to $8$, omitting these byte-grams when $i<n$. These n-grams capture local patterns of bytes in the recent context. The model then maps each n-gram through a hash function into a fixed embedding table. One table is used for each n-gram size, with $n \in \{3,4,5,6,7,8\}$.

The embedding for position $i$ is then formed by adding the byte embedding and all of the hash n-gram embeddings:
$$
e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3,\ldots,8} E_n^{\text{hash}}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))
$$
where
$$
\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \% |E_n^{\text{hash}}|.
$$
The paper states that this sum is normalized by the number of n-gram sizes plus one before being used as input to the local encoder.

These hashed n-gram features give the encoder a compact way to remember useful byte patterns without maintaining an explicit vocabulary of all possible substrings. Since the hash tables are fixed size, the method is computationally efficient even though it enriches the representation with many contextual patterns.

# Constructing byte n-grams from preceding context

The n-gram construction is strictly local and causal. For each byte position $i$, the model forms byte-grams from the bytes that immediately precede and include $b_i$. This means the representation at position $i$ depends on recent history, not on future bytes. The n-gram sizes range from 3 to 8, so the model is looking at short contiguous byte patterns that are large enough to capture useful structure but small enough to remain manageable.

This choice reflects the paper’s broader philosophy about byte modeling. Many important regularities in text are visible at the level of short character sequences. By encoding these local patterns directly, BLT gives the local encoder a richer basis for deciding how to pool bytes into patches. The n-grams are not meant to replace the byte embeddings; rather, they augment them with a compressed view of local history.

# Hashing n-grams into fixed-size embedding tables

Instead of assigning a separate learned vector to every possible n-gram, BLT hashes each n-gram into an index in a fixed-size embedding table. The paper uses a hash function based on RollPolyHash, then applies a modulo operation with the table size:
$$
\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \% |E_n^{\text{hash}}|.
$$
This means many distinct n-grams can share table entries, but the representation remains bounded in size and easy to train at scale.

This hashing strategy is important because the space of possible byte n-grams is enormous. A fixed table makes the method practical, while still allowing the model to exploit recurring local patterns. The paper notes that it also experimented with frequency-based n-gram embeddings, but the main text focuses on the hashed version. In the experiments, the hash embeddings are treated as a key component of the encoder’s expressivity.

# RollPolyHash and embedding aggregation

The paper specifies that the hash function used is RollPolyHash, with details given in the appendix. Conceptually, the role of RollPolyHash is to turn a short byte sequence into a stable index that can be used to retrieve an embedding. The resulting embeddings for all n-gram sizes are added to the byte embedding and then normalized.

Aggregation is simple but effective: the byte representation becomes the sum of its base embedding and several hashed contextual embeddings. This adds local structure without requiring a separate model component for every possible sequence. Because the aggregation is additive, the encoder can combine multiple signals at once: the identity of the current byte, the recent byte context, and the short n-gram patterns that have been observed.

The paper uses these embeddings to support downstream performance and scaling behavior. In ablations, hash n-gram embeddings improve performance across domains, and the gains are especially noticeable in certain settings such as Wikipedia and Github. The authors also find diminishing returns once the hash table becomes sufficiently large, indicating that the method helps most when it provides a meaningful amount of distinct local memory without becoming excessively large.

# Why hashed n-grams improve local expressivity and robustness

The paper’s motivation for hashed n-grams is that byte-level inputs contain structure that single-byte embeddings cannot capture on their own. A byte may be ambiguous in isolation, but the preceding n-gram can clarify its role. By adding short-context features, the encoder becomes more expressive in ways that matter for patch formation.

This added expressivity also supports robustness. Since BLT works directly with bytes, it is naturally exposed to spelling, punctuation, and character-level variation. The n-gram embeddings help the model retain sensitivity to local byte patterns even when the surface form changes. The paper links this byte-level awareness to stronger performance on character-level tasks, noise robustness, and long-tail generalization. In other words, the hashed n-grams help the local encoder see not just isolated symbols, but structured byte sequences that are relevant to language modeling and downstream tasks.

# Encoder cross-attention inspired by the Perceiver

After local transformer processing, BLT uses cross-attention to pool byte-level information into patch representations. The encoder’s cross-attention block is inspired by the input cross-attention module of the Perceiver architecture, but with an important difference: BLT’s latent representations are variable patch representations rather than a fixed set of latent slots.

The role of cross-attention here is to transform a variable number of byte states into a smaller number of patch-level vectors. This is the mechanism that lets BLT compress raw bytes into latent patches. Each patch representation is formed from the bytes that belong to that patch, so the pooling operation respects the dynamic segmentation chosen by the patching scheme.

The paper emphasizes that the encoder cross-attention only attends to the bytes that make up the corresponding patch. This keeps the operation patch-local and ensures that patch representations are derived from the correct byte group. In effect, cross-attention is the bridge between byte-level modeling and patch-level computation.

# Pooling byte states into patch queries

The encoder cross-attention begins by initializing each patch query from the byte representations associated with that patch. The paper states that the query vector for a patch $p_j$ is initialized by pooling the byte representations corresponding to that patch, and then projecting them through a learned matrix $\mathcal{E}_C \in \mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathcal{E}} \times (h_{\mathcal{E}} \times U_{\mathcal{E}})}$, where $U_{\mathcal{E}}$ is the number of encoder cross-attention heads.

The paper uses max-pooling to initialize the queries for the first encoder cross-attention layer in all BLT models. This is a concrete way of turning a collection of byte states into a single starting representation for the patch. Pooling provides a summary of the bytes in the patch, and the subsequent cross-attention operation refines that summary by letting the patch query interact with the individual byte states.

The intuition is that a patch should not be treated as an arbitrary chunk. It should be represented by a latent vector that is informed by the internal byte structure of that chunk. The pooled query serves as this first latent summary.

# Restricting each patch query to its own bytes

A key detail in the encoder cross-attention is that each query $Q_j$ only attends to the keys and values corresponding to the bytes in patch $j$. This restriction is enforced by a masking strategy specific to patching. So although the model uses attention, it does not mix information across different patches at this stage.

This design ensures that patch representations are local to their own byte groups. If the encoder were allowed to attend across all bytes, then the cross-attention could blur boundaries and weaken the meaning of a patch. By restricting each query to its own bytes, BLT preserves the segmentation structure induced by the patching function while still allowing the patch representation to be learned rather than hard-coded.

This is also why the paper distinguishes patching from tokenization. Tokens are drawn from a fixed vocabulary, while patches are dynamically grouped sequences whose representations are learned from the bytes they contain. The encoder cross-attention is the mechanism that makes this dynamic grouping computationally useful.

# Multi-head cross-attention details and dimensionality management

The paper describes the encoder cross-attention using the standard attention components:
$$
Q_j = W_q(P_{l-1,j}), \quad K_i = W_k(h_{l-1,i}), \quad V_i = W_v(h_{l-1,i}),
$$
with output
$$
P_l = P_{l-1} + W_o\left(\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right).
$$
Here the keys and values are projections of the byte representations from the previous layer, or of the initial byte embeddings in the first layer. The output projection $W_o$ maps the attended result back into the patch representation space.

Because the patch representations are typically higher-dimensional than the local encoder states, the paper notes that BLT keeps the cross-attention in multiple heads of dimension $h_{\mathcal{E}}$ during the attention computation, and then concatenates these heads into the global model dimension $h_{\mathcal{G}}$. This is a way of managing the mismatch between byte-level and patch-level representations without losing the benefits of multi-headed attention.

The main point is that cross-attention is not just a pooling trick; it is a learned mechanism for composing multiple byte states into a patch embedding that is rich enough for the global model. The multi-head structure allows the model to look at different aspects of the bytes in parallel before the results are combined.

# Residual connections, normalization, and omission of positional embeddings in cross-attention

The encoder cross-attention block uses a residual connection around the attention operation. The paper also states that pre-LayerNorm is applied to the queries, keys, and values. These choices match common transformer design principles and help stabilize training. The residual path lets the model preserve the previous patch representation while adding new information from attention, which makes the transformation less brittle.

Notably, the paper says that no positional embeddings are used in the encoder cross-attention module. This is a deliberate omission. Since the cross-attention is restricted to the bytes within a patch and the local encoder already processes byte order through its transformer layers, the cross-attention block does not need its own positional encoding. The ordering information has already been handled upstream, and the cross-attention is primarily responsible for selecting and pooling the byte information into a patch-level summary.

Taken together, these details make the local encoder a compact but expressive module. It starts from byte embeddings, enriches them with hashed n-gram context, processes them through local attention, and then uses patch-local cross-attention to produce the latent patch representations that BLT’s global transformer will use for higher-level computation.

8. The Local Decoder: Turning Patch Representations Back into Bytes

# Role of the local decoder in byte-level language modeling

In Byte Latent Transformer ({\textsc BLT}), the local decoder is the component that turns the model’s patch-level reasoning back into byte-level outputs. This is necessary because the architecture does not operate only on bytes or only on patches: it compresses sequences of bytes into patches, processes those patches with a larger global Transformer, and then must recover predictions for the original byte sequence. The local decoder is the part that performs this reconstruction step.

The paper describes the local decoder as a lightweight Transformer-based model, denoted by $\mathcal{D}$, with far fewer layers than the global latent Transformer, written as $l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$. Its job is to decode a sequence of global patch representations $o_j$ into raw bytes $y_i$. In other words, the global model works at the level where computation is concentrated into patches, while the local decoder restores byte-level detail so the system can make autoregressive predictions over raw bytes. This makes the decoder essential to the overall byte-level language modeling pipeline: without it, the architecture would stop at latent patch representations and never produce the actual byte sequence.

The decoder also reflects the broader design principle of {\textsc BLT}: spend expensive compute on the parts of the sequence where abstraction is useful, but keep the byte-level machinery as lightweight as possible. The local decoder therefore does not try to replace the global Transformer. Instead, it acts as a small but necessary bridge that translates latent patch information into concrete byte predictions.

# Using encoder byte states as decoder initialization

The local decoder does not begin from scratch. Instead, it is initialized using the hidden representations produced by the local encoder. The paper states that the decoder takes as input the hidden representations produced by the local encoder for the byte sequence, and that the initial byte representations for the decoder cross-attention are the byte embeddings from the last encoder layer, written as $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$.

This initialization matters because the encoder has already processed the byte stream locally before the global patch reasoning is applied. The encoder therefore provides a byte-wise representation that contains fine-grained information about the input sequence. By reusing these encoder states, the decoder starts from a representation that is already aligned with the byte sequence, rather than beginning with only patch-level information. That gives the decoder a strong byte-level foundation onto which patch-level information can later be injected.

In the paper’s notation, the decoder begins with
\[
D_0 = h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}.
\]
This makes the decoder structurally dependent on the encoder’s byte representations. The decoder is not independent machinery that invents a byte sequence from patch outputs alone; it is a continuation of the same byte-level representation pathway, now conditioned on the global patch outputs.

# Alternating decoder cross-attention and transformer layers

The decoder is built as a stack of alternating cross-attention and Transformer layers. The paper emphasizes that, in the decoder, the cross-attention layer is applied before the Transformer layer. This ordering is deliberate: the cross-attention step first injects global patch information into the byte stream, and then the decoder Transformer layer refines those byte representations.

If we let $D_{l-1}$ be the byte representations entering decoder layer $l$, then the decoder first computes a cross-attention output $B_l$, and then applies a decoder Transformer layer:
\[
D_l = \text{Decoder-Transformer-layer}_l(B_l).
\]
The cross-attention operation is therefore the mechanism that brings in the global patch-level context, while the following Transformer layer processes the resulting byte sequence in the local byte space.

This alternating structure mirrors the encoder in a broad sense, since both sides use a combination of Transformer layers and cross-attention. But the order is reversed in the decoder. In the encoder, byte representations are built up and then pooled into patch representations. In the decoder, patch information is projected back into byte representations before the byte-level Transformer processes them. The alternating structure makes the decoder a controlled, incremental refinement process rather than a single monolithic projection.

# Decoder cross-attention with bytes as queries and patches as keys/values

The decoder cross-attention is the key operation that moves information from patches back to bytes. In this module, the roles of queries and key/values are interchanged relative to the encoder. The byte representations serve as the queries, and the patch representations serve as the keys and values.

Formally, the decoder uses the byte representations from the previous decoder state as queries:
\[
Q_i = W_q(d_{l-1,i}),
\]
and it uses transformed patch representations as keys and values:
\[
K_i = W_k(\mathcal{D}_C(o_j)), \qquad V_i = W_v(\mathcal{D}_C(o_j)).
\]
The output of the cross-attention block is then added residually:
\[
B_l = D_{l-1} + W_o\left(\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right).
\]

The important conceptual point is that each byte position asks the question, “Which patch information is relevant to me?” The patch representations carry the higher-level context produced by the global Transformer, and the byte queries retrieve the part of that context needed at each byte position. In this way, the decoder does not simply copy a single patch vector into every byte. Instead, each byte can selectively draw from the patch information through attention.

The paper also notes that $\mathcal{D}_C$ is a linear transformation and split operation applied to the final patch representations $o_j$. This produces key and value inputs with the right shape for the decoder’s attention mechanism. As in the encoder cross-attention, the decoder uses multiple attention heads, pre-LayerNorm, no positional embeddings, and a residual connection around the cross-attention block.

# How global patch information is broadcast back to byte positions

The decoder’s attention mechanism is the way global patch information is broadcast back down to the byte level. The global Transformer has already processed patch representations in a context-aware way, so the decoder’s task is to distribute that latent information across the individual bytes that belong to the output sequence.

This broadcast is not a simple uniform copy. Because the decoder uses byte queries, different byte positions can receive different mixtures of patch information. That is important in a byte-level autoregressive model, where each byte prediction may depend on slightly different contextual details. The decoder therefore acts as a structured broadcast mechanism: patch-level summaries are made available to all relevant byte positions, but each position can attend differently depending on its local state.

The paper’s formulation makes clear that the cross-attention block is applied before the decoder Transformer layer precisely so that the byte-level sequence entering the decoder Transformer already contains the appropriate global information. The subsequent Transformer layer can then work locally on the byte sequence, now enriched by patch-level context. This gives the model a two-stage flow of information: patch representations are first made accessible to bytes through attention, and then bytes are refined through local sequence modeling.

# Producing final byte-level predictions over the 256-byte vocabulary

The local decoder ultimately exists to predict raw bytes. The paper specifies that the byte input embeddings are drawn from a $\mathbb{R}^{256 \times h_{\mathcal{E}}}$ matrix, reflecting the fact that the vocabulary at the byte level has 256 possible values. This means that the final generation problem is always a byte classification problem over the full byte vocabulary.

The decoder’s output is therefore not a token from a finite subword vocabulary, but a byte drawn from the 256-byte space. This is one of the defining properties of {\textsc BLT}: it reconstructs language generation from raw bytes rather than from tokenized units. The decoder supplies the bridge from latent patch reasoning back to concrete byte outputs, allowing the model to generate text one byte at a time.

The paper presents the decoder as part of a larger autoregressive process. The decoder predicts a sequence of raw bytes as a function of previously decoded bytes. So although the global model works on patches, the final model output remains byte-level and sequential. The decoder is therefore the mechanism that restores autoregressive byte generation from the patch-based latent space.

# Architectural symmetry and asymmetry between encoder and decoder

The encoder and decoder are structurally similar, but not identical. Both are lightweight relative to the global Transformer, both mix Transformer layers with cross-attention, and both rely on patch representations as the interface to the global model. This gives {\textsc BLT} a sense of symmetry: bytes are encoded into patches on one side, and patches are decoded back into bytes on the other.

However, the paper makes clear that there is also deliberate asymmetry. The encoder’s cross-attention pools byte representations into patch representations, while the decoder’s cross-attention does the reverse, distributing patch information back into byte states. The encoder is described as a local model whose main role is to efficiently map bytes into expressive patch representations. The decoder’s role is the mirror image: it maps patch outputs into byte-level predictions.

There is also asymmetry in how much importance the paper assigns to each side. The global Transformer is the main computational core, the encoder is a lightweight preparatory module, and the decoder is similarly lightweight but essential for output reconstruction. In the ablations, the paper finds that cross-attention in the decoder is especially effective, which suggests that the decoder’s job of converting patch-level information back into bytes is particularly important for performance.

# Why the decoder can remain lightweight

The decoder can remain lightweight because it does not have to solve the entire language modeling problem from scratch. The global Transformer has already done the heavy reasoning over patches, and the encoder has already produced byte-level representations. The decoder’s task is narrower: it only needs to combine those two sources of information and produce the final byte predictions.

This division of labor is what makes {\textsc BLT} efficient. The paper repeatedly emphasizes that the global model consumes the bulk of the flops, so controlling when it is invoked is what determines efficiency. Because the decoder is local and lightweight, it can refine byte outputs without greatly increasing the overall computational burden. The architecture thus assigns expensive computation to the patch level, where context and uncertainty justify it, while keeping byte-level generation relatively cheap.

The ablations reinforce this design. The paper reports that {\textsc BLT} can work well with an encoder that is extremely lightweight, even just one layer, when paired with hash $n$-gram embeddings, and that a heavier decoder can be useful. This supports the view that the decoder does not need to be large to be effective; it just needs to be strong enough to convert latent patch context into accurate byte-level outputs. In that sense, the decoder is not a second global model. It is the compact final stage of a hierarchical system whose main computation already happened at the patch level.

9. Training BLT: Data, Objectives, and Experimental Controls

# Pretraining datasets: Llama 2 data and BLT-1T

The paper evaluates Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, on two pretraining datasets. The first is the Llama 2 dataset, which contains $2$ trillion tokens collected from a variety of publicly available sources and then cleaned and filtered to improve quality. The second is a new dataset called BLT-1T, which contains $1$ trillion tokens gathered from various public sources and includes a subset of the pretraining data released by Datacomp-LM. Both datasets exclude data gathered from Meta products or services.

These two datasets serve different roles in the study. The Llama 2 dataset is used for scaling-law experiments, where the goal is to determine good architectural choices for BLT and to compare byte-level and token-based models under matched compute conditions. BLT-1T is used for a full pretraining run intended to compare against Llama 3 on downstream tasks. In other words, the paper uses the Llama 2 data to understand scaling behavior and the BLT-1T data to test end-task performance at large scale.

A useful point for readers is that the paper is careful to separate these evaluation settings. The authors do not use a single dataset for everything, because the question they are asking changes depending on the experiment. When the goal is architectural comparison, the dataset is chosen to support controlled scaling studies. When the goal is downstream task evaluation, the dataset is chosen to support a complete large-scale training run.

# Why fair comparison requires controlling for context and compute

A central theme of the paper is that BLT should be compared fairly to tokenization-based models. This matters because byte-level models naturally operate over longer sequences than token-based models, and longer sequences can make a model appear stronger or weaker for reasons that are not really about the architecture itself. The paper therefore takes care to avoid giving BLT an advantage from simply seeing more bytes of context.

The main control variable is compute. The paper emphasizes that the meaningful comparison is not just whether one model uses bytes and another uses tokens, but whether both are trained and evaluated under similar flop budgets. Flops are used as the measure of computational cost. This is important because the main transformer in BLT is invoked once per patch, so patch size directly affects how often expensive computation happens. If patch sizes differ, the amount of computation per byte changes too.

The paper also controls for average context length. Since larger patch sizes reduce the number of patches in a sequence, they can effectively change how much context is processed by the expensive latent transformer. To avoid this confound, the authors keep the expected number of bytes in each batch constant when comparing models. This means that models with larger patch sizes are given shorter sequences, so that their average byte-level context remains comparable to that of token-based baselines. The goal is to ensure that any performance differences reflect the architecture and patching strategy, not an unfair increase in available context.

# The entropy model used for boundary detection

BLT uses entropy-based patching, which depends on a small byte-level language model that estimates the uncertainty of the next byte. This smaller model is trained on the same training distribution as the full BLT model. Its role is not to generate final outputs, but to identify where patch boundaries should fall by measuring how hard each next-byte prediction is.

In the paper’s default setup, the entropy model is a transformer with $100$ million parameters, $14$ layers, and hidden dimensionality $512$. It uses sliding-window attention over $512$ bytes. The authors note that the remaining hyperparameters are the same as in the local and global transformers. They also report that when the receptive field is small enough, the trained entropy model can be encoded in an efficient lookup table. This suggests that the boundary-detection mechanism itself can be lightweight enough not to dominate training cost.

The purpose of this entropy model is to provide a data-driven alternative to rule-based patching. Rather than assuming that whitespace or fixed byte counts are the right places to split text, BLT estimates where the next-byte prediction becomes more uncertain. That uncertainty is then used to decide where new patches begin.

# Entropy model size, architecture, and receptive field

The paper studies the entropy model as a design choice in its own right. In ablation experiments, the authors vary the entropy model size from $1$ million to $100$ million parameters and the context window length from $64$ to $512$ bytes. They then observe how these choices affect bits-per-byte scaling curves.

The results show that performance improves as either the model size or the receptive field increases, though there are diminishing returns beyond about $50$ million parameters. This is an important empirical result because it shows that the entropy model is not just a technical detail. If the boundary detector is too weak, it may not identify patch boundaries well enough to support strong scaling behavior. If it is sufficiently capable, however, it can provide the patching decisions needed for BLT’s compute allocation strategy.

The receptive field matters because entropy estimates depend on context. A larger window lets the entropy model see more preceding bytes, which can improve its ability to identify structured regions of text. But larger windows also increase cost. The paper’s ablations therefore explore a practical balance between accuracy and efficiency.

# Estimating thresholds to achieve target average patch sizes

Entropy patching needs a rule for turning entropy estimates into actual patch boundaries. The paper uses thresholding to achieve a desired average patch size on the pretraining data mix. In this setup, one first computes next-byte entropies under the small byte language model, and then chooses a threshold that yields the average patch size one wants.

The paper considers two thresholding strategies. The first is a global entropy threshold: a new patch begins when the entropy at position $t$ exceeds a threshold $\theta_g$. Formally,
$$H(x_t) > \theta_g.$$
The second is an approximate monotonicity rule that marks a boundary when entropy rises relative to the previous byte:
$$H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r.$$
This second rule can be interpreted as identifying points where the approximate downward trend in entropy inside a patch is broken.

The threshold is not chosen arbitrarily. It is estimated so that the patching procedure achieves the desired average patch size on the data mixture used for training. This is what lets the authors compare different patch sizes in a controlled way. Rather than changing patching behavior unpredictably, they adjust thresholds until the resulting average patch length matches the target.

# Equalizing average byte context across models

A major fairness concern in the experiments is that different patching strategies can produce different average context lengths for the global transformer. If one model effectively sees more context, it may benefit from that extra information even if the architecture itself is not superior. The paper explicitly tries to prevent this from confounding the comparison.

To do so, the authors ensure that the number of bytes per batch remains constant in expectation. For models with larger patch sizes, the sequence length is reduced so that the total number of bytes processed is comparable across models. This means that a model is not favored simply because it can inspect more bytes. Instead, larger patch sizes save compute by reducing the number of patches, and that saved compute can be redirected elsewhere in the architecture.

The paper also distinguishes between bytes and patches carefully. Even when the number of bytes remains constant, the number of patches can vary substantially depending on the patching scheme. Since the latent transformer operates on patches, the number of patches determines how many expensive transformer steps are executed. Equalizing byte count while allowing patch count to vary is therefore the right way to control for fairness while still testing the architectural idea.

# Patch packing and batching without wasting latent steps

Because the latent transformer is the expensive part of BLT, the implementation is designed to avoid wasting computation on padding. Dynamic patching creates batches in which the ratio of bytes to patches can vary from one example to another. That means a naïve batching strategy would waste compute on padded latent positions.

To avoid this, the paper packs batches of patches so that every batch has the same number of patches. This is an important engineering detail because the latent transformer is run less often than the byte-level modules, and the authors want every latent step to correspond to real data rather than padding. The batching strategy therefore reflects the architecture’s central idea: compute should be spent where there is actual information.

At the same time, the authors note that sequences are padded and possibly truncated to fixed byte lengths during training, specifically $12$k bytes for the Llama 2 data and $24$k bytes for BLT-1T. This is done to avoid memory spikes from examples containing unusually large patches. So although patch packing reduces waste, the implementation still needs byte-level limits for stability.

# Handling variable patch-to-byte ratios during training

One of the practical challenges in BLT is that dynamic patching creates variable patch-to-byte ratios. Some examples produce many small patches, while others produce fewer large patches. This variability is part of the method’s flexibility, but it also complicates training because the latent transformer’s workload changes from batch to batch.

The paper handles this by maintaining constant expected byte count while allowing patch count to vary. Since patch count determines how many expensive latent steps are required, the model’s compute budget remains controlled even as patch boundaries adapt to the data. The implementation also packs patches in batches, as noted above, to ensure that each batch has a consistent number of patches for efficiency.

This design reflects a key principle of BLT: patching is not just compression. It is a way to redistribute computation based on local information density. Variability in the patch-to-byte ratio is therefore a feature, not a bug, as long as it is managed carefully in the training pipeline.

# Resetting entropy context to mitigate entropy drift

The paper reports an additional issue with entropy-based patching: the entropy model’s context can drift, especially in structured or repetitive content. In such cases, the entropy estimates may progressively change as context length changes, which can lead to overly large patches in repetitive regions. The authors call this effect entropy drift.

To address this, the large-scale BLT-Entropy run with patch size $4.5$ resets the entropy context with new lines. It also uses the approximate monotonicity constraint, which the authors say suffers less from entropy drift caused by changes in context length. This adjustment affects only how entropies are computed, not the general procedure for setting the entropy threshold.

The practical lesson is that entropy patching depends not only on the formula for entropy, but also on how the entropy model is conditioned. Resetting context at new lines makes the boundary-detection process more stable on structured inputs, such as multiple-choice tasks, where repeated formatting can otherwise distort patch sizes.

# Transformer hyperparameters inherited from Llama 3

For the transformer blocks in BLT, the paper largely follows the architecture of Llama 3. This means that the local and global transformer components inherit a familiar modern configuration rather than introducing entirely new transformer internals. The model uses SwiGLU activation functions in the feed-forward layers, rotary positional embeddings with $\theta = 500000$ in the self-attention layers, and RMSNorm for normalization.

This choice is important because it isolates the paper’s contribution. The point is not to reinvent every transformer component, but to study how byte-level patching and latent computation can be integrated into a strong transformer backbone. By keeping the core transformer design close to a known strong baseline, the paper makes it easier to attribute performance differences to the BLT-specific ideas.

The paper also uses FlashAttention for self-attention layers with standard fixed masks, such as block causal or fixed-window block causal masks. For cross-attention layers, which require dynamic patch-dependent masks, the implementation uses FlexAttention. This makes it possible to efficiently support the dynamic structure that BLT requires.

# Use of SwiGLU, RoPE, RMSNorm, FlashAttention, and FlexAttention

Each of the inherited components serves a specific role. SwiGLU is used in the feed-forward network, following the Llama 3-style transformer design. Rotary positional embeddings, or RoPE, are used in self-attention with $\theta = 500000$, giving the model positional information in a standard way. RMSNorm is used for layer normalization.

For efficient attention, the paper distinguishes between ordinary self-attention and the dynamic cross-attention layers. FlashAttention is used when the attention mask has a standard fixed structure, because such cases can be fused efficiently. The cross-attention layers are different because their masks depend on the patching structure, so the paper uses FlexAttention to create fused implementations that can handle the dynamic masks. This is a practical necessity, since BLT’s architecture would otherwise be too slow to train efficiently.

The paper’s use of these components shows a recurring pattern: use established transformer machinery whenever possible, and introduce new mechanisms only where BLT needs them for patch-based computation.

# BLT-specific hyperparameters across scales

The paper studies BLT at several model scales: $400$M, $1$B, $2$B, $4$B, and $8$B parameters. These scales are used both for scaling-trend experiments and for downstream evaluation. The architecture hyperparameters for these models are provided in the appendix, but the main text gives several important settings.

The authors use max pooling to initialize the queries for the first cross-attention layer in the local encoder. They use $500{,}000$ hashes with a single hash function for the $n$-gram hash embeddings, and the $n$-gram sizes range from $3$ to $8$. The learning rate is $4 \times 10^{-4}$ for all models. The paper notes that this matches the token-based and BLT models after a hyperparameter search between $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$ at the $400$M and $1$B scales.

For the Llama 2 scaling experiments, batch sizes follow the recommendations of Llama 3 or the equivalent in bytes. These choices are meant to keep the comparison consistent between token-based and byte-based models, so that one is not helped by a different optimization regime.

# Optimization details: AdamW, learning rate, warmup, cosine decay, weight decay, gradient clipping

The optimization setup uses AdamW with $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.95$, and $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$. The learning rate schedule begins with a linear warm-up for $2000$ steps, followed by cosine decay down to $0$. The weight decay is set to $0.1$, and gradients are globally clipped at a threshold of $1.0$.

These choices are standard enough to make the experiments credible, but they are also part of the paper’s experimental control strategy. By using a stable optimizer and a fixed schedule across scales, the authors reduce the chance that performance differences come from optimization quirks rather than from BLT’s architecture. The paper’s broader claim is about scaling and efficiency, so optimization must be consistent and conservative enough to support that claim.

Overall, the training setup is designed to test BLT on equal footing with token-based baselines. The datasets, entropy model, patching thresholds, context equalization, batching strategy, transformer backbone, and optimization settings all work together to make the comparison as controlled as possible.

10. Measuring Efficiency and Quality: FLOPs and Bits-Per-Byte

# Why FLOPs Are the Central Cost Metric in the Paper

A major theme of the paper is that comparing byte-level and token-level language models requires a careful notion of cost. The authors do not treat model quality alone as the full story, because two models can achieve similar performance while using very different amounts of computation. Instead, they use Floating Point Operations, or FLOPs, as the central measure of computational cost. This choice is especially important for {\textsc BLT}, because the architecture is designed to change how compute is distributed across the input sequence: some parts of the input should receive more processing, while other parts should receive less.

The paper emphasizes that standard tokenization-based language models allocate roughly the same amount of compute to each token. That makes comparison across architectures difficult unless the compute budget is made explicit. FLOPs provide a way to do that. By reporting performance at matched FLOP budgets, the authors can ask whether {\textsc BLT} is actually a better use of compute than a token-based baseline, rather than simply using more computation in disguise.

This is also why the paper repeatedly refers to “compute-controlled” or “FLOP-controlled” experiments. The basic idea is to compare models at the same training cost, or at the same inference cost, so that any difference in performance can be interpreted as an architectural difference rather than a difference in budget. In that setting, FLOPs become the key currency for both training and inference.

# Review of Transformer FLOPs Accounting

To measure cost, the paper largely follows the FLOPs accounting used in Chinchilla. In that framework, the computation of a Transformer is broken into the operations inside the feed-forward layers, the $qkvo$ projections in self-attention, and the attention and output projection computation itself. These parts together determine the total amount of work the model performs.

The paper’s accounting assumes that the input embedding layer is not a dense matrix multiplication. Instead, it is treated as an efficient lookup, which makes it a $0$-FLOP operation. This is a notable modeling choice because it simplifies the cost accounting and reflects the way embeddings are used in practice in these experiments. The paper also follows the standard convention that the backward pass costs twice as many FLOPs as the forward pass. This is the assumption used throughout their training-cost estimates.

The important point is that FLOPs are not just an abstract technical detail. They are the basis for the paper’s scaling studies. When the paper says that {\textsc BLT} matches or improves on token-based models “at the same cost,” that claim depends on this accounting. Without such a cost model, it would be impossible to tell whether a model is genuinely more efficient or simply more expensive.

# Forward and Backward Pass Cost Assumptions

The paper makes a clear distinction between training and inference cost, and it relies on a conventional assumption about the relative cost of the forward and backward passes. Specifically, the backward pass is estimated to require twice the FLOPs of the forward pass. This means that when the paper reports training FLOPs, it is accounting for both the forward computation needed to produce predictions and the backward computation needed to update parameters.

This assumption matters because training is far more expensive than inference in total FLOP terms, especially for large models trained on large datasets. When the paper compares models under a fixed training budget, it is implicitly comparing how much progress each architecture makes per unit of optimization work. That is a different question from inference efficiency, where the main concern is how expensive it is to run the model after training.

The paper uses both perspectives. In some experiments, it measures how well models scale during training at matched compute. In other experiments, it holds inference cost fixed and asks whether one can trade patch size for model size. These two views are related but not identical, and the FLOP assumptions make it possible to analyze both in a consistent way.

# BLT FLOPs Decomposition: Encoder, Latent Transformer, Decoder, and Cross-Attention

The FLOPs accounting for {\textsc BLT} is more complex than for a standard token-based Transformer because {\textsc BLT} is built from several interacting components. The paper decomposes the cost into the local encoder, the global latent Transformer, the local decoder, and the cross-attention blocks that connect bytes and patches.

The global latent Transformer is the main consumer of compute. It operates on patch representations rather than on every byte, and it is the component whose invocation is controlled by the patching scheme. The local encoder maps bytes into patch representations, while the local decoder maps patch outputs back into bytes. Both local models are much smaller than the latent Transformer, but they still contribute to the total cost. In addition, the encoder and decoder each include cross-attention blocks, and those blocks have their own FLOP costs.

The paper summarizes this with an expression for the FLOPs per byte:
\[
\text{FL}_{\text{{\textsc BLT}}} = \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m=n_{ctx}/n_p, V=0) / n_p
\]
\[
+ \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0)
\]
\[
+ \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256)
\]
\[
+ \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=n_p, r=n_p/k) \times k/n_p
\]
\[
+ \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=k, r=k/n_p)
\]
This formula captures the idea that the global model’s cost depends strongly on how many patches are produced, while the local models and cross-attention blocks account for the overhead needed to move between bytes and patches.

The variables in this expression are important for interpreting the model. The parameter $n_{ctx}$ is the byte sequence length, $n_p$ is the patch size, $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$ are the local attention window sizes, and $V$ is the vocabulary size used in the local decoder output projection. The quantity $k$ is the ratio of patch dimension to byte dimension. In the paper’s cross-attention setup, $k=2$.

# How Patch Size Enters the FLOPs Equations

Patch size is the main mechanism by which {\textsc BLT} changes compute. Because the latent Transformer operates on patches, larger patches mean fewer latent steps. That reduces the amount of compute spent in the most expensive part of the architecture. This is why the paper says that average patch size is the main factor determining cost during training and inference.

The FLOP formula makes this dependence explicit. The latent Transformer term is divided by $n_p$, so if patches get larger, the number of global Transformer invocations per byte goes down. This is the core efficiency advantage of patching. When data is predictable, larger patches can represent it with fewer global steps, which means fewer FLOPs.

At the same time, patch size does not affect only one part of the model. It also changes the balance between the expensive latent Transformer and the smaller byte-level modules. As patch size grows, the latent Transformer is run less often, so its share of the overall budget decreases. That creates room to increase model size while holding the total inference budget constant. The paper’s fixed-inference scaling experiments are built around exactly this idea.

# Interpreting Training FLOPs vs Inference FLOPs

The paper treats training FLOPs and inference FLOPs as related but distinct measures. Training FLOPs capture how expensive it is to learn a model, including the backward pass. Inference FLOPs capture how expensive it is to use the model after training, when only the forward computation matters.

This distinction matters because a model can be attractive under one budget and not under the other. For example, a model may be more expensive to train but cheaper to run at inference time. Or it may achieve better training efficiency but not improve latency. The paper studies both because {\textsc BLT} is designed to improve not only training scaling but also deployment efficiency.

The inference side is particularly important for patch-based modeling. Because patches can be made larger on average, the model can reduce the number of latent Transformer steps per byte. That means the same inference FLOP budget can support either a larger model or longer patches, or some combination of both. The paper argues that this creates a new scaling dimension unavailable to fixed-vocabulary tokenization.

# Why Perplexity Is Not Suitable Across Different Tokenizations

To compare language modeling quality, one might normally use perplexity. The paper explains why this is not appropriate when the units of modeling differ. Perplexity is defined with respect to a tokenizer, so it measures uncertainty per token. If two models use different tokenization schemes, then their tokens are not directly comparable, and neither are their perplexities.

This is especially problematic for the paper’s setting because {\textsc BLT} operates on raw bytes and dynamically formed patches, while baseline models operate on fixed tokens from a tokenizer. Since the token boundaries and token counts differ, perplexity would confound modeling quality with the choice of input representation. A model could appear better or worse simply because its tokens are larger or smaller, not because it is genuinely modeling the data better.

For that reason, the paper uses Bits-Per-Byte instead. This metric makes sense across byte-level and token-level systems because the byte is a shared underlying unit.

# Definition of Bits-Per-Byte

The paper defines Bits-Per-Byte, or BPB, as:
\[
\text{BPB}(x) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}
\]
Here, $\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})$ is the cross-entropy loss over the sequence $\mathbf{x}$, and $n_{\text{bytes}}$ is the total number of bytes in the input. The division by $\ln(2)$ converts the quantity into bits.

The intuition is straightforward: BPB measures how much uncertainty the model assigns per byte of data. Because bytes are the unit used by {\textsc BLT} and are also the common underlying representation for tokenized text, BPB gives a tokenizer-independent measure of language modeling quality. In effect, it answers the question: how many bits does the model need, on average, to encode each byte?

This makes BPB especially useful for the paper’s comparisons. It allows the authors to compare byte-level models, patch-based models, and token-based baselines on a shared scale. When the paper plots BPB against training FLOPs, it is asking which architecture uses compute more effectively to reduce byte-level uncertainty.

# BPB as a Tokenizer-Independent Language Modeling Metric

The value of BPB in the paper is not just that it is mathematically well defined, but that it aligns with the architecture’s goal. {\textsc BLT} is built to process raw bytes directly, so a metric expressed per byte is naturally suited to its output. At the same time, because token-based models also produce probabilities over byte sequences through their tokenization, BPB lets the paper compare them fairly.

This matters for several of the paper’s conclusions. For example, in the compute-optimal scaling experiments, the paper compares {\textsc BLT} and BPE-based models using BPB as the training loss metric. That allows the authors to show whether {\textsc BLT} matches or outperforms tokenization-based models at the same compute budget. It also underlies the ablations on patching schemes, where the paper compares whether dynamic patching or static patching gives lower BPB for the same amount of training compute.

In this sense, BPB is the bridge between raw-byte modeling and standard language-model evaluation. It preserves comparability without requiring a fixed vocabulary.

# How Compute-Controlled Experiments Support Fair Architectural Comparisons

The paper’s methodology depends on the idea that a fair comparison requires matched compute. This is true both in training and in inference. If one model is allowed to use more FLOPs, it may score better simply because it has been given more resources. To avoid that confound, the authors explicitly control for compute in their experiments.

In training, they compare models under matched FLOP budgets and often at compute-optimal ratios. In inference, they also compare architectures under fixed inference FLOP budgets, which reveals whether patch-based scaling can outperform token-based scaling when the runtime cost is held constant. This is one of the paper’s most important claims: {\textsc BLT} does not only work at the same cost; it can sometimes do better by reallocating compute through patching.

The use of compute-controlled experiments gives the paper its interpretive strength. Because the budgets are held fixed, differences in BPB or downstream task accuracy can be attributed to architectural choices such as dynamic patching, cross-attention, and the division between local and global computation. That is why FLOPs and BPB are central together: FLOPs define the budget, and BPB measures how well that budget is spent on modeling the data.

11. Scaling Laws I: Matching Token-Based Models at Compute-Optimal Training

# Compute-optimal training as a baseline methodology

A central question in the paper is not simply whether a byte-level model can work, but whether it can scale in a way that is competitive with the best token-based language models. To answer that question fairly, the authors use compute-optimal training as the main baseline methodology. The idea is to compare models that are trained with the amount of data and number of parameters that previous scaling studies suggest is best for a given training budget. In other words, the goal is not to give one model more help than another, but to compare each model at the point where it is expected to perform best under a fixed compute budget.

This matters because scaling-law comparisons can easily be misleading if one model is trained with too much data for its size or too little data for its size. The paper follows the compute-optimal training ratios established for Llama 3 and uses those ratios as the reference point for both token-based baselines and {\textsc BLT}. That makes the comparison especially strong: if {\textsc BLT} can match the behavior of token-based models under this setting, then it is not merely a promising byte-level model in isolation. It is a byte-level architecture that can hold up under the same scaling discipline that has made token-based LLMs successful.

The authors apply this methodology first on the Llama 2 dataset for scaling-law experiments. They train several sizes of both BPE-based models and {\textsc BLT} models under matched compute conditions, then examine how training flops relate to language modeling performance on a representative subset of the training mixture. This establishes the core empirical question of the chapter: does a byte-level model, if designed carefully, follow the same favorable scaling trend as modern token-based Transformers?

# Parameter-matched comparisons between BLT and BPE models

To isolate the effect of the architecture itself, the paper uses parameter-matched comparisons. For each BPE model, the authors train a corresponding {\textsc BLT} model with the same data and a latent Transformer that matches the size and architecture of the BPE Transformer. This makes the comparison more controlled than simply comparing two unrelated systems: it asks whether the byte-level design can reproduce token-level scaling behavior when the two models have similar capacity.

The results are presented as training flops versus language modeling performance. In these comparisons, {\textsc BLT} models either match or outperform their BPE counterparts across the sizes studied. This is the paper’s main scaling claim: at compute-optimal training settings, a byte-level model can achieve performance on par with tokenized models rather than suffering the large gap that earlier byte-level approaches often faced.

The authors emphasize that this result is not due to a single lucky design choice. Both the architectural changes and the dynamic patching strategy matter. When they approximate SpaceByte using {\textsc BLT} with space patching but without n-gram embeddings and cross-attention, the model improves over MegaByte, yet it still remains far from Llama 3. By contrast, the full {\textsc BLT} system closes much of that gap. This suggests that competitive scaling comes from the interaction of multiple pieces: better patching, better local representations, and a global latent Transformer that can spend compute where it is most useful.

# Model scales studied from 1B to 8B parameters

The scaling study is substantial enough to matter at realistic model sizes. The paper trains models from 1B to 8B parameters, and the large-scale runs are not trivial toy experiments: they are trained on up to $1T$ tokens or $4T$ bytes depending on the dataset and setting. This is important because earlier byte-level work often demonstrated encouraging behavior only at much smaller scales, where the computational limitations of byte sequences are less severe.

By studying models up to $8B$ parameters, the paper shows that byte-level modeling is not confined to a small-scale niche. The authors explicitly frame this as the first flop-controlled scaling study of byte-level models at this scale. That claim is important because it means the paper is not just showing a new architecture works; it is showing that byte-level training can be organized into the same type of scaling regime that has governed modern large language models.

The paper also uses these size scales to observe a broader pattern: as the model gets larger, larger patch sizes become more plausible and increasingly competitive. That trend becomes part of the next stage of the argument, because it suggests that patching is not only a way to reduce computation, but also a way to reshape the scaling behavior of the model itself.

# Why matching Llama-style scaling is significant for byte-level modeling

The significance of matching Llama-style scaling is that it addresses the main historical obstacle for byte-level Transformers. Earlier byte-level models often suffered because raw bytes create much longer sequences than subword tokens, and longer sequences usually mean much higher cost. For a long time, tokenization seemed like a necessary compromise: if you want to train and scale an LLM efficiently, you need a fixed vocabulary that compresses raw text into larger units.

{\textsc BLT} challenges that assumption. If a byte-level model can match the scaling trend of Llama-style tokenized models, then tokenization is no longer a required ingredient for large-scale language modeling. That is the deeper significance of the result. It means the byte-level route is not merely a fallback for robustness; it can be a serious alternative scaling strategy.

This matters not only for performance, but also for what the model can represent. The paper argues that tokenization introduces biases into how strings are compressed, while byte-level modeling preserves access to the underlying character information. Therefore, matching token-based scaling does more than show parity in a benchmark sense. It shows that the model can retain direct access to raw text while still benefiting from the efficiencies that made token-based LLMs dominant.

# Results showing BLT parity or superiority to BPE baselines

The compute-optimal experiments show that {\textsc BLT} can either match or outperform BPE baselines at each of the model sizes studied. The authors present this as evidence that the architecture is not just competitive in the limit of very large data or very large models, but already follows favorable scaling trends in the compute regime where practical training decisions are made.

The paper also notes that the tokenizer choice itself matters for token-based models. In their experiments, models trained with the Llama 3 tokenizer outperform those trained with the Llama 2 tokenizer on the same data. This is useful context because it shows that even within the token-based family, compression quality affects performance. {\textsc BLT} is compared against these stronger baselines, which strengthens the claim that its gains are meaningful.

Another point emphasized by the authors is that {\textsc BLT} can match these scaling trends even when using average patch sizes that are larger than the average token sizes of Llama 2 or Llama 3. The Llama 2 and Llama 3 tokenizers average about $3.7$ and $4.4$ bytes per token, respectively. In contrast, {\textsc BLT} can achieve similar trends with average patch sizes of $6$ and even $8$ bytes. That is a striking result because it means the byte-level model is not merely copying the tokenization regime; it is operating with even larger units while remaining competitive.

# Comparison against Llama 2 and Llama 3 tokenizers

The paper repeatedly compares {\textsc BLT} to models using both the Llama 2 and Llama 3 tokenizers. This comparison matters because the tokenizer is not a neutral preprocessing step; it affects average token size, vocabulary size, and the amount of compute needed per sequence. In the paper’s experiments, the Llama 3 tokenizer is a stronger baseline than the Llama 2 tokenizer, and the authors use it for many of their main comparisons.

This choice is important because it makes the benchmark more demanding. If {\textsc BLT} can match or exceed a model built with the stronger Llama 3 tokenizer, then the result cannot be dismissed as a consequence of using an outdated or weak tokenization scheme. The paper also observes that Llama 3’s tokenizer increases average token size from $3.7$ to $4.4$ bytes relative to Llama 2, but doing so requires a much larger embedding table. In other words, tokenizers face their own scaling tradeoffs.

{\textsc BLT} changes that tradeoff. Instead of choosing a fixed vocabulary in advance, it dynamically groups bytes into patches and lets the model allocate compute according to the complexity of the data. The comparisons against Llama 2 and Llama 3 therefore show more than raw performance differences; they show that patching gives a different and, in this paper, more favorable way to balance compression and computation.

# Why architecture and dynamic patching are both necessary

A major lesson of the scaling study is that no single component is enough on its own. The paper shows that both architectural changes and dynamic patching are necessary for {\textsc BLT} to close the gap with token-based models. Static patching alone is not enough, and a patching mechanism without the right model structure does not fully solve the problem either.

This is clearest in the comparison with SpaceByte-style patching. When the authors use space patching without the full {\textsc BLT} architectural additions, performance improves relative to older byte-level baselines such as MegaByte, but it remains far from Llama 3. That tells us that simply choosing more linguistically sensible boundaries is helpful, but insufficient. The model still needs a better way to represent patches, propagate information across them, and spend compute selectively.

The full architecture addresses this by combining local byte-level models with a global latent Transformer. The local components handle byte-level detail efficiently, while the global model performs the more expensive reasoning over patches. Dynamic patching then decides when that global model should be invoked. This combination is what makes the scaling results possible: the model is not forced to spend the same amount of compute on every byte, and it is not forced to treat all regions of the sequence as equally difficult.

# Performance of static patching and space patching relative to BLT

The paper evaluates several patching schemes to show why dynamic patching matters. Static patching, where the model groups bytes into fixed-size chunks, is simple and easy to control, but it cannot adapt to the actual complexity of the text. The paper notes that this can waste compute on easy regions such as whitespace or predictable text, while also under-allocating compute in dense or difficult regions such as mathematics. It can also split similar byte sequences inconsistently.

Space patching is a meaningful improvement because it uses natural word boundaries, allocating more compute around word-level units and often around more difficult predictions that follow spaces. But even this approach has limitations. It cannot vary patch size flexibly, and it does not work equally well across all languages and domains.

The full {\textsc BLT} approach, especially entropy-based patching, outperforms these simpler methods in the scaling experiments. In the paper’s compute-optimal comparisons, the dynamic patching models are the ones that match or exceed token-based baselines. The simpler patching methods may be easier to implement, but they do not provide the same adaptation to information density, which is the key idea behind the model.

# Evidence that larger patch sizes become more competitive at larger scales

One of the most interesting scaling observations in the paper is that larger patch sizes become more competitive as model scale increases. This is not obvious a priori. A larger patch means the global Transformer runs less often, which saves compute, but it also means more bytes are compressed into each patch representation. At small scales, that can hurt performance because the model may lose too much detail. At larger scales, however, the larger model capacity may be able to absorb that compression more effectively.

The paper’s results suggest exactly this pattern. {\textsc BLT} with patch size $8$ starts out worse than BPE Llama 2 at the $1B$ scale, but eventually becomes better than BPE at the $7B$ scale. The authors interpret this as evidence that larger patches may become even more effective at larger scales and with more training compute. In other words, patch length itself becomes a scaling dimension.

This is one of the paper’s most distinctive claims. Token-based models are constrained by a fixed vocabulary, so they do not have the same freedom to trade off token size against compute. {\textsc BLT}, by contrast, can use longer patches as the model grows and as the latent Transformer becomes more capable. That is why the paper argues that patches scale better than tokens: longer patches save computation, and that saved computation can be redeployed into a larger and stronger global model.

# Inference-flop implications of average patch sizes such as 6 and 8 bytes

The scaling study is not only about training. It also has direct consequences for inference cost. Since the global latent Transformer is the expensive part of the model and is invoked once per patch, the average patch size has a near-inverse relationship with inference flops. Larger patches mean fewer latent Transformer steps, which reduces inference cost.

The paper makes this concrete by pointing to average patch sizes of $6$ and $8$ bytes. A patch size of $8$ bytes can lead to nearly $50\%$ inference-flop savings relative to shorter patching regimes. This is one of the most practical implications of the work: {\textsc BLT} does not only match token-based scaling trends, it can do so while spending substantially less compute at inference time.

The authors also highlight that larger patch sizes shift compute away from the byte-level local modules and toward the global latent Transformer. Because the local encoder and decoder scale more slowly than the latent Transformer, increasing total model size does not force a proportional increase in local-model cost. That makes larger patch sizes especially attractive at scale. In the paper’s fixed-inference experiments, models with larger patches can devote the saved flops to a larger global Transformer, leading to better scaling trends than token-based architectures under the same inference budget.

Taken together, these results show the paper’s central point in its strongest form: in the compute-optimal regime, byte-level modeling is no longer a disadvantage if the architecture allocates compute dynamically. With the right patching strategy, larger patches do not merely reduce cost; they open a new scaling path in which both model size and patch size can grow together.

12. Scaling Laws II: Beyond Compute Optimality and Fixed-Inference Scaling

# Training 8B models beyond the compute-optimal regime

The paper’s scaling analysis does not stop at compute-optimal training, where a model is trained with the number of steps that theory suggests is best for a given parameter count. That setting is important because it gives a clean baseline for comparing architectures at matched training efficiency. But the authors argue that it is not the whole story. In practical use, a model is often pretrained once and then deployed many times, so inference cost can matter as much as, or even more than, training cost. For that reason, they also examine what happens when an 8B model is trained beyond the compute-optimal ratio.

In this setting, the authors train an $8$B $\textsc{BLT}$ model on the $\textsc{BLT}$-1T dataset, which is larger and higher quality than the Llama 2 dataset used for the compute-optimal scaling experiments. The goal is to see whether the gains from byte-level modeling and dynamic patching persist when the model is not constrained to the compute-optimal point. They compare against a tokenizer-based Llama 3 baseline trained on the same data and under the same flop budget. This makes the comparison especially meaningful: the models differ in architecture and patching strategy, not in the amount of training compute.

The result is that $\textsc{BLT}$ can outperform or match the tokenizer-based baseline even beyond the compute-optimal regime, especially when the patching scheme is entropy-based. The paper’s broader message is that once we allow patch size to vary dynamically, byte-level models are no longer forced to pay a uniform compute cost per input unit. Instead, they can spend more compute where the data is hard and less where it is easy, which becomes particularly valuable once the model is large enough to exploit this flexibility.

# Benchmark suite for reasoning, world knowledge, and coding

To evaluate whether the gains from $\textsc{BLT}$ extend beyond language modeling loss and into downstream utility, the paper tests the $8$B models on a standard suite of classification and code generation benchmarks. The classification tasks are ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, PIQA, and MMLU. These tasks are meant to probe common sense reasoning and world knowledge. The coding tasks are MBPP and HumanEval, which measure program synthesis ability through pass@1 scores.

The authors use prompt scoring for the multiple-choice classification tasks. That means they compute the likelihood of each answer choice under the model and then select the most likely option. MMLU is evaluated in a $5$-shot setting, while the other classification tasks are evaluated in a $0$-shot setting. MBPP is evaluated in a $3$-shot setting, and HumanEval is evaluated in a $0$-shot setting. This mixture of tasks is important because it tests both understanding and generation, and it also spans domains where tokenization could matter differently. For example, code generation may benefit from fine-grained character-level knowledge, while multiple-choice reasoning may depend more on how well the model handles longer stretches of structured text.

The benchmark suite is therefore not just a collection of downstream metrics. In the context of the paper, it is a test of whether the advantages of byte-level modeling survive the transition from training-loss improvements to practical task performance. The authors specifically want to know whether better patching can translate into more useful models, not just cheaper ones.

# Comparison among BPE, BLT-Space, and BLT-Entropy on BLT-1T

The core downstream comparison in this section involves three models trained on the $\textsc{BLT}$-1T dataset under equivalent flop budgets. One is a tokenizer-based Llama 3 baseline using a $128$K vocabulary. The second is $\textsc{BLT}$-Space, which uses space-based patching. The third is $\textsc{BLT}$-Entropy, which uses entropy-based patching with the approximate monotonicity constraint and a reset of the entropy context with new lines.

This comparison matters because it separates the effects of byte-level modeling from the effects of patching strategy. $\textsc{BLT}$-Space is a simpler patching method. It creates patches around whitespace-like boundaries and therefore offers a natural way to group words. $\textsc{BLT}$-Entropy is more adaptive and uses a learned entropy model to decide boundaries based on next-byte uncertainty. The tokenizer baseline, by contrast, uses a fixed vocabulary of subword units and cannot directly adapt compute to contextual uncertainty in the same way.

The paper’s main finding is that $\textsc{BLT}$-Entropy is the strongest of the three on downstream tasks. It outperforms the Llama 3 model on $4$ out of $7$ tasks, despite being trained on the same number of bytes. This is a striking result because it shows that dynamic patching can preserve or even improve benchmark performance while also operating directly on bytes. By contrast, $\textsc{BLT}$-Space underperforms the Llama 3 tokenizer on all but one task, although it achieves a meaningful reduction in inference flops because its average patch size is larger, around $6$ bytes.

The paper uses this comparison to make a subtle point. Larger patch sizes save compute, but they can also push the model further from the compute-optimal point if the same training budget is used. So the performance of $\textsc{BLT}$-Space is not simply worse because space-based patching is inferior in every sense. Rather, it reflects a tradeoff: its larger patches lower inference cost, but they also cover more data per update and may move the model away from the best training allocation for that budget. $\textsc{BLT}$-Entropy hits a more favorable balance between compute use and task performance.

# Task-level performance patterns across models

Across the benchmark suite, the models show different strengths that reveal what byte-level modeling contributes. The authors argue that $\textsc{BLT}$ gains come from two sources: more efficient use of training compute through dynamic patching, and the direct modeling of byte-level information, which tokenizers hide.

On the reasoning and knowledge benchmarks, the paper reports that $\textsc{BLT}$-Entropy is often competitive with or better than the Llama 3 tokenizer model. This suggests that byte-level access does not harm high-level reasoning and can in fact help when the model is allowed to allocate compute more flexibly. The result is especially notable because the $\textsc{BLT}$ models are not trained with a larger flop budget than the baseline. Their advantage comes from architecture and patching, not from simply spending more compute.

On coding tasks, the paper similarly finds that the entropy-based model is strong, though the discussion is more focused on the overall benchmark pattern than on any single task. The broader implication is that byte-level information can be useful even for tasks that are often assumed to favor token-level representations. Since code includes many character-level patterns, punctuation structures, and long-tail fragments, the ability to model raw bytes directly appears to be beneficial.

The paper also points out that model quality is sensitive to the patching choice. $\textsc{BLT}$-Space is attractive for inference efficiency, but its downstream performance lags behind $\textsc{BLT}$-Entropy. This contrast is important because it shows that patching is not just a compression trick. It changes how the model spends compute, and that decision affects what kinds of patterns it learns. In other words, the patching policy is itself a modeling choice.

# Effect of changing entropy threshold at inference time

A distinctive part of the paper’s evaluation is that the entropy threshold used for patching can be adjusted at inference time. For $\textsc{BLT}$-Entropy, the authors report an inference-time change in the entropy threshold from $0.6$ to $0.1$ for the benchmark evaluations. They say this improves task performance, but at the cost of more inference steps.

This is an important practical point. In a tokenizer-based model, the segmentation of the input is essentially fixed before inference begins. In $\textsc{BLT}$, patch formation is part of the computation strategy, so changing the threshold changes how much work the model does and how finely it processes the byte stream. Lowering the threshold leads to more patch boundaries, which means the latent transformer is invoked more often. That increases compute but can improve quality, presumably because the model gets more opportunities to revise its representation in difficult regions.

The paper uses this observation to emphasize that $\textsc{BLT}$ is not locked into one static inference behavior. Instead, it exposes a tradeoff between quality and cost that can be tuned at deployment time. This flexibility is one of the main reasons the authors argue that patches are a better scaling unit than tokens. A tokenized model has a fixed segmentation determined by vocabulary and tokenization rules, but a patch-based model can vary its granularity according to available compute or desired performance.

# Tradeoff between task quality and inference cost

The threshold adjustment underscores a central theme of the paper: better performance often comes from spending more compute where the data is complex. However, the paper does not claim that lower-cost inference is always free. Instead, it frames the system as giving a controllable tradeoff. If the entropy threshold is lower, the model spends more flops and can achieve better task scores. If the threshold is higher, the model uses fewer steps and becomes cheaper to run, but may lose some accuracy.

This tradeoff is not a weakness of the method; it is part of its design. The authors are explicit that $\textsc{BLT}$ can trade minor losses in evaluation metrics for substantial inference savings, and in some settings the opposite choice may be preferable. The key advantage is that the architecture makes this tradeoff accessible and interpretable through patch size and entropy threshold, instead of forcing the same compute on every token.

In the benchmark section, the paper shows that $\textsc{BLT}$-Entropy can outperform the baseline while using the same training budget, and then can be tuned further at inference time. This combination is what makes the method especially appealing in practical settings. The model can be trained once and then deployed in different operating modes depending on whether the goal is maximum quality or lower cost.

# The idea of fixed-inference-flop scaling

Beyond benchmark comparisons, the paper introduces a second scaling perspective: fixed-inference-flop scaling. This asks what happens when the inference budget is held constant, but model size and patch size are allowed to change together. The authors argue that this is a new and important scaling dimension for byte-level models.

The intuition is simple. If the average patch size increases, the model processes fewer patches for the same byte sequence. That reduces the number of times the global latent transformer must run, which saves inference flops. Those saved flops can then be reallocated to increase the size of the global latent transformer itself. So instead of treating compute savings as merely a way to make inference cheaper, the paper treats them as a way to build a larger and potentially stronger model under the same budget.

This is what the authors mean when they say that patches scale better than tokens. Token-based models have a fixed vocabulary and a relatively fixed average token size. That makes it hard to independently adjust segmentation granularity and model size. In $\textsc{BLT}$, by contrast, patch size is a tunable quantity. That flexibility lets the model exploit a new tradeoff: larger patches mean fewer latent steps, which leaves room for more parameters in the global transformer.

# Jointly increasing patch size and model size under a fixed inference budget

The fixed-inference study explicitly examines whether larger models that take fewer steps on larger patches can outperform smaller models that take more steps. To test this, the authors construct flop-equivalent model classes. They begin with model sizes of $400$M and $3.6$B parameters and then find token-based and $\textsc{BLT}$-Entropy counterparts that match the inference budget.

For the $\textsc{BLT}$ models, they use average patch sizes of $6$ and $8$ bytes on the training datamix. For the larger patch-size models, they also use $3$ encoder layers instead of $1$. They then train each model for various training flop budgets and compare the scaling trends across the matched inference classes.

The important result is that the $\textsc{BLT}$ models achieve better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures in both inference-flop classes. At small training budgets, the BPE models do better, but they are eventually overtaken by $\textsc{BLT}$ not far beyond the compute-optimal regime. The paper emphasizes that this crossover is practically relevant because it suggests that spending more during pretraining can pay off if one wants a model that is better at a fixed inference cost later.

This result also shows why fixed-inference scaling is not just a theoretical exercise. It describes a realistic deployment scenario: a user may be willing to invest heavily in one-time training if the resulting model is cheaper or better at inference. The paper’s claim is that $\textsc{BLT}$ makes this strategy more effective than tokenization-based architectures because it allows patch size and model size to co-evolve.

# Constructing flop-equivalent model classes

A technical requirement for the fixed-inference study is that the compared models must have equivalent inference cost. The paper constructs these classes by matching models across tokenizer-based and $\textsc{BLT}$ settings so that the inference flop budget is held fixed. This way, any observed improvement can be attributed to the architectural differences rather than to simply spending more compute at inference.

The model classes are built with different patch sizes and different parameter allocations. Larger patch sizes reduce the frequency with which the latent transformer is used, and that saved compute can be invested in larger model capacity. The authors also note that the local byte-level encoder and decoder modules scale more slowly than the global latent transformer. As a result, increasing patch size primarily affects the cost of the latent component, not the entire system equally.

This construction is essential to the paper’s argument. Without flop-equivalent comparison, one could not tell whether $\textsc{BLT}$ is truly better at the same inference budget or merely benefitting from more compute. The study is designed to rule out that objection and to demonstrate that patch size itself is a useful scaling variable.

# Crossover behavior: when BLT surpasses BPE with more training

One of the most interesting findings in the fixed-inference study is the crossover behavior. At smaller training budgets, token-based models perform better. But as training continues, $\textsc{BLT}$ models catch up and then surpass them. The point at which this happens moves closer to the compute-optimal regime as the inference-flop class gets larger.

The paper reports that for the larger inference-flop class, the crossover happens around $2.5\times$ the compute-optimal budget, compared with about $3\times$ in the smaller class. This suggests that larger models with larger patches benefit more from additional training than the tokenizer-based baselines do under the same inference constraint. The larger patch-size $\textsc{BLT}$ model also shows a steeper scaling trend and overtakes the others sooner in the larger-flop class.

This crossover behavior supports the authors’ broader claim that the advantages of patches become more visible at scale. When the model and training data grow, the flexibility to use larger patches becomes increasingly valuable. The architecture is not merely competitive at one point on the scale; its scaling trajectory is better.

# Why larger models with fewer latent steps can outperform smaller models with more steps

The intuition behind the fixed-inference results is that not every byte sequence needs the same amount of processing. The paper argues that many bytes are relatively easy to predict, so forcing a full transformer step for each token or fixed unit can waste compute. Larger patches can bundle together predictable regions and reduce the number of expensive latent-transformer invocations. That allows the system to use the saved compute to strengthen the global model itself.

In this framework, a larger model with fewer latent steps can outperform a smaller model with more steps because the larger model has more capacity where it matters most. The latent transformer is the main locus of modeling power, and if it is used less often but is larger, the model can still represent more complex global dependencies. Meanwhile, the local byte modules handle the fine-grained byte structure needed to maintain fidelity.

This is why the paper treats patching as a compute-allocation mechanism rather than simply a compression method. The goal is not only to shorten sequences. It is to place expensive computation where the information content is highest and to avoid spending it where the prediction is easy.

# Interpreting steeper scaling curves for larger patch sizes

The paper observes that larger patch-size models have steeper scaling trends, especially at larger model scales. This means that as more training compute is added, the performance of these models improves more rapidly than smaller-patch or token-based baselines. The authors interpret this as evidence that larger patches become increasingly beneficial as model and data scale increase.

A key reason is that larger patches lower the proportion of total flops consumed by the local byte encoder and decoder. Those modules do not grow as quickly as the latent transformer when the model gets larger. As a result, the global model becomes the main beneficiary of the extra capacity. Since larger patches reduce how often the latent transformer must be run, they make it possible to scale that component more effectively within the same inference budget.

The paper’s plots show this phenomenon in a concrete way. For patch size $8$, the model starts worse than the BPE baseline at smaller scales but becomes better at larger scales. This suggests that the relationship between patch size and performance is not monotonic in a trivial sense. Larger patches can look worse early on but become advantageous once the model has enough capacity and training data to exploit them.

# Why local byte modules scale differently from the global latent Transformer

The final conceptual point in this section is that the byte-level local modules and the global latent transformer do not contribute to compute in the same way. The local encoder and decoder are lightweight and operate at the byte level, while the latent transformer is the expensive component that dominates computation. Because of this, changes in patch size mainly alter how often the latent transformer runs, not how much the local modules cost.

The paper notes that when total parameters increase by roughly $20\times$ from $400$M to $8$B, the local model parameters only about double. This means the local modules become a smaller fraction of the total system as the model scales. That asymmetry is important because it explains why larger patch sizes can become more attractive at larger scales: the compute savings they generate are concentrated where the bulk of the flops lie.

This also helps explain why the larger patch-size $\textsc{BLT}$ model becomes more favorable as scale increases. The local modules provide the byte-level fidelity needed for segmentation and reconstruction, but the global latent transformer is where the major representational gains happen. Since the local modules scale more slowly, the architecture can invest more of its budget in the part of the model that benefits most from scale, while still preserving direct access to raw bytes.

Taken together, these results define the paper’s central scaling claim: when compute is fixed at inference time, patches are a better scaling unit than tokens. They let the model reduce unnecessary latent computation, enlarge the main transformer, and improve performance as scale grows.

13. Robustness and Byte-Level Generalization

# Why byte-level access should improve robustness

A central motivation for Byte Latent Transformer, or {\textsc BLT}, is that it works directly with raw bytes rather than with a fixed tokenizer vocabulary. This matters for more than efficiency. The paper argues that tokenization introduces a heuristic compression step that can obscure useful information about the surface form of text. Because tokens are formed by grouping bytes according to a predefined vocabulary, a token-based model does not directly see the byte-level composition of its input. As a result, it can become sensitive to how text happens to be segmented, and it may struggle when the input deviates from the patterns seen during tokenization.

In contrast, byte-level modeling preserves the exact character sequence. That gives {\textsc BLT} direct access to spelling, casing, punctuation, digits, and other fine-grained structure. The paper emphasizes that this is especially valuable for robustness to noisy inputs. If a model truly reasons over bytes, then perturbations that alter surface form but preserve much of the underlying content should be less disruptive than they are for token-based systems. The paper also argues that byte-level access helps with long-tail linguistic phenomena: rare spellings, unusual character sequences, and scripts or language varieties that are poorly covered by tokenization heuristics. In that sense, robustness is not only about resisting noise, but also about handling the parts of language that sit at the edge of a tokenizer’s vocabulary.

# Noisy-input evaluation protocols

To test robustness, the paper evaluates {\textsc BLT} on versions of benchmark classification tasks that have been deliberately corrupted at the character level. The main idea is simple: if a model is genuinely robust, it should retain more of its performance when the input is perturbed. The authors apply noise either to the prompt, the completion, or both, and then average the resulting scores. This makes the evaluation more stringent than testing on clean text alone, because the model must remain effective even when the text it sees has been altered in ways that disrupt ordinary tokenization.

The noisy evaluations are based on benchmark classification tasks discussed earlier in the paper, with noised HellaSwag used as a representative example. The purpose of these protocols is not to simulate a single natural corruption process, but to explore a range of character-level perturbations that stress different aspects of a model’s representation of text. Some perturbations change casing, some remove characters, some repeat characters, and some split the text into sequences of individual uppercase characters. Together, these tests probe whether the model can tolerate changes that preserve a rough semantic signal while breaking the exact byte patterns that tokenizers and token-level models often depend on.

# Character-level perturbation strategies: AntSpeak, Drop, RandomCase, Repeat, UpperCase

The paper uses five distinct character-level noising strategies. Each one alters the input in a different way, and each one is meant to expose a different weakness in token-based processing.

AntSpeak converts the text into uppercase, space-separated characters. This is a particularly aggressive transformation because it breaks words into a sequence of single characters separated by spaces. Drop randomly removes $10\%$ of the characters from the text, so the model must deal with missing information. RandomCase changes the casing of characters randomly, converting half to uppercase and half to lowercase. Repeat repeats $20\%$ of the characters up to a maximum of four times, which inserts spurious length variation and distorts spelling patterns. UpperCase transforms all characters into uppercase, which is a simpler but still meaningful test of sensitivity to surface form.

These perturbations are applied to the prompt, the completion, or both, which allows the evaluation to separate different kinds of robustness. If a model fails when the prompt is noised, that suggests trouble understanding the question or context. If it fails when the completion is noised, that suggests difficulty with the target answer space. If it fails in both settings, then the model may be strongly dependent on exact tokenization or surface-level conventions. Because {\textsc BLT} operates over bytes, the paper expects it to be less brittle under these transformations than a tokenizer-based model trained on the same data.

# Results on noised HellaSwag and robustness comparisons

On noised HellaSwag, the paper reports that {\textsc BLT} outperforms tokenizer-based models across all of the tested perturbations. The average advantage is about 8 points over the model trained on the same data, and the {\textsc BLT} model even improves over Llama 3.1, which was trained on a much larger dataset. This is an important result because it shows that robustness is not merely a side effect of using raw bytes; it can translate into materially better performance under severe input corruption.

The paper attributes this advantage to the combination of direct byte-level modeling and more effective use of training compute through dynamic patching. The byte-level representation means the model is less dependent on the exact token boundaries chosen by a tokenizer, while dynamic patching lets the architecture spend more computation where the input is complex or uncertain. Together, these features help {\textsc BLT} remain stable when the input text is altered in ways that would otherwise change tokenization drastically.

# Grapheme-to-phoneme evaluation and phonological awareness

The paper also evaluates {\textsc BLT} on grapheme-to-phoneme, or G2P, conversion using Phonology Bench in a 5-shot setting. This task asks the model to map written words, represented as graphemes, into their phonetic transcriptions. The task is a useful test of phonological awareness because it requires the model to use the internal structure of words rather than simply matching whole tokens to memorized outputs.

The reported result is that {\textsc BLT} outperforms the baseline Llama 3 1T tokenizer-based model on G2P. The significance of this finding is that byte-level modeling appears to provide a better handle on the relationship between spelling and pronunciation. Since {\textsc BLT} sees the raw character stream, it can, at least in principle, represent the fine-grained structure needed to relate letter patterns to sounds. The paper treats this as evidence that byte-level models are not just robust to corruption, but also better able to exploit sub-word information that token-based models may not use effectively.

# The CUTE benchmark and character manipulation abilities

A particularly striking part of the paper’s robustness analysis is the evaluation on CUTE, a benchmark designed to test character-level understanding. The benchmark groups tasks into three broad categories: understanding composition, understanding orthographic similarity, and the ability to manipulate sequences. These tasks are challenging because they require the model to reason explicitly about the structure of words and strings, not just about their meaning at the token level.

The paper reports that {\textsc BLT}-Entropy outperforms both BPE Llama 3 models by more than 25 points on CUTE. The improvement is especially large on character manipulation tasks, where the model reaches $99.9\%$ on spelling tasks. This suggests that {\textsc BLT} has learned a much stronger representation of character-level structure than the tokenizer-based baselines. The paper notes that these gains are especially impressive because {\textsc BLT} was trained on 16x less data than Llama 3.1, indicating that character-level information is not merely present in the data but is also substantially easier for a byte-based architecture to exploit.

At the same time, the paper is careful not to claim perfect behavior. Word deletion and insertion are the only two tasks on which the BPE models perform better. The authors suggest that manipulating whole words may not be straightforward for a byte-level model. This is an important nuance: byte-level access improves certain forms of character reasoning dramatically, but it does not automatically solve every kind of text manipulation task. The paper’s overall conclusion is that building from characters to words may be easier than the reverse, but the reverse direction still has some advantages on specific word-level operations.

# Orthographic knowledge in byte-level vs token-level models

The paper repeatedly returns to the idea that byte-level models possess better orthographic awareness than token-based models. Orthographic knowledge here means knowledge of the spelling structure of text: how characters combine, how words differ by small changes, and how textual forms can be manipulated. Tokenizers can hide this information because they replace variable-length byte sequences with token IDs from a fixed vocabulary. Once that happens, the model no longer has direct access to the internal spelling of the token unless it learns it indirectly.

{\textsc BLT}, by contrast, can operate on the byte sequence itself and therefore has direct access to orthographic structure. The CUTE results are the clearest demonstration of this in the paper, but the same theme appears in the noisy-input experiments and the G2P results. Across these settings, the byte-level model seems better able to preserve and exploit the form of the input. The paper’s message is not that token-based models have no orthographic knowledge at all, but rather that byte-level access makes such knowledge easier to learn and easier to use.

# Strengths and remaining weaknesses of BLT on character tasks

The paper’s robustness experiments show several strong advantages for {\textsc BLT}. It is more resilient to character-level noise, stronger at grapheme-to-phoneme mapping, and much better at tasks requiring explicit character manipulation. These findings support the broader claim that raw-byte modeling gives the model a more faithful view of the input and reduces dependence on arbitrary segmentation choices.

But the paper also identifies remaining weaknesses. As noted in the CUTE results, word deletion and insertion remain areas where BPE models can do better. The discussion suggests that while byte-level models are strong at composing and manipulating character sequences, some word-level operations may still be easier for token-based systems. The paper also notes that BPE models may benefit from additional prompt engineering on some of these tasks, which means the comparison is not entirely free of practical evaluation choices. Still, the main conclusion is clear: {\textsc BLT} substantially improves character-level generalization, but the boundary between character-level and word-level competence is not fully eliminated.

# Low-resource machine translation on FLORES-101

The paper extends the robustness argument beyond English character tasks to low-resource machine translation using FLORES-101. It evaluates translation into and out of English across six popular language families and twenty-one lower-resource languages with various scripts. The reported metric is SentencePiece BLEU.

The results show that {\textsc BLT} outperforms the Llama 3 tokenizer-based model, with a $2$-point overall advantage in translating into English and a $0.5$-point advantage in translating from English. In popular language pairs, {\textsc BLT} performs comparably to or slightly better than Llama 3. More importantly, it outperforms Llama 3 on many language pairs from lower-resource language families. This matters because such language pairs are precisely where tokenizer assumptions can be weakest and where rare byte sequences, unusual scripts, or low-frequency orthographic patterns are most likely to appear.

The paper uses these results to argue that byte modeling is especially useful for the long tail of data. Unlike a tokenizer, which compresses text into a finite vocabulary learned from a training distribution, a byte-level model can represent any sequence of bytes directly. That makes it naturally suited to diverse scripts and to language varieties that are poorly served by a fixed vocabulary.

# Why BLT helps on long-tail byte sequences and diverse scripts

The long-tail advantage of {\textsc BLT} is one of the paper’s major claims. Because it works over bytes, it does not depend on having seen every subword pattern as a token. This is important in multilingual settings where many scripts, spellings, and morphological forms may be rare. A tokenizer-based model may fragment such text into awkward or inefficient pieces, whereas a byte-level model sees the sequence as it actually appears. That gives it a more uniform representation of the input space.

The paper connects this to both robustness and generalization. Robustness to noisy text is one manifestation of the same principle: when the exact byte sequence changes slightly, the model can still process it directly rather than being forced to reinterpret it through a tokenizer. Low-resource translation is another manifestation: when the input language or script is underrepresented, the byte-level model may still generalize because it does not require the token vocabulary to cover the relevant forms well. In this way, {\textsc BLT} is presented not just as an efficiency improvement, but as a more flexible model of textual variation.

# Implications for multilingual and low-resource language modeling

The paper’s character-level and translation results point to a broader implication for multilingual modeling. Tokenizers can encode biases about which languages and scripts are easy to represent, and these biases can translate into inequities in model performance. By removing the fixed vocabulary and working directly with bytes, {\textsc BLT} reduces some of these constraints. This does not guarantee perfect multilingual performance, but it gives the model a more neutral starting point across scripts and writing systems.

The low-resource translation results on FLORES-101 show that this design choice can matter in practice. {\textsc BLT} does especially well on lower-resource language families, which is exactly where the paper expects byte-level generalization to help most. Combined with the robustness and character-level results, this suggests a consistent pattern: when the structure of text becomes more variable, rare, or difficult to compress with a tokenizer, byte-level modeling becomes increasingly attractive. The chapter’s overall lesson is that {\textsc BLT} is not only a way to save compute; it is also a way to preserve information that tokenization can hide, and that preservation improves performance on noisy, character-sensitive, and multilingual tasks.

14. Byte-ifying Pretrained Token Models

# Motivation for Leveraging Pretrained Token-Based LLMs

A central idea in the paper is that byte-level modeling does not have to begin from scratch. Although the Byte Latent Transformer, or \textsc{BLT}, is designed to learn directly from raw bytes, the authors also explore whether a pretrained token-based language model can be used as a starting point. This matters because token-based models such as Llama 3.1 have already absorbed a large amount of structure from massive pretraining runs. If some of that structure can be transferred into a byte-level architecture, then byte-level training may become much cheaper and faster, while still preserving the benefits of tokenization-free modeling.

This is especially appealing because the paper’s broader goal is to show that byte-level systems can compete with tokenizer-based systems at scale. A model that begins from pretrained token weights gives a practical bridge between the two worlds. Instead of asking whether bytes can win only through full pretraining from random initialization, the paper also asks whether bytes can inherit useful knowledge from existing large token models.

# Initializing BLT’s Global Transformer from Llama 3.1

The paper’s byte-ification experiment initializes the global transformer parameters of \textsc{BLT} from a pretrained Llama 3.1 model. This is an important design choice because the global transformer is the part of \textsc{BLT} that performs the bulk of the computation over patch representations. In the architecture described earlier in the paper, the local encoder and decoder handle byte-level processing around the patching interface, while the global transformer acts as the main autoregressive model over patches. Since this component is structurally closest to a standard transformer language model, it is the natural place to transfer pretrained weights.

The paper presents this as a way to “byte-ify” a tokenizer-based model. In other words, the pretrained Llama 3.1 model is not merely used as a teacher or auxiliary baseline; its parameters are repurposed inside a tokenizer-free system. This makes the experiment more than a simple fine-tuning study. It is a concrete test of whether the internal knowledge learned under tokenization can survive, or at least remain useful, when embedded into a byte-level architecture.

# Differential Learning Rates for Global and Local Modules

After initialization, the paper does not train all parts of the model at the same rate. Instead, the global transformer is updated using one-tenth the learning rate used for the local encoder and local decoder. This differential treatment reflects the idea that the pretrained global transformer already contains useful information and should therefore be adjusted more cautiously than the byte-level modules, which are newly introduced or less directly aligned with the pretrained token model.

This choice is consistent with the paper’s overall framing of the byte-ification process as transfer learning across representation levels. The local encoder and decoder must learn how to map raw bytes into patch representations and then back again, so they likely need stronger updates to adapt to the byte-level setting. By contrast, the global transformer is being reused from Llama 3.1, so the paper preserves more of its pretrained structure by giving it a smaller learning rate.

# Training Schedule for BLT-from-Llama

The paper states that the resulting \textsc{BLT}-from-Llama model is trained for the Llama 3 optimal number of steps. This means the training schedule is tied to the same compute-aware training philosophy used throughout the paper. Rather than arbitrarily extending training, the authors compare models under a fixed and carefully chosen training budget.

This setup is important because it allows the experiment to isolate the effect of initialization. The question is not whether a model trained longer can eventually do better, but whether starting from a pretrained Llama 3.1 model can improve the efficiency and quality of training under a comparable budget. In this sense, the schedule makes the comparison with baseline \textsc{BLT} meaningful: the model is not given extra time simply because it starts from pretrained weights.

# Performance Comparison with Baseline BLT and Llama Models

The paper reports that \textsc{BLT} initialized from Llama 3.1 significantly outperforms both the Llama 3 and baseline \textsc{BLT} models when trained with the same number of flops. This is one of the clearest pieces of evidence in the chapter’s motivating direction: existing tokenizer-based models can provide a strong initialization for a byte-level architecture, and the resulting model can be more efficient than training \textsc{BLT} from scratch under the same compute budget.

The comparison becomes even more striking when the authors compare the byte-ified model against their \textsc{BLT}-Entropy model trained on a much larger dataset of $1$T tokens. Even with that disadvantage in training data scale, \textsc{BLT} from Llama 3.1 still achieves superior performance on MMLU. The paper uses this result to argue that initialization from a pretrained token model can significantly reduce the training flops needed to reach strong downstream performance.

The paper also includes the original Llama 3.1 model, trained on $15$T tokens, as an additional reference point. Against this baseline, the \textsc{BLT} derived from Llama 3 shows only a slight decline on MMLU and HumanEval, but a more substantial drop on other tasks. So the transferred model is not a universal replacement for the original token model, but it demonstrates that the conversion process preserves a meaningful amount of capability.

# Advantages in Convergence and Training Efficiency

The main advantage emphasized by the paper is faster and better convergence under limited compute. By starting from pretrained Llama 3.1 weights, \textsc{BLT} does not need to discover as much structure from scratch in the global transformer. That makes the training process more efficient, because the model can devote more of its learning capacity to adapting the byte-level encoder and decoder around an already competent global module.

The paper interprets this as a reduction in training flops required to obtain strong performance. This is not presented as a minor engineering trick, but as evidence that byte-level language modeling can benefit from the representational knowledge encoded in token-based models. In practical terms, the experiment suggests a path for reducing the cost of moving toward tokenizer-free systems: one can start from a pretrained token model and adapt it, rather than training a byte-level model entirely from scratch.

# Viewing the Process as Converting a Tokenizer-Based Model into a Byte-Level Model

The authors explicitly describe this setup as transforming tokenizer-based models into tokenizer-free ones. That framing is conceptually important. It shifts the perspective from “token models versus byte models” to “how to migrate knowledge from one representation to the other.” In this view, Llama 3.1 becomes a foundation that can be reinterpreted inside \textsc{BLT}’s patch-based architecture.

This interpretation also highlights the role of patching. Since \textsc{BLT} operates on dynamically grouped bytes rather than fixed tokens, the global transformer is no longer tied to a finite vocabulary. The pretrained token model is therefore not being used in exactly the same way as before. Its knowledge is transferred into a system that must learn a new interface between raw bytes and latent patches. The paper’s result suggests that this interface is flexible enough to preserve some of the strengths of the original model while moving to a tokenizer-free framework.

# Current Limitations of This Byte-ification Approach

The paper is careful not to overstate the success of this approach. When the \textsc{BLT}-derived-from-Llama model is compared to the original Llama 3.1 model trained on $15$T tokens, the byte-ified version performs slightly worse on MMLU and HumanEval and more noticeably worse on several other tasks. This means the conversion is not yet a perfect replacement for native token-model training.

The authors therefore conclude that further work is needed, especially around data mixtures and other hyperparameters. They also suggest that the approach may not yet be fully optimized for how pretrained token knowledge should be integrated into a byte-level model. In other words, the experiment is promising, but it is best understood as an initial proof of concept rather than a final solution.

# What the Results Suggest About Transfer Between Token and Byte Representations

The main takeaway from the byte-ification experiment is that token and byte representations are not isolated worlds. The paper shows that a pretrained token model can provide a valuable starting point for a byte-level architecture, and that this can substantially improve training efficiency and downstream performance relative to training \textsc{BLT} from scratch.

At the same time, the remaining performance gap with the original Llama 3.1 model shows that the transfer is incomplete. Some knowledge clearly survives the transition from tokens to bytes, but not all of it is preserved equally well across all tasks. This suggests that token-based pretraining contains reusable linguistic and reasoning structure, but that exploiting it inside a byte-level system still requires careful architectural and optimization choices.

Taken together, the results support the paper’s broader thesis that byte-level modeling can scale successfully and can also benefit from the legacy of tokenizer-based pretraining. The byte-ification experiment is therefore more than a side result: it shows a practical route for bridging today’s tokenized LLMs and tomorrow’s tokenizer-free systems.

15. Ablations: What Really Matters in BLT?

# Ablating entropy model size and context length

A central ingredient in BLT is the entropy model used to decide where patch boundaries should go. This entropy model is not the main language model itself, but a smaller byte-level autoregressive model trained on the same distribution as the full BLT system. Its job is to estimate next-byte uncertainty so that the data can be segmented into patches with relatively uniform information density. The ablation study asks a simple question: how much does the quality of this entropy model matter for the final system?

The paper varies two properties of the entropy model: its size and its context window length. The tested models range from $1$M to $100$M parameters, and the context window ranges from $64$ to $512$ bytes. Performance is measured using bits-per-byte against training flops, using scaling-law curves from 400M and 1B BLT models trained on the Llama-2 dataset. The result is clear: larger entropy models and longer context windows both improve scaling performance. In other words, a better entropy predictor leads to better patch boundaries, and those better boundaries translate into better overall efficiency-performance tradeoffs for BLT.

At the same time, the gains do not continue indefinitely. The paper reports diminishing returns once the entropy model grows beyond about $50$M parameters. This matters because it shows that the entropy model does not need to be large in order to be useful. BLT is designed so that the patching mechanism is lightweight, and the ablations support that design: a moderately sized entropy model is enough to capture most of the benefit, while much larger entropy models bring only small additional gains.

# Diminishing returns in larger entropy models

The diminishing returns of scaling the entropy model are important for understanding BLT as a whole. Since the entropy model is only used to guide patching, its purpose is not to solve language modeling by itself. It only needs to identify regions where the next byte is uncertain enough that a new patch should begin. Once the model is already good enough to separate low-entropy from high-entropy regions reasonably well, further improvements in the entropy predictor become less valuable.

This suggests that the key role of the entropy model is to provide a useful approximation of local prediction difficulty rather than an exact estimate of byte-level uncertainty. The paper’s ablations imply that patching quality improves with better entropy estimates, but only up to a point. After that point, increasing size yields little benefit relative to its cost. That is an important design lesson: BLT gains most from having an entropy model that is good enough to identify the right structure in the data, not from making that model as large as possible.

# Comparing strided, space, BPE-derived, and entropy patching

The paper compares four patching schemes: strided patching, space patching, BPE-derived patching, and entropy-based patching. These differ in how they decide where a patch begins and how much compute is assigned to each region of the byte sequence.

Strided patching is the simplest. Bytes are grouped into fixed-size blocks, such as stride $4$ or $6$. This makes compute easy to control, but it does not adapt to the content. Space patching is a more structured heuristic: new patches are created after space-like bytes, which often align with word boundaries. BPE-derived patching uses the boundaries implied by a tokenizer, in this case the Llama 3 tokenizer. Entropy patching instead uses the output of the small byte entropy model to place boundaries where prediction uncertainty rises.

The ablations show that all of the non-static patching methods outperform static strided patching. That result supports the paper’s central claim that compute should not be distributed uniformly across bytes. Rather, patching should respond to the structure of the sequence. Among the methods compared, space patching is a particularly strong baseline, but entropy patching provides the best overall mechanism for dynamic allocation because it is data-driven rather than rule-based.

# Why dynamic patching outperforms static patching

The intuition behind dynamic patching is that not all bytes are equally hard to predict. Some regions of text, such as repetitive formatting, common suffixes, or predictable continuations, are easy. Others, such as the first byte of a new word, a rare symbol, or a code fragment with more information density, are harder. A fixed patching scheme treats these regions the same, assigning the same amount of global Transformer computation to each patch regardless of difficulty.

Dynamic patching improves on this by aligning compute with uncertainty. If a region is easy, it can be placed inside a larger patch, reducing the number of times the expensive latent Transformer is invoked. If a region is hard, a new patch can begin sooner, ensuring that the model gets more opportunities to process that region with global computation. The ablation results support this principle directly: dynamic patching schemes consistently outperform static striding under matched conditions.

This is one of the main reasons BLT is different from earlier byte-level models. The paper emphasizes that the main computational cost at scale is not just attention, but the repeated use of feed-forward layers in the Transformer. So reducing the number of patch-level Transformer steps where possible is a meaningful efficiency gain, and dynamic patching enables that reduction without simply discarding information.

# Space patching as a strong but limited baseline

Space patching is a surprisingly strong baseline in the paper’s comparisons. It creates a new patch after space-like bytes, which often correspond to word boundaries. That makes intuitive sense: many hard predictions happen at the start of words, while the remainder of a word is often more predictable once the beginning has been generated. The paper uses an example like predicting the answer to “Who composed the Magic Flute?” where the beginning of “Mozart” is much harder to guess than the later letters once the first character is known.

Because of this, space patching already captures an important regularity in language. It gives more compute to the beginnings of words, where uncertainty is often higher, and less compute to the internal bytes of words. The ablations show that this makes space patching a very competitive baseline, close to entropy patching in some settings.

But space patching also has clear limitations. It depends on a whitespace heuristic that does not handle all languages or domains gracefully, and it cannot vary patch size based on context in the same flexible way as entropy patching. Its boundary decisions are tied to a specific surface pattern rather than to predictive difficulty itself. So although it is strong, it remains a limited approximation of the more general dynamic patching idea.

# Cross-attention ablations in encoder and decoder

BLT uses cross-attention in both the encoder and decoder to connect byte-level representations with patch-level representations. The ablations study whether these cross-attention modules are actually useful and where they matter most.

The paper tests cross-attention at different points in the encoder and decoder. For the encoder, it compares several ways of initializing the queries for the first cross-attention layer: using the same learned embedding for every global state, using a hash embedding of the bytes in the patch, or using pooled encoder hidden states from the patch bytes. The results show that encoder cross-attention helps only slightly, and its effectiveness depends on how the queries are initialized. In particular, pooling-based initialization performs best among the tested encoder options.

The more striking result is that decoder cross-attention is especially important. The ablations show that cross-attention in the decoder is the most effective placement overall. This makes sense in the BLT architecture: the decoder must reconstruct byte-level outputs from patch-level representations, so it needs a strong mechanism for mapping global information back down to bytes. If that mapping is weak, the model loses one of the advantages of the patch-based representation. The decoder cross-attention therefore plays a crucial role in making the architecture work end to end.

# Different methods for initializing encoder cross-attention queries

The encoder cross-attention block requires an initial query representation for each patch. The paper studies three ways of creating those queries. One method uses a single learned embedding shared across all global states. Another uses a hash embedding based on the bytes in the patch. The third pools the byte representations of the patch at the current encoder layer.

These options represent different levels of specificity. A shared learned embedding gives the model very little patch-specific information at the start. A hash embedding injects some byte-level identity information immediately. Pooling, however, gives the query direct access to the content of the patch as represented by the encoder itself. The ablation results favor pooling as the most effective initialization strategy for encoder cross-attention, which suggests that the encoder benefits from starting with a patch representation grounded in the actual byte content rather than from a more generic or indirect initialization.

This finding is consistent with the broader BLT philosophy. Since patches are dynamic and content-dependent, the model should preserve as much local information as possible when forming patch-level representations. Pooling provides exactly that kind of bridge from bytes to patches.

# Why decoder cross-attention is especially important

The decoder cross-attention is especially important because it is the mechanism that turns global patch representations back into byte-level predictions. In BLT, the global Transformer operates over patches, but the output of the model must still be raw bytes. The decoder therefore has to recover byte sequences from patch representations while using the already-decoded bytes as context.

The ablations show that this reconstruction step benefits strongly from cross-attention. In effect, the decoder cross-attention lets the byte-level output sequence directly access the patch-level summary information generated by the latent Transformer. Without that bridge, the decoder would be left to reconstruct bytes using only weaker local signals. The paper finds that this module is the most effective among the cross-attention placements tested, confirming that the patch-to-byte conversion is a key part of the architecture.

The results also indicate that cross-attention is particularly helpful on Common Crawl and with larger patch sizes. That observation fits the design of BLT: when patches become larger, the global representations need to carry more information, and cross-attention becomes more valuable as a mechanism for transferring that information back into byte predictions.

# n-gram hash embedding ablations across vocabulary sizes

Another major design element in BLT is the use of hash $n$-gram embeddings in the local encoder. These embeddings augment each byte with information about the preceding byte $n$-grams for $n$ from $3$ to $8$. The paper ablates the effect of different hash vocabulary sizes, including $0$, $100$K, $200$K, $300$K, $400$K, and $500$K hash entries.

The results show that hash embeddings help across all domains, with especially noticeable gains on Wikipedia and GitHub. At smaller scales, the difference can be meaningful, and even at 8B scale the paper reports that reducing the hash vocabulary from $500$K to $300$K changes performance only slightly after 15k steps. This suggests that hash embeddings are important, but only up to a point. Once the model has enough hashed $n$-gram capacity to represent useful local patterns, adding more hashes gives diminishing returns.

The broader lesson is that byte-level models benefit from explicit access to subword-like structure. Hash $n$-gram embeddings are one way BLT injects that structure without relying on a fixed tokenizer vocabulary. The ablations confirm that this local information is a meaningful contributor to performance.

# Complementarity between n-gram hashes and cross-attention

The paper emphasizes that hash embeddings and cross-attention are complementary rather than redundant. Hash embeddings improve the local representation of bytes by giving the encoder stronger knowledge of preceding character patterns. Cross-attention, by contrast, helps move information between byte and patch levels and back again.

The ablation results support this separation of roles. The gains from hash embeddings and the gains from cross-attention appear on different datasets and are therefore not simply doing the same thing. Hash embeddings are especially helpful for representing local byte structure, while cross-attention helps with the interaction between local and global processing. Together, they make BLT stronger than either component alone.

This complementarity also explains why BLT can work with a very lightweight encoder. Once the encoder is equipped with informative byte-level features, it does not need to be deep in order to produce useful patch representations. The heavy lifting can then be done by the latent Transformer, which is where the model spends most of its compute.

# Local encoder and decoder depth ablations

The paper also ablates the number of layers in the local encoder and decoder. This matters because BLT deliberately splits the model into a large global Transformer and smaller local modules. The question is how small those local modules can be while still preserving performance.

The results show that BLT works well with a very lightweight encoder, especially when paired with hash $n$-gram embeddings. In fact, the paper reports that a one-layer encoder can be effective, while the decoder benefits from being heavier. This matches the architectural role of each component. The encoder mainly needs to gather byte information and create patch representations, while the decoder has the more difficult job of converting patch-level outputs back into raw bytes.

This asymmetry is important. It shows that BLT does not need to distribute capacity evenly across all parts of the system. Instead, it can concentrate more of the modeling burden in the global latent Transformer and the decoder, while keeping the encoder relatively small. That is one reason the architecture can gain efficiency without losing performance.

# Why a very lightweight encoder can work when combined with n-gram features

A lightweight encoder works in BLT because the encoder does not need to solve the entire modeling problem by itself. Its job is to build useful patch representations from local byte context. The hash $n$-gram embeddings already provide strong subword-like information, and the cross-attention layer provides a way to pool byte representations into patches. Together, these components make it possible for even a shallow encoder to produce expressive patch summaries.

The ablations show that once these local features are present, increasing encoder depth provides limited additional value compared to the cost it would add. This reinforces the architecture’s core idea: not every part of the sequence should receive the same amount of compute. Bytes that are easy to summarize can be handled by the local encoder, while the expensive global reasoning is reserved for the latent Transformer, and the byte-level reconstruction is handled by the decoder.

In that sense, the local encoder is not meant to be a miniature version of the full model. It is a specialized front end whose effectiveness comes from the combination of local byte features, hashed $n$-grams, and patch-based pooling. The ablations show that this design is sufficient, and that making the encoder much deeper is not necessary for strong performance.

# Summary

Taken together, the ablation studies show that BLT’s success depends on a small number of carefully chosen ingredients. Dynamic patching matters because it aligns compute with uncertainty. Entropy-based patching is more general than space patching, although space patching is itself a strong baseline. Cross-attention matters most in the decoder, because byte reconstruction from patch representations is a central challenge. Hash $n$-gram embeddings provide important local structure, and they work well even when the encoder is shallow. Finally, the entropy model itself only needs to be moderately sized to be useful, since its benefits saturate after a point.

The overall message is that BLT is not strong because of one single trick. It is strong because its components reinforce one another: local byte structure, dynamic patch boundaries, and a latent global model that spends compute where the data actually needs it. The ablations make that design logic visible.

16. Positioning BLT in the History of Byte- and Character-Level Modeling

# Early character-level RNN language models

The history of byte- and character-level language modeling begins with the observation that text can be modeled directly at very small units, rather than being forced into words or subword tokens. Early neural work explored character language modeling because it offered a natural way to handle out-of-vocabulary words and to represent text without depending on a fixed vocabulary. In the paper’s related-work discussion, these early models are described as a foundational line of research that motivated later byte-level approaches precisely because they could model raw textual form more flexibly than word-level systems.

This early direction established an important idea that continues throughout the BLT paper: the model should not lose information by committing too early to a segmentation scheme. Characters and bytes preserve orthographic detail directly. That matters whenever the exact spelling of a string is relevant, or when a word is rare, noisy, or not covered well by a token vocabulary. BLT inherits this motivation, but replaces the older recurrent architecture with a scalable Transformer-based design and a dynamic patching mechanism.

# Character-based models for morphology and machine comprehension

The paper positions character-level modeling as especially useful for languages and tasks where morphology matters. It notes that character-based models could outperform word-level systems on morphologically rich languages because they can represent internal word structure explicitly rather than treating a word as an indivisible symbol. This is an important theme in the broader history of the field: the more a task depends on forms, affixes, spelling patterns, or rare word variants, the more direct access to characters can help.

The related work also highlights machine comprehension at the byte level as another early success. Byte-level LSTM models were used for comprehension tasks and were reported to outperform word-level models in languages such as Turkish and Russian, which again fits the same intuition. In such settings, the unit of modeling matters because the model must cope with variation in surface form, not just with semantic content at a token level. BLT’s later robustness and long-tail results are presented in this same spirit: modeling bytes directly helps with phenomena that tokenizers often hide or compress away.

# CNN- and LSTM-based approaches to character modeling

Before Transformers became dominant, much of the work on character-level modeling used recurrent or convolutional architectures. The paper points to several examples. Some models used convolutional and highway networks over character inputs, which then fed into LSTM-based systems. Others used character-based convolutional classifiers. The common theme is that these approaches tried to extract useful information from raw character sequences by building learned compositions over local patterns.

The paper’s discussion suggests that these methods were effective but limited in scope. They helped demonstrate that character-level representations could be competitive, but they did not remove the underlying computational difficulty of processing very long sequences. Because characters and bytes are much finer-grained than tokens, sequence length inflates quickly. That long-sequence cost is one of the central reasons the field eventually moved toward tokenization, and it remains one of the central challenges that BLT addresses with patching.

# The rise of Transformer models with subword tokenization

The paper describes the rise of Transformers together with subword tokenization as the turning point that made large language models highly successful. Transformers improved the modeling capacity and scaling behavior of neural language models, while subword tokenization made computation tractable by reducing the number of sequence steps. Together, these choices helped establish the modern LLM pipeline: text is first compressed into a fixed vocabulary of subword units, and the Transformer then processes those units end to end.

This arrangement has clear practical advantages, but BLT’s introduction argues that it also creates a strong inductive bias. A tokenizer decides in advance how bytes should be grouped, which means it shapes both the compression of the input and the unit of prediction. The paper emphasizes that this can be beneficial for efficiency, but it also creates sensitivity to domain and modality, input noise, and multilingual inequity. In the historical arc of the paper, tokenization is thus not treated as a neutral preprocessing step, but as a design choice that has costs as well as benefits.

# Challenges faced by earlier character-level Transformers

The paper also reviews earlier attempts to combine Transformers with character-level modeling. These efforts showed that attention-based models could be trained on raw characters, and some achieved strong character-language-modeling performance. However, the paper emphasizes that there remained a substantial gap to word-level systems, especially at scale. One reason is simple: when the input is made of bytes or characters, the sequence becomes much longer, and the computational burden grows quickly.

The paper is explicit that earlier byte-level Transformers were not competitive at large scale partly because of this cost. Even if one improves the attention mechanism, the main compute bottleneck at scale is not only attention itself but also the feed-forward layers that operate over every byte. That observation is important for understanding BLT historically. BLT is not just “a Transformer on bytes”; it is a redesign of where computation should happen so that the model can avoid treating every byte as equally expensive.

# Byte-level encoder-decoder models such as ByT5

The paper uses ByT5 as a representative example of a byte-level encoder-decoder model that demonstrated both the promise and the challenge of byte modeling. ByT5 did not use patching, and it showed improved robustness to noise while remaining competitive with token-based models under certain data conditions. Its significance in the paper’s narrative is that it confirmed direct byte modeling can work, but also exposed the cost problem very clearly.

Because such models operate directly on every byte, they must run expensive attention over very long sequences. The paper describes this as the major reason these models were compute heavy. BLT’s contribution is framed as a response to this limitation: instead of removing bytes from the model, BLT groups them into patches so that the expensive global Transformer is invoked less often when the data is predictable, and more often when the data is complex.

# Alternative efficient sequence architectures such as MambaByte

The paper also situates itself among newer efficient sequence architectures, including MambaByte. The main point made in the related work is that attention-free or alternative sequence models can help byte-level modeling by reducing the cost of long contexts. MambaByte, in particular, is described as using the Mamba architecture with a fixed-size memory state over large contexts and as outperforming byte-level Transformer baselines in some flop-controlled settings at smaller scale.

This is an important comparison because it shows that the field has explored more than one route to byte-level efficiency. Some approaches reduce the cost of the sequence model itself; BLT instead keeps the Transformer framework but changes the unit of computation. The paper’s claim is that patching is especially important for scaling because the compute burden at scale is dominated by per-step feed-forward costs, not just by attention. So BLT seeks efficiency by reducing the number of expensive global steps through dynamic patches.

# Hierarchical and patching-based predecessors

The paper places BLT in a broader family of hierarchical and patching-based models. Earlier work explored the idea that one could first compress a fine-grained sequence into larger units and then process those larger units at a higher level. This is a natural response to the cost of character-level modeling: if every byte is too expensive, perhaps the model should first form groups and then reason over those groups.

This historical line is important because BLT is not the first model to patch bytes. What distinguishes BLT is that it turns patching into a learnable, dynamic mechanism rather than a rigid preprocessing heuristic. The paper’s central argument is that static patching can help, but the best results come from methods that allocate compute according to the difficulty of the prediction, not merely according to a fixed rule like stride or whitespace.

# MegaByte and static patching

MegaByte is the closest predecessor discussed in the paper. It is presented as a decoder-only causal LLM that uses static patching and concatenation of representations to move from bytes to patches, then a local model to reconstruct bytes from patch representations. The paper credits MegaByte with demonstrating that patching can make byte-level modeling more viable, and notes that it achieved competitive results with tokenization-based models at around the 1B scale on a large byte dataset.

At the same time, BLT presents MegaByte as still limited by its static patching scheme. A fixed patch size means that the model allocates compute uniformly, regardless of whether a region of text is easy or hard to predict. The BLT paper argues that this is precisely where more advanced patching becomes necessary. Static patching is an important historical step, but BLT claims to go further by making patch size dynamic and context-dependent.

# SpaceByte and whitespace-based dynamic grouping

SpaceByte is presented as an improvement over static patching by using whitespace-like bytes to define boundaries. This reflects a useful linguistic intuition: many words begin after spaces, and the beginnings of words are often harder to predict than the continuation of a word. The paper gives a simple example in which predicting the start of “Mozart” after a question is harder than predicting the remaining letters once the first letter is known. Space-based grouping therefore allocates compute in a way that is more aligned with linguistic structure than a fixed stride.

However, the paper also emphasizes the limitations of whitespace patching. It cannot vary patch size flexibly, and it does not handle all languages and domains gracefully. It is better than fixed striding because it respects one important kind of boundary, but it still depends on a heuristic. BLT takes the next step by replacing the heuristic boundary rule with entropy-based patching, which attempts to measure difficulty directly from a small byte language model.

# How BLT differs from and improves on prior patch-based work

BLT’s core innovation, as presented in the paper, is that it combines dynamic patching with a full byte-latent architecture. Instead of using a fixed vocabulary or a fixed patch rule, BLT segments bytes into patches based on next-byte entropy, so that more compute is assigned where the prediction is uncertain and less compute where the data is predictable. This makes patching not just a compression trick, but a way to control the allocation of model capacity.

The architecture also matters historically. BLT is not just patching on the input side; it has a local encoder that forms patch representations, a global latent Transformer that does the heavy reasoning, and a local decoder that reconstructs bytes. This structure lets the model work directly from bytes while still limiting the number of expensive global steps. In this way, BLT addresses the main weakness of earlier byte-level models: they either remained too expensive at scale or relied on rigid patching that did not fully align compute with data complexity.

The paper’s broader historical claim is that this combination allows byte-level modeling to cross an important threshold. Earlier systems showed that character and byte modeling could be robust and expressive, and patching-based systems showed that compression could make byte modeling more efficient. BLT unifies those lessons and adds dynamic, entropy-based segmentation. According to the paper, that is what allows byte-level models to match tokenization-based systems at scale while improving robustness and offering a new scaling dimension through larger patches and larger models under fixed inference budgets.

17. Limitations, Open Questions, and Future Directions

# Potential mismatch between BPE-derived compute-optimal laws and BLT

A central limitation acknowledged by the paper is that many of its architectural decisions for {\textsc BLT} are chosen using the same “optimal number of steps” framework developed for token-based models, especially the Llama 3 scaling prescription. This is a practical choice, but the paper explicitly notes that those scaling laws were calculated for BPE-level transformers rather than byte-level models. That means the assumed best ratio of training data to parameter size may not be perfectly matched to {\textsc BLT}. If the relationship between model size, data size, and training compute differs for byte-level architectures, then using BPE-derived compute-optimal settings could slightly misplace {\textsc BLT} away from its own true optimum.

This matters because much of the paper’s evidence is organized around compute-controlled comparisons. If the baseline scaling rule itself is borrowed from a different modeling regime, then the reported results are best understood as strong evidence that {\textsc BLT} is competitive under a standard and reasonable training budget, not as a final answer about the truly optimal training recipe for bytes. The paper therefore treats its scaling results as an important starting point rather than a complete characterization of byte-level scaling.

# Need for BLT-specific scaling-law studies

Because of that mismatch, the authors explicitly identify the calculation of {\textsc BLT}-specific scaling laws as future work. This is not just a minor methodological refinement; it could change the conclusions about what model sizes, patch sizes, and data budgets are best for byte-level training. The paper’s current experiments show that {\textsc BLT} can match tokenization-based models under the Llama 3-style compute-optimal regime, but they do not prove that this regime is optimal for {\textsc BLT} itself.

A {\textsc BLT}-specific scaling study would be especially important because patching introduces a new degree of freedom that token-based models do not have: average patch size. Since patch size affects how often the large latent transformer is invoked, it directly changes the trade-off between efficiency and modeling capacity. The paper shows that larger patch sizes can help under fixed inference budgets and that larger patch sizes can become more attractive as scale increases. However, the best choice may depend on scale in a way that the current experiments do not fully resolve. A dedicated scaling-law study for {\textsc BLT} would help determine how this patch-size dimension should be balanced with model size and data volume.

# Uncertainty about the best architecture at even larger scales

The paper is careful to frame its results as being strongest up to the 8B scale and up to 4T training bytes, but it also repeatedly signals that the best architectural choices may change at even larger scales. This is especially important because some of the gains in {\textsc BLT} appear to strengthen as the model grows, while other components, such as the local encoder and decoder, may contribute a smaller fraction of total flops at larger scale. The paper notes that when total parameters grow substantially, the local model parameters only increase modestly, suggesting that the balance between local and global computation may shift.

That creates an open question: at still larger scales, should {\textsc BLT} continue to rely on the same division between local byte models and a global latent transformer, or should the proportions of these components change? The paper’s evidence suggests that larger patch sizes may become more favorable as scale grows, but it does not establish where that trend stops or whether it continues indefinitely. In other words, the architecture seems promising beyond the tested range, but the best design for very large byte-level models remains unresolved.

# Gap between theoretical FLOPs and wall-clock efficiency

The paper is explicit that its efficiency claims are based on flops, not directly on wall-clock time. That distinction is important. The authors estimate computation using standard transformer flop accounting and compare models under matched flop budgets, but they also acknowledge that existing transformer libraries and codebases are often optimized for tokenizer-based architectures. Even when a model is theoretically cheaper, the actual runtime may not scale in exactly the same way.

This is especially relevant for {\textsc BLT}, because dynamic patching and patch-dependent masking introduce implementation complexity that does not exist in standard token-based pipelines. The paper uses efficient tools such as FlashAttention for standard attention and FlexAttention for dynamic cross-attention masks, but it still notes that its implementations may not yet be at parity with tokenizer-based models in wall-clock time. So the theoretical gains in flops are real within the paper’s measurement framework, yet the translation of those gains into practical training and inference speed still leaves room for systems-level improvement.

# Implementation challenges for dynamic masking and patch-aware attention

{\textsc BLT} depends on patch-aware computation, which means the model must repeatedly decide how bytes are grouped and then apply attention patterns that depend on those groupings. This creates implementation challenges that are not present in fixed-token systems. In the encoder and decoder, cross-attention is constrained so that queries only attend within the bytes that belong to the relevant patch. Those masks are dynamic and patch-specific, which makes them more difficult to optimize than standard full-sequence or fixed-window attention.

The paper’s use of FlexAttention reflects this challenge directly. The dynamic nature of patch boundaries makes it harder to rely solely on the highly optimized kernels used for conventional transformer layers. While the paper successfully demonstrates the feasibility of training at scale, it also implies that future gains in practical efficiency may depend on better support for dynamic masking, patch-aware batching, and more efficient handling of variable patch sizes. In that sense, the model’s conceptual efficiency is ahead of the current software efficiency.

# Possibility of end-to-end learned patching

A particularly interesting open direction in the paper is the possibility of learning patching end-to-end. In the current {\textsc BLT} setup, patch boundaries are determined by a separately trained entropy model, and this patching decision is performed during preprocessing or dataloading. That approach is lightweight and practical, but it also means patching is not trained jointly with the main language model. The authors explicitly identify this as a future direction.

The appeal of end-to-end patching is clear from the paper’s framing: if the model could learn how to segment bytes into patches while also learning the language modeling objective, it might discover better boundaries than a fixed entropy heuristic. Such a system could potentially allocate compute even more precisely to the places where it is most useful. At the same time, the paper does not claim that this is solved; instead, it presents entropy patching as an effective proxy that already captures much of the benefit. End-to-end patching remains an open question because it could improve quality, efficiency, or both, but it would also introduce additional optimization complexity.

# Future opportunities in byte-ifying large pretrained token models

Another future direction highlighted by the paper is “byte-ifying” pretrained tokenizer-based models. The authors present initial experiments in which the global transformer of {\textsc BLT} is initialized from a pretrained Llama 3.1 model. This allows {\textsc BLT} to benefit from existing large-scale token-model pretraining while adapting to a tokenizer-free architecture. The paper reports that this approach can substantially improve performance relative to training {\textsc BLT} from scratch under the same flop budget.

However, the paper also shows that this conversion is not yet fully solved. The byte-ified model sometimes suffers drops on some tasks, indicating that simply transferring the global transformer weights is not enough to recover all the benefits of a fully pretrained token model. The authors therefore suggest that future work may identify better ways to exploit large pretrained models, perhaps by improving data mixtures, optimization, or the way the local byte modules are integrated with the pretrained global model. This makes byte-ification a promising bridge between existing token-based systems and future tokenizer-free models.

# Open questions about better data mixtures and optimization strategies

The paper repeatedly suggests that better data mixtures and optimization choices may improve {\textsc BLT} further. This is especially visible in the discussion of the byte-ified model, where the authors say that the approach likely needs improved data mixtures and other hyperparameters to fully leverage the pretrained weights. The same theme appears in the limitations section, where they note that current experiments are tied to training-step choices derived from Llama 3 rather than from {\textsc BLT}-specific optimization studies.

This leaves several practical questions open. Are the same data proportions best for a byte-level model as for a token-level one? Does the dynamic patching mechanism change what kinds of documents should be emphasized during training? Does the best learning rate schedule or patch-size choice vary with scale in a way the current experiments do not capture? The paper does not answer these questions, but it makes clear that training performance is not determined solely by architecture. Better optimization recipes may be just as important as architectural innovations.

# Broader implications for tokenizer-free language modeling

The paper’s broader message is that tokenizer-free language modeling is not only feasible, but can be competitive with tokenizer-based models at scale. Still, the limitations and open questions show that the field is not finished. {\textsc BLT} demonstrates that raw-byte modeling can match strong baseline systems while improving robustness and enabling new scaling behavior through patch size. At the same time, the paper is careful not to claim that tokenization has been fully obsoleted or that all remaining engineering and modeling questions are solved.

Instead, the work reframes the design space. Tokenization is no longer the only practical way to organize computation in large language models. Patching introduces a new axis for controlling compute, and byte-level modeling exposes information that tokenization can hide. The future directions identified in the paper suggest that the next steps are not just about making byte models larger, but about understanding how to scale them properly, how to implement them efficiently, and how to learn patching itself. In that sense, {\textsc BLT} is presented as both a successful model and a starting point for a wider research program on tokenizer-free language modeling.

18. Synthesis: Why Patches Scale Better Than Tokens

#

The Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, is built around a simple but far-reaching idea: language models do not need to commit to a fixed vocabulary of tokens in order to scale well. Instead, BLT operates directly on raw bytes and groups them into dynamically sized patches that serve as the main units of computation. This design changes the role of preprocessing from a rigid, heuristic compression step into a learned mechanism for allocating compute. The paper’s central claim is not merely that bytes are possible, but that bytes can be made practical at scale if the model is organized so that expensive computation is spent where the data is hardest, rather than uniformly across all positions.

This matters because tokenization-based language models assign the same basic amount of model computation to every token, even though not every part of a sequence is equally difficult. Some predictions are relatively easy, such as completing the end of a word once much of it has already been seen, while others are much harder, such as deciding the first byte of a new word or handling uncommon, noisy, or multilingual text. BLT is designed to reflect this uneven structure in the data. Its architecture therefore combines lightweight byte-level local models with a larger global latent transformer, and its patching scheme decides where the global model should be invoked. In this way, the model’s structure mirrors the paper’s intuition that compute should be concentrated where uncertainty is high.

#

## Recap of the BLT Design Philosophy

BLT is a tokenizer-free architecture trained on raw bytes. Instead of relying on a fixed vocabulary of learned or heuristic token units, it learns how to segment byte sequences into patches that can vary in size depending on context. The paper emphasizes that patching is not the same as tokenization: tokens come from a fixed vocabulary chosen before training, whereas patches are dynamically formed groups of bytes without a fixed vocabulary. This distinction is important because it means BLT is not merely a byte-level model with a different front end, but an architecture that changes how representations are formed and when computation is applied.

The model has three main parts. Two small local models operate directly on bytes, one encoder and one decoder, while the central global component is a latent autoregressive transformer that processes patch representations. The local encoder turns byte sequences into patch representations, the latent transformer performs the main sequence modeling over patches, and the local decoder maps patch outputs back into bytes. The architectural separation is intentional: the local modules handle byte-level detail efficiently, while the global transformer is reserved for the more expensive reasoning over higher-level structure.

A key element of the philosophy is that patch boundaries are chosen dynamically. The paper proposes entropy-based patching, where a small byte-level language model estimates next-byte uncertainty and patch boundaries are placed where entropy is high or changes significantly. This lets BLT adapt patch size to the local complexity of the input. In predictable regions, patches can become longer and reduce computation. In difficult regions, patches can become shorter and allocate more model capacity. The resulting system is not just a byte model, but a byte model with a principled mechanism for concentrating compute.

#

## Dynamic Compute Allocation as the Central Innovation

The most important innovation in BLT is dynamic compute allocation. The paper argues that standard token-based models and static patching methods both miss this opportunity in different ways. Tokenizers group bytes using compression heuristics, but those heuristics are not always aligned with modeling difficulty. Static patching, such as fixed-size byte grouping, also fails because it gives the same treatment to all parts of the sequence. BLT instead uses entropy-aware patching to decide where a patch should begin, so that the model expends more compute in regions where next-byte prediction is more uncertain.

This idea becomes especially clear when the paper contrasts different patching strategies. Fixed strided patching is simple and cheap, but it cannot respond to context. Space patching improves on that by creating boundaries around whitespace, which often aligns with word boundaries and allows a latent model step to be spent on each word. Yet space patching still cannot vary patch size based on difficulty. Entropy patching is the more general solution because it uses data-driven uncertainty estimates rather than a rule tied to language-specific surface structure. In the paper’s framing, entropy patching is the first step toward compute that adapts to information density.

The architecture reinforces this same principle. The local byte models are lightweight because they handle the parts of the task that do not require much computation, while the latent transformer is large because it is where the expensive modeling happens. Since the latent transformer operates less frequently when patches are longer, the amount of compute per byte can be modulated by changing patch size. The whole design therefore embodies a simple but powerful idea: compute should not be fixed to a token boundary; it should be routed by uncertainty.

#

## How BLT Achieves Byte-Level Modeling Without Prohibitive Cost

One of the major reasons tokenization became dominant is that raw byte modeling was historically too expensive. Byte sequences are longer than token sequences, so a straightforward byte-level transformer would have to process many more positions. The paper addresses this directly. Its answer is not to abandon bytes, but to reorganize the computation so that not every byte incurs the full cost of a large transformer step.

The first part of this solution is patching. By compressing sequences of bytes into patches before the global transformer sees them, BLT reduces the number of expensive latent-transformer steps. Because the cost of the main transformer is strongly related to the number of sequence elements it processes, average patch size becomes a direct handle on compute. Longer patches mean fewer global steps, which lowers inference cost and training cost for a fixed model size. The paper also emphasizes that the local encoder and decoder are much smaller than the latent transformer, so the expensive part of the model is not run on every byte.

The second part of the solution is representation quality. BLT does not simply compress bytes into opaque blocks. The local encoder augments byte embeddings with hash-based $n$-gram information, allowing the model to access some local context directly. The cross-attention modules then pool byte representations into patch representations and later reconstruct byte-level outputs from patch-level outputs. In this way, the model preserves access to raw byte structure while still gaining the efficiency of higher-level latent processing. The paper’s ablations show that these architectural choices matter: entropy patching, cross-attention, and hash $n$-gram embeddings all contribute to matching token-based scaling trends.

The empirical result is that BLT can train at scale up to 8B parameters and 4T bytes while matching the flop-controlled performance of Llama 3, and it can do so with up to 50\% fewer flops at inference in some settings. That is the key practical breakthrough: byte-level modeling is no longer presented as an inherently expensive alternative, but as a viable large-scale design.

#

## Why Patch Size Becomes a New Scaling Axis

Token-based models are constrained by a fairly rigid interface: increasing token size usually requires enlarging the vocabulary, which increases the size of embedding and output layers. This creates a tradeoff that limits how much token size can be changed without new costs. BLT breaks this relationship. Because patches do not come from a fixed vocabulary, patch size can be changed much more freely, and changing it does not require a larger vocabulary table. This opens a new scaling dimension.

The paper argues that patch size is now a meaningful axis for scaling language models, alongside parameter count and training data. Larger patches reduce the number of latent transformer steps, which lowers inference flops. That saved compute can then be reinvested into a larger latent transformer. This is the source of the paper’s claim that patches scale better than tokens: with BLT, one can grow both patch size and model size while staying within the same inference budget.

The scaling experiments make this point concrete. In the fixed-inference setting, BLT models with larger patch sizes show better scaling trends than tokenization-based baselines. In the compute-optimal setting, BLT models match or outperform corresponding BPE models, and the paper observes that larger patch sizes can become more favorable as model scale grows. The important implication is that patch size is not just a detail of the preprocessing pipeline; it becomes a knob for navigating the compute-performance frontier. This is different from tokenization, where larger token units are tied to a fixed vocabulary and therefore less flexible.

#

## Efficiency, Robustness, and Long-Tail Generalization Together

The paper’s strongest case for BLT is that it does not trade one desirable property for another. The same byte-level design that improves efficiency also improves robustness and generalization to unusual data. Because BLT works on raw bytes, it is less sensitive to the quirks and limits of a fixed tokenizer. The paper argues that tokenization can be brittle with respect to domain, modality, noise, orthography, and multilingual coverage. BLT avoids these issues by directly modeling the underlying byte sequence.

This is reflected in the robustness experiments. When text is corrupted by noise such as character dropping, case changes, repeated characters, or other transformations, BLT is more robust than tokenizer-based models. The paper also reports gains on character-level tasks, including grapheme-to-phoneme mapping and the CUTE benchmark, which tests spelling, orthographic similarity, and sequence manipulation. These results support the claim that byte-level modeling gives the system better access to subword and character structure, which tokenizers often obscure.

The same logic extends to long-tail generalization. The paper shows gains on low-resource machine translation across a wide set of languages and scripts. This is a natural consequence of byte modeling, because raw bytes can represent any script without relying on a vocabulary that may be unevenly optimized across languages. In the paper’s interpretation, BLT is not only more robust to accidental noise, but also better suited to the long tail of language itself, where rare patterns, rare scripts, and unusual spellings are common. Efficiency, robustness, and long-tail behavior are therefore connected outcomes of the same design choice.

#

## When BLT Is Preferable to Token-Based LLMs

The paper does not claim that BLT is universally superior in every setting. Instead, it identifies contexts where its advantages are especially important. BLT is attractive when one cares about inference efficiency under a fixed budget, when the data includes substantial noise or unusual orthography, or when character-level and multilingual coverage matter. It is also compelling when one wants to scale models beyond what fixed-token interfaces naturally encourage, because patch size gives a new degree of freedom that tokens do not.

At the same time, the paper shows that the exact patching strategy matters. Space patching can deliver meaningful efficiency gains, but it does not match the performance of entropy-based patching. Entropy-based BLT is the stronger general-purpose version, especially when trained at 1T-token scale on the BLT-1T dataset. In the paper’s downstream evaluations, BLT-Entropy outperforms the Llama 3 tokenizer-based model on several tasks while maintaining the same training flop budget. By contrast, BLT-Space achieves larger inference savings but performs less strongly overall. This suggests that the best use of BLT depends on what kind of tradeoff is most important: maximum accuracy, maximum efficiency, or a balance between them.

The paper also explores a path for using pretrained tokenizer-based models to initialize BLT, showing that a BLT model derived from Llama 3.1 can improve convergence and reduce training flops. This implies that BLT is not only relevant for training from scratch, but may also be useful as a conversion strategy when one wants to move from token-based to byte-based modeling without starting over entirely.

#

## What BLT Suggests About the Future of Language Model Interfaces and Representations

The deeper lesson of BLT is that the interface between language models and text does not have to be fixed to tokens. The paper treats tokenization as a heuristic convenience that has long been accepted because it made scaling practical, not because it was necessarily the best representational choice. BLT demonstrates that a different interface is possible: raw bytes can be modeled directly if the architecture learns how to group them into useful units and how to allocate compute adaptively.

This shifts attention from vocabularies to representations. In a tokenized system, the vocabulary is central, and many of the model’s assumptions are shaped by the segmentation scheme. In BLT, the patching mechanism is central, but patching is contextual and dynamic rather than fixed. That suggests a future in which the model, rather than the tokenizer, decides how much structure to impose on the input at each point. The paper’s results indicate that such flexibility can improve both performance and efficiency.

Equally important, BLT suggests that future models may be evaluated not just by parameter count and token count, but by how intelligently they distribute compute over structure in the data. The paper’s scaling studies show that longer patches can be beneficial, especially when paired with larger latent models. So the future may not be about finding the single best tokenization scheme, but about developing architectures that can vary their internal granularity according to the needs of the input.

In that sense, BLT is more than a specific model. It is a demonstration that the right unit of computation does not have to be a token, and that once models are allowed to choose their own patch sizes, new scaling laws and new efficiency gains become available. The paper’s synthesis is therefore a broader one: the path to better language models may lie not in making tokens more sophisticated, but in reducing our dependence on them altogether.
