\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### Why does the paper say feed-forward layers, not attention, dominate the cost of byte-level Transformers at scale?
Question:
A lot of prior work on long sequences emphasizes that self-attention is expensive because it scales quadratically with sequence length. But the BLT paper says that at scale, for byte-level Transformers, the computational cost is dominated by large feed-forward layers that run on every byte rather than by attention.

I'm confused about when this becomes true.

Is the idea that once the model hidden size becomes large enough, the per-token multilayer perceptron cost grows so much that it exceeds the attention cost, even though attention has the $O(n^2)$ term? If so, could someone explain the rough scaling intuition in terms of sequence length $n$, hidden size $d$, and number of layers? I'm trying to reconcile this claim with the usual "attention is the bottleneck" story.
Answer:
Yes — the paper’s claim is basically about a change in the dominant term in the Transformer FLOP budget as you scale models, and the key point is that the relevant sequence length is not “raw bytes” if the architecture uses patches.

In a vanilla Transformer layer, self-attention has a cost that grows like $O(n^2 d)$, where $n$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the hidden size, while the feed-forward network (FFN) costs about $O(n d^2)$. The usual “attention is quadratic” story is true, but it only means attention gets worse faster as $n$ grows. It does not mean attention is always the largest term. If $d$ is large enough compared with $n$, then the $d^2$ factor in the FFN can dominate. Roughly speaking, attention dominates when $n \gtrsim d$, and the FFN dominates when $d \gtrsim n$. In modern large models, $d$ is often in the thousands, and the effective context length per layer may be of the same order or smaller, so the FFN can indeed be the bigger cost.

What BLT is emphasizing is that byte-level modeling makes the “long sequence” problem look different from the classic token-level story. If you processed every byte with a standard Transformer, then yes, the attention cost would explode because $n$ becomes very large. But BLT does not do that: it groups bytes into patches, and the main “latent transformer” runs over patches, not bytes. So the expensive global Transformer sees a much shorter sequence length $n_p$ (the number of patches), while the local byte-level modules are lightweight. That shifts the bottleneck away from attention over a huge byte sequence and toward the per-step cost of the large FFN layers in the main Transformer, which are executed on every patch step.

The paper makes this explicit in its FLOPs discussion: it counts the FLOPs of the feed-forward layers, the $qkvo$ projections, and the attention computation, and then notes that for BLT the average patch size is the main determinant of cost because it controls how many times the main Transformer is invoked. In other words, as patch size grows, the number of latent-transformer steps drops, so attention shrinks proportionally, but the FFN in the large latent Transformer is still very expensive each time it runs.

So the intuition is not “attention stops being quadratic.” It is “once you reduce the number of sequence steps enough, the $O(n d^2)$ FFN term can exceed the $O(n^2 d)$ attention term, especially at large model width.” For byte-level Transformers with patching, that happens sooner because the architecture is designed to avoid paying attention cost on every byte. This is why the paper says that at scale the computational cost is dominated by the large FFN layers running on every byte/patch, rather than by attention itself.
