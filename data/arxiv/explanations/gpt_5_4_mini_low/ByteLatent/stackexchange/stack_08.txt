\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### Why does the decoder need both the encoder byte states and the global patch states?
Question:
In the BLT architecture, the local decoder predicts bytes using cross-attention from byte representations to global patch representations, and the initial decoder states are set to the final encoder byte states $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$.

I'm trying to understand the division of labor here. If the global transformer has already processed patch representations autoregressively, why isn't that enough to decode the bytes in each patch? Why do we still need the local byte-level encoder states from the input side?

Is the idea that the encoder states preserve fine-grained local structure within each patch, while the global transformer mainly injects broader contextual information? If so, how should I think about this during autoregressive generation, where some bytes are observed and some are being predicted?
Answer:
Yes — that is essentially the right way to think about it.

In BLT, the global transformer and the local byte-level modules are doing different jobs at different resolutions. The global transformer operates on patch representations, so it is where the model spends most of its capacity on long-range, higher-level structure. But a patch representation is deliberately compressed: it is meant to summarize a chunk of bytes, not preserve every byte exactly. That is why the decoder still needs the local encoder states.

The key point is that the global model alone does not contain enough information to reconstruct a byte sequence at full resolution. A patch state $o_j$ says something like “this patch is about this content, in this context,” but it does not directly encode the exact byte-by-byte arrangement, spelling, or local orthographic details inside the patch. The local encoder, by contrast, processes raw bytes and produces hidden states $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$ that retain fine-grained byte-level structure. In the paper’s own terms, the local encoder’s role is to “efficiently map a sequence of input bytes into expressive patch representations,” while the local decoder “decodes a sequence of global patch representations into raw bytes.” So the encoder and decoder are not redundant: the encoder preserves and organizes local byte detail, and the global transformer injects broader contextual information at the patch level.

You can think of the architecture as an information bottleneck with a skip-like pathway. The patch transformer learns the abstract, expensive part of the computation, while the local decoder uses the byte-level hidden states as a scaffold for turning that abstract information back into concrete bytes. This is why the decoder cross-attention uses the global patch states as keys and values, but initializes its byte queries from the final encoder byte states. That initialization gives the decoder a byte-positioned representation of the sequence it is currently working on, and the cross-attention lets it inject the patch-level context that was computed globally.

This also explains the autoregressive generation setting. BLT must generate bytes incrementally, patch by patch. For the bytes inside the current patch, the model first decides, via patching, when the next patch boundary occurs. Then the local decoder predicts the bytes in that patch autoregressively, conditioning on previously decoded bytes and on the corresponding global patch representation. The observed prefix has already been encoded into local byte states and patch states; the future bytes are not known, so the decoder cannot rely on them. Instead, it reconstructs the current patch from the encoded prefix structure plus the contextual patch summary. In other words, the encoder provides the “where and what at byte resolution,” and the global transformer provides the “what does this mean in context.”

So the short answer is: the global patch states are not enough because they are compressed summaries, while the local encoder states preserve the fine-grained byte-level information needed to actually spell out the bytes. The decoder uses both because BLT is explicitly separating coarse contextual reasoning from fine local realization.
