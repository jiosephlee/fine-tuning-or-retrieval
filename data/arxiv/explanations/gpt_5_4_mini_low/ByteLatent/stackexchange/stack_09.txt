\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### How should I interpret the BLT FLOPs formula 'per byte' when the global transformer runs on patches?
Question:
The paper gives a FLOPs-per-byte expression for BLT that combines the local encoder, global latent transformer, local decoder, and cross-attention blocks. I'm struggling to interpret terms like

$$\text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m = n_{ctx}/n_p, V = 0) / n_p.$$

If $n_p$ denotes patch size, then $n_{ctx}/n_p$ is the number of patches in the context. But then dividing again by $n_p$ seems like converting from cost per patch to cost per byte.

Is that the right interpretation? More broadly, I am confused by the notation because in some places $n_p$ seems to mean number of patches and in others it seems to mean average patch size. Could someone rewrite the FLOPs expression in words and explain which terms scale with bytes, which with patches, and why larger average patch size reduces inference cost?
Answer:
Yes — the right way to read that term is “cost per patch sequence, divided by the average number of bytes per patch, to convert to cost per byte,” not “patches divided by patches.”

The key is that the paper is a bit overloaded in its notation. In the FLOPs section, $n_p$ is being used as the average patch size in bytes, while the number of patches in a context of $n_{ctx}$ bytes is $m = n_{ctx} / n_p$. So if the global transformer processes one patch per step, then the total global-transformer cost for a sequence is

$$
\text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m = n_{ctx}/n_p, V=0),
$$

and dividing by $n_{ctx}$ bytes gives FLOPs per byte. Since $n_{ctx} = m n_p$, dividing by $n_{ctx}$ is equivalent to dividing by $m n_p$. The paper writes the expression in a slightly compressed way by already using the context length in terms of patches and then normalizing by patch size. So the factor

$$
\text{Transf. FL}(\cdots)/n_p
$$

should be understood as “the per-patch transformer cost spread over the average $n_p$ bytes in each patch,” i.e. a conversion from per-patch to per-byte cost. It is not introducing an extra division by the number of patches.

Intuitively, this is exactly why larger patches reduce inference cost. The global latent transformer is the expensive part, and it runs once per patch, not once per byte. If the average patch size doubles, the number of global-transformer invocations per byte roughly halves, so the global-transformer contribution to FLOPs per byte drops roughly like $1/n_p$. That is the main scaling argument in the paper.

The same logic applies to the other terms. The local encoder and decoder operate on bytes, so their transformer FLOPs scale with byte-level context windows such as $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$, and are comparatively less sensitive to patch size. The cross-attention blocks sit in between: they connect bytes and patches, so their cost depends on both the number of bytes and the number of patches, hence the appearance of factors like $k/n_p$ and $r = n_p/k$. Those factors are bookkeeping for how many byte-side queries and patch-side key/value groups there are.

So, in words, the whole formula means: “total FLOPs per byte equals the global patch transformer cost per byte plus the byte-level encoder cost plus the byte-level decoder cost plus the encoder and decoder cross-attention costs, each normalized to a per-byte basis.” The only term that benefits most directly from increasing patch size is the global transformer term, because fewer patches means fewer expensive transformer steps.
