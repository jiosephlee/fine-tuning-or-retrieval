\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why does QLoRA still need backpropagation through the quantized base model if the base weights are frozen?
Question:
I'm trying to understand the training mechanics in the QLoRA paper. The paper says the pretrained model weights are frozen and only the LoRA adapter weights are updated, but it also says gradients are backpropagated "through" the 4-bit quantized model into the LoRA adapters.

What confuses me is: if the base weights $\mathbf{W}$ are not being updated, why do we need to backpropagate through them at all? Is the idea that we still need the Jacobian of the layer output with respect to the input activations, even though we do not need $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$? In other words, are the frozen quantized weights just part of the computational graph needed to compute gradients for $\mathbf{L}_1$ and $\mathbf{L}_2$?

An intuitive explanation of exactly which gradients are computed and which are skipped in QLoRA would help.
Answer:
Yes — the frozen quantized weights are still part of the computational graph, but only as a fixed transform that the gradients must pass through on their way to the trainable LoRA parameters.

The key point in QLoRA is that “frozen” does not mean “ignored during backpropagation.” It means the base weights $\mathbf{W}$ are not updated by gradient descent. But to update the LoRA matrices $\mathbf{L}_1$ and $\mathbf{L}_2$, the optimizer still needs the loss gradient to flow through the layer output. In the paper’s notation, a layer is

$$
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}}=\mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\operatorname{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}},c_2^{\text{k-bit}},\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}})+\mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}}.
$$

During the forward pass, the frozen base weights are dequantized from storage format to the computation format and used exactly like a normal matrix in the linear layer. During the backward pass, QLoRA does not compute or store gradients for $\mathbf{W}$, but it does need the derivative of the loss with respect to the layer’s activations so that the chain rule can reach $\mathbf{L}_1$ and $\mathbf{L}_2$. That is what the paper means by “backpropagating through the quantized pretrained model into LoRA.”

Intuitively, think of the frozen base model as a fixed highway and the LoRA adapters as the only roads you are allowed to build or modify. To know how to adjust those roads, you still need to know how traffic flows through the highway, because the adapter’s effect is added on top of the base model’s output. So the backward pass must propagate through the frozen layer to compute things like $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{X}}$ and then use that to obtain $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_1}$ and $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_2}$. What is skipped is $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$ and any optimizer state for $\mathbf{W}$.

The paper states this explicitly: “For parameter updates only the gradient with respect to the error for the adapters weights $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$ are needed, and not for 4-bit weights $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$. However, the calculation of $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$ entails the calculation of $\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$ which proceeds via ... dequantization from storage $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$ to computation data type $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$.”

So your interpretation is basically right: the quantized frozen weights are part of the computational graph only insofar as they are needed to compute the gradients for the LoRA adapters. The gradients through the base model are used as a conduit, not as an update signal for the base weights themselves.

A useful way to summarize QLoRA is:

- the base model weights are stored in 4-bit format and kept fixed;
- they are dequantized to BF16 for forward and backward computation;
- gradients are computed through those operations so the loss can be differentiated with respect to the LoRA parameters;
- only the LoRA parameters receive updates.

That is why QLoRA can train a very large model with much lower memory use: it avoids storing and optimizing gradients and optimizer states for the massive base model, while still using the base model’s full representational power during differentiation.

### How can 4-bit finetuning match 16-bit full finetuning if quantization already loses information?
Question:
The paper claims that QLoRA can recover the performance of 16-bit finetuning even though the base model is stored in 4-bit NF4 format. I thought quantization introduces irreversible approximation error, so I would expect some permanent loss relative to the original 16-bit model.

What is the correct way to think about this? Is the claim that the LoRA adapters learn a correction term $s\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$ that compensates for the quantization error in the frozen base model? Or is the idea that most of the information important for downstream finetuning survives 4-bit quantization anyway?

I am looking for a conceptual explanation of how "matching" 16-bit performance is possible despite lossy quantization.
Answer:
The key point is that QLoRA is not claiming that the 4-bit model is numerically equivalent to the original 16-bit model. It is claiming something weaker and more practical: after you freeze the 4-bit base model and train LoRA adapters on top of it, the resulting *task performance* can match the performance of a 16-bit finetuned model.

That distinction matters. Quantization is indeed lossy, so the base weights stored in 4 bits are only an approximation of the original pretrained weights. But finetuning does not need the exact pretrained model back. It only needs a parameterization that is still expressive enough to reach a good solution for the downstream task. QLoRA’s empirical claim is that, for the models and tasks they tested, 4-bit NF4 is accurate enough that the remaining quantization error does not prevent the adapters from finding a solution comparable to 16-bit finetuning.

Your proposed view, that the adapters learn a correction term like $s\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$ to cancel the quantization error, is partly right but a bit too literal. The adapters are not trained to invert quantization error directly. They are trained to reduce the downstream loss. Of course, in doing so they can compensate for some of the base model’s approximation error, but the learned low-rank update is better thought of as a task-specific adjustment around a slightly perturbed pretrained model. In other words, the adapter is absorbing whatever changes are needed to make the quantized model work well on the finetuning objective, not explicitly reconstructing the original 16-bit weights.

The other part of the answer is that a lot of the useful structure in pretrained weights survives 4-bit quantization, especially with the NF4 datatype the paper introduces. The paper emphasizes that pretrained weights are approximately normally distributed, and NF4 is designed to be information-theoretically well matched to that distribution. So the quantized base model is not an arbitrary coarse approximation; it is a carefully chosen one that preserves the bulk of the pretrained model’s information in a compact form. That is why the authors find NF4 substantially better than generic FP4 or Int4.

So the right intuition is this: 4-bit quantization does lose information, but not necessarily information that is crucial for downstream finetuning. QLoRA relies on two things at once: a quantization scheme that preserves the pretrained model well enough, and a trainable low-rank adapter that can recover the remaining gap for the downstream task. The paper’s result is empirical: with NF4 and sufficiently good LoRA placement, that combination is enough to match 16-bit finetuning performance on the benchmarks they tested.

One subtle but important caveat is that “matches 16-bit performance” here means on the evaluated tasks and setups, not as a universal theorem. The paper is careful to frame it as evidence from their experiments, not a guarantee that every task or every larger scale will behave identically.

### Why is NF4 called information-theoretically optimal only for normally distributed weights?
Question:
The paper introduces NF4 and says it is information-theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights. I understand that the quantization levels are based on quantiles of $N(0,1)$, but I do not understand what exactly is being optimized.

Is the optimization criterion minimizing expected quantization error under the assumption that the input values are distributed as a normal random variable? If so, what error metric is being minimized, such as mean squared error or something else?

Also, if the true weight distribution is only approximately normal, why does NF4 still seem to work well in practice? I would appreciate a more precise explanation of the "optimal" claim.
Answer:
In the paper, “information-theoretically optimal” is being used in a fairly specific sense, and it is not the same thing as “minimizes mean squared quantization error.”

What QLoRA/NF4 is optimizing is the use of the available $2^k$ codewords. The paper’s NormalFloat construction is based on quantiles of a standard normal distribution so that, after scaling, each quantization bin contains the same expected number of values from a normally distributed tensor. In other words, if the weights really are drawn from $N(0,\sigma^2)$ and you quantize by normal-quantile thresholds, the discrete symbol distribution becomes as close to uniform as possible. That matters because a quantized variable with uniform symbol probabilities has maximal entropy for a fixed alphabet size, so you are making the most efficient use of the $k$ bits available. That is the “information-theoretic” part.

So the claim is not “NF4 gives the smallest reconstruction error” in the classical signal-processing sense. A Lloyd-Max quantizer would be the natural object if the goal were to minimize expected squared error. NF4 is instead designed so that, under a normal source model, each code is used equally often, which is optimal from the standpoint of entropy / information usage. The paper explicitly ties this to “quantile quantization,” i.e. equal expected occupancy of each bin.

Why does this still work when weights are only approximately normal? Because the paper argues that pretrained weights are usually close to zero-centered normal, especially after blockwise normalization by the absolute maximum. NF4 is therefore a very good match to the empirical weight distribution, even if not exact. Also, in QLoRA the quantized base model is frozen and the trainable LoRA adapters can absorb some of the residual quantization error. So NF4 does not need to be perfect; it only needs to be good enough that the remaining error is small relative to what the adapters can correct.

So the short version is: NF4 is “optimal” for normally distributed weights in the sense that it makes the quantized symbols maximally informative by matching the code bins to the source quantiles, not in the sense of minimizing MSE. Its practical success comes from the fact that neural weights are often close to normal and from the robustness added by adapter finetuning.

### Why does NF4 use an asymmetric construction if the weight distribution is assumed to be symmetric around zero?
Question:
In the NF4 section, the paper says pretrained weights are usually zero-centered and approximately normally distributed, which suggests a symmetric distribution. But then the paper says that a symmetric $k$-bit quantization scheme does not represent zero exactly, so they construct an asymmetric data type by separately estimating quantiles for the negative and positive parts and then removing one duplicate zero.

I do not understand this design choice. If the underlying distribution is symmetric, why is an asymmetric codebook better? Is this purely a practical trick to ensure that $0$ is represented exactly, rather than something that improves approximation quality for nonzero values?

Could someone explain the tradeoff here more concretely?
Answer:
The key point is that the paper is not using an asymmetric codebook because it believes the weight distribution itself is asymmetric. It is using an asymmetric *representation* to get one very important special value exactly right: zero.

In NF4, the authors start from the assumption that pretrained weights are approximately zero-centered and normally distributed. For such a distribution, the information-theoretically natural idea is to place quantization bins so that each bin has equal probability mass. If you do that with a perfectly symmetric $k$-bit codebook, and $k$ is even, you run into a small but practical problem: the quantization levels come in symmetric pairs, so $0$ typically lies between two representable values rather than being representable exactly. In other words, symmetry forces the grid to “straddle” zero instead of landing on it.

That matters because exact zero is not just a mathematical nicety. In neural networks, zero occurs a lot: padding values, masked positions, and any weight or activation that has effectively been driven to zero. If zero is not representable, then even an actual zero gets quantized to a small positive or negative number, which introduces unnecessary error. So the paper’s solution is to keep the distribution-matching property of quantile quantization, but break the tie at zero by making the codebook slightly asymmetric: they estimate quantiles separately for the negative and positive halves, then merge them and remove one duplicate zero. This gives them a discrete zero-point while still using all $2^k$ codes.

So the asymmetry is best understood as a representational trick, not as a claim that the weights are asymmetrically distributed. It is a way to preserve the advantages of a symmetric normal-based codebook while fixing the one thing a strictly symmetric even-bit codebook cannot do: represent zero exactly.

As for whether this improves approximation of nonzero values, the answer is “slightly, but indirectly.” The main gain is exact zero and full utilization of the codebook. Any improvement for nonzero values comes from the fact that one code value is no longer wasted on a duplicated zero, so the remaining levels can be placed more effectively. But the paper’s stated motivation is primarily the exact zero representation, and the empirical advantage they report is that NF4 performs better than FP4 and Int4 overall.

### What exactly is being quantized in double quantization, and how does the bit accounting work?
Question:
I'm confused by the double quantization section. My current understanding is:

1. The weight blocks are quantized to 4-bit values using per-block constants $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$.
2. Those constants themselves are then quantized to 8-bit values, producing $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$, plus a second set of constants $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$.

But I do not fully follow how this leads to the memory estimate going from $32/64 = 0.5$ bits per parameter to
$8/64 + 32/(64 \cdot 256) = 0.127$ bits per parameter.

Can someone derive this carefully? In particular, why is the second term divided by $64 \cdot 256$, and what exactly are the block sizes at each level?
Answer:
What is being quantized in “double quantization” is not the weights themselves a second time, but the per-block quantization scales used by the first 4-bit quantization.

In the paper’s notation, the base model weights are first split into blocks of size $64$. Each block of weights gets its own scale $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$, and those scales are what let the 4-bit codebook use the available range effectively. Without double quantization, every block of $64$ weights needs one 32-bit scale, so the overhead is simply

$$
\frac{32}{64} = 0.5
$$

bits per parameter, because one 32-bit number is amortized over 64 weights.

Double quantization then quantizes those scales $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ themselves. The paper uses a second quantization level with block size $256$. That means you take groups of $256$ of the first-level scales and represent them using 8-bit values $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$, plus one second-level scale $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ for the whole group. So after double quantization, the storage cost for the scales is:

- $8$ bits for each first-level scale $c_2$,
- plus $32$ bits for one second-level scale $c_1$ per group of $256$ first-level scales.

Now the key point is the bit accounting must be done per original weight parameter. One first-level scale corresponds to one block of $64$ weights. So a group of $256$ such scales corresponds to

$$
64 \cdot 256
$$

weights in the original model. Therefore the second-level $32$-bit constant contributes

$$
\frac{32}{64 \cdot 256}
$$

bits per original parameter. That is why the total overhead becomes

$$
\frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64 \cdot 256}.
$$

Numerically,

$$
\frac{8}{64} = 0.125,\qquad \frac{32}{64\cdot 256} = \frac{32}{16384} \approx 0.00195,
$$

so the total is about

$$
0.125 + 0.00195 \approx 0.127
$$

bits per parameter.

Intuitively, the first term is the cost of storing an 8-bit scale for every 64 weights, and the second term is just the tiny overhead of storing the meta-scale that makes those 8-bit scales interpretable. The paper’s advertised savings, $0.373$ bits per parameter, is just the difference between the original $0.5$ overhead and this new $0.127$ overhead.

So the hierarchy is:

$$
\text{weights} \xrightarrow[\text{block size }64]{c_2} \text{4-bit weights + FP32 scales}
\xrightarrow[\text{block size }256]{c_1} \text{4-bit weights + FP8 scales + FP32 meta-scales}.
$$

The only thing being “double-quantized” is the scale tensor, not the model weights again.

### Why does QLoRA dequantize to BF16 for computation instead of computing directly in 4-bit?
Question:
The paper says QLoRA uses one storage datatype, typically NF4, and one computation datatype, typically BF16. During training, weights are dequantized to BF16 before the matrix multiplication.

I initially thought the whole point of 4-bit finetuning was to do computation in 4-bit as well, so this seems counterintuitive. If the forward and backward passes are still done in BF16, then where exactly are the memory savings coming from? Is it mostly from storing the frozen pretrained weights in 4-bit rather than from reducing arithmetic precision?

I would appreciate a breakdown of why 4-bit storage plus BF16 compute is the preferred design.
Answer:
The short answer is that QLoRA is saving memory mostly by compressing the *frozen base model weights*, not by doing all arithmetic in $4$-bit. The paper is very explicit about this: QLoRA “has one low-precision storage data type, usually $4$-bit NormalFloat, and one computation data type, usually BFloat16,” and “whenever a QLoRA weight tensor is used, we dequantize the tensor to BFloat16, and then perform a matrix multiplication in $16$-bit.”

That design is not a contradiction; it is the point of the method. In finetuning, you do not actually need to update the pretrained weights. You only need gradients for the small LoRA adapter matrices. So the base model can stay compressed in memory as $4$-bit weights, and only be expanded temporarily to BF16 when a layer is being used in the forward or backward pass. This gives you the big memory win where it matters most: the parameters of the pretrained model dominate the footprint. The paper’s own memory breakdown says that for a 7B LLaMA model, the $4$-bit base model alone consumes far more memory than the LoRA parameters, and that activation gradients are another major cost; the LoRA weights themselves are tiny by comparison.

Why not compute directly in $4$-bit? Because training is much more numerically sensitive than inference. The backward pass needs stable gradients, and low-bit arithmetic would introduce a lot of quantization noise exactly where you are trying to make small updates. QLoRA avoids that problem by keeping the *computation* in BF16, which is a good compromise: it is much more stable than $4$-bit, but still much cheaper than full FP32 training. In other words, the quantization is used as a storage strategy for the frozen model, not as a way to force all training math into low precision.

So the memory savings come from three places, in the paper’s framing. First, the pretrained weights are stored in $4$-bit instead of $16$-bit, which is the main reduction. Second, double quantization compresses the quantization constants too, shaving off extra bits per parameter. Third, paged optimizers help avoid memory spikes from optimizer state and checkpointing. The LoRA adapters themselves remain in BF16, but they are tiny, and the paper notes that aggressively shrinking them further would not help much because the base model and activations dominate memory anyway.

So yes, your intuition is right: QLoRA is not “doing the whole model in $4$-bit.” It is “storing the frozen model in $4$-bit and computing in BF16.” That is exactly why it works: you get most of the memory benefit from compression, without paying the quality penalty that would likely come from low-bit training arithmetic.

### Why does adding LoRA adapters to all linear layers matter more than increasing the LoRA rank $r$?
Question:
One result in the paper surprised me: they say that using LoRA on all linear transformer block layers is required to match full finetuning performance, while changing the rank $r$ does not matter much.

I would have expected the rank $r$ to be the main bottleneck, since low-rank adaptation seems limited by capacity. Instead, the paper suggests the coverage of where adapters are inserted matters more.

Is the intuition that adapting more locations in the network gives better control over the function, even if each individual adapter is low-rank? Why would this dominate the effect of making a few adapters wider? A conceptual explanation would help.
Answer:
Yes — that is essentially the intuition, and it is the one the paper is pointing to.

In QLoRA, the important question is not just “how many degrees of freedom does each adapter have?” but also “which parts of the transformer are allowed to change at all?” The paper finds that using the standard LoRA setup only on the query and value projections is not enough to recover full finetuning performance, while placing LoRA adapters on all linear transformer block layers is. At the same time, increasing the rank $r$ of those adapters has much less effect.

The reason is that rank mainly changes the capacity of each individual adapter, but coverage changes the set of computations that can be modified. A LoRA adapter on a linear layer changes that layer’s map by adding a low-rank update, so if you only adapt a couple of matrices, you are still leaving most of the network’s internal transformations frozen. In a transformer block, different linear layers play different roles: some control attention routing, some mix information across heads, and others reshape features in the MLP. If only query and value projections are trainable, the model can adjust attention behavior, but it cannot fully reshape how information is transformed and propagated through the block. Full finetuning changes all of those pieces together, so to match it with adapters you need adapters in all those places too.

Why doesn’t a larger $r$ fix this? Because making a few adapters “wider” only improves expressiveness inside those same few locations. It does not give you access to the missing transformations in the other layers. In other words, a higher-rank adapter gives you a richer correction to one matrix, but if the main limitation is that the network needs coordinated changes at many points, then extra rank in one or two spots is the wrong knob to turn. The paper’s experiments suggest that the bottleneck is mostly where adaptation happens, not how large each local update is.

There is also a practical reason this works well in QLoRA: the memory cost of LoRA parameters is tiny compared to activations and the $4$-bit base model. The paper explicitly notes that adapter weights are a small part of the training footprint, so adding adapters to more layers is cheap. That means you can spread a small amount of trainable capacity across the whole transformer rather than concentrating it in just a few projections. Empirically, that broad coverage is what lets QLoRA recover full $16$-bit finetuning performance.

So the short version is: rank controls the size of each correction, but putting adapters everywhere controls whether the model can correct all the right computations. In the paper’s setting, the second matters more than the first.

### Why are LoRA parameter gradients said to be less important for memory than activation gradients?
Question:
In the background section, the paper says that for LoRA finetuning, most of the training memory footprint comes from activation gradients rather than the trainable LoRA parameters themselves. They even give an example where LoRA weights are only 26 MB, while LoRA input gradients are 567 MB before checkpointing.

I am having trouble reconciling this with the usual idea that model size dominates memory use. Why do the activations and their gradients take so much more memory than the LoRA parameters here? Is this mainly because activations scale with sequence length, hidden size, and number of layers, whereas the adapter weights are tiny and shared across tokens?

A simple memory accounting explanation for why activations dominate in PEFT would be helpful.
Answer:
The short answer is that LoRA makes the *number of trainable parameters* small, but it does not make the *backward pass* cheap in terms of activation storage. In QLoRA, the base model weights are frozen and quantized, so you do not need to store optimizer states or weight gradients for the full model. However, during backpropagation you still need the intermediate activations from the forward pass in order to compute gradients for the LoRA layers. Those activations are large because they are proportional to batch size, sequence length, hidden size, and number of layers.

That is exactly why the paper says the memory footprint is dominated by activation gradients rather than LoRA parameters. The LoRA parameters themselves are tiny because each adapted linear layer adds only low-rank factors $L_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times r}$ and $L_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times o}$, with $r \ll h,o$. So the total number of LoRA weights is a small fraction of the base model, and in the paper’s example it is only $26$ MB. But to compute gradients for those weights, the backward pass has to retain and use the layer inputs $X$ for many tokens and many layers. Those “input gradients” are really the stored activations needed for backprop, and they can easily be hundreds of MB, as in the paper’s $567$ MB example before checkpointing.

Intuitively, the difference is between something that is learned once per layer and something that exists for every token at every layer. LoRA weights are shared across all positions in the sequence, so their memory cost depends mostly on model architecture and rank $r$. Activations, by contrast, scale with the current minibatch and sequence length, so a long sequence can inflate memory even when only a tiny adapter is trainable. This is why PEFT methods like LoRA can drastically reduce parameter and optimizer-state memory without eliminating the main bottleneck of training large transformers.

The paper also makes the role of gradient checkpointing explicit: it reduces the activation memory substantially, from $567$ MB to about $18$ MB per sequence in their example, which is why checkpointing matters so much in QLoRA. But even after that, the key point remains that adapter parameters are not the dominant term. In other words, once the base model is frozen and quantized, “model size” stops being the main memory driver; the dominant cost becomes the temporary per-token, per-layer activations needed for backpropagation.

### How do paged optimizers help with memory spikes from gradient checkpointing?
Question:
The paper says paged optimizers use NVIDIA unified memory to handle memory spikes that occur during training, especially with long sequences and gradient checkpointing. I thought gradient checkpointing was supposed to reduce memory usage, so I do not understand why it causes spikes.

What is the mechanism here? At what point in training does memory temporarily increase enough to cause out-of-memory errors? And why is paging optimizer states to CPU RAM a good solution if optimizer states are only needed during the update step?

I'm looking for a more operational explanation of what memory is resident on the GPU during forward pass, backward pass, and optimizer step in this setup.
Answer:
Gradient checkpointing reduces one kind of memory, but it does not eliminate all peak memory. In QLoRA, the paper’s point is that the dangerous spikes come from the *combination* of long sequences, checkpointing, and the fact that optimizer states still have to be brought onto the GPU at update time.

Operationally, during the forward pass the frozen base model is stored in $4$-bit form on GPU, but whenever a layer is used its weights are dequantized to $\mathrm{BF16}$ for the matrix multiply. Because of gradient checkpointing, many intermediate activations are *not* kept from the forward pass; instead, they are recomputed during backpropagation. That is the whole memory-saving trick. However, this means that during the backward pass the GPU is simultaneously holding the recomputation work, the current activations needed for backprop, the gradients for the LoRA adapters, and whatever temporary buffers the backward kernels need. For long sequences, those temporary activation-related buffers can still become large enough to create a short-lived peak, even though checkpointing lowered the average footprint substantially. The paper explicitly notes that with checkpointing the “input gradients” drop to about $18\ \mathrm{MB}$ per sequence, but the overall training memory is still dominated by activations, not by the tiny LoRA weights.

The “spike” that paged optimizers are meant to absorb is not that optimizer states are needed throughout the forward/backward pass. It is that, at the update step, the optimizer states must be accessed at the same time as the gradients are still present, and this can coincide with a transient peak in memory usage. In other words, the GPU does not just need memory for the model and the gradients; at the worst moment it may also need extra room for the optimizer’s moment estimates and update buffers. If the mini-batch has a long sequence length, that extra momentary demand can push the process over the GPU limit and cause an out-of-memory error.

Paged optimizers solve this by using NVIDIA unified memory to let the optimizer states live in a paged allocation. If the GPU runs short of memory, those pages are automatically evicted to CPU RAM and brought back only when the optimizer update actually needs them. That is a good fit here because optimizer states are indeed only needed at the update step, but they are *large* and they contribute to the peak precisely when memory is tight. By paging them out, QLoRA prevents the optimizer state from being the thing that tips a near-full training step into OOM.

So the short version is: gradient checkpointing lowers activation memory, but long-sequence training can still create temporary peaks during backward and especially around the optimizer step. Paged optimizers do not reduce the total amount of optimizer state; they simply keep it from competing with activations for scarce GPU memory at the exact moment when the peak occurs.

### Why do the authors say MMLU and chatbot benchmarks are partially orthogonal?
Question:
The paper argues that strong MMLU performance does not imply strong chatbot performance, and vice versa. For example, they say FLAN v2 finetuning does well on MMLU but poorly on Vicuna-style chatbot evaluation.

I think I understand this informally, but I am unsure how to interpret it scientifically. Does this mean the benchmarks are measuring genuinely different capabilities, or could it just be dataset overlap and style mismatch between finetuning data and evaluation data? In other words, is the result evidence of capability specialization, benchmark contamination effects, or something else?

How should I think about the relationship between instruction-tuning dataset choice and downstream benchmark performance in this paper?
Answer:
In the paper, “partially orthogonal” is best read as an empirical statement about these *particular benchmarks*, not as a strong theoretical claim that they measure completely unrelated abilities.

The authors’ main observation is that instruction-tuning dataset choice changes which evaluation you do well on. In their experiments, FLAN v2 finetuning gives strong MMLU results but comparatively weak Vicuna-style chatbot results, while other datasets such as OASST1 behave more like chatbot data and do better on Vicuna/OA but not necessarily on MMLU. So the point is not merely “more finetuning helps everything”; rather, the *kind* of supervision matters.

Scientifically, that suggests two things at once. First, MMLU and chatbot benchmarks are not identical measures of one single latent “instruction-following skill.” MMLU is a 5-shot multiple-choice test over academic and factual knowledge, whereas Vicuna/OA are free-form generative dialogue evaluations that reward helpfulness, conversational style, and response quality in open-ended contexts. Those are overlapping but not the same capabilities. Second, the paper does *not* argue that the difference is only contamination or dataset overlap. In fact, they explicitly say they checked for overlap between OASST1 and the Vicuna prompts and did not find it by fuzzy matching. So at least for that case, their explanation is not “the benchmark was memorized.”

The more plausible interpretation, and the one the paper itself leans toward, is “dataset suitability.” FLAN v2 is broad and very useful for tasks like MMLU-style knowledge and reasoning in a prompted format, but it is not as aligned with the interaction patterns that chatbot benchmarks reward. Conversely, chat-oriented datasets teach models to produce good conversational responses, but that does not automatically translate to strong multiple-choice benchmark performance. That is why the authors say that “strong MMLU performance does not imply strong chatbot performance and vice versa.”

So the right mental model is that the paper provides evidence for *partial specialization* induced by instruction-tuning data, with some shared underlying capability but different emphasis. It is not strong evidence that one benchmark is “wrong” or that the result is purely a contamination artifact. Rather, it is evidence that benchmark scores depend on whether the finetuning data matches the evaluation target. In the authors’ own words, the benchmarks are partially orthogonal because they probe different facets of model behavior, and current instruction-tuning datasets push the model toward one facet or the other.

### Is the reported '99.3% of ChatGPT' result actually a reliable claim about model quality?
Question:
The paper prominently states that Guanaco reaches $99.3\%$ of ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark, but later the authors also say there are wide confidence intervals, strong ordering effects in GPT-4 scoring, and only moderate agreement between GPT-4 and human evaluations.

This leaves me confused about how seriously to take the headline number. Is the relative-to-ChatGPT score mainly an artifact of the specific Vicuna evaluation protocol, rather than a robust measure of overall chatbot quality? If the authors themselves recommend Elo from pairwise judgments instead of absolute scores, should the $99.3\%$ figure be seen mostly as a benchmark-specific result?

I would like help reconciling the strong headline claim with the paper's later caveats about evaluation reliability.
Answer:
Yes: in the paper, the $99.3\%$ figure should be read as a **benchmark-specific headline result**, not as a universally reliable statement that Guanaco is “$99.3\%$ as good as ChatGPT” in any general sense.

The key point is that the paper itself is quite explicit about the limitations of the Vicuna-style absolute scoring protocol. The $99.3\%$ number comes from a very particular setup: GPT-4 is asked to assign each model a score out of $10$ on the Vicuna prompt set, and the model’s performance is then reported relative to ChatGPT’s score on that same protocol. That makes the number useful as a compact summary of how Guanaco behaved under that exact evaluation procedure, but it is not a measurement of some intrinsic, global chatbot quality.

The authors themselves immediately qualify the headline claim. They report wide confidence intervals and note that many models overlap in performance. More importantly, they observe a strong ordering effect: GPT-4 tends to give a higher score to whichever response appears earlier in the prompt. That means the absolute $0$--$10$ scores are not very stable, so small differences in the resulting percentage can be partly an artifact of prompt ordering rather than a robust quality gap. This is exactly why they recommend reporting the mean over both orders, and why they ultimately say the absolute-scale benchmark is not well grounded.

That is also why the paper shifts toward pairwise comparisons and Elo. Pairwise judgments are easier to interpret because they ask a simpler question: “Which answer is better?” rather than “How many points out of $10$ is this answer worth?” The authors argue that the latter suffers from unclear scale calibration, while Elo based on head-to-head judgments is more reliable for ranking models. In other words, they are not saying the $99.3\%$ figure is meaningless, but they are saying it is a weaker form of evidence than the pairwise tournament ranking.

So your intuition is right: the $99.3\%$ claim is best understood as a **Vicuna-benchmark result under a particular GPT-4 scoring protocol**, not as a robust, general-purpose estimate of chatbot quality. The paper’s own later caveats are not an afterthought; they are part of the interpretation of the result. The authors still use the number to support the claim that Guanaco is highly competitive on that benchmark, but they do not present it as a definitive or universally trusted measure. In fact, the paper’s broader conclusion is almost the opposite: current chatbot benchmarks, especially ones relying on absolute scores, are not trustworthy enough to settle fine-grained model comparisons, so pairwise Elo-style evaluation is preferred.

### What does it mean mathematically that quantizing with absmax rescaling is 'equivalent to rescaling the standard deviation'?
Question:
In the NF4 section, the paper says that after normalizing weights into the range $[-1,1]$ using absolute maximum rescaling, this is "equivalent to rescaling the standard deviation of the weight tensor to match the standard deviation of the $k$-bit data type."

This sentence confused me because absmax normalization uses the maximum magnitude, not the standard deviation. Those are different statistics, especially in finite samples.

So in what sense is the statement true? Is it only a heuristic statement assuming the tensors are sampled from the same zero-mean normal family up to scale? Or is there a more exact probabilistic argument connecting absmax normalization and matching the effective scale of the normal quantile codebook?

I would appreciate clarification on this specific point.
Answer:
In the paper, “equivalent to rescaling the standard deviation” is not meant as a literal identity between the sample maximum and the sample standard deviation. It is a shorthand for the fact that, in their NF4 construction, the only thing that matters is the overall *scale* of the weight distribution, and absmax normalization is just one way to estimate and remove that scale before applying a fixed normal codebook.

The key point is that NF4 is built from quantiles of a standard normal $N(0,1)$, then normalized into the range $[-1,1]$. So the codebook itself has a fixed shape and fixed spread. When a weight tensor is quantized blockwise, the tensor is first rescaled by a constant
\[
c = \frac{1}{\max |X|}
\]
or, in the Int8 example in the paper, by $127/\mathrm{absmax}(X)$. This maps the block into the target interval, after which the values are compared to the fixed NF4 codebook.

Mathematically, what this means is that the quantizer is of the form
\[
x \mapsto Q\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right),
\]
for some scale parameter $s$, followed by dequantization by multiplying back by $s$. In the paper’s implementation, that scale parameter is estimated by the block’s absolute maximum. If the block really were drawn from a zero-mean Gaussian family $X \sim N(0,\sigma^2)$, then all such blocks differ mainly by the single scalar $\sigma$; the quantiles are the same up to scale. In that sense, choosing the absmax scale is an estimate of the block’s effective standard deviation, and applying the fixed standard-normal codebook after that is equivalent to “normalizing away” the variance.

So the equivalence is statistical, not exact sample-by-sample equality. They are not claiming
\[
\mathrm{absmax}(X) = \sigma
\]
for a finite tensor. Rather, they are saying that if the weights are approximately zero-centered and Gaussian-shaped, then rescaling by absmax is a practical way to bring them into the same normalized coordinate system as the NF4 quantile table, whose entries were computed from $N(0,1)$. After this rescaling, the tensor and the codebook are matched in *shape* and *scale*.

That is also why the paper phrases this as “we can transform all weights to a single fixed distribution by scaling $\sigma$ such that the distribution fits exactly into the range of our data type.” The mathematical ideal is variance normalization; absmax is just the chosen blockwise estimator used to implement it efficiently. The statement is therefore best read as a heuristic/operational equivalence under the Gaussian-weight assumption, not as a rigorous identity between the two statistics.
