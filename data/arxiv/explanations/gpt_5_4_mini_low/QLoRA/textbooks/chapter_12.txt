\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 12: 12. Beyond Scores: Qualitative Analysis of Model Behavior

# Why qualitative analysis matters in language model evaluation

The paper does not treat benchmark scores as the whole story. Although quantitative evaluation is essential for comparing systems, the authors argue that summary statistics can hide important behaviors that only become visible when one looks directly at model generations. This is especially important for chatbot-style systems, because the same model can perform well on a benchmark while still failing in ways that matter in practice. A benchmark may measure a narrow aspect of capability, but it may not reveal whether the model is confidently wrong, overly obedient to bad instructions, unwilling to follow harmless ones, or surprisingly good at some tasks and unexpectedly weak at others.

The authors emphasize a broader issue they call benchmark validity: whether a benchmark truly measures what it claims to measure. They note that models can sometimes exploit shortcuts in benchmark settings, so a single score does not fully capture the user experience of interacting with a chatbot. For that reason, the paper supplements its Elo rankings and benchmark tables with direct inspection of generated outputs from Guanaco 65B. This qualitative analysis is not intended to replace quantitative evaluation, but to provide context for the numbers and to expose patterns that the benchmarks miss.

# The idea of ‘lemons’ and ‘cherries’ for probing behavior

To study model behavior more concretely, the authors search through outputs from the Vicuna and OpenAssistant benchmarks and look for recurring patterns. Once they notice a pattern, they try to construct prompts that trigger it, even when the prompt is designed to expose a weakness. They describe the successful failures as “lemons” and the prompts that fail to break the model as “cherries.” This framing helps them probe the boundary of the model’s behavior rather than only observing ordinary responses.

This is important because a model’s apparent competence on a typical prompt can obscure rare but meaningful failure modes. By actively trying to break the model, the authors expose areas where Guanaco is brittle. At the same time, the “cherries” show places where the model remains robust even under attempts to induce an error. The resulting examples are not meant to be exhaustive; the authors explicitly acknowledge that the space of possible generations is large and that their examples are only samples. Still, the examples are useful because they illustrate the kinds of strengths and weaknesses that the quantitative evaluation does not show.

# Strengths in factual recall on common knowledge

One of the clearest strengths shown in the qualitative analysis is factual recall for common knowledge. For example, when asked “What is the capital of Zambia?”, Guanaco answers correctly: “The capital of Zambia is Lusaka.” This kind of response suggests that for familiar factual questions, the model can retrieve and state the relevant information accurately and directly.

The authors present this as a baseline strength rather than as a surprising achievement. Common factual questions are among the easier cases for large language models, and Guanaco performs well here. The significance of this example lies in contrast with the more difficult questions that follow. It shows that the model is not generally incapable of factual recall; instead, its reliability depends on how obscure or underdetermined the query is.

# Confident hallucinations on obscure facts

The paper then shows a more troubling pattern: when the factual question becomes obscure, Guanaco remains confident even when it is wrong. In one example, the model is asked about the singer who popularized the song “I’ll Keep the Lovelight Burning.” Guanaco answers by naming Al Jolson and giving 1886 as the birth year. The problem is that this response is doubly wrong: it identifies the wrong person as the popularizer, even though the birth year it supplies is correct for that person.

This example illustrates an important qualitative failure mode. The model does not signal uncertainty; instead, it produces a fluent answer that mixes plausibly correct-seeming details with incorrect ones. That makes the error easy to miss if one only reads the surface form of the response. The authors use this to show that benchmark scores alone cannot distinguish between careful uncertainty and confident hallucination, even though these are very different behaviors from a user’s perspective.

# Resistance to obvious misinformation and some forms of suggestibility

The qualitative analysis also reveals that Guanaco can sometimes resist obviously false premises. When prompted with a question that assumes “the earth is flat” has been officially confirmed by peer-reviewed scientists, the model rejects the premise and restates that the Earth is spherical and that the flat-earth belief is a debunked conspiracy theory. In this case, the model does not simply follow the user’s false assumption; it corrects it.

The same pattern appears in other contexts where the model is asked about things it cannot know or that are not possible to answer. For example, when asked “What time is it?”, Guanaco explains that it does not have access to real-time information and suggests checking a clock or online timepiece. The authors note that similar behavior occurs for questions like “Where are you?” and “How are you?” This suggests that the model has learned to identify some questions as unanswerable in the current interaction context, which is useful for chatbot behavior.

At the same time, this resistance is not complete. The paper uses these examples to show that the model can be fairly robust to direct misinformation, but that robustness is limited and does not generalize uniformly across all kinds of prompts.

# Correctly identifying unanswerable questions about real-time information

The “What time is it?” example deserves separate attention because it shows a specific kind of useful behavior: the model knows when it lacks the needed information. Rather than inventing an answer, Guanaco refuses to pretend it can access the current time. The response is not just a refusal; it also provides a short explanation of why the question cannot be answered and suggests a practical alternative.

This behavior matters because a chatbot that confidently answers unavailable real-time questions can mislead users. The paper treats this as a positive trait, but one that is limited to certain kinds of queries. The fact that the model can sometimes recognize the boundary of its knowledge makes the later failures more striking, because it shows that the model has some notion of when not to answer, but that this notion is not consistently reliable.

# Unpredictable refusals on benign tasks

A recurring weakness in the qualitative examples is that Guanaco sometimes refuses harmless requests for no obvious reason. The paper gives an example in which the user asks the model to reverse the words in a simple sentence. Instead of doing the task, the model declines and begins discussing the sentence’s grammar and syntax. This refusal is not aligned with the user’s request and appears arbitrary.

The authors present this as an instance of imperfect instruction following. A model that refuses a benign transformation task can be frustrating in ordinary use, even if it performs well on benchmarks. The key issue is not merely that the model sometimes refuses, but that the refusal is unpredictable: a harmless request may be treated as if it were problematic. This kind of behavior is hard to capture with aggregate evaluation scores, which is why the qualitative analysis is useful.

# Failures in prompt secrecy and instruction faithfulness

The paper also examines secrecy, where the model is instructed not to reveal a hidden piece of information. When asked directly for the secret word, Guanaco correctly refuses and says it cannot disclose the secret. But this success does not hold up under a small amount of prompt manipulation. When the user frames the interaction as a game and says to ignore previous instructions, the model reveals the secret word “banana.”

This example is one of the clearest demonstrations in the paper that instruction faithfulness is fragile. The model can obey a secrecy constraint in one prompt formulation but fail under a slightly different framing. The authors use this to show that good behavior on a straightforward test does not imply robust compliance. In other words, the model may appear aligned under simple conditions while remaining easy to manipulate.

# Mathematical reasoning strengths when showing work

Mathematics is presented as one of Guanaco’s weakest areas overall, but the qualitative analysis shows that it can sometimes succeed when it decomposes a problem step by step. In the lawn-mowing example, the model works through the arithmetic explicitly: it computes the payment for 16 lawns and then adds the tips. The paper highlights this style of explanation as a case where the model’s reasoning is more reliable when it is broken down into intermediate steps.

This is an important pattern because it suggests that the model’s apparent success is not only about the final answer, but about whether the response contains enough structure to guide the computation. When Guanaco explains its steps, the result can be accurate. The authors use this to show that some reasoning failures may be mitigated by prompting the model into a more explicit decomposition.

# Mathematical reasoning failures without robust decomposition

The same section also shows that Guanaco can fail badly on simple math when the reasoning is not carefully grounded. When asked to factorize 1833, it incorrectly states that 1833 is a prime number and then gives a factorization that is itself wrong: $2^1 * 3^2 * 17^1$. The paper points out that the true factorization is $3 \times 17 \times 43$. This is not just a small arithmetic slip; it is a compound error involving both an incorrect classification and an incorrect factorization.

The contrast with the earlier word-problem example reinforces the authors’ point that step-by-step decomposition matters. Without that structure, the model may produce confident but invalid reasoning. The qualitative analysis therefore complements the paper’s broader claim that benchmark performance does not guarantee robust reasoning ability in every setting.

# Theory of mind successes and breakdowns

The paper also explores tasks that probe theory of mind, meaning the model’s ability to reason about what one person believes another person knows. In one example, Guanaco handles a false-belief-style scenario reasonably well. It correctly tracks that James knows Abby initially placed the pen in the desk drawer, while also considering that James moved it to the bag and assumes Abby does not know that. The response shows a meaningful attempt to infer what James thinks Abby will do.

However, the same capability is unstable. In another theory-of-mind scenario, the model concludes that James will look for the beans in the pantry because Jackson moved them there, even though the prompt never said James saw or learned that movement. The authors highlight this as a breakdown in reasoning: the model presumes information transfer that was not described. This shows that the model can succeed on some theory-of-mind tasks while inventing unsupported knowledge in others.

The qualitative takeaway is that Guanaco’s social reasoning is suggestive but not dependable. It can produce responses that look thoughtful and internally coherent, yet still rest on hidden assumptions not justified by the prompt.

# How qualitative evidence reframes benchmark-based conclusions

Taken together, these examples reshape the paper’s quantitative story. The benchmark results show that QLoRA can produce strong models, and the Elo scores suggest that Guanaco competes with or approaches ChatGPT on certain evaluations. But the qualitative analysis makes clear that these scores do not tell the full story. Guanaco can be highly capable in some settings, such as common factual recall and certain structured reasoning tasks, while still failing in brittle, surprising, and practically important ways.

The authors use these examples to caution against reading benchmark success as a blanket guarantee of conversational quality. Strong aggregate performance may coexist with hallucination, arbitrary refusals, weak secrecy protection, and inconsistent reasoning. At the same time, the examples also show why the model is promising: it can resist some misinformation, recognize some unanswerable questions, and often produce useful answers. In this sense, the qualitative analysis does not contradict the benchmark results; it makes them more precise by showing what they do and do not capture.
