\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 11: 11. Guanaco: Building a State-of-the-Art Open Chatbot with QLoRA

#
What Guanaco Is and How It Is Trained

Guanaco is the name the paper gives to its best family of chat-oriented models finetuned with QLoRA. It is not a separate training method; rather, it is the application of QLoRA to instruction-following and chatbot modeling at scale. The key idea is that the base language model is first quantized to 4-bit precision, and then training is performed only through low-rank adapter weights. In this way, the model can be finetuned efficiently while keeping the large pretrained weights frozen.

In the paper, Guanaco is trained on the OpenAssistant dataset variant OASST1. The training setup is deliberately simple: it uses cross-entropy loss with supervised learning, not reinforcement learning. This matters because it means the model’s gains are attributable to the combination of QLoRA and high-quality instruction data rather than to a more complex training recipe. The authors finetune models at multiple scales, including 7B, 33B, and 65B parameters, and they train adapters for the model families they study.

A major point of the chapter’s story is that Guanaco is the flagship demonstration that QLoRA can scale to very large models while remaining accessible. The paper emphasizes that the 33B and 65B Guanaco models are trained on a single GPU, and the largest model reaches state-of-the-art open chatbot performance in the authors’ evaluation. This is the practical proof that the paper’s memory-saving methods are not merely technical tricks; they enable a new class of experiments and models that would otherwise be out of reach.

#
Why OASST1 Finetuning Was Especially Effective

The paper’s strongest Guanaco models are trained on OASST1, and the authors argue that the quality and structure of this dataset are central to their success. OASST1 is a multilingual collection of crowd-sourced multiturn conversations between a user and an assistant. Unlike datasets that are simple instruction-response pairs, OASST1 contains conversation structure, which gives the model exposure to dialogue flow and response behavior in a more realistic chatbot setting.

The authors also note that OASST1 is one of several instruction datasets they study, and that data quality matters more than sheer dataset size. This is one of the paper’s broader findings: a smaller, cleaner dataset can outperform a much larger one if it better matches the target behavior. In their analysis, OASST1 performs especially well on chatbot-style evaluation compared with much larger instruction corpora that are less aligned with conversational performance.

Another reason OASST1 is notable is that it was used in a setting designed for chatbot development but not necessarily for reinforcement learning in the Guanaco experiments. The paper’s use of OASST1 shows that strong conversational models can emerge from supervised finetuning alone when the data is suitable. This reinforces a recurring theme of the paper: the choice of finetuning dataset is often more important than increasing size indiscriminately.

#
Performance of Guanaco 7B, 33B, and 65B

The paper reports a clear scale trend across Guanaco models. The 7B model is the smallest and most deployable, but it still performs impressively relative to older open models. According to the paper, the 7B Guanaco model requires only about 5 GB of memory when deployed and outperforms Alpaca 13B by more than 20 percentage points on the Vicuna benchmark. That result is important because it shows that QLoRA does not merely make large models feasible; it also improves the practical usefulness of smaller ones.

The 33B Guanaco model is especially important because it becomes competitive with the strongest open chatbot systems of the time while remaining much more memory efficient than full-precision alternatives. The paper states that Guanaco 33B uses 4-bit precision for its weights and requires 21 GB of memory, compared with 26 GB for Vicuna 13B. Even though the 33B model is much larger in parameter count, the quantized representation keeps its footprint small enough to run on a single consumer GPU class device in the paper’s training regime.

The 65B model is the strongest Guanaco system. It is the model that the authors identify as the best-performing open-source chatbot in their evaluations. Its performance is the one that comes closest to ChatGPT in the Vicuna benchmark, and it is also the one that receives the strongest Elo-based ranking among the Guanaco models. The paper uses this result to argue that QLoRA makes it possible to train very large, high-performing chatbots without the usual memory barrier.

#
Relative Performance to ChatGPT on the Vicuna Benchmark

The Vicuna benchmark is the paper’s most visible chatbot evaluation. It compares model outputs against ChatGPT-style responses using GPT-4 or human judgments, and the paper reports performance relative to ChatGPT’s score. In this setting, Guanaco 65B achieves 99.3% of ChatGPT’s performance, which the authors present as essentially closing the gap on this benchmark. Guanaco 33B also performs very strongly, reaching 97.8% of ChatGPT’s level in the paper’s reported comparisons.

The paper is careful, however, to point out that the Vicuna benchmark has wide confidence intervals and some uncertainty in absolute scoring. Because of this, the authors recommend pairwise comparison and Elo ranking as a more stable way to compare models. Even so, the headline result remains striking: a finetuned open model trained with QLoRA on one GPU can approach the benchmark performance of a commercial closed model.

This relative performance is important not just because of the numbers, but because of what they imply. ChatGPT is treated as the reference point for strong conversational ability, and Guanaco’s near-match on Vicuna suggests that the combination of efficient quantized finetuning and the right dataset can yield results that are competitive with much larger, proprietary systems. The chapter’s central lesson is that memory-efficient finetuning can translate into real benchmark gains, not just engineering convenience.

#
Memory Footprint and Deployment Efficiency of Guanaco Models

One of the most concrete achievements in the paper is the reduction in memory needed to finetune and deploy large chat models. The paper emphasizes that regular 16-bit finetuning of a 65B model would require more than 780 GB of GPU memory, which is far beyond the reach of a single machine. QLoRA reduces this to under 48 GB, making single-GPU finetuning feasible.

That efficiency carries over to the Guanaco family. The smallest Guanaco model, 7B, is reported to require only about 5 GB of memory when deployed. The 33B model requires about 21 GB, which is still much lower than what full-precision finetuning or deployment would demand. These numbers matter because they show that the same method that enables training also makes practical deployment more realistic.

The paper’s deployment discussion is not just about inference convenience. It is part of a broader argument that quantization plus adapters can make advanced language model development accessible to researchers with limited hardware. Guanaco is therefore not only a strong chatbot, but also an example of how a strong chatbot can be built and used under tight memory constraints. The result is a model family that fits the paper’s goal of broadening access to state-of-the-art NLP systems.

#
Comparison to Alpaca, Vicuna, Open Assistant, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Bard

The paper compares Guanaco against both open and commercial systems. Among open models, it includes Alpaca, Vicuna, and Open Assistant. Among commercial systems, it includes GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4, and Bard. These comparisons help locate Guanaco within the landscape of available chat models rather than treating it as an isolated result.

Compared with Alpaca, Guanaco is substantially stronger on the Vicuna benchmark. The paper specifically notes that the 7B Guanaco model, despite being compact, beats Alpaca 13B by more than 20 percentage points on Vicuna. Compared with Vicuna itself, Guanaco 33B and 65B are stronger in the authors’ evaluations and are more memory efficient because they use 4-bit weights. Compared with Open Assistant, Guanaco’s best models do better in the paper’s pairwise and Elo evaluations, especially on the Vicuna benchmark.

Against commercial systems, the picture is more nuanced but still impressive. Guanaco 65B reaches 99.3% of ChatGPT’s Vicuna performance, and the authors report that Guanaco 33B and 65B have an expected win probability of about 30% against GPT-4 in their human pairwise comparisons. Bard is also part of the comparative set, though the paper’s main emphasis is on the GPT-based systems. The overall conclusion is that Guanaco is not merely competitive with earlier open models; it reaches into the performance range of the strongest commercial chatbots in the benchmark framework used by the paper.

#
Why Guanaco 33B and 65B Stand Out Among Open Models

The 33B and 65B Guanaco models stand out because they combine scale, efficiency, and strong chatbot performance. In the paper’s evaluations, these models are the ones that most consistently rank near the top of the open-source field. Their strength is especially visible in the Vicuna and OA benchmarks, where the larger Guanaco models outperform all systems except GPT-4 in the Elo rankings presented by the authors.

The reason they stand out is not just that they have more parameters. The paper repeatedly emphasizes that the QLoRA recipe matters: 4-bit NF4 quantization, double quantization, and adapters placed broadly across the network are all part of what allows these larger models to retain quality after quantization. The result is not simply “a bigger model in lower precision,” but a model whose training procedure has been engineered to preserve the performance of standard finetuning.

Another key factor is that these models benefit from the dataset choice. OASST1 appears to be particularly effective for chatbot behavior, and this advantage compounds with scale. In the paper’s analysis, the 33B and 65B Guanaco models show that the combination of a strong conversational dataset and efficient finetuning can produce results that are difficult to match with older open-source chatbots.

#
Expected Win Probabilities Against Stronger Systems

The paper uses Elo ratings to summarize pairwise judgments between model outputs. In that framework, Guanaco 65B and 33B are compared against stronger systems such as ChatGPT and GPT-4. The authors report that when compared to GPT-4, these Guanaco models have an expected win probability of 30% based on human-annotator pairwise comparisons. That is a very strong result for an open model, because it means the model is not merely weakly competitive but wins a meaningful fraction of the time.

The Elo framing is important because it avoids relying only on absolute score judgments, which the paper finds to be noisy and sensitive to prompt order. By turning comparisons into tournament-style matches, the authors produce a ranking that better captures relative strength. In that ranking, Guanaco 65B is among the highest-rated open systems, and the paper treats the 30% expected win rate against GPT-4 as evidence of real competitiveness.

At the same time, the paper is careful not to overclaim. A 30% expected win probability still means that GPT-4 wins more often. The result indicates narrowness, not dominance. But in the context of open-source model development, the authors present it as a major milestone: an openly finetuned model trained with QLoRA can enter the same competitive space as the leading commercial systems.

#
The Significance of Achieving Strong Performance Without Proprietary Data

A particularly important claim in the paper is that Guanaco is the only top-performing model in their evaluation that is not trained on proprietary data. The authors note that the OASST1 collection guidelines explicitly forbid the use of GPT models, which makes Guanaco’s strong performance especially notable. In contrast, several of the strongest competing systems in the comparison either use proprietary data directly or are distilled from proprietary systems.

This matters because it changes the interpretation of the results. Guanaco is not strong because it borrowed from a closed model during training. It is strong because a high-quality open dataset, combined with QLoRA finetuning, was enough to produce a model that can compete at a very high level. That is one of the paper’s central contributions to open model development: strong performance does not require privileged access to proprietary data if the training and evaluation pipeline is carefully designed.

The paper also contrasts Guanaco with the next best open-only model, which the authors identify as the Anthropic HH-RLHF model, and note that it trails Guanaco by 30 percentage points on the Vicuna benchmark. That gap reinforces the significance of the result. Guanaco is not only strong in absolute terms; it is strong relative to other open models trained without proprietary sources.

#
What the Guanaco Results Demonstrate About QLoRA’s Practical Impact

The Guanaco experiments are the clearest demonstration of QLoRA’s practical impact. The paper’s technical contributions on quantization and paging matter because they enable the training regime that makes Guanaco possible. Without 4-bit finetuning, the 33B and 65B experiments would be infeasible on the hardware described in the paper. With QLoRA, they become routine enough to support broad experimentation across more than 1,000 trained models.

The Guanaco results also show that efficiency is not merely about saving resources; it can change the direction of research. Because QLoRA drastically lowers memory use, the authors are able to study which datasets matter, how evaluation should be done, and how chatbot performance scales. That broader empirical program would not be possible if the method only shaved costs without preserving quality. Guanaco demonstrates that the quality is retained.

Finally, the Guanaco family serves as the paper’s strongest evidence that accessible finetuning can produce state-of-the-art open chatbots. The models are strong, relatively small in memory footprint, and trainable on a single GPU. They also illustrate the paper’s larger argument: when combined with careful quantization and a good dataset, parameter-efficient finetuning can close much of the gap between open-source and commercial chat systems. Guanaco is therefore both a model family and a proof of concept for what QLoRA enables in practice.
