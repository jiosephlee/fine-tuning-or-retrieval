\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why does the paper claim that models without memory mixing cannot solve tasks like parity?
Question:
In the synthetic experiments, the authors say that Transformers and State Space Models without memory mixing cannot solve regular grammar tasks like parity, and they connect this to state tracking. I thought parity is a very simple regular language, so I am puzzled by the claim that powerful sequence models cannot solve it.

Is the issue not expressivity in the abstract sense, but rather length extrapolation under the specific architectures and training setup? Or do the cited results actually show a stronger theoretical limitation for these model classes when using finite precision or fixed-size continuous states?

I would like help reconciling the intuition that parity only requires one bit of state with the paper's statement that memory mixing is necessary here.
Answer:
The key to reconciling the statement with the fact that parity is “just one bit” is that the paper is not claiming an abstract impossibility in the sense of ordinary automata theory. It is talking about whether these architectures, as used in practice, can learn and extrapolate a *state-tracking algorithm* for formal-language tasks such as parity under finite model size, finite precision, and the training regimes in the experiments.

Parity is indeed a regular language and a deterministic finite automaton with two states can compute it. So in principle, a sequence model with enough carefully used state could represent it. But the paper’s point is that this requires the model to maintain a persistent internal state that is updated step by step according to the current input symbol and the previous state. That is exactly what the authors mean by “memory mixing”: the hidden state at time $t$ must depend nontrivially on the hidden state at time $t-1$, so that the model can implement transitions like “flip the parity bit when you see a $1$.”

In the paper’s terminology, models such as Transformers or State Space Models “without memory mixing” do not have the right kind of hidden-hidden recurrence to do this kind of state tracking robustly. They may aggregate context, but they do not naturally implement the kind of mutable finite-state controller needed for regular-grammar tasks. That is why the paper says they “cannot solve, e.g., regular grammars like the parity task” in their synthetic benchmark: the issue is not that parity is too hard in principle, but that these architectures, as trained and parameterized here, fail to learn the correct algorithm and fail to extrapolate it to longer lengths.

This matches the broader literature the paper cites, where “state tracking” is presented as a distinguishing capability of RNN-style models with recurrent hidden state. The paper’s claim is therefore best read as a statement about *learned, length-extrapolating computational behavior* under practical constraints, not a theorem that parity is outside the expressivity of all continuous-state models. In fact, a fixed-size continuous state with infinite precision can often encode surprisingly much, but that is not the regime these experiments are testing. Finite precision and finite training typically matter a lot, and the paper is arguing that memory mixing gives the model the right inductive bias to use its state as an actual automaton state rather than as a passive summary.

So the short version is: parity is simple as a language, but hard for these architectures to *realize as a stable, length-generalizing state transition rule* unless they have memory mixing. That is why the authors connect it to state tracking rather than to “expressivity” in the purely abstract sense.
