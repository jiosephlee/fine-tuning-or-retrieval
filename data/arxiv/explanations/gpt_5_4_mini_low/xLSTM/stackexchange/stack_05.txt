\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why is the mLSTM update called a covariance update rule if it looks like an outer product without centering?
Question:
In the mLSTM section, the paper says the memory update uses the covariance update rule

$ \mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top $,

and then mentions that inputs are assumed to be layer-normalized so keys and values have zero mean.

I am confused because in statistics covariance usually involves centering, e.g. something like $(x - \mu_x)(y - \mu_y)^\top$, often with averaging as well. Here I only see a running sum of outer products.

Is the terminology historical from associative memory literature, where "covariance rule" means using outer products under a zero-mean assumption? If so, how important is the zero-mean assumption for the retrieval properties claimed in the paper?
Answer:
Yes — in this paper “covariance update rule” is being used in the older associative-memory / fast-weights sense, not in the strict statistical sense of computing an empirical covariance matrix from centered samples.

The key point is that the mLSTM stores associations with an outer product,
$$
\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
$$
and then retrieves by multiplying the query with this matrix. This is exactly the kind of Hebbian-style, fast-weight update that appears in the classic BAM / fast weights literature cited by the paper. In that literature, “covariance rule” usually means that the weights store correlations between key and value directions, as opposed to a plain “correlation” or “Hebbian” rule that may be biased by nonzero means. So the terminology is historical, but it is not arbitrary: the paper is pointing to the fact that, under the right preprocessing assumptions, the outer product behaves like a covariance-like association operator.

Why does centering matter? In ordinary statistics, covariance is something like
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}\big[(x-\mu_x)(y-\mu_y)^\top\big].
$$
If $\mu_x=\mu_y=0$, this reduces to $\mathbb{E}[xy^\top]$. That is exactly why the paper says that the inputs are layer-normalized before projection to keys and values: the zero-mean assumption makes the raw outer product a reasonable proxy for covariance, because there is no mean term to subtract. Without zero-mean inputs, the memory would also encode mean biases, which creates extra interference between stored items and hurts retrieval.

So the answer is: the paper is not claiming that $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ is a covariance matrix in the textbook statistical sense at each step. Rather, it is using the covariance-update terminology from associative memory theory, where a zero-mean assumption makes the outer product the right object to accumulate.

As for how important the zero-mean assumption is: it is important for the clean retrieval story, but it is not a fragile mathematical identity that must hold exactly. The paper’s retrieval argument is that, with zero-mean keys and values, the stored associations are maximally separable in the sense of Dayan’s result cited there, equivalently giving a better signal-to-noise ratio. Intuitively, when keys and values are centered, irrelevant cross-terms tend to cancel over time, so the query $\mathbf{q}_t$ can recover the intended value with less interference from other stored pairs. If the mean were not near zero, the memory would accumulate a strong common component that acts like structured noise.

So the zero-mean assumption is not just cosmetic; it is what makes the update behave like a covariance rule and supports the retrieval properties the paper emphasizes. But the mechanism itself is still fundamentally an outer-product fast-weight memory.
