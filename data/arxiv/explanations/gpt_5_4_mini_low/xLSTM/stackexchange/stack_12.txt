\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why does xLSTM[1:0] sometimes outperform xLSTM[7:1] on PALOMA even though the paper motivates adding sLSTM blocks?
Question:
The paper motivates combining mLSTM and sLSTM so that matrix memory improves capacity while sLSTM contributes memory mixing and state tracking. However, later results say that xLSTM[1:0] performs better than xLSTM[7:1] on PALOMA language tasks, even though xLSTM[7:1] includes some sLSTM blocks.

Does this mean the sLSTM blocks mainly help on specific algorithmic or state-tracking tasks but can hurt pure language modeling on some distributions? Or is it more likely that the mixture ratio and placement of sLSTM blocks were not yet optimized?

I am trying to understand whether the paper's message is "hybrid is best in general" or rather "mLSTM is strongest for language modeling, while sLSTM adds capabilities on particular tasks."
Answer:
Not really a contradiction. The paper is actually quite careful about separating two claims: that sLSTM adds a useful capability, and that this capability always improves every language-modeling benchmark. It does not claim the latter.

The key point is that the two variants are designed for somewhat different roles. The paper presents sLSTM as the component that introduces exponential gating plus memory mixing, and explicitly says this is what helps with “state tracking problems” and similar tasks where the model must revise or maintain discrete internal state. In contrast, mLSTM is the part meant to increase storage capacity via the matrix memory, and the paper emphasizes that this is especially important for language modeling and associative recall. So the intuition is not “sLSTM always helps language modeling,” but rather “sLSTM gives the model an extra ability that may matter on some tasks, while mLSTM gives stronger storage.”

That is exactly consistent with the empirical results you quoted. On PALOMA, the paper reports that $x\mathrm{LSTM}[1\!:\!0]$ performs better than $x\mathrm{LSTM}[7\!:\!1]$. Since PALOMA is a suite of next-token prediction benchmarks across many text domains, it is entirely plausible that the additional sLSTM blocks do not help there, and may even slightly hurt under the particular architecture/training setup used. The paper itself gives a strong clue for this interpretation in the limitations section: it says they “did neither fully optimize the architecture nor the hyperparameters, especially for larger xLSTM architectures,” and that “an extensive optimization process is needed for xLSTM to reach its full potential.” So the ratio and placement of sLSTM blocks were not presented as fully tuned.

At the same time, the paper does not say sLSTM is useless for language modeling. In the 300B-token experiments, both $x\mathrm{LSTM}[1\!:\!0]$ and $x\mathrm{LSTM}[7\!:\!1]$ are reported as the best models on validation perplexity for all sizes. So the hybrid can be competitive overall, but the PALOMA result suggests that the benefit is not uniform across all language distributions and metrics. A reasonable reading is therefore: mLSTM is the stronger default for pure next-token prediction, while sLSTM adds modeling power for tasks that require memory mixing or explicit state tracking, and its advantage may only show up on certain distributions or when the hybrid is better tuned.

So the paper’s message is closer to “the hybrid architecture broadens the model’s capabilities, and xLSTM as a family is strong for language modeling,” rather than “adding some sLSTM blocks monotonically improves every language benchmark.” The PALOMA result is best understood as an ablation outcome, not as a refutation of the motivation for sLSTM.
