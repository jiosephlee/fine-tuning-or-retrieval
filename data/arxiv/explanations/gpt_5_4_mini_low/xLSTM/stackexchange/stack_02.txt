\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### What exactly is the normalizer state $n_t$ in sLSTM doing, and why is $c_t / n_t$ the right quantity to output?
Question:
The sLSTM forward pass introduces a second recurrence

$ n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t $

and then uses

$ h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t, \quad \tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t $.

The paper says that $n_t$ "sums up the product of the input gate times all future forget gates," but I am having trouble seeing why this is the correct normalization.

If I unroll the recurrence, $c_t$ looks like a weighted sum of past inputs $z_j$, and $n_t$ looks like the same weighted sum but with each $z_j$ replaced by $1$. So is $c_t / n_t$ basically a weighted average of past content? If that is the intended interpretation, why is a weighted average better than the usual LSTM behavior where the cell state is left unnormalized and only squashed by something like $\tanh$?

I would appreciate either a derivation or a simple concrete example.
Answer:
Yes — in the sLSTM, $n_t$ is exactly a normalization accumulator, and your intuition is right: after unrolling, $c_t / n_t$ behaves like a gated, decayed average of the past content values $z_j$. The key point is that the sLSTM does not want to store an unnormalized sum anymore, because with exponential input gates the raw cell state can grow or shrink by large factors. The paper’s “normalizer state” is there to make the hidden state depend on the *relative* contributions of past inputs, not on their absolute scale.

To see this, unroll the recurrences
\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \qquad n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t.
\]
Assuming for simplicity that the initial states are $c_0 = 0$ and $n_0 = 0$, repeated substitution gives
\[
c_t = \sum_{j=1}^t \left( i_j \prod_{k=j+1}^t f_k \right) z_j,
\qquad
n_t = \sum_{j=1}^t \left( i_j \prod_{k=j+1}^t f_k \right).
\]
So both $c_t$ and $n_t$ use the same weights
\[
w_j = i_j \prod_{k=j+1}^t f_k,
\]
except that $c_t$ weights each past content $z_j$, while $n_t$ weights a constant $1$. Therefore
\[
\frac{c_t}{n_t}
=
\frac{\sum_{j=1}^t w_j z_j}{\sum_{j=1}^t w_j},
\]
which is exactly a weighted average of past contents. So your interpretation is correct.

Why is this better than the usual LSTM form? In a standard LSTM, the cell state is an unnormalized sum and then squashed by something like $\tanh(c_t)$. That works when gates are bounded in $[0,1]$, but xLSTM deliberately uses exponential gates, $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and possibly $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$, to give the model more expressive multiplicative control over memory. Once you allow those gates to vary over a much wider range, the scale of $c_t$ itself becomes less meaningful: one sequence may produce a huge $c_t$ simply because all gates were large, even if the *content* being represented is essentially the same as another sequence with smaller gates. Dividing by $n_t$ removes that arbitrary scale and turns the memory into a scale-invariant “summary of what was stored.”

A useful intuition is to think of $n_t$ as tracking the total effective “mass” of memory currently present. Then $c_t$ tracks the total mass times content, and $c_t/n_t$ gives the average content per unit mass. Without this division, the hidden state would conflate two things: what the model remembers and how strongly it happened to write it. The paper explicitly says the normalizer “keeps record of the strength of the gates,” which is exactly this idea.

A concrete example makes the difference clear. Suppose at time $t$ the model has seen two pieces of content, $z_1 = 10$ and $z_2 = 0$, with weights $w_1 = 1$ and $w_2 = 9$. Then
\[
c_t = 1 \cdot 10 + 9 \cdot 0 = 10, \qquad n_t = 1 + 9 = 10,
\]
so
\[
c_t / n_t = 1.
\]
That says the stored state is “mostly like the second input,” which is reasonable because the second write was much stronger. If you instead output $c_t$ directly, the value $10$ mixes content and confidence in a way that is harder for the next layers to interpret.

This is also why the paper pairs the normalizer with stabilization via the extra state $m_t$: because exponential gates can explode, they rewrite the gates in a numerically safe form without changing the network output. The normalization by $n_t$ is part of the same theme: make the exponential-gated recurrence behave like a well-scaled, content-based memory readout rather than an unbounded accumulator.

So the short answer is: $n_t$ is not an extra memory channel with independent semantics; it is the denominator that turns the cell state into a normalized weighted average. In the sLSTM, $c_t / n_t$ is the right output because the model’s exponential gates make the absolute magnitude of $c_t$ unreliable as a representation of content, while the ratio captures the content itself in a gate-scale-invariant way.
