\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### What is the role of the denominator $\max\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \}$ in mLSTM retrieval?
Question:
The mLSTM hidden state is computed as

$ \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \}. $

I understand that $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ retrieves a weighted combination of stored values, but I do not understand the exact purpose of dividing by $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ (with absolute value and lower bound 1).

Is this acting like a normalization by the total matching mass, similar to the denominator in attention? If yes, why is the normalizer based on the weighted sum of keys $\mathbf{n}_t$ rather than something like a sum of positive similarities? Also, why is taking $|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|$ theoretically sensible if the sign itself seems meaningful?

I would appreciate an interpretation of this denominator in relation to linear attention or retention.
Answer:
Yes — in the paper this denominator is exactly a normalization term, and it plays the same broad role as the denominator in attention or in linear-attention-style retrieval, but adapted to the way mLSTM stores information.

If you expand the matrix memory update
$$
\mathbf{C}_t=\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top,
$$
then retrieval with a query $\mathbf{q}_t$ gives
$$
\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t
$$
which is a sum of stored values $\mathbf{v}$ weighted by their similarity to $\mathbf{q}_t$, namely by $\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q}_t$, and also by the accumulated gating factors from the forget/input dynamics. So, just like in attention, the raw retrieved vector can grow or shrink depending on how much “matching mass” is present. The normalizer
$$
\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}
$$
corrects for that scale.

The key point is that $\mathbf{n}_t$ is not an arbitrary extra vector: the paper defines it as
$$
\mathbf{n}_t=f_t\mathbf{n}_{t-1}+i_t\mathbf{k}_t,
$$
so it is the running, gate-weighted sum of keys. In other words, $\mathbf{n}_t$ records how much key mass has been stored in the memory. Then $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ acts like the total compatibility between the current query and all stored keys, i.e. the analogue of the attention denominator. This is why the paper says the normalizer state “keeps record of the strength of the gates.”

Why not sum only positive similarities? Because mLSTM is not explicitly computing a softmax over positive attention scores. Its retrieval is linear, based on outer-product memory, so the natural normalization is the accumulated key-query overlap induced by the same state that built the memory. In that sense, $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ is the linear-attention/retention-style analogue of the denominator: it measures the total effective weight of the retrieved content without requiring an explicit nonlinear attention distribution.

The absolute value and the lower bound by $1$ are there for stability, not for semantic modeling. The paper explicitly notes that the dot product can be close to zero, so dividing by it could blow up the hidden state. Taking $|\cdot|$ makes the scale factor nonnegative, and $\max(\cdot,1)$ prevents extremely small denominators. The sign is not meant to be carried by the normalizer; any directional information is already in $\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t$. The denominator’s job is only to keep the retrieval magnitude well-behaved, much like normalization in attention keeps outputs comparable across different amounts of retrieved evidence.

So the short interpretation is: $\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t$ retrieves the content, while $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$ estimates and stabilizes the amount of retrieved mass. This makes mLSTM behave more like a normalized linear memory readout than an unscaled associative sum.
