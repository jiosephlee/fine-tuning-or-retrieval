\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why does removing hidden-to-hidden memory mixing make mLSTM parallelizable, but not sLSTM?
Question:
The paper says that mLSTM is fully parallelizable because it abandons memory mixing, while sLSTM is not parallelizable due to hidden-hidden connections. I am trying to understand exactly what recurrence structure prevents parallelization.

In sLSTM, the pre-activations depend on $h_{t-1}$ through terms like $r_z h_{t-1}, r_i h_{t-1}, r_f h_{t-1}, r_o h_{t-1}$. In mLSTM, the gates and projections depend only on $x_t$, and the recurrence is only in the state update itself.

Is the idea that once the per-token quantities $(f_t, i_t, \mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t, \mathbf{q}_t)$ are known independently from all other timesteps, the remaining recurrence can be rewritten as a prefix-scan or chunked parallel computation? If so, could someone explain at a high level what algebraic property makes this possible?
Answer:
Yes — that is essentially the right intuition, and the key point is the difference between a recurrence whose coefficients are known up front versus one where the coefficients themselves depend on the evolving hidden state.

In the paper, sLSTM keeps the classic LSTM-style hidden-to-hidden terms, so quantities like $\tilde{i}_t$, $\tilde{f}_t$, $\tilde{o}_t$, and $\tilde{z}_t$ depend on $h_{t-1}$. That means you cannot determine the gate values at time $t$ without first computing $h_{t-1}$, which itself depends on all earlier steps. So the gates form a genuine causal dependency chain: step $t$ needs the result of step $t-1$, not just the input $x_t$. This is what the paper calls “memory mixing,” and it is exactly what prevents full parallelization.

By contrast, in mLSTM the paper explicitly removes those hidden-hidden connections. The query, key, value, input gate, forget gate, and output gate are all functions of $x_t$ only:

$$
\mathbf{q}_t, \mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t, i_t, f_t, \mathbf{o}_t \;\text{depend only on}\; x_t.
$$

So once you have the whole input sequence, you can compute all per-token quantities independently in parallel. After that, the remaining recurrence is just

$$
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t.
$$

The reason this is parallelizable is that it is an affine recurrence with a very special structure: the update at each step is “multiply the previous state by a scalar, then add a new term.” Such recurrences can be rewritten as a scan/prefix computation because composition is associative. Concretely, if you define the per-step update as the transformation

$$
S \mapsto f_t S + u_t,
$$

then composing two steps gives

$$
S \mapsto f_2(f_1 S + u_1) + u_2 = (f_2 f_1)S + (f_2 u_1 + u_2),
$$

and this composition rule is associative. That is the algebraic property that enables parallel prefix-scan methods.

You can also see the closed form:

$$
\mathbf{C}_t
=
\left(\prod_{j=1}^t f_j\right)\mathbf{C}_0
+
\sum_{j=1}^t
\left(\prod_{\ell=j+1}^t f_\ell\right)
i_j \mathbf{v}_j \mathbf{k}_j^\top,
$$

and similarly for $\mathbf{n}_t$. Once written this way, each position’s contribution is just a gated prefix product times a local update, which is exactly the kind of structure GPUs like to parallelize.

So the short answer is: sLSTM is sequential because the gates depend on the previous hidden state, making the per-step coefficients themselves recurrent. mLSTM removes that dependency, leaving a purely input-driven affine state update. That update has an associative composition law, so it can be implemented with parallel scan/chunking, much like other linear-time recurrent models and parallel prefix algorithms.
