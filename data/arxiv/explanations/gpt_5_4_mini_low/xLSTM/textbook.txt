\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Why Revisit LSTMs in the Era of Large Language Models?

# Historical Role of Recurrent Neural Networks and LSTMs in Sequence Modeling

Before Transformers became the dominant architecture in large language modeling, recurrent neural networks, and especially Long Short-Term Memory networks, played a central role in sequence modeling. The paper presents LSTMs as a long-standing and successful family of models that were designed to handle sequence data by maintaining a memory across time steps. This made them useful in a wide range of tasks involving text, handwriting, translation, program evaluation, image captioning, source code generation, rainfall-runoff modeling, hydrological forecasting, and reinforcement learning.

The reason LSTMs remained so influential for so long is that they were not just another recurrent model; they addressed a fundamental weakness of earlier recurrent neural networks. In practice, they became the first large language models and were widely used for learning abstractions, storing semantic information in memory cells, and discovering specialized neurons such as number, syntax, linguistic, and sentiment neurons. The paper emphasizes that LSTMs are still relevant in important applications, which helps explain why they are worth revisiting rather than being treated as obsolete.

# The Original Goals of LSTM: Mitigating Vanishing Gradients with Constant Error Flow and Gating

The original motivation for LSTM was to solve the vanishing gradient problem that made standard recurrent neural networks difficult to train over long sequences. The paper highlights the two core ideas introduced in the early LSTM work: the constant error carousel and gating. These ideas are reflected in the basic update equation

$$
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \qquad h_t = o_t \psi(c_t).
$$

This equation captures the central intuition. The memory cell $c_t$ is updated additively rather than being repeatedly overwritten in a purely multiplicative way. The previous memory $c_{t-1}$ flows forward through the forget gate $f_t$, while new content $z_t$ is added through the input gate $i_t$. The hidden state $h_t$ is then produced from the memory cell through the output gate $o_t$.

The paper explains that this additive structure is what the authors refer to as the constant error carousel. Because information can be carried forward directly in the memory cell, gradients can propagate more reliably through time. The gates control what is stored, what is forgotten, and what is exposed as output. In the original LSTM, these gates are sigmoid functions, which keep them bounded between $0$ and $1$. The cell state is typically normalized or squashed by an activation function $\psi$, often $\tanh$, so that the memory does not grow without bound. This combination of additive memory and gating was the key design that made LSTMs successful.

# How Transformers Changed Large-Scale Language Modeling

The paper treats the Transformer as the architecture that displaced LSTMs in large-scale language modeling. The important change was the use of parallelizable self-attention. Unlike recurrent models, which process a sequence step by step, Transformers can compute dependencies between tokens more directly and in parallel. This made them much better suited for scaling to large models and large datasets.

The authors frame this shift historically: LSTMs dominated earlier sequence modeling and text generation, but once Transformers arrived in 2017, they outpaced LSTMs at scale. This was not because the original LSTM ideas were flawed, but because the Transformer architecture offered a different computational tradeoff that worked better with modern training regimes and hardware. The paper therefore asks not whether LSTMs were ever useful, but whether their limitations can be addressed and whether they can be scaled in a way that makes them competitive again.

# The Central Research Question of the Paper: Can LSTMs Compete Again at Billion-Parameter Scale?

The paper’s central question is simple and direct: how far can we get in language modeling if we scale LSTMs to billions of parameters and equip them with modern techniques from large language models, while also removing the known limitations of classical LSTMs? This question is the driving force behind xLSTM.

The authors do not present xLSTM as a minor tweak. Instead, they ask whether the LSTM family can be extended in a way that makes it viable at the scale where Transformers currently dominate. The paper’s experiments are designed around this question: synthetic tasks probe specific weaknesses of standard LSTMs, while language modeling experiments test whether the resulting architecture can match or exceed modern alternatives in real large-scale settings.

# Practical Motivations for Linear-Time, Constant-Memory Sequence Models

A major motivation in the paper is computational practicality. The authors contrast xLSTM with Transformers by noting that xLSTM networks have linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length. This matters because long contexts make attention expensive, while recurrent models can process sequences incrementally.

The paper also argues that a compressive memory is attractive for industrial applications and edge deployment. Since xLSTM does not need to store attention over the entire sequence, it can be more memory efficient at inference time. This is especially relevant in the context of long sequences and autoregressive generation, where memory and throughput become important bottlenecks. In this sense, xLSTM is motivated not only as a scientific curiosity but as a practical architecture for sequence modeling under resource constraints.

# Overview of xLSTM’s Two Core Ideas: Exponential Gating and New Memory Structures

xLSTM is built around two main modifications to the classic LSTM idea. The first is exponential gating with normalization and stabilization. The second is a change in memory structure. These modifications are meant to directly address the limitations the authors identify in standard LSTMs: inability to revise storage decisions, limited storage capacity, and lack of parallelizability.

The exponential gating idea changes how gates behave. Instead of being restricted to sigmoid outputs, some gates can use exponential activations. This gives the model a stronger mechanism for revising memory decisions, but it also creates numerical stability challenges. The paper therefore introduces a stabilizer state to keep the computation well behaved. The authors emphasize that this stabilization does not change the network output or the derivatives with respect to the parameters when the stabilized gates replace the original ones.

The memory structure is also extended. One variant, sLSTM, retains a scalar memory but introduces a normalizer state and new memory mixing. The other, mLSTM, replaces the scalar memory with a matrix memory and uses a covariance-style update. Together, these two extensions define the xLSTM family.

# Preview of the Two xLSTM Variants: sLSTM and mLSTM

The paper introduces two complementary extensions of LSTM. The first is sLSTM, which keeps the memory scalar but adds exponential gating and normalization. Its role is to improve the model’s ability to revise what it stores and to introduce a new form of memory mixing. The second is mLSTM, which expands the memory from a scalar into a matrix and uses a covariance update rule. Its role is to increase storage capacity and enable fully parallelizable computation.

These two variants reflect different goals. sLSTM focuses on the dynamics of memory revision and state tracking. mLSTM focuses on richer memory capacity and parallel computation. The paper also notes that both variants can be arranged into residual block architectures and stacked into full xLSTM models. This makes the family compatible with modern deep learning design patterns.

# High-Level Summary of Experimental Claims

The paper’s experimental story is that xLSTM addresses the weaknesses of traditional LSTMs and performs favorably against current alternatives. On synthetic tasks, xLSTM is designed to demonstrate improved state tracking, stronger associative recall, and strong long-context behavior. In the language modeling experiments on SlimPajama, the paper reports that xLSTM outperforms the compared methods in validation perplexity at 15B tokens and remains strong when scaled to 300B tokens.

The authors also report that xLSTM performs well on downstream tasks and on the PALOMA benchmark, and that its scaling behavior suggests continued competitiveness at larger sizes. In addition, the paper highlights favorable generation-time and throughput properties, especially relative to Transformer models with quadratic scaling in context length. The overall claim is not merely that xLSTM is a viable recurrent alternative, but that scaling LSTMs with the right modifications can make them serious competitors to modern Transformers and State Space Models.

2. Foundations: The Original LSTM Revisited

# Recurrent neural networks and the vanishing gradient problem

Long Short-Term Memory, or LSTM, was introduced as a way to address a central difficulty in recurrent neural networks: the vanishing gradient problem. In an ordinary recurrent network, information is passed from one time step to the next through repeated nonlinear transformations. While this gives the model a notion of state or memory, it also makes learning long-range dependencies difficult, because gradients propagated backward through many steps can shrink and become ineffective. The paper frames LSTM as a direct response to this problem.

The key idea is that a recurrent model should have a mechanism for storing information over time without repeatedly transforming it in a way that destroys the gradient signal. LSTM accomplishes this by introducing a memory cell and gating mechanisms that regulate how information enters, remains in, and leaves the memory. In the paper’s notation, the central conceptual update is expressed as
\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \quad h_t = o_t \psi(c_t).
\]
This formulation already shows the basic departure from a plain recurrent update: instead of overwriting memory entirely at each time step, the cell state $c_t$ combines a previous memory state with a new candidate update.

# The constant error carousel as the key LSTM innovation

The paper describes the “constant error carousel” as the central LSTM innovation. The intuition is that the cell state provides a path along which information, and therefore gradient signal, can persist over time. Rather than forcing all information to pass through repeatedly squashed nonlinear transitions, LSTM uses an additive update to the cell state. This additive structure is crucial, because it allows the network to preserve relevant information more directly.

In the paper’s explanation, the previous cell state $c_{t-1}$ is updated by adding a candidate input $z_t$, with the contribution of each term controlled by gates. The forget gate $f_t$ decides how much of the previous cell state is retained, while the input gate $i_t$ controls how much new information is written. This update is what the paper refers to when describing the constant error carousel. The important point is not just that information can be stored, but that storage is mediated in a way that supports stable learning over long sequences.

# Standard LSTM update equations for cell state and hidden state

The paper gives the standard LSTM update equations in a fully explicit form. At time step $t$, the cell state is computed as
\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t.
\]
This is the memory update. It says that the new cell state is a combination of the old memory and a new candidate input. The hidden state is then defined by
\[
h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t, \quad \tilde{h}_t = \psi(c_t),
\]
where $\psi$ is a squashing or normalization function, typically $\tanh$. Thus the hidden state is not the memory itself, but a gated view of it.

The paper also writes out how each gate and the cell input are computed from the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$. The cell input is
\[
z_t = \varphi(\tilde{z}_t), \quad \tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z h_{t-1} + b_z,
\]
and similarly for the gates,
\[
i_t = \sigma(\tilde{i}_t), \quad \tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i h_{t-1} + b_i,
\]
\[
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t), \quad \tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f h_{t-1} + b_f,
\]
\[
o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t), \quad \tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_o h_{t-1} + b_o.
\]
This makes clear that the standard LSTM is not just a memory buffer. It is a learned dynamical system that uses both the current input and its own previous output to decide how to update itself.

# Input, forget, and output gates: meanings and functions

Each gate in the LSTM has a distinct role, and the paper emphasizes that all of them are important. The input gate $i_t$ controls how much of the new candidate value $z_t$ is written into memory. If $i_t$ is close to $0$, the model mostly ignores the new input; if it is close to $1$, the candidate is allowed to influence the memory strongly. The forget gate $f_t$ controls retention of the previous cell state. If $f_t$ is near $1$, past memory is preserved; if it is near $0$, the previous state is largely discarded. The output gate $o_t$ controls how much of the cell state is exposed as the hidden state.

This division of labor is important because it lets the model make separate decisions about writing, keeping, and reading memory. The paper stresses that the gates are sigmoid-valued, so each gate produces a number between $0$ and $1$. That choice makes the gates interpretable as soft switches or soft masks. In the original LSTM, the gates are driven by the current input and the previous hidden state, so each decision can depend on both what the model sees now and what it has already accumulated.

# Cell input activation and hidden-state squashing

The paper distinguishes between the activation used for the cell input and the activation used to normalize or squash the cell state. The candidate cell input $z_t$ is produced through a function $\varphi$, typically $\tanh$, applied to a linear combination of input and recurrent terms. This keeps the candidate update in a bounded range. Then the memory state $c_t$ is passed through another squashing function $\psi$, again typically $\tanh$, before it is exposed through the hidden state.

This design serves two purposes in the original LSTM. First, it allows the cell state itself to remain a flexible internal storage variable. Second, it prevents the output from becoming unbounded, since the memory can accumulate over time. The paper explicitly notes that $\psi$ is used to normalize or squash the cell state, which would otherwise be unbounded. So the hidden state is not simply the cell state itself, but a controlled, output-gated transformation of it.

# Scalar memory cells versus vectorized modern LSTM formulations

In the original formulation, the paper presents a scalar memory cell, with $c_t \in \mathbb{R}$ for a single cell. Later formulations generalize this to multiple scalar memory cells collected into a vector $\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$. This vectorized version allows the use of recurrent weight matrices rather than just scalars, which enables the outputs of different memory cells to be mixed through learned recurrent connections.

The paper notes that this later, more general formulation permits the use of recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ to mix cell outputs. This is an important step in moving from a single memory unit to a system of interacting memory units. In the xLSTM paper, this vectorized view matters because it sets up the distinction between scalar-memory designs and the later matrix-memory design. But the baseline LSTM idea remains the same: a memory state is updated over time through gated additive dynamics.

# Memory mixing through recurrent hidden-to-hidden connections

A key feature of the standard LSTM formulation in the paper is that the gates and cell input depend not only on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ but also on the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$. These hidden-to-hidden connections mean that the model does not treat each time step independently. Instead, the current computation is conditioned on the network’s previous output.

The paper refers to this as memory mixing when multiple memory cells are used and their outputs are combined through recurrent weight matrices. This mixing is a way of letting different memory components influence one another. It can improve expressiveness, since the model is not restricted to isolated memory slots. At the same time, this dependence on the previous hidden state is exactly what makes processing sequential: the update at time $t$ cannot be computed until the state from time $t-1$ is available.

# Why classic LSTMs remained effective for many tasks

Despite later developments in Transformers, the paper emphasizes that LSTMs remained successful across many domains for a long time. They were used in text generation, handwriting generation, sequence-to-sequence translation, program evaluation, image captioning, source code generation, rainfall-runoff modeling, and hydrological forecasting. They also played a major role in reinforcement learning systems such as AlphaStar and OpenAI Five.

The reason for this durability, according to the paper, is that LSTMs are good at learning abstractions and storing semantic information in their memory cells. The paper points to observations such as number and syntax neurons, linguistic neurons, and sentiment neurons as evidence that LSTMs can form meaningful internal representations. In other words, the model is not merely memorizing sequences mechanically; it can distill information into its memory in ways that capture structure relevant to the task. This ability helped LSTMs remain strong and useful even as other sequence models appeared.

# Limitations of standard LSTMs that motivate xLSTM

The paper identifies three main limitations of standard LSTMs, and these limitations motivate the xLSTM extension. First, standard LSTMs have difficulty revising storage decisions. Once a value is stored, it can be hard for the model to update or replace it when later evidence suggests a better choice. The paper illustrates this with nearest neighbor search, where the model must scan a sequence and keep track of the most similar item. A standard LSTM struggles to revise a previously stored value when a more similar vector appears later.

Second, the paper argues that standard LSTMs have limited storage capacity because information must be compressed into scalar cell states. This becomes visible in rare token prediction, where performance degrades on infrequent tokens. The model simply has less room to preserve detailed information. Third, standard LSTMs are not well parallelizable because memory mixing introduces hidden-to-hidden dependencies across time. Since each step depends on the last, sequential processing is required.

These limitations are exactly what xLSTM is designed to address. The paper’s broader argument is that by revisiting the original LSTM ideas and modifying gating and memory structure, one can overcome these bottlenecks while keeping the strengths that made LSTMs effective in the first place.

Diagnosing the Limits of Conventional LSTMs

# Inability to revise storage decisions once information has been written

The paper identifies a first major limitation of conventional LSTMs: once information has been written into memory, the mechanism for updating that memory is too rigid to revise earlier storage decisions effectively. In the original LSTM, the cell state is updated by an additive rule,
$$c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,$$
so the model can preserve past information through the forget gate and add new information through the input gate. This is the famous constant error carousel idea, and it is precisely what made LSTMs so effective at learning long-range dependencies. However, the paper argues that this mechanism is still not enough when the model must repeatedly compare incoming information against what it has already stored and then replace an earlier choice with a better one.

The key intuition is that the original LSTM update is sequential and conservative: it can retain or decay existing content, but it does not naturally provide a strong mechanism for revisiting a stored decision after later evidence suggests that the earlier choice was suboptimal. In other words, the model can remember, but it does not easily “change its mind.” The paper presents this as a practical weakness in tasks where the relevant item is not simply the first or last thing encountered, but the best one among many candidates seen over time. This kind of problem requires the memory system to support revision, not just accumulation.

This is one of the central motivations for the paper’s new exponential gating. The authors introduce it specifically to let xLSTM overcome this inability to revise storage decisions. They frame the limitation not as a minor implementation detail, but as a structural weakness of the conventional LSTM gating design.

# Nearest Neighbor Search as a motivating failure case

To make the revision problem concrete, the paper uses the Nearest Neighbor Search task. In this setting, a reference vector is given, and then a sequence must be scanned in order to find the most similar vector. At the end of the sequence, the model must output the value attached to that best match. This task is especially useful because it forces the model to compare candidates over time and to update its memory when a more similar vector appears later in the sequence.

The paper explains that the conventional LSTM struggles on this task because it has difficulty revising a stored value once it has already committed to one. If the model stores an early candidate, then later encounters a better one, the ideal behavior would be to overwrite or sharpen the memory in favor of the later, more relevant item. Conventional LSTM gating does not support this behavior well enough, so performance degrades.

The significance of this example is that it isolates the problem of selective revision from other challenges. It is not just about remembering many things; it is about knowing when to replace one remembered item with another. The paper uses this failure case to show that the issue is not merely theoretical. It appears in a simple synthetic task where the desired behavior is easy to state, yet difficult for the standard LSTM to carry out reliably. xLSTM addresses this through exponential gating, which the paper claims improves the model’s ability to revise storage decisions.

# Limited storage capacity of scalar cell states

The second major limitation identified in the paper is limited storage capacity. In standard LSTMs, the memory cell is scalar at its core, and even when multiple cells are combined, the memory representation remains fundamentally compressed into scalar states. The paper emphasizes that this compression constrains how much information can be stored and how precisely it can be retained for later retrieval.

This limitation matters because language modeling often requires storing multiple pieces of information simultaneously, some of which are rare, subtle, or context-specific. A scalar state can preserve coarse summaries, but it is not a natural structure for storing richer associations between items. The paper argues that this restricted memory capacity is one reason why standard LSTMs struggle more on problems that require recalling specific, infrequent pieces of information.

To address this, xLSTM introduces a matrix memory in mLSTM. The idea is that increasing the memory from a scalar to a matrix gives the model a much larger storage surface. Instead of compressing information into a single value, the model can store relationships between vectors through matrix updates. This change is not just larger in size; it changes the kind of information the memory can represent. The paper treats this as a direct response to the capacity bottleneck of conventional LSTM memory.

# Rare Token Prediction as evidence of memory bottlenecks

The paper supports the storage-capacity argument with the Rare Token Prediction example. Here, the validation perplexity of token prediction on Wikitext-103 is examined across different partitions of token frequency. The important observation is that standard LSTM performs worse on rare tokens, and the paper interprets this as evidence that the model’s limited storage capacity makes it harder to retain information needed for infrequent events.

This is a meaningful diagnostic because rare tokens are precisely the kind of items that cannot be handled well by generic statistical smoothing alone. A model must preserve specific contextual signals if it is to predict them accurately. When memory is too compressed, those signals can be lost or blurred. In the paper’s framing, rare-token performance exposes the fact that scalar cell states do not provide enough representational room for the kinds of details language modeling demands.

The matrix memory in xLSTM is presented as the solution to this bottleneck. By enabling key-value style storage through a covariance update rule, mLSTM gives the model more capacity to preserve distinct pieces of information. The paper notes that xLSTM improves rare-token performance through this larger memory structure, which directly supports the claim that the standard LSTM’s scalar memory is a limiting factor in real language tasks.

# Sequential dependence caused by memory mixing and lack of parallelization

The third major limitation is the lack of parallelizability caused by memory mixing, meaning the hidden-to-hidden connections between successive time steps. In the original LSTM, the hidden state at time $t-1$ affects the gates and inputs at time $t$, which creates a strict sequential dependency. Because the computation at each step depends on the previous hidden state, the model cannot be fully parallelized across time.

The paper treats this as a major practical limitation, especially at the scale of modern language modeling. Sequence models are only competitive if they can be trained and executed efficiently on large datasets and long contexts. A strictly sequential recurrence becomes expensive and can constrain throughput. This is one of the reasons Transformers became dominant: they use parallelizable self-attention rather than time-step-by-time-step recurrence.

The paper’s response is twofold. For sLSTM, it keeps memory mixing and therefore retains sequential dependence, but it modifies gating to improve behavior. For mLSTM, it removes memory mixing and thereby makes the recurrence fully parallelizable. This distinction is central to xLSTM’s design: one variant keeps the LSTM-like sequential memory interaction, while the other trades that interaction for parallel execution and larger matrix memory. The paper presents this as a deliberate design choice to balance modeling power and efficiency.

# Why these issues matter at language-model scale

The paper’s broader argument is that these LSTM limitations become especially important when scaling to billions of parameters and training on massive text corpora. At small scale, a recurrent model can appear adequate on many tasks. But language modeling at modern scale asks for more: the model must handle long contexts, retain rare information, revise earlier commitments, and do so efficiently enough to benefit from large-scale training.

The paper emphasizes that the limitations are not abstract architectural preferences; they show up in performance on concrete tasks and benchmarks. In language modeling, the model must continuously decide what to preserve, what to forget, and what to replace. When those decisions are too rigid or too compressed, the model’s perplexity and downstream task performance suffer. This is especially true for rare tokens, long-range dependencies, and situations that require state tracking over many steps.

In this sense, the limitations of conventional LSTMs are not presented as reasons to abandon recurrence altogether, but as reasons to extend it. The paper’s main question is precisely how far LSTMs can go if they are scaled with modern techniques while addressing these bottlenecks. The diagnosis of the limits is therefore the basis for the entire xLSTM design.

# Comparison of LSTM limitations with Transformer advantages

The paper explicitly frames these LSTM issues against the rise of Transformers. Transformers became successful in language modeling because their self-attention mechanism is parallelizable and can access information across the context without the same sequential hidden-state dependence. This gave them a major computational advantage at scale, and they eventually outpaced LSTMs in large language modeling.

The comparison in the paper is not that Transformers solve every problem better in an absolute sense, but that they avoid some of the structural limitations that hinder standard LSTMs. In particular, the inability of LSTMs to revise storage decisions, their limited scalar memory, and their sequential dependence all become more painful as sequence lengths and model sizes grow. Transformers, by contrast, gained dominance partly because their architecture better suits large-scale training and long-context processing.

At the same time, the paper argues that LSTMs still have strengths worth preserving. They are compact, compressive, and capable of strong memory behavior when properly designed. The paper’s thesis is not that recurrence is obsolete, but that the LSTM idea can be modernized to recover competitiveness. xLSTM is thus presented as a way to combine the enduring strengths of LSTMs with design choices that address the weaknesses that Transformers exposed.

# Design requirements for an extended LSTM architecture

From these limitations, the paper derives the design requirements for xLSTM. First, the architecture must support revisable storage decisions, which motivates exponential gating with normalization and stabilization. This is intended to make the model more flexible about when to strengthen, weaken, or replace stored information.

Second, the architecture must increase memory capacity beyond scalar cell states. This motivates the new matrix memory in mLSTM, together with the covariance-style update rule that stores key-value associations. The paper treats this as a way to move from compressed scalar storage to a richer memory structure that can better support retrieval.

Third, the architecture must address the sequential bottleneck caused by memory mixing. This leads to two variants: sLSTM, which keeps memory mixing but modifies gating, and mLSTM, which removes memory mixing and becomes fully parallelizable. Together, these form the basis of xLSTM blocks and, when stacked residually, xLSTM architectures.

The broader takeaway is that xLSTM is not a single tweak to the original LSTM, but a response to three specific failures diagnosed in the paper. The model is designed around the idea that to compete at language-model scale, recurrent networks must be able to revise, store more, and compute efficiently. The chapter’s three shortcomings are therefore not side issues; they define the problem that xLSTM is built to solve.

Exponential Gating: A New Write-and-Forget Mechanism

# Limitations of Sigmoid Gating for Revising Prior Memory

The paper begins from a practical weakness of the original LSTM gating mechanism: once information is written into the memory cell, it can be difficult to revise that decision later when better evidence appears. In the standard LSTM update, the cell state is updated additively as $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, where both the forget gate $f_t$ and the input gate $i_t$ are sigmoid gates. Because sigmoid outputs lie between $0$ and $1$, these gates act as bounded multipliers. This is useful for stability, but it also means the model can only softly retain or erase past content.

The authors use the nearest neighbor search problem to illustrate the limitation. In that setting, the sequence must be scanned to find the most similar vector, and the correct output depends on being able to revise earlier storage decisions when a more relevant vector appears later. The paper argues that a sigmoid-gated LSTM struggles here because it cannot strongly increase the importance of a newly observed item relative to what has already been stored. In other words, bounded gates make it hard to decisively overwrite previous memory when a better candidate arrives.

This problem is not presented as a minor implementation detail, but as one of the main obstacles that motivates xLSTM. The paper’s answer is to change the gating nonlinearity itself. Instead of only using sigmoid-based write control, xLSTM introduces exponential gating, which gives the network a much more flexible mechanism for emphasizing new evidence or downweighting old content.

# Definition and Intuition of Exponential Input Gates

The key new ingredient in xLSTM is the exponential input gate. In the sLSTM formulation, the input gate is defined as $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ rather than as a sigmoid. The same basic affine structure is preserved: $\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i h_{t-1} + b_i$. So the change is not in how the gate is computed from the input and recurrent state, but in the activation applied to that preactivation.

Intuitively, this changes the meaning of writing to memory. A sigmoid gate can only decide “write a fraction between $0$ and $1$.” An exponential gate can represent values greater than $1$, so it can amplify the incoming update rather than merely scale it down. That means a newly arrived signal is not forced to compete on equal footing with what is already stored. It can be given much stronger influence when the model decides it is important.

The paper keeps the original LSTM-style idea of input- and hidden-dependent gating plus bias terms, but transfers it to this exponential form. This is important because it preserves the recurrent structure while changing the dynamic range of the gates. The result is a more expressive write mechanism that can revise stored information more aggressively than the classical sigmoid gate.

# Optional Exponential Forget Gates Versus Sigmoid Forget Gates

The paper applies the same idea not only to the input gate but also optionally to the forget gate. In the sLSTM and mLSTM equations, the forget gate is written as either $f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ or $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$. This means the model can use either the original bounded sigmoid forget gate or an exponential variant. The paper presents both as choices in the new architecture.

This flexibility matters because the forget gate controls how much of the previous memory is carried forward. With a sigmoid forget gate, the contribution of the previous state is always attenuated by a factor between $0$ and $1$. With an exponential forget gate, the gate can also scale the previous memory more strongly. In the presence of the stabilizing machinery introduced later, this creates a richer and more adaptive relationship between past and present information.

The paper’s main point is not that the forget gate must always be exponential, but that exponential gating opens a new regime of memory control. The gating functions are no longer limited to a purely suppressive role. Instead, they can support a more nuanced interaction between retention and revision, which is central to the write-and-forget behavior of xLSTM.

# How Exponential Gating Changes the Balance Between Old and New Memory

In the standard LSTM, the cell state update is an additive combination of retained memory and new content. The two terms are mediated by bounded gates, so the balance between them is naturally conservative. Exponential gating changes that balance by allowing the model to express much larger relative differences in strength. This is especially clear in the sLSTM, where the cell state is still updated as $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, but now both $f_t$ and $i_t$ may be exponential.

The practical consequence is that the model can more sharply prefer one piece of information over another. If a new input is deemed much more relevant than what was stored previously, the input gate can assign it a much larger weight. Conversely, if the old memory should dominate, the forget gate can preserve that dominance. The point is not only that the memory can be written to, but that the model can revise the relative weighting of old and new content in a more decisive way.

This is why the paper connects exponential gating to the ability to solve state tracking problems. A state-tracking model needs to update its internal state when the relevant entity or condition changes. If gating is too conservative, the model lags behind the actual state of the sequence. Exponential gating gives the model the power to make stronger corrective updates when needed.

# Why Exponential Gates Improve Selective Overwriting

The paper’s central claim about exponential gating is that it improves selective overwriting. In tasks such as nearest neighbor search, the model must retain the best candidate seen so far and replace it when a better candidate appears. The difficulty is not just remembering; it is knowing when to revise what has already been stored. Exponential gating helps because it can give a newly observed signal enough strength to override previous content.

This is a more precise form of selectivity than ordinary gating provides. A sigmoid gate can only weaken or preserve a signal within a limited range. An exponential gate can instead magnify the contribution of particularly relevant inputs. That means the model can more easily change its mind when the evidence changes, rather than being locked into earlier choices by bounded update magnitudes.

The paper uses this idea to explain why xLSTM can improve performance on formal language tasks and other state-tracking problems. The new gating mechanism is not merely a different mathematical choice; it directly addresses a known failure mode of classic LSTMs. By making the write operation more flexible, the network becomes better at revising storage decisions as a sequence unfolds.

# Numerical Instability Risks Introduced by Exponentials

The paper is explicit that exponential gating comes with a serious numerical drawback: exponentials can become very large and lead to overflow. This is a natural consequence of replacing bounded sigmoid outputs with unbounded positive values. Without additional safeguards, the gate values could explode and make the recurrence unstable.

To address this, the paper introduces stabilization through an additional state variable. For the sLSTM, this is the stabilizer state $m_t$, defined by
$ m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t)) $.
Using this state, the model computes stabilized gates $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ so that the forward pass can be rewritten in a numerically safer form. The paper emphasizes that replacing $f_t$ and $i_t$ by their stabilized versions does not change the output of the whole network or the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters.

This is an important design principle in the chapter’s topic. Exponential gating is powerful precisely because it can represent large relative strengths, but that same flexibility makes numerical care essential. The paper’s stabilization mechanism is therefore not an optional detail; it is what makes exponential gating usable in practice.

# The Role of Gate Normalization in Recurrent Memory Systems

Normalization is the second part of the stabilization strategy, and it is tightly connected to the role of exponential gating. In the sLSTM, the paper introduces a normalizer state $n_t$ alongside the cell state. The normalizer accumulates the same gate factors that determine memory strength, via $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$. The hidden state is then computed as $h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t$ with $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$.

This normalization has a clear intuition. Since exponential gates can produce very different magnitudes over time, the raw cell state alone would not provide a stable basis for output. The normalizer keeps track of the overall strength of the gating history, so the hidden state is scaled by how much write and forget activity has occurred. In this way, the model does not just accumulate content; it also accumulates a notion of how strongly that content should be interpreted.

The same principle appears in the mLSTM, where the normalizer state is a weighted sum of key vectors. There, too, the purpose is to preserve information about gate strength so that retrieval is properly normalized. Across both variants, normalization is the mechanism that makes exponential gating compatible with stable recurrent computation.

# Relationship Between Gating, Learning Rates, and Decay Rates

The paper draws an explicit connection between gating in xLSTM and the notions of learning rate and decay rate. In the mLSTM, the covariance update rule is
$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$.
Here, the forget gate acts like a decay rate on the old memory, while the input gate acts like a learning rate on the new update. This interpretation is not just metaphorical. It explains how the recurrence can be seen as a controlled update of memory content, where gating determines how much of the past survives and how much of the new evidence is written in.

The paper uses this perspective to situate xLSTM within the broader history of fast weight and associative memory ideas. The covariance update rule stores key-value pairs in a way that resembles a learned memory system, and the gates regulate the strength of storage and retention. Exponential gating strengthens this interpretation because the gates can now express more dramatic adjustments to how strongly the model learns from new inputs or decays old content.

In this sense, xLSTM’s gating mechanism is not merely a filter on activations. It is the control system for memory revision itself. The exponential form makes that control more expressive, while normalization and stabilization keep the system numerically well behaved. Together, these components implement the paper’s new write-and-forget mechanism: a recurrent memory that can revise past storage decisions more effectively than the original sigmoid-gated LSTM.

5. sLSTM: Scalar-Memory xLSTM with Normalization and Memory Mixing

# sLSTM design goals and relation to standard LSTM

The sLSTM is the scalar-memory member of xLSTM. Its purpose is to preserve the core LSTM idea of a memory cell that can carry information forward through time, while addressing limitations of the standard LSTM through exponential gating, normalization, and stabilization. In the paper’s framing, this means keeping the essential recurrence of the original LSTM idea,
\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \qquad h_t = o_t \psi(c_t),
\]
but changing the way the gates behave and how the memory is interpreted. The result is still a scalar memory cell at its core, but one that is more flexible in deciding when to keep, revise, or emphasize stored information.

Compared with the original LSTM, sLSTM is designed to overcome the limitation that a standard sigmoid-gated memory update can struggle to revise earlier storage decisions. The paper motivates this with tasks such as nearest neighbor search, where the network must keep scanning a sequence and replace a previously stored candidate if a better one appears later. Standard LSTM may have difficulty making such revisions because its gating is bounded and its memory update is not normalized in a way that makes storage decisions directly comparable over time. sLSTM introduces exponential gating precisely to make these updates more adaptive.

At the same time, sLSTM remains close to the original LSTM structure. It still has a cell state, a hidden state, and the familiar roles of input, forget, and output gates. It also preserves recurrent dependence on the previous hidden state. So sLSTM is not a departure from LSTM in spirit; rather, it is an extended version that keeps the same recurrent memory logic while improving the mechanism by which the memory is controlled.

# The sLSTM forward equations

The scalar sLSTM forward pass is given by
\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,
\]
\[
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,
\]
\[
h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t, \qquad \tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t.
\]
These equations show that sLSTM maintains two coupled states: the main memory cell \(c_t\) and a normalizer state \(n_t\). The memory cell stores content, while the normalizer keeps track of the effective strength of the gating that has contributed to that content. The hidden state is not just a direct squashing of the memory cell, but rather a normalized version of it.

The cell input \(z_t\) is computed from the current input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) and the previous hidden state \(h_{t-1}\), and the gates \(i_t\), \(f_t\), and \(o_t\) are also computed from the current input and previous hidden state. This makes the sLSTM a recurrent model in the same general sense as the original LSTM: the current step depends on both the new input and the previous internal state.

The key difference is that the hidden state is formed from the ratio \(c_t / n_t\). This ratio makes the representation more stable and interpretable, because the memory content is normalized by how much gate mass has accumulated into it. In this way, the hidden state reflects not just what has been stored, but also how strongly it has been stored.

# Cell state update with exponential gating

The central modification in sLSTM is the use of exponential gates for the input gate, and optionally for the forget gate as well. The paper writes
\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t),
\]
and for the forget gate,
\[
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t) \quad \text{or} \quad \exp(\tilde{f}_t).
\]
This means the input gate is no longer constrained to lie in \([0,1]\), as in a standard sigmoid-gated LSTM. Instead, it can take positive values of varying magnitude. The forget gate may remain sigmoid-gated or also be exponential, depending on the specific form used.

This is important because the gating no longer merely decides whether to pass information or not; it can also express stronger or weaker relative emphasis. In the paper’s interpretation, exponential gating enables the model to revise storage decisions. If a later token is more relevant than an earlier one, the model can assign a larger input contribution to the later information rather than being limited by a bounded gate.

The cell state update remains additive in structure, which preserves the LSTM’s constant error carousel idea. Information flows forward through an additive recurrence, but the scale of the incoming and retained contributions is now controlled by unbounded positive gates. This combination of additive recurrence and exponential weighting is one of the main mechanisms that makes sLSTM more expressive than standard LSTM.

# The normalizer state and its purpose

Because exponential gates can become large, the paper introduces a stabilizer state \(m_t\) and an associated normalization procedure. First, the normalizer state \(n_t\) is updated by
\[
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t.
\]
This state accumulates the total gating strength that has contributed to the memory cell. It is described in the paper as summing up the product of the input gate and all future forget gates, so it acts as a record of how much “weight” has been placed into the memory over time.

The purpose of the normalizer is to make the hidden state a normalized version of the stored content. Without this normalization, exponential gating could cause the memory values to grow without bound or become hard to interpret. By dividing by \(n_t\), the model converts the raw memory accumulation into a form that reflects relative contributions rather than only absolute magnitude.

The paper emphasizes that this is not merely a numerical trick. The normalizer is conceptually tied to the gating structure. It keeps track of how much information has been admitted into memory and how much of it has survived through subsequent forget gates. In this sense, the normalizer is an essential part of the sLSTM’s memory semantics, not just a stabilizing add-on.

# Computing the hidden state through normalized memory

The hidden state in sLSTM is computed as
\[
h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t, \qquad \tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t.
\]
This means that before output gating is applied, the cell state is normalized by the normalizer state. The paper’s intuition is that this produces a more balanced representation of the stored memory, especially when gates have varying magnitudes over time.

The output gate then controls how much of this normalized memory is exposed as the hidden state. This preserves the standard LSTM role of the output gate: the memory cell can hold information internally while the hidden state reveals only what is useful for the current computation. In sLSTM, the hidden state thus depends on both the content of memory and the accumulated gate strength that produced it.

This normalized hidden-state computation is also what makes the stabilization procedure possible without changing the network’s output. The paper states that replacing the original gates with stabilized versions does not change the output of the whole network nor the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters. This is important because it means the normalization can be introduced in a mathematically consistent way rather than as an approximation.

# Choice of activation functions for cell input and output

The paper keeps the cell input activation \(\varphi\) and hidden-state activation \(\psi\) in the same spirit as the original LSTM. In the standard LSTM review, these functions are typically \(\tanh\). The role of \(\varphi\) is to transform the preactivation \(\tilde{z}_t\) into the cell input \(z_t\), while \(\psi\) normalizes or squashes the cell state before output.

This separation of roles is important. The cell input activation shapes what gets written into memory, while the hidden-state activation shapes what gets read out. Even though sLSTM modifies the gating structure, it does not abandon the LSTM design principle that memory content should be transformed before being used as output. The memory cell is still the central storage unit, and the hidden state remains a filtered view of that memory.

The paper also emphasizes that \(\psi\) is needed because the cell state would otherwise be unbounded. This is true in the original LSTM and remains true in the sLSTM setting. The normalized memory helps, but the model still relies on activation functions to produce well-behaved outputs for downstream computation.

# Interpretation of the normalizer as accumulated gate strength

A useful way to understand sLSTM is to see the normalizer state \(n_t\) as a running summary of gate strength. Each time step contributes to \(n_t\) through the current input gate \(i_t\), and the previous normalizer contribution is carried forward through the forget gate \(f_t\). This makes \(n_t\) a memory of how strongly the cell has been updated over time.

The paper describes this normalizer as summing the product of the input gate and all future forget gates. Intuitively, that means every piece of content stored in the cell is associated with a weight determined by how much it was written and how much it was preserved. The hidden state then divides the raw memory by this accumulated weight, so the output reflects a normalized average of the stored information.

This interpretation is particularly important because it shows that the model is not simply storing larger numbers when gates are larger. Instead, it is maintaining a form of weighted memory in which the strength of gating is explicitly represented. That gives the model a principled way to compare competing stored items and revise what is most important.

# How sLSTM supports memory revision better than LSTM

One of the paper’s main claims is that sLSTM is better able than standard LSTM to revise storage decisions. The key ingredient is exponential gating. Because the input and forget gates can take exponential values, the model is not limited to the bounded scaling of sigmoid gates. This allows later evidence to outweigh earlier evidence more effectively when the task requires it.

The paper motivates this with nearest neighbor search. In such a task, the sequence must be scanned and the model must remember the most similar vector seen so far. If a later vector is more similar than an earlier one, the model should revise its stored choice. Standard LSTM may struggle to do this robustly because its gate values are constrained. sLSTM, by contrast, can assign stronger relative weight to the later candidate and normalize the resulting state so that the revised choice is reflected in the output.

The stabilizer state is important here as well. Exponential gating alone could make the model numerically unstable, but the stabilization procedure ensures that the gate magnitudes can be handled safely. Thus, sLSTM improves memory revision not by abandoning the LSTM structure, but by making the gate dynamics more expressive while preserving stable computation.

# Retaining recurrent hidden-state dependence and memory mixing

sLSTM still uses recurrent hidden-state dependence. The equations for the cell input and the gates each involve \(h_{t-1}\), so the current step depends on the previous hidden state as well as the current input. This preserves the LSTM’s sequential processing structure and means that the model can condition its updates on prior context.

The paper also highlights memory mixing in sLSTM. In the original LSTM, multiple scalar memory cells can be combined into a vector, and recurrent weight matrices can mix the outputs of the memory cells. The same idea applies to sLSTM. Multiple memory cells allow information from different channels to interact through the recurrent connections from the hidden state vector to the cell input and gates.

An additional point in the paper is that sLSTM can have multiple heads, with memory mixing occurring within each head but not across heads. The combination of heads and exponential gating establishes a new way of memory mixing. This is important because it means sLSTM is not just a single scalar recurrence replicated many times; the architecture can organize memory into multiple interacting subspaces while still retaining the scalar-memory update logic inside each cell.

# When scalar memory remains useful despite limited capacity

Although sLSTM uses only scalar memory in each cell, the paper presents it as useful rather than weak. The scalar memory is still the foundation of the original LSTM’s success, and the paper argues that with the right gating and normalization it can be made substantially more effective. The main limitation of scalar memory is capacity: it compresses information into a single value per cell, so it cannot explicitly store rich vector-like associations the way matrix memory can.

Even so, scalar memory remains valuable when the task benefits from sequential state tracking rather than large associative storage. The paper explicitly links memory mixing in sLSTM to state tracking problems. In these problems, what matters is not storing many items independently, but maintaining and revising the current state of a sequence, narrative, or formal process. For such tasks, a well-controlled scalar memory can be enough, especially when the architecture is allowed to use multiple cells and heads.

The experiments in the paper also suggest that sLSTM contributes positively even in settings where matrix memory is available. In the associative recall task, the sLSTM block does not reduce memory capacity and may even leverage it. More broadly, the paper treats scalar memory as a useful component of xLSTM because it preserves the LSTM’s recurrent reasoning ability while adding the flexibility of exponential gating and normalization.

6. Stabilizing Exponential Gates in sLSTM and mLSTM

# Why exponential gating can overflow during training and inference

xLSTM introduces exponential gating as a core modification to the original LSTM idea. In the paper, the input gate in sLSTM and mLSTM is replaced by an exponential function, and the forget gate may also be exponential. This change is important because it allows the model to revise storage decisions more effectively than a standard sigmoid gate. However, the paper emphasizes a practical problem: exponential activations can become very large, and large values can cause numerical overflow.

This issue is not merely theoretical. In a recurrent system, gate values are used repeatedly across many time steps, so even moderately large exponentials can accumulate into values that are too large for stable computation. Since the gates influence both the memory update and the normalization mechanism, instability in these quantities would affect the entire recurrent process. The paper therefore treats stabilization as a necessary part of making exponential gating usable in practice.

The goal of stabilization is not to change what the model computes in principle, but to reparameterize the computation so that it can be evaluated safely. In other words, xLSTM keeps the benefits of exponential gating while avoiding the numerical dangers that would otherwise come with it.

# The stabilizer state $m_t$

The paper introduces an additional state variable, denoted $m_t$, to stabilize the exponential gates. This stabilizer state is defined recursively as

\[
m_t = \max \left( \log ( f_t ) + m_{t-1}, \log ( i_t ) \right).
\]

This quantity keeps track of the relative scale of the forget and input contributions over time. The recurrence is built from logarithms of the gates, which is the key to making the stabilization work. Rather than directly manipulating large exponential values, the model compares them in log space and stores the running maximum.

Intuitively, $m_t$ acts like a reference level that follows the largest relevant gate magnitude seen so far. If the forget contribution accumulated from previous steps is large, or if the current input gate is large, then $m_t$ increases to reflect that scale. By maintaining this reference, the model can subtract off large common factors before exponentiating, preventing the raw gate values from blowing up.

The paper explicitly uses the stabilizer state as part of the gate reparameterization in both sLSTM and mLSTM. It is not an optional trick added afterward, but an essential ingredient in the definition of the stabilized gates.

# Log-domain reasoning for stable recurrent updates

The recurrence of xLSTM naturally suggests a log-domain interpretation. The memory update contains products and sums of gate values over time, and such expressions can become numerically unwieldy when exponentials are involved. The stabilizer state addresses this by representing the scale of the recurrence in terms of logarithms.

This is especially important because recurrent updates are multiplicative across time. In the stabilized formulation, the model does not directly attempt to store the full product of many exponentials. Instead, it tracks the dominant scale through $m_t$ and expresses the effective gates relative to this scale. That is why the stabilization formulas involve terms like $\log(i_t)$, $\log(f_t)$, and differences involving $m_t$ and $m_{t-1}$.

The paper also connects this design to the normalizer state in sLSTM and mLSTM. In both cases, the normalizer is designed to keep track of the total strength of the gates. Stabilization and normalization therefore work together: one prevents overflow, while the other ensures that the hidden state is properly scaled when the memory contents are read out.

# Derivation of stabilized input and forget gates

In sLSTM, the forward pass is originally defined with exponential input gates and either sigmoid or exponential forget gates. To stabilize the exponential version, the paper defines new gates

\[
i'_t = \exp \left( \log \left( i_t \right) - m_t \right) = \exp \left( \tilde{i}_t - m_t \right),
\]

\[
f'_t = \exp \left( \log \left( f_t \right) + m_{t-1} - m_t \right).
\]

These stabilized gates are constructed so that the large exponential scale is factored out through the stabilizer state. The key idea is that $m_t$ absorbs the largest relevant magnitude, and the remaining terms are brought back into a numerically manageable range.

The derivation is straightforward in spirit: if one wants to compute recurrent products of gates without overflow, one should first factor out the dominant scale. The paper does this in a recurrent way, which is why the stabilizer state depends on the previous stabilizer value $m_{t-1}$ and the current gate values. This ensures consistency over time and makes the update compatible with the recurrent structure of the model.

Importantly, the paper states that replacing $f_t$ by $f'_t$ and $i_t$ by $i'_t$ in the forward pass does not change the output of the whole network or the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters. This means the stabilized gates are a reparameterization, not a different model.

# Connection to numerically stable softmax-style computations

The stabilization mechanism used in xLSTM is closely related in spirit to numerically stable softmax computations. In softmax, one subtracts the maximum logit before exponentiating so that the largest exponent becomes manageable and the others are measured relative to it. This avoids overflow while preserving the result up to an exact normalization.

xLSTM uses the same general idea, but in a recurrent setting. Instead of subtracting a maximum from a fixed vector of logits, the model maintains the stabilizer state $m_t$ over time. The recurrence

\[
m_t = \max \left( \log ( f_t ) + m_{t-1}, \log ( i_t ) \right)
\]

plays a role analogous to the “subtract the maximum” trick: it identifies the dominating scale and uses it to stabilize exponentials. The stabilized gates are then computed by exponentiating quantities measured relative to this scale.

This analogy is useful because it shows that the stabilization is not an ad hoc fix. It is a principled numerical method based on the same general log-sum-exp intuition that underlies stable softmax-like computations. In xLSTM, however, the idea is applied to recurrent gating and memory updates rather than to normalization across classes.

# Why stabilization preserves functional equivalence

A central claim in the paper is that the stabilized version is functionally equivalent to the original exponential-gate formulation. More precisely, substituting the stabilized gates $f'_t$ and $i'_t$ for the original gates does not change the output of the entire network, nor does it change the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters.

This equivalence matters because it guarantees that stabilization is purely numerical. The model does not lose expressive power, and training does not optimize a different objective. Instead, the computation is reorganized so that the same mathematical function is evaluated in a safer way. That is exactly what is needed when a theoretically attractive mechanism, such as exponential gating, must be made practical for real training runs.

The paper emphasizes this point because stabilization would be much less useful if it altered the network’s behavior in uncontrolled ways. Here, the stabilization is designed so that the recurrence, the memory dynamics, and the gradient computations remain intact. The result is a numerically stable implementation of the same architecture.

# Implications for gradient propagation and optimization

The reason stabilization is so valuable is that xLSTM is designed to scale to large models and long sequences. In such settings, gradient behavior is crucial. Without stabilization, very large exponential gate values could make training unstable, either through overflow in the forward pass or through badly conditioned derivatives.

By keeping the gate computations within a manageable numerical range, the stabilizer state helps preserve the intended flow of information through time. This is especially important for xLSTM because the model aims to overcome limitations of standard LSTMs while retaining their recurrent strengths. A gated recurrent architecture only works well if its gates can be computed reliably over long sequences.

The paper does not present stabilization as a separate optimization technique in the abstract sense, but as a necessary step for enabling the whole xLSTM design. In that sense, stabilization supports the broader training objective: making exponential gating compatible with large-scale language modeling.

# Practical considerations for implementation

In practice, the paper applies the same stabilization technique to both sLSTM and mLSTM. In sLSTM, the stabilized gates are used in the scalar memory update and normalizer update. In mLSTM, the same stabilization is used alongside the matrix memory update and the matrix normalizer state. Thus, the stabilization mechanism is shared across both variants even though the memory structures are different.

The paper also notes that the forget gate initialization must be chosen carefully. This is listed among the model’s limitations, which indicates that stabilization alone does not eliminate all numerical sensitivity. Careful design of the gate parameters still matters for successful training.

From an implementation perspective, the stabilized formulation is useful because it turns potentially dangerous exponentials into controlled quantities. This is particularly important for long sequences, where repeated multiplication or accumulation would otherwise amplify numerical problems. The paper’s overall message is that exponential gating is viable only when paired with the stabilizer state and the corresponding normalization logic.

Finally, the stabilization is part of what makes xLSTM practical at scale. The paper’s experiments show that xLSTM can be trained effectively and can perform favorably against other modern sequence models. That result depends not just on the new memory structures, but also on the numerical care that makes the gates computable in the first place.

7. Advanced Memory Mixing in sLSTM

# Multiple scalar memory cells in an sLSTM layer

The scalar sLSTM extends the original LSTM idea by keeping the basic scalar memory-cell update, but it is not restricted to just one memory cell. In the paper’s formulation, multiple scalar memory cells can be combined into a vector, just as in later LSTM formulations. This means that instead of maintaining a single scalar state $c_t$, an sLSTM layer can maintain several scalar memory cells at once. The role of each cell is still to store and update information over time, but now the layer has a richer internal state because it can distribute information across multiple cells rather than compressing everything into one.

This matters because the paper’s goal is not just to preserve information, but to improve how information is organized and revised as a sequence unfolds. A single scalar memory cell can store only a limited amount of information in a compressed form. By using multiple cells, the sLSTM creates a larger internal workspace for memory. The paper emphasizes that this is one part of the new memory-mixing behavior: the cells are not isolated in the layer, but can interact through recurrent connections. In this sense, the extension to multiple cells is not merely a size increase; it is part of a larger structural change in how memory is handled.

# Recurrent mixing matrices for cell input and gates

In the original scalar LSTM, the cell input and gates depend on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ through learned weights. The sLSTM preserves this basic idea, but when multiple memory cells are used, the recurrent connections become matrix-valued. The paper states that the hidden-state vector $\mathbf{h}$ can influence the memory cell input $\mathbf{z}$ and the gates $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{f}$, and $\mathbf{o}$ through recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{z}}$, $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{i}}$, $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{f}}$, and $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{o}}$.

This is what the paper calls memory mixing. The hidden state from one step does not merely feed back into a separate scalar gate computation; instead, it mixes information across the memory cells. This makes the next update depend on a transformed combination of the previous memory output. Intuitively, memory mixing allows one cell’s contents to influence how other cells are updated, gated, or read out. That is a stronger and more flexible form of recurrence than a completely separable update, because it allows the model to build internal dependencies among its memory components.

The paper also notes that exponential gating contributes to this new memory-mixing behavior. So the mixing effect is not only about the existence of recurrent matrices, but also about how the gates themselves are formed and stabilized in the sLSTM. The gates can be input-dependent and hidden-dependent, and their exponential form changes how strongly information is retained or revised.

# Within-head memory mixing versus across-head independence

The paper introduces heads as another organizational level for sLSTM memory. An sLSTM can have multiple heads, but the important detail is that memory mixing does not occur across heads. Instead, it happens within each head, across the memory cells assigned to that head. So the model is partitioned into separate memory-mixing groups, and each group processes its own internal recurrent interactions.

This distinction is important because it shows that the paper is not using heads in the same way as attention heads in a Transformer. Here, a head is a unit of recurrent memory organization. Inside a head, memory cells can interact through the recurrent mixing matrices, but different heads remain independent. That means the model can maintain several separate streams of recurrent processing at once, while still preserving a controlled form of mixing within each stream.

The paper presents this as a new way of memory mixing. The novelty is not simply “more heads” or “more cells,” but the combination of heads with exponential gating and recurrent mixing inside each head. This structure gives the sLSTM a more modular memory organization: each head can specialize, while still performing rich internal state updates.

# Multi-head sLSTM as a new form of recurrent memory organization

Multi-head sLSTM should be understood as a recurrent memory architecture rather than as a direct copy of Transformer-style attention heads. The paper’s description makes clear that in sLSTM, heads are introduced together with memory cells and recurrent mixing. This creates a layered organization: within a head, multiple memory cells can mix, but across heads there is no mixing. As a result, the model can maintain several recurrent subspaces in parallel.

This organization is significant because it changes the way information can be stored and processed over time. Instead of forcing all temporal information through one shared memory pathway, the model can distribute different kinds of information across heads. At the same time, because each head still has internal recurrence and memory mixing, each head can perform meaningful state tracking on its own.

The paper positions this as an extension of the LSTM family rather than a replacement of its core logic. The essential LSTM idea remains: memory is updated recurrently and gated selectively. What changes is the structure in which these updates occur. Multi-head sLSTM creates a recurrent system with multiple specialized memory compartments, each capable of internal mixing.

# Why memory mixing increases expressivity

The paper argues that memory mixing makes LSTMs more expressive than architectures that do not have it. The central intuition is that mixing allows the model to revise and reorganize stored information as new input arrives. If memory components are independent, then each component can only preserve its own local summary. If they are mixed recurrently, then the model can use previous hidden information to reshape future updates across multiple memory cells.

This increased expressivity is connected in the paper to the ability to separate histories or contexts. An xLSTM block is designed to non-linearly summarize the past in a high-dimensional space so that different histories can be better separated. Within the sLSTM, memory mixing contributes to that goal by allowing the recurrence to form richer internal representations. The model is therefore not merely remembering more; it is building a more structured memory of the past.

The paper also links this to the idea that LSTMs excel at learning abstractions and storing semantic information in memory cells. Memory mixing gives the model additional freedom in how it organizes those abstractions. Because the hidden state influences future cell updates and gates through recurrent matrices, the memory system can represent more than a simple linear accumulation of past inputs.

# State tracking as a core capability enabled by recurrent mixing

A major point in the paper is that memory mixing is crucial for state tracking. The authors explicitly connect this to formal language tasks, where a model must keep track of an evolving discrete state as the sequence unfolds. In their synthetic experiments, models without memory mixing, such as Transformers or State Space Models in the tested configurations, cannot solve certain regular grammars, such as parity. The paper interprets this as evidence that state tracking is a capability those models lack in the tested setting.

In contrast, sLSTM with memory mixing is designed to support exactly this kind of behavior. Because recurrent connections allow the hidden state to influence future memory updates, the model can revise its internal state as it processes new symbols. This is especially important when the correct output depends not just on accumulated evidence, but on maintaining a latent state that changes according to the sequence’s structure.

The paper’s formal language experiments are used to support this claim. They are presented as a test of whether the model can handle problems requiring state tracking, and the results are used to argue that the new sLSTM’s memory mixing contributes directly to solving such tasks. In other words, memory mixing is not just a capacity enhancement; it is a mechanism for representing and updating the kinds of latent states needed in structured sequence processing.

# Contrast with architectures lacking memory mixing

The paper contrasts sLSTM with architectures that do not have memory mixing. In particular, the mLSTM is explicitly designed to abandon hidden-hidden memory mixing in order to become fully parallelizable. More broadly, the paper contrasts xLSTM with Transformers, State Space Models, and other recurrent approaches that do not use the same recurrent mixing structure. The key claim is that without memory mixing, a model is less able to solve certain state tracking problems.

This contrast is not presented as a generic statement about all sequence models, but as a specific claim supported by the paper’s synthetic tasks. On those tasks, models without memory mixing fail on examples that require revising an internal discrete state during sequence processing. The parity task is the clearest example given: the paper says that Transformers or State Space Models without memory mixing cannot solve such regular grammars in the tested setup.

So the paper uses these comparisons to make a structural point. Memory mixing changes not only performance numbers, but the class of problems the model can represent effectively. The absence of memory mixing is associated with weaker state tracking, while its presence supports richer recurrent computation. This is why the paper treats sLSTM as a meaningful extension of the LSTM family rather than just another gated recurrent variant.

# Trade-off between expressivity and parallelizability

The paper is careful to emphasize that memory mixing has a cost. In sLSTM, memory mixing through hidden-hidden connections makes the recurrence sequential, which means the model is not fully parallelizable. This is one of the reasons why sLSTM cannot be implemented with the same kind of parallel efficiency as mLSTM. The recurrent dependencies require processing one time step after another.

At the same time, the paper argues that this cost buys expressive power. The trade-off is therefore between computational parallelizability and recurrent flexibility. sLSTM keeps the recurrent mixing because it supports state tracking and richer memory interactions. mLSTM removes memory mixing to regain parallelizability, but that comes with a different memory structure based on a matrix memory and covariance updates.

The paper’s overall design makes this trade-off explicit. The sLSTM represents the more sequential, mixing-rich end of the spectrum, while mLSTM represents the more parallelizable end. For the reader, the important takeaway is that memory mixing is not an accidental detail: it is the mechanism that gives sLSTM its state-tracking capability, even though it reduces parallel execution efficiency.

8. mLSTM: Matrix Memory for High-Capacity Associative Storage

# Motivation for moving from scalar to matrix memory

The mLSTM is introduced in xLSTM to address one of the main limitations of the original LSTM: its restricted storage capacity. In the standard LSTM, the memory cell is a scalar, or more generally a collection of scalar cells, and information must be compressed into those scalar states. That makes the memory compact, but it also limits how much structure can be retained and how precisely different pieces of information can be distinguished later. The paper uses rare token prediction and associative recall as examples of where this matters: when the model must remember many distinct items, scalar memory can become a bottleneck.

The mLSTM changes the memory cell from a scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$ to a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This is not just a larger container in a superficial sense. The matrix memory lets the model store interactions between vectors, which gives it a richer internal structure for memory and retrieval. In the paper’s framing, this is a way to improve the capacity of the LSTM while preserving the general recurrent idea of storing something over time and later using it to produce an output.

The key intuition is that if a scalar memory stores one compressed summary, a matrix can store many relationships at once. That makes the memory suitable for associative retrieval: the model sees a pattern now, stores it in the memory, and later uses a related cue to recover the associated content. This is the role mLSTM is designed to play.

# Associative memory and the key-value retrieval perspective

The mLSTM is described using the language of associative memory. At time $t$, the model stores a pair consisting of a key $\mathbf{k}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and a value $\mathbf{v}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$. Later, at a future time $t+\tau$, the model receives a query vector $\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and uses it to retrieve the value that was associated with a relevant key. This is the associative-memory viewpoint: keys index memories, values are what should be recovered, and queries are the cues used for retrieval.

This framing is borrowed from the Transformer terminology, but in mLSTM it is embedded inside a recurrent memory system. The goal is not to perform attention in the Transformer sense, but to realize a similar kind of key-value association using a recurrent update rule and a matrix memory. The paper explicitly connects this to bidirectional associative memories, which historically studied how one vector can cue the retrieval of another.

The intuition is simple: if the memory has stored many pairs of the form “this key goes with this value,” then a query similar to a stored key should return the corresponding value. The matrix memory in mLSTM is the mechanism used to make this possible.

# Connection to Bidirectional Associative Memories

The paper notes that the setting of mLSTM is the setting of Bidirectional Associative Memories, or BAMs. In this view, memory does not merely hold a hidden representation of the past. Instead, it stores associations between two vector spaces so that one vector can later evoke another. This is precisely the relationship between keys and values in the mLSTM description.

BAMs provide the conceptual foundation for thinking about the matrix memory as an associative structure. The model does not need to memorize an entire sequence verbatim; it needs to preserve associations in a form that can be queried later. The matrix memory is therefore not an arbitrary design choice. It is a structured memory that supports recall through vector interactions.

This is important because the paper contrasts mLSTM with the scalar-memory LSTM. A scalar cell can only store a limited amount of information about the past, whereas an associative matrix can store many pairwise relations. The BAM perspective helps explain why this should improve memory capacity: the model is no longer just tracking a single running quantity, but storing a collection of associations that can be accessed through matching queries.

# The covariance update rule for storing value-key pairs

The central memory update rule in mLSTM is the covariance update

$$
\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top .
$$

This rule adds an outer product between the value vector and the key vector to the previous memory matrix. The result is a matrix that accumulates associations over time. Each outer product contributes a structured update that links the current value with the current key.

The paper refers to this as a covariance update rule and notes that it is optimal for maximizing the separability of retrieved binary vectors, which is equivalent to maximizing signal-to-noise ratio in the retrieval setting. The intuition is that the update should store associations in a way that makes later retrieval as clean as possible. A key and a value are paired in a rank-one update, and these updates accumulate in the matrix memory.

The paper also explains that this rule is connected to Fast Weight Programmers. Those methods similarly maintain a rapidly changing weight matrix that is updated online. In mLSTM, however, the update is integrated into the LSTM framework by interpreting the forget gate as a decay factor and the input gate as a learning-rate factor. That gives the memory update a recurrent-gated structure rather than an unconstrained additive one.

# mLSTM cell state as a memory matrix

In mLSTM, the cell state is the matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$. This matrix plays the same conceptual role as the scalar cell state in the original LSTM: it is the long-lived memory that carries information forward through time. The difference is that the stored information is now structured as a matrix rather than a scalar or vector.

The update in the recurrent formulation is

$$
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top .
$$

This looks like a gated version of the covariance rule. The previous memory is decayed by $f_t$, while the new key-value association is inserted with strength $i_t$. In this way, the matrix memory is not merely accumulating associations passively; it is being regulated by the same gating principles that are central to LSTM.

The paper emphasizes that this memory update is parameter-free in the sense that the memory matrix itself does not require learned parameters. Instead, the parameters are used to compute the key, value, query, and gates from the input. The memory content evolves dynamically from the stream of data.

# The matrix normalizer state and its meaning

Alongside the matrix memory, mLSTM maintains a normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$. This state is updated by

$$
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t .
$$

The normalizer state tracks the weighted sum of key vectors, where each key is weighted by the input gate and by the future forget gates. The paper says this state keeps record of the strength of the gates. In other words, it summarizes how much memory has been written and how strongly it should influence retrieval.

This normalizer is essential because retrieval from a matrix memory can otherwise have unstable scale. When the model computes a query against the stored memory, it also needs to account for how much evidence has been accumulated in the normalizer. The purpose of $\mathbf{n}_t$ is therefore not to store content in the same way as $\mathbf{C}_t$, but to record the effective magnitude of the stored associations so that the hidden state can be normalized appropriately.

The paper’s description makes clear that this normalizer is a stabilizing companion to the matrix memory. It does not replace the memory matrix; it helps make the matrix memory usable in practice.

# Query, key, and value projections from inputs

The mLSTM computes query, key, and value vectors from the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ through learned linear projections. The equations are

$$
\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q,
$$

$$
\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k,
$$

$$
\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v.
$$

These projections define the components used in associative storage and retrieval. The query is what will later be used to retrieve content, the key is what identifies the stored association, and the value is the content associated with that key. The scaling of the key by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}$ is part of the paper’s formulation.

The gates are also computed from the input. The input gate and forget gate are scalar in the formulation shown in the paper, and the output gate is vector-valued:

$$
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad \tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_i,
$$

$$
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t) \ \text{or} \ \exp(\tilde{f}_t), \qquad \tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_f,
$$

$$
\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t), \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t = \mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{o}} \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_{\mathbf{o}}.
$$

So the input determines not only what is written and queried, but also how strongly the memory is updated and exposed.

# Retrieval through matrix-vector multiplication

The hidden state in mLSTM is computed by using the query vector to read from the matrix memory. The unnormalized retrieved vector is

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \}}.
$$

This is the core retrieval operation. The matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ acts on the query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$ to produce a vector that summarizes the memory content relevant to the query. Because the memory is a matrix, the interaction is richer than a scalar update: the query can interact with different directions in the memory space and recover a corresponding output vector.

The hidden state is then gated by the output gate:

$$
\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t.
$$

So retrieval produces a candidate hidden vector, and the output gate controls how much of that retrieved information is exposed to the rest of the network. This preserves the LSTM principle that memory content and visible output are not the same thing.

# Denominator normalization using query-normalizer interaction

A distinctive part of the mLSTM retrieval rule is the denominator

$$
\max\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \}.
$$

This quantity normalizes the retrieved vector using the interaction between the query and the normalizer state. The paper explains that the dot product between the query and the normalizer can be close to zero, so a stabilization step is needed. Without such a denominator, the retrieved output could become numerically unstable or badly scaled.

The role of this denominator is to regulate the magnitude of the retrieved content based on the accumulated strength of what has been stored. Since $\mathbf{n}_t$ records the weighted key history, the dot product with the query gives a measure of how strongly the current query aligns with the stored memory statistics. Normalizing by this interaction keeps retrieval grounded in the amount of evidence actually present in memory.

The use of the maximum with $1$ is a further safeguard. It prevents division by very small values and ensures that the normalization remains well behaved even when the query and normalizer are nearly orthogonal.

# Absolute-value and lower-bound stabilization in retrieval

The paper specifically states that it uses the absolute value of the query-normalizer dot product and lower bounds it by a threshold, typically $1.0$. This is done because the dot product can be small or even potentially unstable in sign and magnitude. By taking the absolute value, the model avoids sign-related cancellation issues in the denominator. By lower bounding it, the model avoids excessive amplification of the retrieved vector.

This stabilization is part of the broader effort to make exponential gating and matrix memory practical. The memory can be powerful, but because it relies on multiplicative and exponential components, it also needs careful numerical control. The denominator is therefore not an incidental implementation detail; it is a key part of making retrieval reliable.

In the paper’s formulation, this stabilization is analogous to the stabilizer state used for the exponential gates in sLSTM. The mLSTM uses the same stabilization ideas to ensure that the benefits of matrix memory do not come at the cost of unstable dynamics.

# Interpretation of input and forget gates as learning and decay rates

One of the important conceptual points in mLSTM is that the gates can be interpreted as learning and decay rates. The forget gate multiplies the previous memory matrix $\mathbf{C}_{t-1}$, so it acts like a decay factor: it controls how much of the old memory is retained. The input gate multiplies the new outer-product update $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$, so it acts like a learning rate: it controls how strongly new information is written into memory.

This interpretation connects mLSTM to the covariance update rule and to fast-weight methods. The paper makes clear that the gating is what turns the basic associative-memory update into a recurrent neural mechanism. The model can selectively keep, overwrite, or attenuate memory based on the current input and the previous hidden state.

The use of exponential gates is also important here. In mLSTM, the input gate is exponential, and the forget gate can be either sigmoid or exponential. The exponential formulation is part of xLSTM’s broader strategy for enabling storage decisions to be revised more flexibly than with standard sigmoid-only gating.

# Output gating for controlling exposed hidden state

Finally, the output gate determines how much of the retrieved memory is visible to the next layers. After the matrix memory is queried and normalized, the result is multiplied elementwise by the output gate:

$$
\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t.
$$

This keeps the familiar LSTM separation between internal memory and exposed hidden state. The memory matrix can store a large amount of information, but the hidden state that flows forward in the network is still filtered. The output gate therefore provides control over what portion of the retrieved associative content becomes part of the model’s active representation.

In the context of xLSTM, this gating is especially important because the matrix memory is meant to support high-capacity recall without sacrificing the ability to regulate what is used downstream. The output gate ensures that the memory system remains selective, not merely expansive. It is part of what allows mLSTM to function as a controlled associative memory rather than an unbounded accumulator of retrieved content.

9. mLSTM in Context: Fast Weights, Linear Attention, and Parallel Recurrence

# Covariance memories and optimal separability arguments

The mLSTM variant in xLSTM replaces the original scalar LSTM memory with a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This change is central to its role as a memory-augmented sequence model, because it lets the model store and retrieve information through matrix multiplication rather than through a single scalar state. In the paper, the memory is designed around a key-value setting: at time $t$, the model stores a key $\mathbf{k}_t$ together with a value $\mathbf{v}_t$, and later retrieves the value using a query $\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau}$. That is exactly the kind of associative memory problem that motivates the covariance update rule.

The update itself is
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top .
\]
This is called a covariance update rule in the paper. The intuition is that each new key-value pair contributes an outer product, which adds a structured association to memory. The paper emphasizes that if a layer normalization is applied before projecting inputs to keys and values, then these vectors have zero mean. In that setting, the covariance update rule is said to be optimal for maximal separability of retrieved binary vectors, which the paper identifies with a maximal signal-to-noise ratio. In other words, the update rule is chosen so that associations stored in memory can be separated as clearly as possible when later queried.

The paper also notes an important trade-off. If retrieval is limited to pairwise interactions, then higher separability can be achieved by allowing quadratic complexity, as in attention-style mechanisms. This places mLSTM in a middle ground: it uses an associative matrix memory to enhance storage capacity, but it still follows a recurrent update structure. That makes it a memory model inspired by associative recall and fast weight ideas, while remaining part of the LSTM family.

# Relationship to Fast Weight Programmers

The paper explicitly states that the covariance update rule is equivalent to Fast Weight Programmers. This is an important conceptual link because it shows that mLSTM is not inventing a completely new memory principle, but rather integrating a known associative memory mechanism into the LSTM framework. Fast Weight Programmers had already used matrix-like memory updates to store relationships between inputs, and the xLSTM paper builds directly on that idea.

The paper also notes that later versions of fast weight methods introduced a constant decay rate multiplied to $\mathbf{C}_{t-1}$ and a constant learning rate multiplied to the outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. In the mLSTM formulation, these roles are taken over by the forget gate and input gate, respectively. The forget gate acts like a decay rate on past memory, while the input gate acts like a learning rate that determines how strongly the new key-value association is written. This is precisely how the LSTM gating idea is merged with the fast-weight-style memory update.

What makes this integration distinctive is that the output gate still controls how the retrieved information is exposed as hidden state. So the mLSTM is not merely a fast-weight model with gating added afterward. Instead, the gating, update rule, and retrieval operation are all integrated into the recurrent cell. The paper’s framing makes this a deliberate extension of LSTM rather than a separate architecture.

# Connections to RWKV, Retention, Linear Transformers, and HGRN2

The paper situates mLSTM among a set of recent methods that also aim to improve efficiency or memory behavior in sequence modeling. In the related work discussion, it names RWKV-5 and RWKV-6, Retention, Linear Transformer, and HGRN2 as methods that build on a covariance-style update mechanism or closely related ideas. These models are grouped with mLSTM because they all share some version of a memory or accumulation process that supports sequence modeling without standard quadratic attention.

The closest conceptual relatives identified by the paper are Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2. These approaches share matrix memory and/or gating, which makes them similar in spirit to mLSTM. However, the paper draws a clear distinction: unlike the new sLSTM, these approaches do not allow memory mixing. That matters because the paper argues that memory mixing enables state tracking, and state tracking is necessary for tasks like code evaluation and tracking entities in long narratives. So while RWKV-style and retention-style models are close to mLSTM in their use of gated accumulation or memory-like state, xLSTM’s broader design emphasizes a more expressive recurrent structure through the inclusion of sLSTM alongside mLSTM.

The paper also connects linear Transformer variants to this broader family. Linear Transformers, Retention, and similar methods are motivated by the desire to avoid the quadratic cost of standard attention. mLSTM shares that motivation for efficiency, but it does so through recurrent matrix memory rather than through a direct reformulation of attention. This is why the paper presents mLSTM as part of a wider design space of sequence models that includes attention approximations, state space models, and recurrent neural networks, while still preserving a distinct LSTM-based memory principle.

# Similarity and differences between mLSTM and attention-style key-value memory

mLSTM is close to attention-style memory in that both use key-value associations. The paper explicitly adopts Transformer terminology for the mLSTM memory: the model stores a key $\mathbf{k}_t$ and a value $\mathbf{v}_t$, and later uses a query $\mathbf{q}_t$ to retrieve information. This makes the analogy to attention very natural. In both cases, retrieval depends on compatibility between a query and previously stored content.

But the mechanism is different. In attention, retrieval is usually based on comparing the query to many stored keys and forming weighted combinations of values. In mLSTM, the memory is compressed into a matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$, and retrieval happens through a single matrix multiplication:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t \, / \, \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}.
\]
This means the key-value associations are not kept as a list of explicit pairs to be scanned one by one at every step. Instead, they are accumulated into the matrix state. The paper presents this as a way to increase storage capacity while retaining a recurrent structure.

The paper also introduces a normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$ for mLSTM. This normalizer is the weighted sum of key vectors, where each key is weighted by the input gate and all future forget gates. Its role is to track the strength of the stored associations and stabilize retrieval. Because the dot product $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ can be close to zero, the paper uses the absolute value and lower bounds it by a threshold, typically $1.0$. This stabilization step shows that mLSTM is not just a raw associative memory; it is a carefully controlled gated memory system designed to make retrieval numerically reliable.

# Why mLSTM drops hidden-to-hidden memory mixing

One of the paper’s key design choices is that mLSTM abandons memory mixing, meaning it removes hidden-hidden recurrent connections from the memory update path. This is done specifically to enable parallelization. In the original LSTM, the hidden state at time $t-1$ influences the gates and cell input at time $t$, which creates sequential dependencies. Those dependencies force the computation to proceed step by step.

In mLSTM, the paper removes this kind of memory mixing so that the recurrence can be reformulated into a parallel version. The gates in the mLSTM forward pass are computed directly from the input $\mathbf{x}_t$:
\[
\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q,\quad
\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k,\quad
\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v,
\]
with the input and forget gates also depending on $\mathbf{x}_t$ alone in the formulation shown in the paper. This is a deliberate simplification relative to the original LSTM. By removing hidden-to-hidden recurrence from the memory update, the memory evolution becomes more amenable to parallel computation.

The trade-off is that the model gives up the kind of memory mixing available in sLSTM. The paper is explicit that mLSTM does not have this feature. But that is precisely the point: mLSTM is the fully parallelizable branch of xLSTM, designed to get the benefits of matrix memory without the sequential bottleneck caused by recurrent mixing.

# Equivalence of multiple heads and multiple cells in mLSTM

The paper notes that mLSTM can have multiple memory cells like the original LSTM. It also states that for mLSTM, multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent because there is no memory mixing. This equivalence follows from the fact that the different memory components do not interact through recurrent hidden-hidden connections. As a result, splitting the memory into heads or cells does not change the essential computational structure.

This is different from sLSTM, where the introduction of heads together with exponential gating creates a new form of memory mixing. In mLSTM, by contrast, the absence of memory mixing makes the organizational choice between heads and cells less consequential. The memory units can be viewed as parallel associative modules, each operating independently within the same general matrix-memory framework.

This distinction matters conceptually because it highlights how the xLSTM family uses the same LSTM heritage in two different ways. One branch, sLSTM, explores expressive mixing across memory cells and heads. The other branch, mLSTM, prioritizes parallelizable matrix memory. The equivalence of heads and cells in mLSTM is another sign that the architecture is structurally simpler in its recurrence, even though the memory itself is more powerful than a scalar cell.

# How the recurrence can be reformulated for parallel execution

The paper states directly that because mLSTM has no memory mixing, its recurrence can be reformulated in a parallel version. The forward pass is expressed as a sequence of matrix updates and normalizations:
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t.
\]
These equations resemble a recurrent accumulation, but the lack of hidden-to-hidden mixing means the computation can be reorganized using standard matrix operations.

The paper compares this property to the parallelizability of FlashAttention and GLA. The key idea is that if each time step’s update depends only on the input and the previous memory state, and not on a recursively transformed hidden state, then the computation can be arranged more efficiently on modern hardware. The paper emphasizes that the mLSTM computations can be done in parallel on GPUs, so although the memory state is a $d \times d$ matrix, the wall-clock cost is moderated by parallel execution.

This is also why the paper frames mLSTM as “fully parallelizable.” The recurrence still exists mathematically, but its structure permits a reformulation that avoids the strict sequential bottleneck typical of recurrent networks. In that sense, mLSTM is a recurrence that behaves much more like a parallel sequence operator than like a classic step-by-step RNN.

# Benefits and costs of parallelizable matrix memory

The main benefit of mLSTM is the increase in storage capacity. By moving from a scalar memory to a matrix memory, the model can store key-value associations with much greater richness. This is especially relevant for associative recall and for tasks that require the model to remember and later retrieve multiple items. The paper presents mLSTM as a way to overcome the limited storage capacity of the original LSTM, which compresses information into scalar cell states.

At the same time, the paper is careful to point out the costs. A matrix memory of size $d \times d$ requires a $d \times d$ update and associated computation. That is more expensive than scalar updates. So mLSTM trades computational complexity for memory capacity. The paper argues that this trade-off is worthwhile because the computations are parallelizable and therefore the wall-clock overhead is not as severe as the matrix size might suggest.

The paper also notes that mLSTM memory does not require parameters. This is an important distinction: the memory is part of the runtime state, not a set of learned weights. So the model gains capacity without directly increasing parameter count in the memory itself. The overall picture is one of a computationally heavier but more expressive associative memory that remains practical because of GPU parallelism.

# Comparison of mLSTM memory capacity with Transformer attention

The paper compares mLSTM’s storage behavior to Transformer attention in a subtle way. On the one hand, attention is presented as a gold standard for associative recall in the Multi-Query Associative Recall task, because Transformers have a memory that is exponential in the coding dimension. On the other hand, mLSTM is still able to perform very well among non-Transformer models, and the paper highlights that xLSTM with an mLSTM component achieves the best results among the non-Transformer methods tested.

This comparison clarifies the role of mLSTM. It is not claimed to surpass attention by offering the same kind of memory structure. Instead, it provides a different route to strong associative memory: a recurrent matrix memory with gated covariance updates. The paper suggests that the matrix memory substantially improves storage capacities relative to scalar LSTM memory, and that this improvement shows up especially in harder associative recall settings.

At the same time, the paper’s experimental discussion indicates that the sLSTM block does not hurt memory capacity and may even leverage it further in the hardest recall settings. This reinforces the view that mLSTM is one part of the xLSTM design rather than a complete replacement for all other sequence mechanisms. Its main contribution is to give LSTM a much richer, parallelizable memory store that can compete well with other modern sequence models on long-context and associative tasks.

In summary, mLSTM sits at the intersection of recurrent memory, fast weights, and linearized sequence modeling. It inherits the LSTM philosophy of gated storage, but it replaces the scalar cell with a matrix associative memory and removes hidden-to-hidden mixing so that the recurrence can be parallelized. That combination is what makes mLSTM both conceptually close to older memory-based models and practically suited to modern large-scale sequence processing.

10. Building xLSTM Blocks and Full xLSTM Architectures

# Why residual architectures matter for deep sequence models

The paper does not treat sLSTM and mLSTM as isolated recurrent cells. Instead, it places them inside residual block architectures, because the authors want xLSTM to function as a modern deep sequence model in the same style as contemporary large language models. This matters because the paper’s goal is not only to modify the memory mechanism of LSTM, but also to scale it to billions of parameters in a way that is compatible with deep stacking.

Residual architectures provide a way to build very deep networks while preserving the ability to train them effectively. In the paper, xLSTM blocks are residually stacked, following the same broad design philosophy used in deep convolutional networks and Transformers. The residual path lets each block refine an existing representation rather than completely replace it. For sequence modeling, this is especially useful because each layer can contribute a different level of temporal abstraction while still keeping the information from earlier layers available.

The paper’s xLSTM design therefore combines recurrent memory with the residual-block paradigm of modern deep learning. This is an important architectural decision: it allows the LSTM idea to be extended from a classic recurrent cell into a scalable backbone for language modeling.

# The xLSTM block concept

An xLSTM block is the basic building unit of the architecture. The authors define it as a residual block that contains one of the new LSTM variants, either sLSTM or mLSTM, and surrounds it with linear projections and nonlinearity in a way that fits into a modern deep backbone. The purpose of the block is to nonlinearly summarize the past in a high-dimensional representation so that different histories or contexts become easier to separate.

This “summarize the past” viewpoint is central to the paper. The xLSTM block is not just a place where recurrence happens; it is a computational module that compresses sequence history into a representation useful for next-token prediction or other sequence tasks. The paper emphasizes that separating histories is a prerequisite for predicting the next sequence element correctly. In other words, if the model can keep distinct past contexts distinct in its internal representation, it can better decide what should come next.

The xLSTM block therefore acts as the place where the new memory mechanisms of sLSTM and mLSTM are embedded into a larger architecture. The block design is what makes these memory mechanisms usable at scale in stacked deep models.

# Cover’s theorem and nonlinear summarization in high-dimensional spaces

To justify the block design, the paper invokes Cover’s theorem. The relevant intuition is that patterns that are not linearly separable in a low-dimensional space are more likely to become separable after a nonlinear embedding into a higher-dimensional space. The authors use this idea to motivate why xLSTM blocks should include an up-projection into a higher-dimensional space before applying the memory mechanism or, depending on the block type, after it.

This is an architectural argument about representation geometry. The model should not merely store information; it should store information in a space where different histories can be disentangled. The paper frames this as a way to improve the model’s ability to predict the next element in a sequence, since prediction depends on choosing among competing histories or contexts.

The theorem is not used as a formal proof of performance. Rather, it serves as the conceptual guide for why the xLSTM block is built around dimension expansion and nonlinear processing. The block is designed to make the sequence history easier to separate, which is especially valuable when the model is used inside a deep residual architecture.

# Post up-projection residual blocks for sLSTM

For xLSTM blocks containing sLSTM, the paper uses a post up-projection residual block. The authors describe this as analogous to the block style used in Transformers. The structure first summarizes the past in the original space, and then linearly maps the result into a higher-dimensional space, applies a nonlinearity, and maps back to the original space.

The important point is that the sLSTM itself already includes memory mixing through hidden-hidden recurrent connections across cells. Because of this, the paper places the sLSTM in a residual block design that performs the expansion after the memory operation. This arrangement fits the role of sLSTM as a sequence-processing module that maintains scalar memory while also allowing richer interactions through memory mixing.

The paper presents this as a way to integrate the new sLSTM into a modern backbone without changing its basic recurrence logic. The post up-projection block is therefore part of the mechanism by which the sLSTM becomes a usable component inside large deep models.

# Pre up-projection residual blocks for mLSTM

For xLSTM blocks containing mLSTM, the paper uses a pre up-projection residual block. The structure linearly maps the input to a higher-dimensional space first, then performs the nonlinear summary in that high-dimensional space, and finally maps back to the original dimension. The paper notes that this is similar to the style used in State Space Models.

This choice is tied directly to the nature of mLSTM. Since mLSTM uses a matrix memory, its storage capacity is larger in a higher-dimensional space. The paper explicitly states that the mLSTM block uses the pre up-projection form because the memory capacity becomes larger in the high-dimensional space. So the projection is not a cosmetic choice; it is part of how the model takes advantage of the matrix-valued memory.

In this design, the expansion happens before the memory operation, which means the matrix memory acts on richer representations. That is consistent with the paper’s view of mLSTM as the variant intended to increase storage capacity and to support full parallelization.

# Why mLSTM benefits from high-dimensional memory space

The paper’s explanation for the mLSTM design is that increasing the memory from a scalar to a matrix naturally increases storage capacity. Since the matrix memory stores key-value associations through outer-product updates, it can preserve richer relationships than a scalar memory cell. Putting this mechanism into a higher-dimensional space makes the memory more expressive.

The block design reflects this storage perspective. In the paper, the mLSTM is associated with a matrix memory and a covariance-style update rule, so the block is constructed to give that memory a larger representational space to operate in. The paper treats this as a direct architectural advantage: the model can store more information and retrieve it more precisely when the memory is applied in a higher-dimensional setting.

This is one of the reasons the paper distinguishes mLSTM from sLSTM. The mLSTM is the variant that emphasizes larger storage capacity and parallelization, and the pre up-projection block is chosen to support that role.

# Pre-LayerNorm residual backbones in modern LLM design

The paper states that xLSTM architectures use pre-LayerNorm residual backbones, which are described as the most commonly used design in contemporary large language models. This places xLSTM within the standard modern deep-learning recipe for training stable, large residual networks.

The use of pre-LayerNorm is important because it is part of the backbone rather than part of the recurrent cell itself. The paper is not proposing a completely alien architecture; instead, it adopts the backbone organization common in today’s large models and inserts the new LSTM variants into it. This is one way xLSTM is made compatible with the scaling regime of modern language models.

The overall point is that the authors want xLSTM to be architecturally comparable to systems used in large-scale language modeling. The pre-LayerNorm residual backbone is therefore part of the engineering framework that allows the new memory mechanisms to be stacked deeply and trained at scale.

# Stacking xLSTM blocks into deep architectures

Once xLSTM blocks are defined, the full model is built by stacking them residually. The paper explicitly says that xLSTM architectures are constructed by residually stacking building blocks. This is the same broad strategy that enabled deep convolutional networks and Transformers to scale successfully.

The full xLSTM architecture is therefore not a single recurrent module but a deep stack of many such modules. Each block can refine the sequence representation passed from earlier blocks, and the residual connections preserve the flow of information through depth. The paper presents this as the architectural form that makes xLSTM suitable for large language modeling.

This stacking strategy is also where the new xLSTM block design becomes practically important. A single sLSTM or mLSTM cell would not suffice to compete with large modern models. The architecture becomes powerful only when many xLSTM blocks are layered together in a residual backbone.

# Interpreting xLSTM[a:b] block ratios

For the experiments, the paper introduces the notation xLSTM[$a:b$], where $a/b$ denotes the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. This notation is a compact way to describe how a model is composed from the two xLSTM block types.

For example, xLSTM[7:1] means that among eight blocks, seven are mLSTM-based and one is sLSTM-based. The paper gives a concrete example: if the total number of blocks is 48, then xLSTM[7:1] corresponds to 42 mLSTM-based blocks and 6 sLSTM-based blocks. This notation lets the paper compare different architectural mixtures systematically.

The ratio is not just bookkeeping. It expresses a design choice about how much of the model should emphasize matrix-memory parallel blocks and how much should emphasize scalar-memory blocks with memory mixing. By using this notation, the paper can study how different compositions affect performance on language modeling and synthetic tasks.

# Design rationale for mixing mLSTM and sLSTM blocks in one model

The paper presents xLSTM as a family with two complementary members: sLSTM and mLSTM. The sLSTM provides scalar memory with memory mixing, while the mLSTM provides matrix memory and full parallelization. Because they solve different limitations of the original LSTM, the paper uses them together in the same architecture.

The rationale for mixing them is that the two variants contribute different strengths. The mLSTM improves storage capacity and parallelization, while the sLSTM preserves the memory-mixing behavior that helps with state tracking and revision of storage decisions. The paper even notes that sLSTM can leverage memory capacity rather than diminishing it in the mixed models. This is why architectures like xLSTM[7:1] are meaningful: they are not arbitrary mixtures, but deliberate combinations of two recurrent block types.

In the architecture section, the paper describes xLSTM as an architecture family built by residually stacking these blocks. In the experiments, the ratio of mLSTM to sLSTM blocks becomes a way to tune the balance between capacity, memory mixing, and computational efficiency. The overall design goal is to make the full model suitable for large-scale language modeling while retaining the advantages introduced by both xLSTM variants.

11. Computational Complexity, Memory Efficiency, and Systems Considerations

# Linear computation and constant memory with respect to sequence length

A central systems claim of xLSTM is that, like other recurrent sequence models, it scales linearly with sequence length and uses constant memory with respect to sequence length. This is stated explicitly in the paper as a contrast to Transformers, whose attention mechanism grows quadratically with context length. The key intuition is that xLSTM processes a sequence step by step while maintaining a fixed-size internal state, rather than storing and comparing every past token against every current token.

This matters because the cost of processing a longer sequence does not force xLSTM to store a larger and larger history in the way attention-based models do. Instead, the model compresses what it has seen into its recurrent state. As a result, the sequence length mainly affects the number of recurrent updates, not the size of the model’s active memory footprint. The paper emphasizes that this makes xLSTM especially attractive for industrial use and edge deployment, where memory budgets are often limited.

# Why compressive memory differs from attention caches

The paper describes xLSTM memory as “compressive,” which is an important distinction from the memory usage pattern of Transformer inference. In a Transformer, a cache is used to retain past key-value pairs so that future tokens can attend to them. That cache grows with the sequence length, because every new token adds more stored information. In xLSTM, by contrast, the recurrent state summarizes history in compressed form rather than keeping a growing list of tokenwise representations.

This distinction is especially visible in the paper’s discussion of generation and throughput. Because xLSTM does not rely on an ever-expanding cache in the same way as attention does, it can handle larger batch sizes under the same memory constraints. The paper explicitly notes that this constant-memory property enables xLSTM to achieve high throughput. So the practical advantage is not only theoretical efficiency, but also the ability to run with more favorable batching and memory usage in actual inference settings.

# Computation cost of mLSTM’s $d$-by-$d$ matrix memory

The mLSTM increases storage capacity by replacing the scalar memory cell with a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This is powerful, but it comes with a clear computational price. Each update touches a full $d \times d$ matrix, and the hidden state retrieval also operates through matrix-vector multiplication. The paper is direct about this trade-off: the memory capacity is larger, but the computational cost is higher because both the memory and its update are quadratic in the hidden dimension.

The update rule is based on a covariance-style outer product, $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$, so the model is not merely storing a single value but a structured association between a key and a value. This makes mLSTM more expressive for storage-heavy tasks, but it also means that the model must process a much larger state object at each step. The paper frames this as a deliberate trade-off between memory capacity and computational complexity.

# Parallel matrix operations and their GPU advantages

Although the mLSTM’s matrix memory is computationally expensive, the paper stresses that its computations can be carried out in parallel on GPUs. This is an important systems point: a large operation is not necessarily a slow operation if it can be expressed as standard matrix operations that modern hardware executes efficiently. The paper states that the mLSTM recurrence can be reformulated in a parallel version because it has no memory mixing, and this makes it analogous in spirit to other parallelizable sequence methods such as FlashAttention and GLA.

The practical implication is that the higher arithmetic cost of the matrix memory does not translate directly into a proportional wall-clock penalty. In the paper’s view, GPU parallelism offsets much of the overhead. So while mLSTM has a heavier state update than a scalar-memory recurrent model, its implementation can still be efficient enough to be competitive in practice.

# Sequential bottleneck in sLSTM due to memory mixing

The sLSTM keeps the scalar-memory formulation but adds exponential gating and memory mixing. The paper is explicit that memory mixing is the source of its lack of parallelizability. Because the hidden state at time $t$ depends on the previous hidden state through recurrent connections, the computation must proceed sequentially across time steps. This creates a bottleneck that cannot be removed simply by batching or reordering operations.

This is the main systems drawback of sLSTM relative to mLSTM. The paper positions sLSTM as the variant that enriches the original LSTM’s ability to revise storage decisions and mix information across memory cells, but this gain in expressiveness comes at the cost of sequential execution. So sLSTM is not the fully parallel sequence model that mLSTM can be.

# CUDA optimization strategies mentioned in the paper

The paper mentions dedicated CUDA implementation work as a practical response to the sequential nature of sLSTM and the computational burden of mLSTM. For sLSTM, the authors report a fast CUDA implementation with GPU memory optimizations down to the register level. The stated result is that sLSTM is typically less than two times slower than mLSTM, despite its sequential memory mixing.

For mLSTM, the paper notes that the CUDA kernels are not yet optimized. This is presented as an implementation limitation rather than a conceptual one. The implication is that the current performance is not necessarily the ceiling for the architecture; rather, the software stack still leaves room for improvement. The paper suggests that faster kernels could be developed in the style of FlashAttention.

# Speed comparisons with FlashAttention and Mamba-style scans

The paper directly compares its current implementation speed against FlashAttention and the scan used in Mamba. It states that the current mLSTM implementation is about four times slower than FlashAttention or the Mamba scan. This comparison is useful because it separates architectural promise from engineering maturity. Even if xLSTM has favorable scaling properties, the exact runtime depends on how well the kernels are optimized.

At the same time, the paper emphasizes that the wall-clock impact of mLSTM’s complex memory is still minor relative to its representational benefits, since the memory update and retrieval can be parallelized with standard matrix operations. In other words, the cost exists, but it is not presented as prohibitive. The comparison is therefore not meant to dismiss mLSTM, but to clarify where the current implementation stands relative to established high-performance sequence kernels.

# Trade-offs between memory capacity, throughput, and latency

The paper repeatedly frames xLSTM as a set of trade-offs rather than a single universally superior choice. mLSTM offers greater storage capacity through its matrix memory, but it pays for that with a larger per-step computational burden. sLSTM retains sequential memory mixing and therefore cannot be fully parallelized, but it provides new gating behavior and can still be implemented efficiently enough to be useful. The paper’s experiments show that the two variants can be combined within architectures, and that different mixtures can be favorable for different tasks.

Throughput and latency follow from these design choices. The paper reports that xLSTM can use much higher batch sizes than Llama because of its constant memory, which leads to higher throughput. At the same time, the linear scaling in generation time is favorable compared with the quadratic scaling of Llama. Thus, xLSTM is presented as a system with practical advantages not only for batch throughput but also for running long sequences without the same memory growth pressure faced by attention-based models.

# Edge and industrial deployment implications

The paper explicitly states that xLSTM’s linear computation and constant memory complexity make it well suited for industrial applications and edge implementations. The reason is straightforward: systems with limited hardware budgets benefit from models whose memory footprint does not grow with context length. If a model can keep processing long sequences without storing an expanding cache, it becomes easier to deploy in environments where RAM and GPU memory are constrained.

This deployment angle is tied to the broader design goal of xLSTM. The architecture is not only about achieving strong language modeling performance, but also about making recurrent memory-based models viable at modern scale. The paper suggests that xLSTM’s compressive memory, favorable throughput, and scalable recurrent processing could make it attractive wherever long-context reasoning must be done under practical hardware constraints.

12. Positioning xLSTM Among Competing Sequence Models

# Linear attention approaches for long-context efficiency

A major motivation behind many recent sequence models is the high cost of standard Transformer self-attention, which scales quadratically with context length. The paper places several “linear attention” methods in this category because they attempt to reduce this cost while preserving some of the modeling advantages of attention. These methods include the Synthesizer, Linformer, Linear Transformer, Performer, Structured Global Convolution, and the Hyena hierarchy.

The common idea is to avoid full token-to-token interaction at every position, or to approximate it more cheaply. The Synthesizer learns synthetic attention weights without direct token-token interactions. Linformer uses a low-rank matrix to approximate self-attention. Linear Transformer reformulates attention in a linearized way, while Performer approximates the attention softmax using positive orthogonal random features. In other approaches, attention is replaced more radically by long convolutions, as in SGConv and Hyena.

From the perspective of the xLSTM paper, these methods are important because they target the same practical problem: how to process long contexts efficiently. However, they do so by modifying or replacing attention, whereas xLSTM comes from the recurrent side and extends the LSTM instead. This creates a useful contrast. The paper treats these models as part of the broader search for sequence models that scale better than standard Transformers in context length while still supporting strong language modeling performance.

# State-space models such as S4, DSS, GSS, S5, H3, and Mamba

The paper also groups a large family of State Space Models, or SSMs, as another major alternative to Transformers. These include S4, DSS, GSS, S5, BiGS, H3, and Mamba. Their appeal is similar to that of linear attention methods: they are linear in context length and therefore avoid the quadratic cost of full self-attention.

In the paper’s framing, SSMs represent a modern sequence-modeling paradigm that competes directly with Transformers on long contexts. They are presented as one of the key families that made it reasonable to ask whether LSTM-like models could be revived at scale. The paper highlights that SSMs can be strong language models, but also uses them as a point of comparison when evaluating whether xLSTM’s modifications are sufficient to make LSTM competitive again.

The relationship to xLSTM is especially important because the paper explicitly positions its own architecture alongside SSMs in terms of architectural design. xLSTM blocks can use a pre up-projection style similar to State Space Models, especially for mLSTM, where the memory capacity is increased in the high-dimensional space. So the comparison is not merely about performance; it is also architectural. xLSTM borrows the modern residual-block style of contemporary SSMs while keeping the recurrent memory idea at its core.

# Modern recurrent alternatives including RWKV, HGRN, and HGRN2

The paper also situates xLSTM among modern recurrent alternatives. These include RWKV-4, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, HGRN, and HGRN2. Like xLSTM, these models are recurrent, and like SSMs and linear attention methods, they are designed to avoid the quadratic cost of Transformer attention.

RWKV is especially important because it is presented as a well-known recurrent approach to large language modeling, with competitive performance to Transformers. HGRN and HGRN2 are similarly modern recurrent models that reuse gating ideas and aim to be efficient on long sequences. In the paper’s organization of the field, these models belong to the same general effort to build scalable sequence models without relying on standard self-attention.

What makes this family especially relevant to xLSTM is that it shows how recurrent modeling never disappeared; instead, it evolved. The paper presents xLSTM as a new member of this broader line of work, but one that modifies the LSTM idea more deeply than these alternatives do. In the experiments, the paper compares xLSTM directly against RWKV and HGRN-style models, reinforcing that they are among its closest practical competitors.

# The recurring importance of gating across model families

Gating is one of the central ideas that ties the paper’s related work together. In the original LSTM, gates control how information is written, retained, and read. The paper emphasizes that this idea has reappeared in many later sequence models, even outside the LSTM family.

The related work section notes that gating is used in HGRN, HGRN2, GLA, GSS, BiGS, MEGA, RWKV, and Mamba. This matters because it shows that gating is not merely an old LSTM trick, but a broadly useful mechanism for controlling information flow in recurrent and sequence models. In xLSTM, gating is taken seriously as a core design principle rather than as an auxiliary feature. The paper’s new exponential gating is an explicit attempt to extend this idea in a way that helps with storage revision and stability.

This commonality helps explain why xLSTM is placed near these models in the literature. Even when the underlying sequence-processing mechanism differs, many recent methods share the need to regulate how information is accumulated over time. The paper uses this observation to argue that xLSTM is not a disconnected novelty; it is part of a broader trend in which gating remains a powerful mechanism for sequence modeling.

# Matrix-memory and covariance-update approaches across the literature

A second major theme in the paper’s related work is the use of matrix memory and covariance-style updates. This is especially relevant to mLSTM, which replaces the scalar LSTM cell state with a matrix memory. The paper links this idea to several earlier and contemporary approaches.

The most direct historical connection is to Fast Weight Programmers, where memory is updated using an outer-product-like rule. The paper also mentions RWKV-5, RWKV-6, Retention, Linear Transformer, and HGRN2 as methods related to covariance update mechanisms or fast-weight-like memory. These models all explore ways of storing richer information than a simple scalar state while keeping the update rules structured and efficient.

In xLSTM, the covariance update rule takes the form of storing key-value associations through a matrix memory. The paper frames this as increasing the storage capacity of LSTMs, especially for associative recall tasks. This is one of the reasons mLSTM can be viewed as a natural extension of recurrent memory: it keeps the sequential update idea but upgrades the content that can be stored. In this sense, the paper’s matrix-memory contribution is not isolated; it belongs to a broader lineage of models that use structured memory updates to improve retrieval.

# Why Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2 are the closest relatives

Among all related methods, the paper explicitly says that the conceptually closest models to xLSTM are Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2. The reason is that these models share one or more of the same ingredients: matrix memory, gating, or both. This makes them especially relevant points of comparison when trying to understand where xLSTM sits.

The key difference, according to the paper, is memory mixing. Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2 do not allow the kind of memory mixing that the new sLSTM introduces. In xLSTM, memory mixing means hidden-hidden connections can influence updates, allowing the model to revise and refine stored information across time. This is presented as important for state tracking problems, which require following entities or dependencies over long narratives or code.

The paper’s argument is that these closest relatives may share surface similarities with xLSTM, but xLSTM adds something structurally essential. It does not merely use gates or structured memory; it combines them with recurrent memory mixing in the sLSTM, and with a fully parallelizable matrix-memory variant in the mLSTM. That combination is what the paper claims helps xLSTM move beyond what these related models can do.

# What distinguishes xLSTM from these related methods

xLSTM is distinguished in the paper by two coordinated modifications to the original LSTM: exponential gating and new memory structures. The gating change is meant to improve the model’s ability to revise storage decisions. The memory change produces two variants: sLSTM with scalar memory and memory mixing, and mLSTM with matrix memory and covariance updates.

This makes xLSTM different from both the Transformer-side models and the modern recurrent alternatives. Compared with linear attention or SSMs, xLSTM preserves explicit recurrent memory updates rather than replacing recurrence with another mechanism. Compared with RWKV, Retention, and related models, xLSTM adds memory mixing in sLSTM and a particular stabilized exponential-gating framework. Compared with standard LSTMs, it is more expressive in memory and more flexible in scaling.

The paper is careful to present these differences as practical, not merely formal. The synthetic tasks show that the two xLSTM variants specialize differently: sLSTM is important for state tracking and formal language tasks, while mLSTM is valuable for memory-intensive associative recall. The large-scale language experiments then show that combining the two in xLSTM architectures yields strong overall performance.

# Memory mixing as a key differentiator in expressivity

One of the paper’s central claims is that memory mixing is a crucial source of expressivity. In the LSTM family, memory mixing refers to hidden-hidden recurrent connections between states from one time step to the next. The paper argues that this mechanism is necessary for solving state tracking problems.

This point is used in the related work discussion to distinguish xLSTM from models that have structured memory but no memory mixing. The paper states that memory mixing enables state tracking, and therefore LSTMs are more expressive than State Space Models and Transformers for such problems. The formal language experiments support this claim: models without memory mixing cannot solve tasks like parity, while xLSTM can.

This is an important positioning statement. It suggests that the xLSTM paper is not merely proposing another efficient sequence model. It is arguing that a specific property of recurrent architectures, namely memory mixing, remains valuable and should be preserved even as models are scaled and modernized. In the paper’s view, this is one reason xLSTM can compete with or surpass other sequence models on tasks requiring long-term structure and entity tracking.

# Residual stacking as a common architectural pattern across modern models

The final piece of the landscape is architectural rather than algorithmic. The paper emphasizes that xLSTM architectures are built by residually stacking blocks, just as deep convolutional networks and Transformers are built from residual blocks. This is presented as part of the standard design pattern of modern large deep learning systems.

This places xLSTM in direct continuity with contemporary large language models. Instead of using a single recurrent layer in the older sense, xLSTM is embedded inside a residual block backbone, and many such blocks are stacked. The paper explicitly uses pre-LayerNorm residual backbones, which are common in modern LLMs. This means xLSTM is not only an LSTM extension at the cell level; it is also an LLM-style architecture at the system level.

The paper’s architectural discussion also notes two residual block styles: post up-projection, used for sLSTM, and pre up-projection, used for mLSTM. This mirrors design choices seen in Transformers and State Space Models and helps xLSTM integrate into the same modern scaling framework. In short, residual stacking is the mechanism that lets the paper take LSTM ideas and place them into the same class of deep architectures that made large Transformers successful.

Taken together, these points show where xLSTM fits. It is positioned as a modern recurrent architecture that draws from gating, matrix memory, and residual stacking, while directly comparing itself to linear attention methods, SSMs, and other recurrent models. The paper’s central claim is that xLSTM combines the strengths of these trends while preserving the recurrent memory mechanisms that make LSTMs especially expressive for certain sequence problems.

13. Synthetic Experiments: Testing Expressivity, Memory, and Long-Context Behavior

# Purpose of synthetic evaluations in architecture analysis

The paper uses synthetic evaluations to isolate specific properties of xLSTM that are difficult to disentangle in full language modeling. This is an important methodological step because real text corpora mix many phenomena at once: long-term dependencies, rare events, ambiguous context, and domain variation. By contrast, synthetic tasks can be designed so that success depends on one capability in particular. In this paper, the authors use such tasks to test whether the architectural changes in xLSTM actually address the limitations they identified in standard LSTMs.

The synthetic experiments are organized around three goals. First, they probe whether xLSTM can revise storage decisions while processing a sequence, which is tied to state tracking and memory mixing. Second, they test whether xLSTM can store and retrieve many key-value associations, which measures memory capacity. Third, they evaluate how the model behaves on long sequences, which gives a controlled view of long-context processing. In all three cases, the synthetic setting is meant to expose architectural behavior directly, before moving on to the more complicated setting of language modeling.

# Formal language tasks for testing state tracking and memory mixing

To test xLSTM’s exponential gating together with memory mixing, the paper uses formal language tasks adapted from prior work and extended to allow multi-length training for length extrapolation. These tasks are chosen because they require the model to keep track of the state of a computation as a sequence unfolds. The paper explicitly connects these tasks to state tracking problems, meaning that the model must maintain and update an internal representation of where it is in a structured sequence.

The authors compare xLSTM against a set of representative alternatives, including Transformers, State Space Models, Recurrent Neural Networks, Retention, Hyena, and LSTM-based baselines. The evaluation focuses on the tokens that are relevant to the task, and accuracy is normalized so that $0$ corresponds to random performance and $1$ to perfect performance. The experiments use 2-block architectures and compare xLSTM variants with different compositions: xLSTM[$0:1$] for only sLSTM, xLSTM[$1:0$] for only mLSTM, and xLSTM[$1:1$] for a hybrid model.

This setup is useful because it separates the role of the two proposed extensions. The sLSTM introduces exponential gating and memory mixing, while the mLSTM introduces matrix memory. On formal language tasks, the core question is not just whether the model can memorize tokens, but whether it can revise its internal state as new information arrives. That is exactly the kind of behavior memory mixing is intended to support.

# Why parity and related tasks expose limitations of non-recurrent models

The paper highlights the parity task as an example of a regular grammar that reveals the limits of models without memory mixing. In such tasks, the model must track a running state that depends on the full sequence of prior inputs. This is not just a matter of storing information somewhere; the model must repeatedly update and revise that state as the sequence grows. The paper argues that Transformers and State Space Models without memory mixing cannot solve such tasks, because they lack the relevant mechanism for state tracking.

The intuition is that parity is simple for a machine that can maintain a hidden state and update it consistently, but difficult for a model that does not have the right kind of recurrent state evolution. The paper explicitly links this to findings that Transformers and State Space Models are fundamentally less powerful than recurrent models for these state-tracking problems. In other words, the synthetic task does not merely measure pattern recognition; it measures whether the architecture can implement a computation that depends on a continuously updated internal state.

For xLSTM, this matters because the paper’s first major modification is exponential gating, which is intended to improve the model’s ability to revise storage decisions. The formal language experiments are therefore a direct test of whether that change accomplishes its purpose. The reported results show that xLSTM can solve these tasks better than the models without memory mixing, supporting the idea that the new gating mechanism changes the kind of computation the model can carry out.

# Comparing sLSTM-only, mLSTM-only, and hybrid xLSTM variants

The paper’s formal language experiments compare three xLSTM configurations: sLSTM-only, mLSTM-only, and a hybrid combination. This comparison is important because the two variants are designed to address different limitations. The sLSTM focuses on exponential gating and memory mixing, while the mLSTM focuses on increasing storage capacity through matrix memory. By comparing them directly, the authors can see whether one component dominates or whether the two are complementary.

The experimental setup shows that the models without memory mixing are not sufficient for the state-tracking tasks. The hybrid xLSTM is the main object of interest because it combines the strengths of both variants. The paper reports that xLSTM, especially when the new gating and memory mixing are present together, performs well on these formal language tasks. This supports the paper’s broader claim that the architectural changes are not isolated tricks but work together to improve expressivity.

The comparison also helps explain why the paper introduces xLSTM as a family rather than a single cell design. The sLSTM and mLSTM are not redundant. One provides a mechanism for revising stored information through exponential gating and memory mixing, while the other expands storage into a higher-dimensional matrix memory. The synthetic tasks show that both kinds of modifications matter, but in different ways depending on the problem.

# Multi-Query Associative Recall as a test of memory capacity

The second major synthetic benchmark is Multi-Query Associative Recall. Here the task is to memorize key-value pairs drawn randomly from a large vocabulary and retrieve the associated values later in the sequence. The paper uses this task specifically to test memory capacity, since successful performance requires the model to store many associations and recover the correct one when prompted.

This task is especially well matched to the mLSTM design. In the paper’s formulation, the memory cell is upgraded from a scalar to a matrix, and retrieval is performed through matrix multiplication. This makes the memory more expressive than a scalar cell state, because it can represent many relationships between keys and values. The associative recall task is therefore a natural setting for testing whether this extra capacity matters in practice.

The paper also emphasizes that the task is made more difficult than in the original formulation by increasing the number of key-value pairs up to $256$ and extending the context length up to $2048$. This creates a stronger stress test for memory systems. A model that can handle only a small number of associations may fail once the sequence becomes dense with retrieval demands. The modified benchmark is therefore designed to reveal whether the architecture can sustain memory under substantially heavier load.

# Increasing key-value count and context length to stress memory systems

A major part of the Multi-Query Associative Recall experiment is the deliberate increase in difficulty. By raising the number of key-value pairs up to $256$ and extending context length to $2048$, the authors force the models to maintain a larger set of stored associations over longer spans. This matters because a model’s apparent success on a smaller version of the task can hide weakness that only appears when the memory demands become more realistic.

The paper uses this scaling of the task itself as a diagnostic tool. If a model’s memory mechanism is fragile, then increasing the number of pairs or the sequence length should degrade performance. If a model has a stronger memory structure, it should remain effective under the harder regime. In this sense, the benchmark is not only about retrieval accuracy, but also about how gracefully the memory system handles increased load.

This is where the distinction between scalar and matrix memory becomes important. A scalar memory cell must compress information aggressively, whereas a matrix memory can preserve richer relationships between stored vectors. The experiment is designed so that these differences are observable in the final retrieval accuracy.

# Why Transformers serve as a gold standard on associative recall

In the associative recall experiment, the paper treats Transformers as a gold standard. The reason is not that Transformers are recurrent, but that their memory is effectively exponential in the coding dimension. This gives them a very strong retrieval mechanism for key-value associations, making them an appropriate reference point for the task.

The paper compares xLSTM against Llama, Mamba, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, and xLSTM variants. Because the task requires remembering arbitrary associations, it is especially informative to compare against a model class known for strong associative retrieval. If xLSTM approaches that performance, it suggests that the matrix memory and gating changes are doing something genuinely powerful, not merely improving conventional recurrent behavior.

The experimental framing therefore sets a high bar. The goal is not just to beat ordinary recurrent baselines, but to show that a recurrent architecture with the proposed modifications can compete in a domain where attention-based models are naturally strong. The reported results indicate that xLSTM[$1:1$] performs best among the non-Transformer models, which makes the comparison particularly meaningful.

# Evidence that mLSTM improves storage capacity

The associative recall results provide direct evidence that the matrix memory in mLSTM improves storage capacity. Since the task requires storing many key-value pairs and later retrieving them accurately, success depends on how much relational structure the memory can preserve. The paper reports that xLSTM[$1:1$] performs best among the non-Transformer models, and that this holds even for small models. This suggests that the matrix memory offers a clear advantage over scalar-memory approaches when the task is retrieval-heavy.

The paper also notes that the sLSTM block does not diminish memory capacity; instead, it seems to leverage it. This is important because one might worry that adding the sLSTM’s memory mixing could interfere with the retrieval precision of the mLSTM. The experiments indicate the opposite: when the task becomes hardest, such as at $256$ key-value pairs, the hybrid model remains strong. That implies that the sLSTM component can complement the mLSTM rather than simply competing with it.

The result is consistent with the paper’s interpretation of mLSTM as a mechanism for enlarging storage while preserving efficient update rules. The matrix memory and covariance-style update allow the model to maintain richer associations than a scalar cell state. The associative recall task is the cleanest demonstration of that claim.

# Evidence that sLSTM can complement mLSTM in difficult recall settings

One of the paper’s more interesting findings is that the sLSTM component does not merely preserve performance; it can actually help in the most demanding recall conditions. This is visible in the multi-query recall results, where the hybrid xLSTM performs especially well at the highest difficulty. That is notable because the sLSTM’s main advertised benefit is not larger raw storage, but improved gating and memory mixing.

The paper’s interpretation is that the sLSTM leverages the memory capacity of the hybrid system rather than reducing it. In other words, the scalar-memory component with exponential gating appears to improve how the model manages and accesses stored information, even when the matrix memory is doing the heavy lifting in terms of capacity. This is an example of the paper’s broader theme: xLSTM’s components are designed to address different weaknesses, and their combination can be more effective than either one alone.

The hybrid result also reinforces the idea that memory quality is not only about size. A larger memory is useful, but the model must also be able to decide what to keep, what to revise, and how to retrieve it. That is precisely where the sLSTM’s gating mechanisms matter.

# Long Range Arena as a benchmark for long-sequence processing

The third synthetic evaluation uses the Long Range Arena benchmark to assess how xLSTM handles long sequences and large contexts. Unlike the formal language and associative recall tasks, Long Range Arena is not focused on a single narrow capability. Instead, it provides a broader test of whether a model can process long inputs effectively across several tasks.

The paper uses Long Range Arena to examine xLSTM’s behavior on long-context problems more generally. The reported conclusion is that xLSTM demonstrates consistently strong performance on all tasks in the benchmark. This suggests that the architecture is efficient at handling different aspects of long-context processing, not just one specialized pattern.

This is an important bridge between the controlled synthetic tasks and the later language modeling experiments. A model that performs well on Long Range Arena has evidence of robustness in sequence processing, which makes it more plausible that it can also perform well on large-scale text modeling. The benchmark thus serves as a general-purpose stress test for the architecture’s ability to manage long dependencies.

# Overall lessons from synthetic experiments

Taken together, the synthetic experiments support the paper’s central claim that xLSTM addresses concrete weaknesses of standard LSTMs in a targeted way. The formal language tasks show that exponential gating together with memory mixing helps with state tracking and revising storage decisions. The associative recall task shows that matrix memory substantially improves storage capacity, especially when the number of stored associations becomes large. The Long Range Arena results show that xLSTM can handle long sequences effectively across a range of tasks.

The broader lesson is that the two major modifications introduced by xLSTM solve different problems. Exponential gating and memory mixing improve the model’s ability to maintain and update state. Matrix memory expands the amount and richness of information that can be stored and retrieved. The synthetic tasks are carefully chosen to make these effects visible in isolation.

These experiments also prepare the reader for the later language modeling results. Before the paper asks whether xLSTM can compete with Transformers and State Space Models on large corpora, it first establishes that the architecture behaves as intended on controlled tasks. In that sense, the synthetic evaluations function as a proof of concept: they show that xLSTM is not only a scaled-up LSTM, but a redesigned architecture with measurable advantages in expressivity, memory, and long-context processing.

14. Large-Scale Comparison on 15B Tokens: Main Benchmark and Ablations

# Experimental setup on SlimPajama with 15B training tokens

The first major language-modeling benchmark in the paper asks a direct question: if LSTMs are scaled up to modern large-model settings and equipped with the proposed xLSTM modifications, how do they compare to contemporary alternatives? To answer this, the authors train a collection of models on the same data budget: 15B tokens from SlimPajama. The setting is autoregressive language modeling, so the models are trained to predict the next token, and performance is measured on the validation set.

This benchmark is designed to be a fair comparison across model families. The same training corpus and training objective are used for all methods, and the models are matched to a common size corresponding to a GPT-3-like model with 350M parameters. In the paper’s description, this means an embedding dimension of 1024 and 24 residual blocks. The setup therefore isolates architectural differences rather than differences in scale or data exposure. The purpose is not merely to show that xLSTM can be trained, but to test whether the specific changes introduced by xLSTM matter under realistic large-scale language-modeling conditions.

The benchmark also serves as the basis for later scaling analysis. Because all methods are trained on the same amount of data and evaluated consistently, the authors can compare not only final validation perplexity but also how the methods behave as model size changes. In this sense, the 15B-token experiment is both a direct performance comparison and a controlled setting for understanding scaling behavior.

# Model classes compared: Transformers, SSMs, RNNs, and linear Transformers

The benchmark compares xLSTM against a broad range of current sequence-modeling approaches. The paper organizes them into several families: Transformers, State Space Models, Recurrent Neural Networks, and linear Transformers. This grouping reflects the broader landscape of sequence modeling, where different architectures trade off expressivity, memory, and computational efficiency in different ways.

Among the Transformers, the paper includes GPT-3 and Llama. Among State Space Models, it includes H3 and Mamba. The recurrent family includes RWKV-4, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, HGRN, and HGRN2. The linear Transformer group includes GLA, RetNet, and Hyena. The paper also includes xLSTM variants, specifically xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1], where the notation indicates the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks.

The inclusion of these categories is important because the paper is not comparing xLSTM to a single baseline but to the major architectural directions that have been proposed to address the limitations of Transformers. Transformers dominate modern language modeling, but they are computationally expensive in context length. State Space Models and recurrent methods offer more favorable sequence-length scaling, and linear Transformers attempt to reduce attention cost. xLSTM is tested against all of them to determine whether extending LSTM can bring it into the same competitive range.

# Perplexity as the primary evaluation metric

The main metric in this benchmark is validation perplexity. In next-token prediction, perplexity measures how well a model assigns probability to the validation data; lower perplexity means better predictive performance. Because the benchmark is carried out in a standard autoregressive language-modeling setting, perplexity is an appropriate and familiar measure of model quality.

Using perplexity also makes the comparison across architectures straightforward. Even though the models have different internal mechanisms, all are judged by the same outcome: how accurately they predict the next token in the validation set. This is especially useful in a benchmark like this, where the objective is to compare architectural effectiveness rather than task-specific capabilities. The paper reports these validation perplexities in Table 15B model results, and the conclusions are drawn directly from those numbers.

Perplexity also becomes a useful bridge to the scaling analysis. If a model family consistently achieves lower perplexity at a given size and exhibits favorable scaling behavior, then one can infer that larger versions of the same family may continue to improve competitively. Thus, perplexity is not only a performance score in the present experiment but also a signal for projected future behavior.

# Results showing xLSTM outperforming other tested methods

The central result of the 15B-token benchmark is that xLSTM outperforms all existing methods in validation perplexity. This is the paper’s main empirical claim for the benchmark: after being trained on the same data and evaluated under the same conditions, xLSTM achieves the best next-token prediction performance among the compared architectures.

This result is notable because the comparison includes strong representatives from all major families. Transformers such as GPT-3 and Llama are highly successful in language modeling. Mamba and H3 represent strong State Space Model baselines. RWKV, HGRN, and HGRN2 represent recurrent alternatives, while GLA, RetNet, and Hyena represent linearized or long-convolution approaches. That xLSTM comes out on top in this setting suggests that the combination of exponential gating and modified memory structure is effective at the scale relevant to language modeling.

The paper’s discussion emphasizes that this outcome is not only a win over a single class of competitors but a broader result across the current landscape. xLSTM is compared to models that make very different tradeoffs, yet it still achieves the best validation perplexity. This supports the paper’s broader claim that extending LSTM in the proposed way can make it competitive with the strongest contemporary methods.

# Scaling trends inferred from the 15B-token benchmark

The benchmark does more than identify a best-performing model at one scale. It also provides evidence about scaling trends. The paper reports scaling behavior in Figure 15B, and the key takeaway is that xLSTM appears to scale favorably relative to the comparison methods. The observed trend suggests that the advantage of xLSTM is not limited to the specific model size used in the benchmark.

The importance of scaling analysis is that language models are often evaluated not just by their current performance but by how reliably they improve with more parameters or more data. A model that performs well at one size but scales poorly may not be a strong candidate for future large-scale systems. In contrast, a model that already leads at moderate size and also follows a favorable scaling trend is more promising as a platform for larger models.

The paper uses this benchmark to motivate that conclusion. The scaling curves indicate that xLSTM should continue to compare favorably at larger sizes, which is consistent with the authors’ later large-scale experiments on 300B tokens. In that sense, the 15B-token benchmark is both a result and a forecast: it shows that xLSTM is strong now and may remain strong as scale increases.

# Step-by-step ablation from vanilla LSTM to xLSTM

To understand why xLSTM performs well, the paper performs ablation studies that gradually transform a vanilla LSTM into the full xLSTM architecture. This is a particularly important part of the benchmark because it identifies which design choices are responsible for the gains. Rather than presenting xLSTM as a monolithic new model, the authors break the transition into stages.

The ablation path proceeds in three main steps. First, vanilla LSTM layers are integrated into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones. Second, this is extended to a post up-projection block. Third, the new core xLSTM ideas are added: exponential gating and matrix memory. This staged approach allows the authors to ask how much each architectural refinement contributes.

By morphing the architecture gradually, the paper makes the ablation study more informative than a simple before-and-after comparison. It becomes possible to separate gains due to modern residual-block engineering from gains due to the new LSTM modifications themselves. This matters because xLSTM is not only “an LSTM with new memory”; it is an LSTM family adapted to modern large-model design conventions.

# Adding residual pre-LayerNorm backbones

The first ablation step places LSTM layers inside pre-LayerNorm residual backbones. This reflects the fact that xLSTM is intended to function as part of modern deep architectures rather than as a standalone recurrent module. The paper uses pre-LayerNorm residual backbones because they are the commonly used setting in contemporary large language models.

This step matters because it brings the LSTM into a training regime that is more suitable for large-scale deep networks. The ablation shows that architecture matters at multiple levels: even before the new xLSTM-specific mechanisms are added, putting LSTM into a residual backbone changes its behavior in the larger system. The paper treats this as part of the path from classical LSTM toward a modern large-model architecture.

The residual backbone is not presented as an optional detail but as an integral part of the final design. The ablation therefore helps distinguish between the contributions of the backbone and the contributions of the memory mechanism itself.

# Introducing post up-projection blocks

The second step in the ablation extends the architecture to a post up-projection block. In the paper’s terminology, this is one of the two residual block styles considered for xLSTM blocks. The post up-projection version first nonlinearly summarizes the past in the original space and then linearly maps into a higher-dimensional space, applies a nonlinearity, and maps back.

This design choice is associated in the paper with the sLSTM-based block. The reason for using the post up-projection form is that the architecture is intended to summarize history in a way that can better separate different contexts. The paper explicitly connects this to the intuition behind Cover’s Theorem: in a higher-dimensional space, nonlinearly embedded patterns are more likely to become linearly separable.

The ablation indicates that this transition contributes to the model’s performance. It is not just the LSTM recurrence itself that matters, but how the recurrent summary is embedded into the residual block. The post up-projection block is therefore part of the full xLSTM design, not merely a packaging detail.

# Effects of exponential gating

One of the most important innovations in xLSTM is exponential gating. In the ablation study, the paper attributes a major performance improvement to this change. Exponential gating is introduced to help the model revise storage decisions, which is a limitation of standard LSTM gating. The paper’s motivation is that ordinary sigmoid gates are limited in how they can modulate updates, while exponential gates allow more flexible weighting of memory updates.

The ablation results support this claim. When exponential gating is added, the model gains a meaningful improvement over earlier ablation stages. The paper therefore presents exponential gating as one of the main reasons xLSTM performs better than vanilla LSTM. This is consistent with the paper’s broader argument that the ability to revise stored information is important for difficult sequence modeling tasks.

The study also notes that gate design matters in general. In the separate gating ablations, the authors find that making each gate learnable and influenced by the input has an incremental positive effect. This reinforces the idea that xLSTM’s performance gains come not from a single isolated modification but from a carefully designed gating system.

# Effects of matrix memory

The other major xLSTM innovation is the matrix memory used in mLSTM. The ablation study shows that matrix memory is another major source of improvement. Whereas vanilla LSTM compresses information into scalar cell states, mLSTM uses a $d \times d$ matrix memory. This gives the model much larger storage capacity.

In the paper’s account, this increased capacity is crucial for language modeling because some information is simply too rich to be compressed into a scalar memory without loss. The matrix memory allows retrieval through matrix multiplication and is linked to a covariance update rule. In the 15B-token benchmark, adding this mechanism helps explain why xLSTM can outperform competing methods.

The ablation results indicate that the performance improvement is not due solely to more parameters or a larger backbone. Instead, the matrix memory itself contributes materially. This supports the paper’s broader thesis that a modified memory structure is necessary if LSTMs are to compete at modern scale.

# Ablation of different gating mechanisms

Beyond the main architectural ablations, the paper also investigates different gating mechanisms. This is important because gating is one of the defining features of LSTM-like architectures and is central to xLSTM’s design. The authors vary the gating setup to see how much each choice matters.

The paper’s conclusion from these tests is that having each gate be learnable and influenced by the input provides an incremental positive effect. This suggests that gate flexibility is beneficial and that the model should not rely on overly rigid gating rules. The gating ablations reinforce the earlier finding that exponential gating is useful, but they also show that the exact parameterization of gates matters.

This part of the study helps explain xLSTM’s design philosophy. The gains are not just from making the memory bigger; they also come from carefully redesigning how updates are controlled. In a recurrent architecture, the gates determine what gets stored, what gets forgotten, and what gets exposed, so it is natural that changing them changes performance in a measurable way.

# What the ablations reveal about the sources of performance gain

Taken together, the ablations identify two main sources of improvement: exponential gating and matrix memory. The paper explicitly states that the strong performance gains are attributable to both. This is the key interpretive result of the ablation study. It shows that xLSTM’s advantage is not accidental and not merely due to adopting modern training infrastructure.

The ablations also clarify the role of the residual backbone. Integrating LSTM into a pre-LayerNorm residual architecture and using the appropriate up-projection block are necessary steps in the move from a classic LSTM to a scalable xLSTM system. But the largest functional gains come from the new memory and gating mechanisms. The full architecture works because the backbone enables large-scale training while the new recurrent machinery changes what the model can store and how it can revise that storage.

In short, the 15B-token benchmark and its ablations show that xLSTM is competitive with strong Transformers, State Space Models, and recurrent alternatives, and they explain why. The model’s success comes from a combination of modern residual-block design, exponential gating, and matrix-based memory. The benchmark therefore serves both as a performance comparison and as evidence that the proposed architectural changes are the central drivers of xLSTM’s behavior.

15. xLSTM as a Large Language Model: 300B-Token Study

# Motivation for the 300B-token large-scale study

The paper’s largest experiment is designed to answer the central question of whether LSTMs, when scaled up with modern design choices, can compete with today’s dominant large language model architectures. Earlier sections of the paper argue that classical LSTMs have three important limitations: they cannot revise storage decisions well, they compress information into scalar cell states, and they are not naturally parallelizable because of memory mixing. The xLSTM proposal addresses these issues by combining exponential gating with new memory structures, namely the sLSTM and the mLSTM. The 300B-token study is the paper’s most direct test of whether these architectural changes matter when the model is trained at large scale on a language modeling workload.

This study is especially important because smaller-scale experiments can show that a model solves particular synthetic tasks or improves validation loss in a controlled setting, but they do not necessarily establish that the model can function as a practical large language model. The authors therefore increase the training data volume substantially, using 300B tokens from SlimPajama. They also evaluate multiple model sizes so that they can study both absolute performance and scaling behavior. In this way, the experiment is not just a performance comparison at a single size, but a broader test of whether the xLSTM family behaves like a scalable foundation model class.

# Compared models: xLSTM, RWKV-4, Llama, and Mamba

For the 300B-token study, the authors compare xLSTM against one representative from each of three major families: RWKV-4 for recurrent neural networks, Llama for Transformers, and Mamba for state space models. This choice is deliberate. Rather than comparing against every model named in earlier sections, the paper focuses on a compact set of strong and recognizable baselines from the main architectural categories discussed throughout the paper. The comparison is meant to show how xLSTM stands relative to the major alternatives for large-scale sequence modeling.

The selected baselines also reflect the paper’s broader narrative. Llama represents the Transformer family that dominates current large language modeling. Mamba represents a prominent state space model that aims to offer strong sequence modeling with linear-time behavior. RWKV-4 represents a recurrent alternative that, like xLSTM, avoids the standard Transformer attention mechanism. By comparing to these models, the paper situates xLSTM among the most relevant competing approaches for long-context and large-scale language modeling.

# Model sizes from 125M to 1.3B parameters

The large-scale experiments are performed at four model sizes: $125$M, $350$M, $760$M, and $1.3$B parameters. This range allows the authors to observe how xLSTM behaves as capacity increases, and whether the advantage over other methods persists across sizes. The paper does not treat xLSTM as a single fixed model, but as an architecture family whose behavior should be judged at multiple scales, just as modern language models are commonly analyzed.

Studying several sizes also makes the scaling-law analysis meaningful. If a model performs well only at one scale, that result is much less informative than if it shows a consistent pattern across a progression of sizes. By using these four model sizes, the paper can ask not only whether xLSTM is competitive, but also whether its improvement pattern suggests it will remain competitive as models become larger.

# Sequence-length extrapolation beyond the 2048-token training context

One of the key tests in the 300B-token study is sequence-length extrapolation. All of the compared models are trained with a context length of $2048$, but then evaluated on much longer contexts, up to $16384$. This test asks whether a model can continue to function when asked to process sequences substantially longer than those it saw during training.

The motivation for this evaluation comes directly from the paper’s discussion of xLSTM’s design. Since xLSTM is designed to have constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length and to support strong long-context behavior, it should ideally remain stable when context lengths grow. The authors therefore use extrapolation as a practical probe of whether xLSTM’s memory mechanisms generalize beyond the training regime. A model that degrades sharply once the sequence exceeds the training length would be less convincing as an LLM candidate, especially for tasks requiring long-range context retention.

# Perplexity behavior up to 16384-token evaluation length

The extrapolation results show that xLSTM maintains low perplexities for longer contexts, in contrast to the other methods. The paper emphasizes this as a qualitative distinction: rather than only performing well at the training context length, xLSTM continues to behave favorably as the evaluation length increases. This makes the long-context result more than a marginal gain; it indicates that the architecture is robust in regimes that stress memory and sequence handling.

Perplexity is the relevant measure here because the task is next-token prediction. Lower perplexity indicates better predictive fit. In the extrapolation setting, the important observation is not merely which model has the lowest perplexity at one point, but whether perplexity remains controlled as the context becomes longer. The paper’s conclusion from this experiment is that xLSTM is notably resilient, whereas the comparison models do not maintain the same level of performance under extended context lengths.

# Validation perplexity across model sizes

Beyond extrapolation, the paper evaluates all four model sizes on the SlimPajama validation set for next-token prediction. This provides a more standard language modeling comparison under the same training setup. The authors report that both xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] are the best models for all model sizes with respect to validation perplexity.

This result is important because it shows that xLSTM’s benefits are not limited to a special long-context test. It also means that the gains appear consistently across the range of model sizes considered. The paper uses validation perplexity as one of its main quantitative indicators of language modeling quality, and the result supports the claim that xLSTM is competitive with, and in this setting better than, the compared Transformer, SSM, and recurrent baselines.

The distinction between xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] matters here. According to the paper’s notation, xLSTM[1:0] is composed entirely of mLSTM blocks, while xLSTM[7:1] mixes mostly mLSTM blocks with some sLSTM blocks. That both variants perform best suggests that the xLSTM design is strong in either of these configurations, though the relative advantages of the two variants differ across tasks, as the paper later shows.

# Downstream reasoning-task evaluation and interpretation

The authors do not restrict evaluation to perplexity. They also test the models on downstream tasks that measure common sense reasoning. This is important because perplexity alone does not always reveal whether a model has useful general-purpose representations. A model may predict text well in the next-token sense without necessarily transferring well to tasks that require broader reasoning ability.

In the downstream evaluation, the paper reports that xLSTM is the best method in the vast majority of tasks and across all model sizes. The only exception mentioned is the ARC task, where Mamba is in some cases the best model. This pattern supports the view that xLSTM is not merely a strong language model in the narrow sense of next-token prediction, but also a strong general sequence model with useful downstream behavior.

The interpretation offered by the paper is that xLSTM’s architectural changes help it learn representations that are effective beyond the training objective. Since the study is framed as a test of whether scaled LSTMs can become serious LLM candidates, strong downstream performance is an important part of the answer. It shows that the architecture is not just optimizing perplexity in isolation but is producing model behavior that transfers to practical evaluation settings.

# PALOMA benchmark over 571 text domains

The paper also evaluates xLSTM on PALOMA, a benchmark consisting of $571$ text domains. This benchmark is meant to test next-token prediction across a wide variety of text sources, from news sites such as nytimes.com to domain-specific internet communities such as r/depression on Reddit. The breadth of the benchmark is important because it probes whether the model is robust across different genres and topic distributions rather than being specialized to a narrow slice of language.

In this evaluation, performance is measured by perplexity on each text domain. The paper groups the results into language modeling categories and finer-grained domain benchmarks. This large and diverse benchmark is particularly useful for assessing whether xLSTM generalizes well as a broad language model, since a model that performs strongly across many domains is more convincing as an LLM-like system than one that only excels on the validation set of a single pretraining corpus.

# Comparing xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] on domain-specific language modeling

On PALOMA, the paper reports that xLSTM[1:0] performs better than xLSTM[7:1]. This is an important contrast with the validation perplexity result, where both variants were the best across model sizes. On the domain-specific language modeling tasks, the pure mLSTM configuration appears to have the edge.

The paper quantifies this advantage in a striking way. xLSTM[1:0] has lower perplexity than Mamba in $568$ out of $571$ text domains, lower perplexity than Llama in $486$ out of $571$ domains, and lower perplexity than RWKV-4 in $570$ out of $571$ domains. These counts make the result concrete: xLSTM[1:0] is better than the competing systems on nearly all domains in the benchmark. The comparison is therefore not just about average performance, but about broad dominance across many heterogeneous language settings.

This outcome also helps clarify the role of the xLSTM variants. The sLSTM blocks do not always improve every language modeling measure; in this benchmark, the model without sLSTM blocks does better. The paper thus presents the two variants as complementary, with different strengths depending on the task family. In the context of PALOMA, xLSTM[1:0] is the stronger language-modeling variant.

# Power-law scaling analysis and offset comparisons

The paper also studies scaling laws. This is a crucial part of the large-scale investigation because one of the main questions in LLM research is not only how a model performs at a given size, but how performance changes as the model grows. Scaling laws help estimate whether a method is likely to remain competitive when increased to even larger parameter counts.

The reported scaling behavior shows that all models share a similar power-law pattern but differ in offset. In the paper’s comparison, RWKV-4 performs worst, followed by Llama and Mamba, while xLSTM performs better than Mamba with approximately the same margin that Mamba has over Llama. This is a useful way to interpret the results: the curves have similar shapes, but xLSTM is shifted to a better regime.

The significance of this offset comparison is that it suggests xLSTM is not merely benefiting from a one-off optimization artifact. If the scaling behavior remains similar in form while the offset improves, then larger xLSTM models may continue to enjoy an advantage as they are scaled up. The paper explicitly uses this interpretation to argue that larger xLSTM models are likely to remain favorable compared with Transformers and state space models.

# Generation-time measurements and decoding throughput

The final part of the 300B-token study examines inference efficiency. The authors measure text generation time and maximal decoding throughput for the $1.3$B-scale models. This matters because an LLM is not only judged by accuracy, but also by how it behaves at inference time, especially in settings where latency and batch processing are important.

In the generation-time comparison, xLSTM and the other recurrent models show linear scaling, while Llama exhibits quadratic scaling. This difference reflects the fundamental computational structure of the architectures. Since xLSTM does not rely on full self-attention over the entire prefix, its generation cost grows differently from a Transformer’s. The paper uses this comparison to highlight the practical efficiency of xLSTM for text generation.

For throughput, the paper finds that xLSTM can use much higher batch sizes than Llama because of its constant memory, and this enables xLSTM to achieve the highest throughput in the reported experiments. This is an important systems-level result: the architecture’s memory behavior is not only useful conceptually, but also has direct consequences for runtime performance.

# Why constant-memory recurrence can enable larger batch sizes at inference

The inference advantage is tied to xLSTM’s constant-memory recurrence. Because xLSTM’s memory does not grow with sequence length in the way that a Transformer cache does, it can handle longer sequences without the same memory pressure. That gives it more freedom in choosing larger batch sizes during decoding. In practical terms, if a model’s memory footprint grows too quickly, batch size must be reduced to fit computation on the device; constant-memory recurrence helps avoid that constraint.

The paper uses this point to explain why xLSTM can achieve better throughput than Llama in the decoding benchmark. The argument is not merely that xLSTM is theoretically efficient, but that its memory structure enables a concrete runtime advantage under the tested conditions. This makes the architecture more attractive for deployment settings where throughput matters, especially when the goal is to generate text over long contexts or at high batch sizes.

Taken together, the 300B-token study presents xLSTM as a serious large language model candidate. It performs strongly on long-context extrapolation, validation perplexity, downstream reasoning tasks, and broad domain-level language modeling, while also showing promising scaling and inference behavior. In the paper’s framing, this large-scale experiment is the strongest evidence that extended LSTM design can compete with the methods that currently dominate large language modeling.

16. Understanding the Results: Why Does xLSTM Work?

# How exponential gating improves revisability of memory

A central empirical message of the paper is that exponential gating helps xLSTM revise what it stores. In the original LSTM, the forget gate and input gate are sigmoid-valued, so the memory update is constrained in a way that tends to preserve earlier decisions unless the gates strongly change. The paper argues that this creates difficulty on tasks where a model must repeatedly compare candidates and replace an earlier stored item with a better one. The nearest-neighbor search example is used to make this point concrete: as a sequence is scanned, the model must keep track of the most similar vector seen so far and replace that choice when a more similar vector appears later.

xLSTM addresses this by replacing the standard gating behavior with exponential gating, together with normalization and stabilization. The key intuition is that exponential gates make memory updates more flexible because they are not limited to the same bounded form as sigmoid gates. At the same time, this flexibility could make the dynamics unstable, so the paper introduces the stabilizer state $m_t$ and normalized gates $i'_t$ and $f'_t$. The paper emphasizes that this stabilization changes neither the output of the network nor the derivatives of the loss with respect to parameters, while making the exponential mechanism numerically safe. The result is a memory system that can more readily revise stored information when later inputs deserve to replace earlier ones.

This explains why xLSTM improves on tasks that require selective retention rather than passive accumulation. In the paper’s synthetic formal-language experiments, models without memory mixing and revised gating struggle with tasks such as parity. xLSTM’s exponential gating is presented as one of the reasons it can solve these state-tracking problems more effectively.

# How matrix memory expands associative storage capacity

The second major mechanism behind xLSTM’s performance is the matrix memory of mLSTM. In the original LSTM, memory is compressed into scalar cell states. The paper argues that this creates a storage bottleneck, especially when the task requires storing and later retrieving richer associations. mLSTM replaces the scalar memory with a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, and retrieval is done via matrix multiplication rather than scalar gating alone.

The paper frames this design using a key-value-query picture. At time $t$, the model stores a key $\mathbf{k}_t$ and a value $\mathbf{v}_t$, and later a query $\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau}$ should retrieve the corresponding value. The covariance update rule,
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\]
is presented as the mechanism that stores these associations. This means that mLSTM does not merely remember that something happened; it remembers a structured relation between two vectors. That extra structure gives it greater associative capacity than scalar memory.

The empirical consequences are clearest in the Multi-Query Associative Recall task. There, the model must remember many key-value pairs over long contexts and later retrieve them accurately. The paper reports that xLSTM, especially the hybrid version, performs best among non-Transformer models and that the sLSTM block does not harm memory capacity but can even help it. The important conceptual point is that matrix memory can encode more information per stored update than scalar memory, which makes it better suited to tasks that demand broad associative recall.

# Why memory mixing matters for formal language and state-tracking tasks

The paper repeatedly returns to memory mixing as a distinction between models that can merely store information and models that can actively track evolving state. Memory mixing refers to hidden-hidden connections between one time step and the next. In the original LSTM and in sLSTM, these recurrent connections allow information from previous hidden states to influence future updates. The paper treats this as crucial for tasks where the model must maintain a coherent internal state over a long sequence.

This is especially important in the formal-language experiments. The paper argues that models without memory mixing, including certain Transformers and State Space Models, cannot solve tasks like parity because they lack the kind of state-tracking mechanism that RNN-style recurrence provides. The reported results are used to support the broader claim that memory mixing enables the model to revise and maintain internal state in a way that is essential for such problems. In this sense, the benefit is not only that the model remembers more, but that it remembers in a dynamically updateable way.

The paper also connects this to its broader claim about expressivity. It states that memory mixing enables state tracking, which is required to evaluate code or track entities in long narratives. That is why the sLSTM, despite being derived from a scalar-memory idea, is not treated as a simple simplification. Its recurrent mixing and new gating together make it capable of behaviors that purely non-mixing models cannot reproduce.

# Why hybrid xLSTM designs can outperform pure variants

An important empirical pattern in the paper is that neither pure sLSTM nor pure mLSTM is always the best choice. Instead, hybrid xLSTM architectures that combine both can outperform pure variants. The notation xLSTM[$a:b$] is used to denote the ratio of mLSTM-based to sLSTM-based blocks. This makes it possible to mix the stronger associative storage of mLSTM with the state-tracking and memory-mixing properties of sLSTM.

The experiments suggest that different tasks reward different components. On language modeling and validation perplexity, xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] are reported as the best models across several settings, which means the pure mLSTM variant and the mixed variant both perform strongly. On the PALOMA language tasks, xLSTM[1:0] does better than xLSTM[7:1], suggesting that the matrix memory is particularly advantageous for broad next-token prediction across many domains. By contrast, on the synthetic formal-language tasks, the hybrid or sLSTM-containing variants are emphasized as especially effective because they benefit from memory mixing.

This gives a clear lesson: xLSTM is not a single mechanism but a family in which different components solve different subproblems. The hybrid design works because it balances two sources of power. mLSTM contributes large-capacity associative memory, while sLSTM contributes adaptive state tracking through recurrent mixing and exponential gating. The paper’s results suggest that the best architecture depends on whether the task emphasizes storage, tracking, or both.

# How residual backbone design contributes to scaling performance

The paper does not present the LSTM variants in isolation. Instead, it integrates them into residual block backbones, and this design choice is part of what allows xLSTM to scale. The authors explicitly describe two residual block styles: post up-projection blocks, which are used for sLSTM, and pre up-projection blocks, which are used for mLSTM. This design is motivated by the idea that xLSTM should summarize the past in a way that separates different histories or contexts in a higher-dimensional space.

The intuition is borrowed from Cover’s theorem: in a sufficiently high-dimensional space, nonlinearly embedded patterns are more likely to become linearly separable. The paper uses this principle to justify why the LSTM extensions are placed inside residual blocks and why the projection order matters. For sLSTM, summarizing in the original space and then projecting upward fits the role of memory mixing. For mLSTM, projecting upward first gives the matrix memory more capacity to represent associations in a richer space.

This matters for scaling because the architecture is not simply a recurrent core but a residual deep network. The paper follows modern large language model practice by using pre-LayerNorm residual backbones and stacking blocks residually. This makes xLSTM structurally comparable to contemporary deep architectures while preserving its recurrent memory mechanism. In the paper’s experiments, this backbone design is part of the reason xLSTM can be trained and compared at scales from hundreds of millions to billions of parameters.

# Interpreting strong perplexity results against Transformers and Mamba

One of the paper’s main empirical claims is that xLSTM achieves strong perplexity results relative to Transformers, State Space Models, and other recurrent methods. On the 15B-token SlimPajama setting, xLSTM is reported to outperform all existing methods in validation perplexity. This is important because it places xLSTM not merely as a specialized sequence model, but as a competitive language model in the same general arena as GPT-3, Llama, Mamba, RWKV, and related systems.

The paper’s interpretation is that the gains come from combining the strengths that earlier models had separately emphasized. Transformers are strong because attention gives powerful retrieval over context, but it is expensive in sequence length. Mamba and other State Space Models are efficient and scale linearly with context, but the paper argues that they still lack the state-tracking ability conferred by memory mixing. xLSTM tries to combine the efficiency of recurrent processing with a memory structure that is richer than ordinary scalar recurrence. The perplexity results are therefore read as evidence that this combination works.

The paper also reports that scaling behavior favors xLSTM. The scaling curves show that xLSTM has a better offset than Mamba and other alternatives, and the authors interpret this as a sign that larger xLSTM models will remain favorable as scale increases. In this sense, the perplexity results are not treated as isolated benchmarks; they are presented as evidence that the model family has the kind of scaling profile needed for large language modeling.

# Why xLSTM extrapolates well to longer contexts

A notable result in the paper is that xLSTM extrapolates well to longer contexts than those seen in training. This appears both in synthetic tasks and in the large-scale language experiments. For the 300B-token setting, models are trained with context length $2048$ and then tested up to context length $16384$. The paper reports that xLSTM maintains low perplexities over these longer contexts better than the comparison methods.

The conceptual reason offered in the paper is that xLSTM’s memory is compressive but persistent. In other words, it stores information in a way that does not grow with sequence length, unlike Transformer attention caches, but still preserves enough information to remain useful over long spans. Because the recurrent state is updated incrementally, the model does not need to reprocess the full context at each step. This gives xLSTM a natural advantage when the sequence becomes much longer than the training regime.

The paper also suggests that the matrix memory helps here because it can store richer associations over time, and the stabilizing normalization helps keep gate dynamics well behaved over long sequences. Together, these properties make xLSTM less brittle when the context extends beyond the training length. The result is not just a model that works on long inputs, but one whose performance degrades more gracefully as the sequence grows.

# The balance between compressive memory and expressive representation

A useful way to understand xLSTM is as a balance between compression and expressiveness. The model does not store the entire past explicitly, as attention can in principle do through a cache. Instead, it compresses the past into recurrent memory. This is what gives xLSTM constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length. At the same time, the paper argues that the memory is expressive enough to support more than simple summarization.

The scalar sLSTM memory is compressive but still supports revision through exponential gating and memory mixing. The matrix mLSTM memory is more expressive because it stores key-value associations via outer-product updates. These two forms of memory represent different points on the tradeoff curve between storage size and representational richness. The paper’s results suggest that neither extreme alone is sufficient for all tasks. Scalar memory alone is too limited for broad associative recall, while matrix memory alone does not provide the same kind of state mixing that helps on formal-language tasks.

The hybrid architecture therefore makes sense as a compromise that is not merely halfway between two ideas, but a deliberate combination of complementary strengths. This is why the paper emphasizes both components throughout the experiments. The design supports the idea that useful sequence models need to compress long histories while still retaining enough structure to differentiate contexts and retrieve relevant information.

# Broader implications for sequence-model design

The paper’s broader implication is that sequence models may not need to be organized around a single mechanism such as attention to be competitive at scale. xLSTM shows that an extended recurrent architecture, when equipped with exponential gating, stabilization, matrix memory, and residual stacking, can compete strongly with Transformers and State Space Models on language modeling.

The experiments suggest a general design principle: efficient long-sequence processing is not enough by itself. The model also needs mechanisms for revisability, associative storage, and state tracking. Exponential gating addresses revisability. Matrix memory addresses storage capacity. Memory mixing addresses state tracking. Residual backbones and high-dimensional projections then provide a scaling-friendly scaffold that lets these components operate inside deep architectures.

The paper is careful not to claim that xLSTM solves every problem or that the architecture is fully optimized. It notes limitations, especially around parallelization of sLSTM and the computational cost of mLSTM. But the empirical evidence supports a clear conclusion: the classical LSTM ideas can still be extended in a way that makes them highly relevant for modern sequence modeling. The larger message is that the core principles of recurrence and gating remain powerful when adapted to the needs of contemporary large-scale learning.

17. Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Directions

# Sequential Nature of sLSTM and Its Impact on Parallel Efficiency

The paper is explicit that the sLSTM inherits a fundamental limitation from the original LSTM family: memory mixing makes it sequential. In the sLSTM, the hidden state at time $t$ depends on the hidden state at time $t-1$, which means the computation cannot be fully parallelized across sequence positions. This is the same kind of recurrence that gives LSTMs their expressive power, especially for state tracking, but it also prevents the kind of fast parallel execution that makes Transformers and some state space models attractive at scale.

The paper does not present this as a fatal flaw, but rather as a tradeoff. The authors report that they implemented a fast CUDA kernel for sLSTM with GPU memory optimizations down to the register level, and that this implementation is typically less than two times slower than their parallel mLSTM implementation. Even so, the key point remains that sLSTM does not have the same parallel execution pathway as mLSTM. This means that, while sLSTM contributes valuable memory mixing behavior, it is not the ideal choice when maximizing throughput is the primary goal.

# Current Lack of Highly Optimized mLSTM Kernels

The mLSTM is designed to be fully parallelizable, but the paper also makes clear that its current implementation is not yet fully optimized. The authors state that the CUDA kernels for mLSTM are not optimized, and that the current implementation is about $4$ times slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba. This is an important limitation because it shows that the theoretical parallelizability of the mLSTM does not automatically translate into best-in-class wall-clock performance.

At the same time, the paper suggests that this gap is not intrinsic to the model design. The authors point to FlashAttention as an example of how specialized kernel engineering can dramatically improve performance. The implication is that the current mLSTM runtime reflects an implementation stage rather than a settled computational limit. For students reading the paper, this is a reminder that algorithmic efficiency and systems efficiency are related but distinct issues: a model can be structurally parallelizable while still being slow in practice if its kernels are not mature.

# Computational Burden of Matrix-Memory Operations

A central innovation of xLSTM is the transition from scalar memory to matrix memory in the mLSTM, but this comes with a clear computational cost. The matrix memory uses a $d \times d$ cell state and a $d \times d$ update, so both storage and computation are substantially more expensive than in scalar-memory systems. The paper frames this as a deliberate tradeoff: higher memory capacity is purchased at the cost of heavier matrix operations.

This burden is especially relevant because the advantage of the matrix memory is not that it is cheap, but that it is powerful. The authors emphasize that the memory update and retrieval can be parallelized using standard matrix operations and that the memory itself does not require parameters. Even so, the arithmetic intensity is high. For a reader trying to understand why mLSTM is not a simple drop-in replacement for lighter recurrent blocks, this cost is one of the main reasons. The model is promising precisely because it can store richer associations, but those associations are represented in a more demanding format.

# Sensitivity to Forget-Gate Initialization

The paper explicitly notes that the initialization of the forget gates must be chosen carefully. This is one of the few optimization-related warnings in the limitations section, and it matters because the forget gate directly controls how information is retained or discarded over time. In both sLSTM and mLSTM, the forget gate interacts with the new exponential gating and stabilization mechanisms, so an unfortunate initialization can affect the dynamics of memory accumulation and retention.

Although the paper does not provide a general recipe for solving this issue, the warning itself is important. It signals that the model’s behavior depends not only on architecture but also on the starting conditions of training. For students, this means that strong results from xLSTM should not be interpreted as automatic or trivial to reproduce without care. The model’s performance is tied to a delicate balance between gating, memory update rules, and training setup.

# Potential Matrix-Memory Overload at Very Long Contexts

The paper also raises a concern about extremely long contexts: because the matrix memory is independent of sequence length, increasing the sequence length might overload the memory for longer context sizes. This is a subtle point. On one hand, the memory does not grow with the sequence length, which is one reason xLSTM has favorable memory complexity. On the other hand, exactly because the memory is fixed in size, there is a potential limit to how much information it can accumulate as contexts become longer and more demanding.

The authors note, however, that this does not appear to be a limitation for contexts up to $16$k, based on their sequence length extrapolation experiments. So the issue is not presented as a problem already observed in the paper’s tested regime, but rather as a possible boundary condition to watch in future work. This is an important distinction: the architecture looks robust for the context lengths studied in the paper, but its behavior at substantially larger scales remains open.

# Need for Further Architecture and Hyperparameter Tuning

The paper is candid that, due to the expensive computational load of large language experiments, the architecture and hyperparameters were not fully optimized, especially for larger xLSTM architectures. This is a significant limitation because it means the reported results may not reflect the strongest possible performance of the model family. In other words, xLSTM is presented as highly promising, but not yet fully searched over in the design space.

The authors explicitly anticipate that an extensive optimization process will be needed for xLSTM to reach its full potential. That statement opens a broad set of research questions. Which block compositions work best? How should the ratio of mLSTM-based to sLSTM-based blocks be selected? How should the gating and normalization choices be tuned at scale? The paper does not answer these questions completely, but it clearly identifies them as future directions necessary for realizing the full power of the architecture.

# Open Questions on Scaling to Larger Than 1.3B Parameters

The experiments in the paper go up to $1.3$B parameters, and the authors report favorable scaling behavior up to that range. However, the paper does not claim that scaling beyond $1.3$B has been solved. Instead, it argues that the observed scaling laws suggest xLSTM should continue to perform favorably compared to Transformers and state space models as models get larger. This is a projection, not a completed demonstration.

That leaves a major open question: what happens when xLSTM is pushed well beyond the largest model size tested here? The paper’s conclusion is optimistic, but it is still based on extrapolation from scaling trends rather than direct evidence at much larger scales. For a student, this is a classic case of how empirical scaling laws are used in modern language modeling: they guide expectations, but they do not eliminate the need for further experiments at larger sizes.

# Possible Improvements in Kernels, Training Methods, and Hybrid Block Design

The paper points toward several kinds of improvements without fully developing them. On the systems side, better kernels for mLSTM are an obvious opportunity, and the authors suggest that faster kernels could be obtained in the vein of FlashAttention. On the modeling side, the architecture itself may benefit from more careful tuning, especially at larger scale. On the training side, the paper notes that some methods required precision choices that were only settled after large-scale training had already begun, which suggests that training methodology remains an active area for refinement.

The hybrid block design is also implicitly open for further exploration. The paper uses both sLSTM and mLSTM blocks, with specific ratios such as xLSTM[$a$:$b$], and observes that different mixtures can be better suited to different tasks. For example, xLSTM[1:0] performs better on the PALOMA language tasks, while xLSTM[7:1] and xLSTM[1:0] are both strong on validation perplexity. This suggests that there is no single universally best composition yet. Future work could refine how these block types are combined, how many of each should be used, and how their roles should be assigned within a network.

# Potential Applications Beyond Language Modeling

Although the paper is centered on language modeling, it explicitly points to other application domains where xLSTM may have impact. The conclusion mentions reinforcement learning, time series prediction, and the modeling of physical systems. This is consistent with the broader motivation of the paper: xLSTM is a sequence model, and the architectural changes are intended to improve its ability to store, revise, and retrieve information over time.

The paper’s broader discussion also highlights that LSTMs have historically been successful in domains such as reinforcement learning, hydrology, and other sequence-related problems. xLSTM is positioned as an extension of that lineage, not merely as a language model. The implication is that the new memory structures and exponential gating could matter anywhere long-horizon state tracking or compact but expressive memory is important. The paper does not provide experiments in these domains, so this remains a future direction rather than a demonstrated result. Still, the authors clearly view xLSTM as a general sequence modeling framework whose relevance extends beyond text.

# Concluding Perspective

Taken together, the limitations section of the paper presents xLSTM as a strong but unfinished model family. The main achievements of the paper are real: exponential gating, matrix memory, and modified residual integration improve performance and scaling. But the authors also make it clear that xLSTM is not yet fully optimized, not yet fully benchmarked at far larger scales, and not yet fully explored across all possible design choices.

That honesty is valuable. It shows how new model families are developed in practice: first by solving key structural bottlenecks, then by testing whether the resulting system can compete with established architectures, and finally by identifying the implementation and optimization work that still remains. In the case of xLSTM, the paper’s answer is encouraging: the model is already competitive, but there is still substantial room for future research.

18. Conclusion: The Case for Extended LSTMs

# Recap of the paper’s motivating question

The paper begins with a simple but ambitious question: how far can language modeling go if we scale LSTMs to billions of parameters while also addressing the limitations that have historically made them less competitive than Transformers at large scale? This question is important because LSTMs were once the central sequence model, and they had already demonstrated strong performance across many domains. Yet the rise of Transformers changed the landscape by making large-scale training more effective and more parallelizable. The paper’s conclusion is therefore not a dismissal of LSTMs, but a re-examination of what becomes possible when their original ideas are extended using modern design choices.

The authors frame xLSTM as an answer to that question. Rather than treating the classic LSTM as obsolete, they identify specific bottlenecks and modify the architecture in targeted ways. The overall claim is that the core LSTM ideas of gating and additive memory are still powerful, but they need to be adapted for modern-scale language modeling. In that sense, the paper’s conclusion is not simply that xLSTM works, but that revisiting older architectural principles can still be fruitful when those principles are updated to meet the demands of contemporary large models.

# Summary of sLSTM and mLSTM innovations

The central architectural contribution of the paper is the introduction of two new LSTM variants, sLSTM and mLSTM, each designed to address different weaknesses of the original LSTM. The sLSTM keeps the scalar-memory spirit of the original model but replaces standard gating with exponential gating. To make that stable, the model introduces normalization through a normalizer state and stabilization through a separate state $m_t$. This allows the model to revise storage decisions more effectively, which is presented as a response to the inability of standard LSTMs to overwrite previously stored values when better information arrives later in the sequence.

The mLSTM changes the memory structure more radically. Instead of a scalar cell state, it uses a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and a covariance-style update rule based on outer products of values and keys. This increases storage capacity and gives the model a more associative form of retrieval, where a query vector can recover stored content from the matrix memory. In the paper’s framing, this is the mechanism that addresses LSTM’s limited storage capacity on tasks that require keeping many key-value associations in memory.

The distinction between the two variants is important to the paper’s overall conclusion. The sLSTM is about better decision-making in storage through exponential gates and memory mixing, while the mLSTM is about more powerful storage and retrieval through matrix-valued memory. Together, they form the two complementary building blocks of xLSTM.

# Key empirical findings across synthetic and language benchmarks

The paper supports its architectural claims with experiments that span synthetic tasks, long-context benchmarks, and large-scale language modeling. On formal language tasks designed to probe state tracking, the authors report that models without memory mixing cannot solve tasks such as parity, while xLSTM is able to do so. This supports the claim that memory mixing combined with gating gives xLSTM an advantage on problems that require tracking state over long sequences. In the paper’s interpretation, this is precisely where some Transformer and State Space Model variants fall short.

On associative recall, the mLSTM-based models are tested on a more difficult version of the Multi-Query Associative Recall task, with more key-value pairs and longer contexts than in the original setup. Here, xLSTM performs best among the non-Transformer models, and the authors emphasize that the sLSTM block does not reduce memory capacity but can actually help it. This is consistent with the broader argument that xLSTM’s modifications are not merely cosmetic; they improve the model’s ability to store and retrieve information in challenging settings.

The paper also reports strong results on the Long Range Arena, where xLSTM shows consistent performance across the tasks used to evaluate long-context handling. Taken together, these synthetic and benchmark results support the conclusion that xLSTM is not limited to one narrow capability. Instead, the model appears to handle both state tracking and memory-intensive retrieval while also maintaining strong behavior on long sequences.

# Evidence that xLSTM is competitive with Transformers and SSMs

A major conclusion of the paper is that xLSTM is competitive with, and in several settings better than, the main families of modern sequence models. On the 15B-token SlimPajama training run, xLSTM is reported to outperform the compared Transformer, State Space Model, recurrent, and linear-attention methods in validation perplexity. The paper includes GPT-3, Llama, H3, Mamba, RWKV variants, GLA, HGRN, HGRN2, RetNet, and Hyena in this comparison, and xLSTM comes out ahead in the reported results.

The larger-scale 300B-token experiments strengthen this conclusion. On validation perplexity, both xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] are the best across model sizes. On downstream reasoning tasks, xLSTM is the best method in the vast majority of cases, with the paper noting only occasional exceptions on ARC, where Mamba sometimes performs best. On the PALOMA benchmark, xLSTM[1:0] achieves especially strong perplexities across 571 text domains and outperforms Mamba, Llama, and RWKV-4 on most of them. These results make the paper’s comparative claim concrete: xLSTM is not simply a theoretically interesting variant, but a model that can compete directly with widely used modern architectures in language modeling.

The conclusion drawn by the authors is therefore cautious but clear. xLSTM has achieved performance at least on par with current technologies such as Transformers and State Space Models, while preserving the recurrent and compressive character of LSTM-style sequence processing. This is the basis for the paper’s claim that xLSTM is a serious competitor in the present sequence-model landscape.

# What the scaling laws suggest about future xLSTM models

The scaling-law analysis in the paper is one of the strongest reasons the authors remain optimistic about xLSTM’s future. Across the tested model sizes, xLSTM follows a similar power-law trend to the other methods, but with a better offset. This matters because scaling-law behavior is used in the paper as evidence about what might happen when models become larger. If a model family improves predictably with scale and already shows a favorable offset, then larger versions are likely to remain competitive.

The authors interpret these results as evidence that xLSTM should continue to perform favorably compared with Transformers and State Space Models at larger scales. This is especially important because the experiments cover several model sizes, including models up to 1.3B parameters. The paper does not claim to have exhausted the scaling potential of xLSTM; instead, it suggests that the reported gains may only be the beginning, especially since the architecture was not fully optimized in all settings. The conclusion is that scaling xLSTM appears promising not only in principle but also in the observed empirical trend.

The inference-time results support this forward-looking view as well. The paper reports linear generation-time scaling for xLSTM and other recurrent models, in contrast to the quadratic scaling of the Transformer baseline. It also reports that xLSTM can use higher batch sizes because of its constant memory, leading to high throughput. These properties reinforce the idea that scaling xLSTM could be practically attractive as well as statistically effective.

# The broader significance of revisiting classic architectures with modern design principles

One of the paper’s broader messages is that older architectural ideas should not be judged only by their historical limitations. The original LSTM was powerful, but it was constrained by scalar memory, standard gating, and sequential processing. xLSTM shows that when those ideas are reworked using exponential gating, normalization, stabilization, matrix memory, and modern residual backbones, they can become viable again at modern scale. The paper’s conclusion is therefore also a methodological statement: revisiting classic models with current design principles can produce meaningful advances.

The authors explicitly place xLSTM within the same broader engineering pattern that made deep residual networks and Transformers successful: stacking strong building blocks inside residual architectures. In that sense, xLSTM is presented as a modern sequence model built from classical recurrent ideas but adapted to the practices of contemporary large-scale deep learning. The paper’s conclusion implies that progress in sequence modeling may come not only from inventing entirely new paradigms, but also from rethinking established ones in light of current constraints and opportunities.

This broader significance is especially visible in how the paper frames memory. Instead of treating memory as a single scalar state, xLSTM experiments with both revised scalar memory and matrix memory. Instead of assuming standard sigmoid gating is the only option, it explores exponential gating with stabilization. The conclusion is that these modifications are enough to make an old family of models relevant again, which is an important lesson for the field.

# Final reflections on xLSTM’s place in the evolving sequence-model landscape

The paper ends by positioning xLSTM as a serious contender in the current sequence-model landscape rather than as a niche alternative. The conclusion is not that Transformers are obsolete or that xLSTM has solved every problem, but that xLSTM has demonstrated enough capability to belong in the same conversation as leading large language model architectures. Its strengths include long-context handling, strong validation perplexity, good downstream performance, favorable scaling behavior, and linear-time generation properties.

At the same time, the paper remains honest about limits. The authors note that sLSTM is not fully parallelizable because of memory mixing, that the current CUDA kernels are not fully optimized, and that careful hyperparameter and architecture tuning remains necessary. These caveats matter because they show that the conclusion is aspirational but grounded. xLSTM has shown what is possible, but the authors believe it still has room to grow.

The overall takeaway is that LSTM-like architectures are not merely historical artifacts. In the form of xLSTM, they can be extended into a modern sequence model that performs competitively with Transformers and State Space Models. The paper’s final claim is therefore both practical and conceptual: practical, because xLSTM works well in the reported experiments; conceptual, because it shows that the LSTM idea still has unexplored potential when combined with modern engineering and large-scale training.
