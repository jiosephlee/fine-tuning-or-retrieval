\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 12: 12. Positioning xLSTM Among Competing Sequence Models

# Linear attention approaches for long-context efficiency

A major motivation behind many recent sequence models is the high cost of standard Transformer self-attention, which scales quadratically with context length. The paper places several “linear attention” methods in this category because they attempt to reduce this cost while preserving some of the modeling advantages of attention. These methods include the Synthesizer, Linformer, Linear Transformer, Performer, Structured Global Convolution, and the Hyena hierarchy.

The common idea is to avoid full token-to-token interaction at every position, or to approximate it more cheaply. The Synthesizer learns synthetic attention weights without direct token-token interactions. Linformer uses a low-rank matrix to approximate self-attention. Linear Transformer reformulates attention in a linearized way, while Performer approximates the attention softmax using positive orthogonal random features. In other approaches, attention is replaced more radically by long convolutions, as in SGConv and Hyena.

From the perspective of the xLSTM paper, these methods are important because they target the same practical problem: how to process long contexts efficiently. However, they do so by modifying or replacing attention, whereas xLSTM comes from the recurrent side and extends the LSTM instead. This creates a useful contrast. The paper treats these models as part of the broader search for sequence models that scale better than standard Transformers in context length while still supporting strong language modeling performance.

# State-space models such as S4, DSS, GSS, S5, H3, and Mamba

The paper also groups a large family of State Space Models, or SSMs, as another major alternative to Transformers. These include S4, DSS, GSS, S5, BiGS, H3, and Mamba. Their appeal is similar to that of linear attention methods: they are linear in context length and therefore avoid the quadratic cost of full self-attention.

In the paper’s framing, SSMs represent a modern sequence-modeling paradigm that competes directly with Transformers on long contexts. They are presented as one of the key families that made it reasonable to ask whether LSTM-like models could be revived at scale. The paper highlights that SSMs can be strong language models, but also uses them as a point of comparison when evaluating whether xLSTM’s modifications are sufficient to make LSTM competitive again.

The relationship to xLSTM is especially important because the paper explicitly positions its own architecture alongside SSMs in terms of architectural design. xLSTM blocks can use a pre up-projection style similar to State Space Models, especially for mLSTM, where the memory capacity is increased in the high-dimensional space. So the comparison is not merely about performance; it is also architectural. xLSTM borrows the modern residual-block style of contemporary SSMs while keeping the recurrent memory idea at its core.

# Modern recurrent alternatives including RWKV, HGRN, and HGRN2

The paper also situates xLSTM among modern recurrent alternatives. These include RWKV-4, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, HGRN, and HGRN2. Like xLSTM, these models are recurrent, and like SSMs and linear attention methods, they are designed to avoid the quadratic cost of Transformer attention.

RWKV is especially important because it is presented as a well-known recurrent approach to large language modeling, with competitive performance to Transformers. HGRN and HGRN2 are similarly modern recurrent models that reuse gating ideas and aim to be efficient on long sequences. In the paper’s organization of the field, these models belong to the same general effort to build scalable sequence models without relying on standard self-attention.

What makes this family especially relevant to xLSTM is that it shows how recurrent modeling never disappeared; instead, it evolved. The paper presents xLSTM as a new member of this broader line of work, but one that modifies the LSTM idea more deeply than these alternatives do. In the experiments, the paper compares xLSTM directly against RWKV and HGRN-style models, reinforcing that they are among its closest practical competitors.

# The recurring importance of gating across model families

Gating is one of the central ideas that ties the paper’s related work together. In the original LSTM, gates control how information is written, retained, and read. The paper emphasizes that this idea has reappeared in many later sequence models, even outside the LSTM family.

The related work section notes that gating is used in HGRN, HGRN2, GLA, GSS, BiGS, MEGA, RWKV, and Mamba. This matters because it shows that gating is not merely an old LSTM trick, but a broadly useful mechanism for controlling information flow in recurrent and sequence models. In xLSTM, gating is taken seriously as a core design principle rather than as an auxiliary feature. The paper’s new exponential gating is an explicit attempt to extend this idea in a way that helps with storage revision and stability.

This commonality helps explain why xLSTM is placed near these models in the literature. Even when the underlying sequence-processing mechanism differs, many recent methods share the need to regulate how information is accumulated over time. The paper uses this observation to argue that xLSTM is not a disconnected novelty; it is part of a broader trend in which gating remains a powerful mechanism for sequence modeling.

# Matrix-memory and covariance-update approaches across the literature

A second major theme in the paper’s related work is the use of matrix memory and covariance-style updates. This is especially relevant to mLSTM, which replaces the scalar LSTM cell state with a matrix memory. The paper links this idea to several earlier and contemporary approaches.

The most direct historical connection is to Fast Weight Programmers, where memory is updated using an outer-product-like rule. The paper also mentions RWKV-5, RWKV-6, Retention, Linear Transformer, and HGRN2 as methods related to covariance update mechanisms or fast-weight-like memory. These models all explore ways of storing richer information than a simple scalar state while keeping the update rules structured and efficient.

In xLSTM, the covariance update rule takes the form of storing key-value associations through a matrix memory. The paper frames this as increasing the storage capacity of LSTMs, especially for associative recall tasks. This is one of the reasons mLSTM can be viewed as a natural extension of recurrent memory: it keeps the sequential update idea but upgrades the content that can be stored. In this sense, the paper’s matrix-memory contribution is not isolated; it belongs to a broader lineage of models that use structured memory updates to improve retrieval.

# Why Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2 are the closest relatives

Among all related methods, the paper explicitly says that the conceptually closest models to xLSTM are Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2. The reason is that these models share one or more of the same ingredients: matrix memory, gating, or both. This makes them especially relevant points of comparison when trying to understand where xLSTM sits.

The key difference, according to the paper, is memory mixing. Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2 do not allow the kind of memory mixing that the new sLSTM introduces. In xLSTM, memory mixing means hidden-hidden connections can influence updates, allowing the model to revise and refine stored information across time. This is presented as important for state tracking problems, which require following entities or dependencies over long narratives or code.

The paper’s argument is that these closest relatives may share surface similarities with xLSTM, but xLSTM adds something structurally essential. It does not merely use gates or structured memory; it combines them with recurrent memory mixing in the sLSTM, and with a fully parallelizable matrix-memory variant in the mLSTM. That combination is what the paper claims helps xLSTM move beyond what these related models can do.

# What distinguishes xLSTM from these related methods

xLSTM is distinguished in the paper by two coordinated modifications to the original LSTM: exponential gating and new memory structures. The gating change is meant to improve the model’s ability to revise storage decisions. The memory change produces two variants: sLSTM with scalar memory and memory mixing, and mLSTM with matrix memory and covariance updates.

This makes xLSTM different from both the Transformer-side models and the modern recurrent alternatives. Compared with linear attention or SSMs, xLSTM preserves explicit recurrent memory updates rather than replacing recurrence with another mechanism. Compared with RWKV, Retention, and related models, xLSTM adds memory mixing in sLSTM and a particular stabilized exponential-gating framework. Compared with standard LSTMs, it is more expressive in memory and more flexible in scaling.

The paper is careful to present these differences as practical, not merely formal. The synthetic tasks show that the two xLSTM variants specialize differently: sLSTM is important for state tracking and formal language tasks, while mLSTM is valuable for memory-intensive associative recall. The large-scale language experiments then show that combining the two in xLSTM architectures yields strong overall performance.

# Memory mixing as a key differentiator in expressivity

One of the paper’s central claims is that memory mixing is a crucial source of expressivity. In the LSTM family, memory mixing refers to hidden-hidden recurrent connections between states from one time step to the next. The paper argues that this mechanism is necessary for solving state tracking problems.

This point is used in the related work discussion to distinguish xLSTM from models that have structured memory but no memory mixing. The paper states that memory mixing enables state tracking, and therefore LSTMs are more expressive than State Space Models and Transformers for such problems. The formal language experiments support this claim: models without memory mixing cannot solve tasks like parity, while xLSTM can.

This is an important positioning statement. It suggests that the xLSTM paper is not merely proposing another efficient sequence model. It is arguing that a specific property of recurrent architectures, namely memory mixing, remains valuable and should be preserved even as models are scaled and modernized. In the paper’s view, this is one reason xLSTM can compete with or surpass other sequence models on tasks requiring long-term structure and entity tracking.

# Residual stacking as a common architectural pattern across modern models

The final piece of the landscape is architectural rather than algorithmic. The paper emphasizes that xLSTM architectures are built by residually stacking blocks, just as deep convolutional networks and Transformers are built from residual blocks. This is presented as part of the standard design pattern of modern large deep learning systems.

This places xLSTM in direct continuity with contemporary large language models. Instead of using a single recurrent layer in the older sense, xLSTM is embedded inside a residual block backbone, and many such blocks are stacked. The paper explicitly uses pre-LayerNorm residual backbones, which are common in modern LLMs. This means xLSTM is not only an LSTM extension at the cell level; it is also an LLM-style architecture at the system level.

The paper’s architectural discussion also notes two residual block styles: post up-projection, used for sLSTM, and pre up-projection, used for mLSTM. This mirrors design choices seen in Transformers and State Space Models and helps xLSTM integrate into the same modern scaling framework. In short, residual stacking is the mechanism that lets the paper take LSTM ideas and place them into the same class of deep architectures that made large Transformers successful.

Taken together, these points show where xLSTM fits. It is positioned as a modern recurrent architecture that draws from gating, matrix memory, and residual stacking, while directly comparing itself to linear attention methods, SSMs, and other recurrent models. The paper’s central claim is that xLSTM combines the strengths of these trends while preserving the recurrent memory mechanisms that make LSTMs especially expressive for certain sequence problems.
