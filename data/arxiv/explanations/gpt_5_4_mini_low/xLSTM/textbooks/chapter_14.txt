\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 14: 14. Large-Scale Comparison on 15B Tokens: Main Benchmark and Ablations

# Experimental setup on SlimPajama with 15B training tokens

The first major language-modeling benchmark in the paper asks a direct question: if LSTMs are scaled up to modern large-model settings and equipped with the proposed xLSTM modifications, how do they compare to contemporary alternatives? To answer this, the authors train a collection of models on the same data budget: 15B tokens from SlimPajama. The setting is autoregressive language modeling, so the models are trained to predict the next token, and performance is measured on the validation set.

This benchmark is designed to be a fair comparison across model families. The same training corpus and training objective are used for all methods, and the models are matched to a common size corresponding to a GPT-3-like model with 350M parameters. In the paper’s description, this means an embedding dimension of 1024 and 24 residual blocks. The setup therefore isolates architectural differences rather than differences in scale or data exposure. The purpose is not merely to show that xLSTM can be trained, but to test whether the specific changes introduced by xLSTM matter under realistic large-scale language-modeling conditions.

The benchmark also serves as the basis for later scaling analysis. Because all methods are trained on the same amount of data and evaluated consistently, the authors can compare not only final validation perplexity but also how the methods behave as model size changes. In this sense, the 15B-token experiment is both a direct performance comparison and a controlled setting for understanding scaling behavior.

# Model classes compared: Transformers, SSMs, RNNs, and linear Transformers

The benchmark compares xLSTM against a broad range of current sequence-modeling approaches. The paper organizes them into several families: Transformers, State Space Models, Recurrent Neural Networks, and linear Transformers. This grouping reflects the broader landscape of sequence modeling, where different architectures trade off expressivity, memory, and computational efficiency in different ways.

Among the Transformers, the paper includes GPT-3 and Llama. Among State Space Models, it includes H3 and Mamba. The recurrent family includes RWKV-4, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, HGRN, and HGRN2. The linear Transformer group includes GLA, RetNet, and Hyena. The paper also includes xLSTM variants, specifically xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1], where the notation indicates the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks.

The inclusion of these categories is important because the paper is not comparing xLSTM to a single baseline but to the major architectural directions that have been proposed to address the limitations of Transformers. Transformers dominate modern language modeling, but they are computationally expensive in context length. State Space Models and recurrent methods offer more favorable sequence-length scaling, and linear Transformers attempt to reduce attention cost. xLSTM is tested against all of them to determine whether extending LSTM can bring it into the same competitive range.

# Perplexity as the primary evaluation metric

The main metric in this benchmark is validation perplexity. In next-token prediction, perplexity measures how well a model assigns probability to the validation data; lower perplexity means better predictive performance. Because the benchmark is carried out in a standard autoregressive language-modeling setting, perplexity is an appropriate and familiar measure of model quality.

Using perplexity also makes the comparison across architectures straightforward. Even though the models have different internal mechanisms, all are judged by the same outcome: how accurately they predict the next token in the validation set. This is especially useful in a benchmark like this, where the objective is to compare architectural effectiveness rather than task-specific capabilities. The paper reports these validation perplexities in Table 15B model results, and the conclusions are drawn directly from those numbers.

Perplexity also becomes a useful bridge to the scaling analysis. If a model family consistently achieves lower perplexity at a given size and exhibits favorable scaling behavior, then one can infer that larger versions of the same family may continue to improve competitively. Thus, perplexity is not only a performance score in the present experiment but also a signal for projected future behavior.

# Results showing xLSTM outperforming other tested methods

The central result of the 15B-token benchmark is that xLSTM outperforms all existing methods in validation perplexity. This is the paper’s main empirical claim for the benchmark: after being trained on the same data and evaluated under the same conditions, xLSTM achieves the best next-token prediction performance among the compared architectures.

This result is notable because the comparison includes strong representatives from all major families. Transformers such as GPT-3 and Llama are highly successful in language modeling. Mamba and H3 represent strong State Space Model baselines. RWKV, HGRN, and HGRN2 represent recurrent alternatives, while GLA, RetNet, and Hyena represent linearized or long-convolution approaches. That xLSTM comes out on top in this setting suggests that the combination of exponential gating and modified memory structure is effective at the scale relevant to language modeling.

The paper’s discussion emphasizes that this outcome is not only a win over a single class of competitors but a broader result across the current landscape. xLSTM is compared to models that make very different tradeoffs, yet it still achieves the best validation perplexity. This supports the paper’s broader claim that extending LSTM in the proposed way can make it competitive with the strongest contemporary methods.

# Scaling trends inferred from the 15B-token benchmark

The benchmark does more than identify a best-performing model at one scale. It also provides evidence about scaling trends. The paper reports scaling behavior in Figure 15B, and the key takeaway is that xLSTM appears to scale favorably relative to the comparison methods. The observed trend suggests that the advantage of xLSTM is not limited to the specific model size used in the benchmark.

The importance of scaling analysis is that language models are often evaluated not just by their current performance but by how reliably they improve with more parameters or more data. A model that performs well at one size but scales poorly may not be a strong candidate for future large-scale systems. In contrast, a model that already leads at moderate size and also follows a favorable scaling trend is more promising as a platform for larger models.

The paper uses this benchmark to motivate that conclusion. The scaling curves indicate that xLSTM should continue to compare favorably at larger sizes, which is consistent with the authors’ later large-scale experiments on 300B tokens. In that sense, the 15B-token benchmark is both a result and a forecast: it shows that xLSTM is strong now and may remain strong as scale increases.

# Step-by-step ablation from vanilla LSTM to xLSTM

To understand why xLSTM performs well, the paper performs ablation studies that gradually transform a vanilla LSTM into the full xLSTM architecture. This is a particularly important part of the benchmark because it identifies which design choices are responsible for the gains. Rather than presenting xLSTM as a monolithic new model, the authors break the transition into stages.

The ablation path proceeds in three main steps. First, vanilla LSTM layers are integrated into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones. Second, this is extended to a post up-projection block. Third, the new core xLSTM ideas are added: exponential gating and matrix memory. This staged approach allows the authors to ask how much each architectural refinement contributes.

By morphing the architecture gradually, the paper makes the ablation study more informative than a simple before-and-after comparison. It becomes possible to separate gains due to modern residual-block engineering from gains due to the new LSTM modifications themselves. This matters because xLSTM is not only “an LSTM with new memory”; it is an LSTM family adapted to modern large-model design conventions.

# Adding residual pre-LayerNorm backbones

The first ablation step places LSTM layers inside pre-LayerNorm residual backbones. This reflects the fact that xLSTM is intended to function as part of modern deep architectures rather than as a standalone recurrent module. The paper uses pre-LayerNorm residual backbones because they are the commonly used setting in contemporary large language models.

This step matters because it brings the LSTM into a training regime that is more suitable for large-scale deep networks. The ablation shows that architecture matters at multiple levels: even before the new xLSTM-specific mechanisms are added, putting LSTM into a residual backbone changes its behavior in the larger system. The paper treats this as part of the path from classical LSTM toward a modern large-model architecture.

The residual backbone is not presented as an optional detail but as an integral part of the final design. The ablation therefore helps distinguish between the contributions of the backbone and the contributions of the memory mechanism itself.

# Introducing post up-projection blocks

The second step in the ablation extends the architecture to a post up-projection block. In the paper’s terminology, this is one of the two residual block styles considered for xLSTM blocks. The post up-projection version first nonlinearly summarizes the past in the original space and then linearly maps into a higher-dimensional space, applies a nonlinearity, and maps back.

This design choice is associated in the paper with the sLSTM-based block. The reason for using the post up-projection form is that the architecture is intended to summarize history in a way that can better separate different contexts. The paper explicitly connects this to the intuition behind Cover’s Theorem: in a higher-dimensional space, nonlinearly embedded patterns are more likely to become linearly separable.

The ablation indicates that this transition contributes to the model’s performance. It is not just the LSTM recurrence itself that matters, but how the recurrent summary is embedded into the residual block. The post up-projection block is therefore part of the full xLSTM design, not merely a packaging detail.

# Effects of exponential gating

One of the most important innovations in xLSTM is exponential gating. In the ablation study, the paper attributes a major performance improvement to this change. Exponential gating is introduced to help the model revise storage decisions, which is a limitation of standard LSTM gating. The paper’s motivation is that ordinary sigmoid gates are limited in how they can modulate updates, while exponential gates allow more flexible weighting of memory updates.

The ablation results support this claim. When exponential gating is added, the model gains a meaningful improvement over earlier ablation stages. The paper therefore presents exponential gating as one of the main reasons xLSTM performs better than vanilla LSTM. This is consistent with the paper’s broader argument that the ability to revise stored information is important for difficult sequence modeling tasks.

The study also notes that gate design matters in general. In the separate gating ablations, the authors find that making each gate learnable and influenced by the input has an incremental positive effect. This reinforces the idea that xLSTM’s performance gains come not from a single isolated modification but from a carefully designed gating system.

# Effects of matrix memory

The other major xLSTM innovation is the matrix memory used in mLSTM. The ablation study shows that matrix memory is another major source of improvement. Whereas vanilla LSTM compresses information into scalar cell states, mLSTM uses a $d \times d$ matrix memory. This gives the model much larger storage capacity.

In the paper’s account, this increased capacity is crucial for language modeling because some information is simply too rich to be compressed into a scalar memory without loss. The matrix memory allows retrieval through matrix multiplication and is linked to a covariance update rule. In the 15B-token benchmark, adding this mechanism helps explain why xLSTM can outperform competing methods.

The ablation results indicate that the performance improvement is not due solely to more parameters or a larger backbone. Instead, the matrix memory itself contributes materially. This supports the paper’s broader thesis that a modified memory structure is necessary if LSTMs are to compete at modern scale.

# Ablation of different gating mechanisms

Beyond the main architectural ablations, the paper also investigates different gating mechanisms. This is important because gating is one of the defining features of LSTM-like architectures and is central to xLSTM’s design. The authors vary the gating setup to see how much each choice matters.

The paper’s conclusion from these tests is that having each gate be learnable and influenced by the input provides an incremental positive effect. This suggests that gate flexibility is beneficial and that the model should not rely on overly rigid gating rules. The gating ablations reinforce the earlier finding that exponential gating is useful, but they also show that the exact parameterization of gates matters.

This part of the study helps explain xLSTM’s design philosophy. The gains are not just from making the memory bigger; they also come from carefully redesigning how updates are controlled. In a recurrent architecture, the gates determine what gets stored, what gets forgotten, and what gets exposed, so it is natural that changing them changes performance in a measurable way.

# What the ablations reveal about the sources of performance gain

Taken together, the ablations identify two main sources of improvement: exponential gating and matrix memory. The paper explicitly states that the strong performance gains are attributable to both. This is the key interpretive result of the ablation study. It shows that xLSTM’s advantage is not accidental and not merely due to adopting modern training infrastructure.

The ablations also clarify the role of the residual backbone. Integrating LSTM into a pre-LayerNorm residual architecture and using the appropriate up-projection block are necessary steps in the move from a classic LSTM to a scalable xLSTM system. But the largest functional gains come from the new memory and gating mechanisms. The full architecture works because the backbone enables large-scale training while the new recurrent machinery changes what the model can store and how it can revise that storage.

In short, the 15B-token benchmark and its ablations show that xLSTM is competitive with strong Transformers, State Space Models, and recurrent alternatives, and they explain why. The model’s success comes from a combination of modern residual-block design, exponential gating, and matrix-based memory. The benchmark therefore serves both as a performance comparison and as evidence that the proposed architectural changes are the central drivers of xLSTM’s behavior.
