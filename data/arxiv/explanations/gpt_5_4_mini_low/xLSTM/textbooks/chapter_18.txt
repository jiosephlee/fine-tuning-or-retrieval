\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 18: 18. Conclusion: The Case for Extended LSTMs

# Recap of the paper’s motivating question

The paper begins with a simple but ambitious question: how far can language modeling go if we scale LSTMs to billions of parameters while also addressing the limitations that have historically made them less competitive than Transformers at large scale? This question is important because LSTMs were once the central sequence model, and they had already demonstrated strong performance across many domains. Yet the rise of Transformers changed the landscape by making large-scale training more effective and more parallelizable. The paper’s conclusion is therefore not a dismissal of LSTMs, but a re-examination of what becomes possible when their original ideas are extended using modern design choices.

The authors frame xLSTM as an answer to that question. Rather than treating the classic LSTM as obsolete, they identify specific bottlenecks and modify the architecture in targeted ways. The overall claim is that the core LSTM ideas of gating and additive memory are still powerful, but they need to be adapted for modern-scale language modeling. In that sense, the paper’s conclusion is not simply that xLSTM works, but that revisiting older architectural principles can still be fruitful when those principles are updated to meet the demands of contemporary large models.

# Summary of sLSTM and mLSTM innovations

The central architectural contribution of the paper is the introduction of two new LSTM variants, sLSTM and mLSTM, each designed to address different weaknesses of the original LSTM. The sLSTM keeps the scalar-memory spirit of the original model but replaces standard gating with exponential gating. To make that stable, the model introduces normalization through a normalizer state and stabilization through a separate state $m_t$. This allows the model to revise storage decisions more effectively, which is presented as a response to the inability of standard LSTMs to overwrite previously stored values when better information arrives later in the sequence.

The mLSTM changes the memory structure more radically. Instead of a scalar cell state, it uses a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and a covariance-style update rule based on outer products of values and keys. This increases storage capacity and gives the model a more associative form of retrieval, where a query vector can recover stored content from the matrix memory. In the paper’s framing, this is the mechanism that addresses LSTM’s limited storage capacity on tasks that require keeping many key-value associations in memory.

The distinction between the two variants is important to the paper’s overall conclusion. The sLSTM is about better decision-making in storage through exponential gates and memory mixing, while the mLSTM is about more powerful storage and retrieval through matrix-valued memory. Together, they form the two complementary building blocks of xLSTM.

# Key empirical findings across synthetic and language benchmarks

The paper supports its architectural claims with experiments that span synthetic tasks, long-context benchmarks, and large-scale language modeling. On formal language tasks designed to probe state tracking, the authors report that models without memory mixing cannot solve tasks such as parity, while xLSTM is able to do so. This supports the claim that memory mixing combined with gating gives xLSTM an advantage on problems that require tracking state over long sequences. In the paper’s interpretation, this is precisely where some Transformer and State Space Model variants fall short.

On associative recall, the mLSTM-based models are tested on a more difficult version of the Multi-Query Associative Recall task, with more key-value pairs and longer contexts than in the original setup. Here, xLSTM performs best among the non-Transformer models, and the authors emphasize that the sLSTM block does not reduce memory capacity but can actually help it. This is consistent with the broader argument that xLSTM’s modifications are not merely cosmetic; they improve the model’s ability to store and retrieve information in challenging settings.

The paper also reports strong results on the Long Range Arena, where xLSTM shows consistent performance across the tasks used to evaluate long-context handling. Taken together, these synthetic and benchmark results support the conclusion that xLSTM is not limited to one narrow capability. Instead, the model appears to handle both state tracking and memory-intensive retrieval while also maintaining strong behavior on long sequences.

# Evidence that xLSTM is competitive with Transformers and SSMs

A major conclusion of the paper is that xLSTM is competitive with, and in several settings better than, the main families of modern sequence models. On the 15B-token SlimPajama training run, xLSTM is reported to outperform the compared Transformer, State Space Model, recurrent, and linear-attention methods in validation perplexity. The paper includes GPT-3, Llama, H3, Mamba, RWKV variants, GLA, HGRN, HGRN2, RetNet, and Hyena in this comparison, and xLSTM comes out ahead in the reported results.

The larger-scale 300B-token experiments strengthen this conclusion. On validation perplexity, both xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] are the best across model sizes. On downstream reasoning tasks, xLSTM is the best method in the vast majority of cases, with the paper noting only occasional exceptions on ARC, where Mamba sometimes performs best. On the PALOMA benchmark, xLSTM[1:0] achieves especially strong perplexities across 571 text domains and outperforms Mamba, Llama, and RWKV-4 on most of them. These results make the paper’s comparative claim concrete: xLSTM is not simply a theoretically interesting variant, but a model that can compete directly with widely used modern architectures in language modeling.

The conclusion drawn by the authors is therefore cautious but clear. xLSTM has achieved performance at least on par with current technologies such as Transformers and State Space Models, while preserving the recurrent and compressive character of LSTM-style sequence processing. This is the basis for the paper’s claim that xLSTM is a serious competitor in the present sequence-model landscape.

# What the scaling laws suggest about future xLSTM models

The scaling-law analysis in the paper is one of the strongest reasons the authors remain optimistic about xLSTM’s future. Across the tested model sizes, xLSTM follows a similar power-law trend to the other methods, but with a better offset. This matters because scaling-law behavior is used in the paper as evidence about what might happen when models become larger. If a model family improves predictably with scale and already shows a favorable offset, then larger versions are likely to remain competitive.

The authors interpret these results as evidence that xLSTM should continue to perform favorably compared with Transformers and State Space Models at larger scales. This is especially important because the experiments cover several model sizes, including models up to 1.3B parameters. The paper does not claim to have exhausted the scaling potential of xLSTM; instead, it suggests that the reported gains may only be the beginning, especially since the architecture was not fully optimized in all settings. The conclusion is that scaling xLSTM appears promising not only in principle but also in the observed empirical trend.

The inference-time results support this forward-looking view as well. The paper reports linear generation-time scaling for xLSTM and other recurrent models, in contrast to the quadratic scaling of the Transformer baseline. It also reports that xLSTM can use higher batch sizes because of its constant memory, leading to high throughput. These properties reinforce the idea that scaling xLSTM could be practically attractive as well as statistically effective.

# The broader significance of revisiting classic architectures with modern design principles

One of the paper’s broader messages is that older architectural ideas should not be judged only by their historical limitations. The original LSTM was powerful, but it was constrained by scalar memory, standard gating, and sequential processing. xLSTM shows that when those ideas are reworked using exponential gating, normalization, stabilization, matrix memory, and modern residual backbones, they can become viable again at modern scale. The paper’s conclusion is therefore also a methodological statement: revisiting classic models with current design principles can produce meaningful advances.

The authors explicitly place xLSTM within the same broader engineering pattern that made deep residual networks and Transformers successful: stacking strong building blocks inside residual architectures. In that sense, xLSTM is presented as a modern sequence model built from classical recurrent ideas but adapted to the practices of contemporary large-scale deep learning. The paper’s conclusion implies that progress in sequence modeling may come not only from inventing entirely new paradigms, but also from rethinking established ones in light of current constraints and opportunities.

This broader significance is especially visible in how the paper frames memory. Instead of treating memory as a single scalar state, xLSTM experiments with both revised scalar memory and matrix memory. Instead of assuming standard sigmoid gating is the only option, it explores exponential gating with stabilization. The conclusion is that these modifications are enough to make an old family of models relevant again, which is an important lesson for the field.

# Final reflections on xLSTM’s place in the evolving sequence-model landscape

The paper ends by positioning xLSTM as a serious contender in the current sequence-model landscape rather than as a niche alternative. The conclusion is not that Transformers are obsolete or that xLSTM has solved every problem, but that xLSTM has demonstrated enough capability to belong in the same conversation as leading large language model architectures. Its strengths include long-context handling, strong validation perplexity, good downstream performance, favorable scaling behavior, and linear-time generation properties.

At the same time, the paper remains honest about limits. The authors note that sLSTM is not fully parallelizable because of memory mixing, that the current CUDA kernels are not fully optimized, and that careful hyperparameter and architecture tuning remains necessary. These caveats matter because they show that the conclusion is aspirational but grounded. xLSTM has shown what is possible, but the authors believe it still has room to grow.

The overall takeaway is that LSTM-like architectures are not merely historical artifacts. In the form of xLSTM, they can be extended into a modern sequence model that performs competitively with Transformers and State Space Models. The paper’s final claim is therefore both practical and conceptual: practical, because xLSTM works well in the reported experiments; conceptual, because it shows that the LSTM idea still has unexplored potential when combined with modern engineering and large-scale training.
