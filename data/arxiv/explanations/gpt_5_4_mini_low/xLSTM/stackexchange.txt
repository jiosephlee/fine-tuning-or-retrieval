\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why does replacing sigmoid gates with exponential gates help sLSTM revise earlier storage decisions?
Question:
In the xLSTM paper, one of the stated limitations of standard LSTM is the "inability to revise storage decisions," and the proposed fix is to use exponential gating together with normalization and stabilization in sLSTM. I understand that in a standard LSTM the update is $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$ with $f_t, i_t in (0,1)$ due to sigmoid gates, while in sLSTM the input gate is $i_t = exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and sometimes the forget gate can also be exponential.

What I do not understand is the mechanism by which this actually improves revisability. Is the idea simply that the ratio between old and new content can become arbitrarily large because the gates are no longer bounded by 1? Or is the key point really the normalized output $h_t = o_t (c_t / n_t)$ rather than the exponential gate by itself?

An intuitive explanation in terms of how a later token can "overwrite" or outvote an earlier one would help a lot.
Answer:
The key idea is not just that the gates can exceed $1$; it is that exponential gating makes the model behave more like a soft competition over “how much evidence” each stored item has accumulated, and the normalization makes that competition meaningful and stable.

In a standard LSTM, the update
$$
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t
$$
uses sigmoid gates, so both $f_t$ and $i_t$ lie in $(0,1)$. That means the model can only attenuate contributions; it cannot make a new token’s update decisively outweigh what was stored before except indirectly through repeated updates. In the paper’s terminology, this makes storage decisions hard to revise. Once earlier content has been written into the cell, later inputs are limited in how strongly they can “argue against it.”

In sLSTM, the gates are changed to exponentials, for example
$$
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)\ \text{or}\ \sigma(\tilde{f}_t),
$$
and the cell is paired with a normalizer state
$$
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,\qquad h_t = o_t \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
$$
This is the crucial part. The memory is no longer read out as the raw sum $c_t$, but as the normalized ratio $c_t / n_t$. Expanding the recurrence shows that
$$
\frac{c_t}{n_t}
$$
is essentially a weighted average of past candidate contents, where the weights are the products of input and forget gates along the path. So when a later token gets a much larger effective gate weight than an earlier token, it does not merely add a little more to the cell; it can dominate the normalized average and thereby revise the effective memory.

That is the intuitive “outvote” mechanism: earlier storage is no longer protected by the boundedness of sigmoid gates. Instead, each write contributes a positive weight, and later writes can receive much larger weight than earlier ones. The normalized output then selects the content with the strongest accumulated support. This is exactly what the paper means when it says exponential gating helps on the nearest-neighbor search task, where the model must replace an earlier stored item when a more similar one appears.

So the short answer is: the exponential gate is what allows a later update to have arbitrarily larger relative weight than an earlier one, but the normalization $c_t / n_t$ is what turns that into a stable and interpretable revisable memory. Without normalization, exponentials alone would just make the state blow up. With normalization, sLSTM can treat memory as a weighted competition rather than a one-way write-once accumulator.

### What exactly is the normalizer state $n_t$ in sLSTM doing, and why is $c_t / n_t$ the right quantity to output?
Question:
The sLSTM forward pass introduces a second recurrence

$ n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t $

and then uses

$ h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t, \quad \tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t $.

The paper says that $n_t$ "sums up the product of the input gate times all future forget gates," but I am having trouble seeing why this is the correct normalization.

If I unroll the recurrence, $c_t$ looks like a weighted sum of past inputs $z_j$, and $n_t$ looks like the same weighted sum but with each $z_j$ replaced by $1$. So is $c_t / n_t$ basically a weighted average of past content? If that is the intended interpretation, why is a weighted average better than the usual LSTM behavior where the cell state is left unnormalized and only squashed by something like $\tanh$?

I would appreciate either a derivation or a simple concrete example.
Answer:
Yes — in the sLSTM, $n_t$ is exactly a normalization accumulator, and your intuition is right: after unrolling, $c_t / n_t$ behaves like a gated, decayed average of the past content values $z_j$. The key point is that the sLSTM does not want to store an unnormalized sum anymore, because with exponential input gates the raw cell state can grow or shrink by large factors. The paper’s “normalizer state” is there to make the hidden state depend on the *relative* contributions of past inputs, not on their absolute scale.

To see this, unroll the recurrences
\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \qquad n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t.
\]
Assuming for simplicity that the initial states are $c_0 = 0$ and $n_0 = 0$, repeated substitution gives
\[
c_t = \sum_{j=1}^t \left( i_j \prod_{k=j+1}^t f_k \right) z_j,
\qquad
n_t = \sum_{j=1}^t \left( i_j \prod_{k=j+1}^t f_k \right).
\]
So both $c_t$ and $n_t$ use the same weights
\[
w_j = i_j \prod_{k=j+1}^t f_k,
\]
except that $c_t$ weights each past content $z_j$, while $n_t$ weights a constant $1$. Therefore
\[
\frac{c_t}{n_t}
=
\frac{\sum_{j=1}^t w_j z_j}{\sum_{j=1}^t w_j},
\]
which is exactly a weighted average of past contents. So your interpretation is correct.

Why is this better than the usual LSTM form? In a standard LSTM, the cell state is an unnormalized sum and then squashed by something like $\tanh(c_t)$. That works when gates are bounded in $[0,1]$, but xLSTM deliberately uses exponential gates, $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and possibly $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$, to give the model more expressive multiplicative control over memory. Once you allow those gates to vary over a much wider range, the scale of $c_t$ itself becomes less meaningful: one sequence may produce a huge $c_t$ simply because all gates were large, even if the *content* being represented is essentially the same as another sequence with smaller gates. Dividing by $n_t$ removes that arbitrary scale and turns the memory into a scale-invariant “summary of what was stored.”

A useful intuition is to think of $n_t$ as tracking the total effective “mass” of memory currently present. Then $c_t$ tracks the total mass times content, and $c_t/n_t$ gives the average content per unit mass. Without this division, the hidden state would conflate two things: what the model remembers and how strongly it happened to write it. The paper explicitly says the normalizer “keeps record of the strength of the gates,” which is exactly this idea.

A concrete example makes the difference clear. Suppose at time $t$ the model has seen two pieces of content, $z_1 = 10$ and $z_2 = 0$, with weights $w_1 = 1$ and $w_2 = 9$. Then
\[
c_t = 1 \cdot 10 + 9 \cdot 0 = 10, \qquad n_t = 1 + 9 = 10,
\]
so
\[
c_t / n_t = 1.
\]
That says the stored state is “mostly like the second input,” which is reasonable because the second write was much stronger. If you instead output $c_t$ directly, the value $10$ mixes content and confidence in a way that is harder for the next layers to interpret.

This is also why the paper pairs the normalizer with stabilization via the extra state $m_t$: because exponential gates can explode, they rewrite the gates in a numerically safe form without changing the network output. The normalization by $n_t$ is part of the same theme: make the exponential-gated recurrence behave like a well-scaled, content-based memory readout rather than an unbounded accumulator.

So the short answer is: $n_t$ is not an extra memory channel with independent semantics; it is the denominator that turns the cell state into a normalized weighted average. In the sLSTM, $c_t / n_t$ is the right output because the model’s exponential gates make the absolute magnitude of $c_t$ unreliable as a representation of content, while the ratio captures the content itself in a gate-scale-invariant way.

### How can the stabilization with $m_t$ leave both the forward outputs and the gradients unchanged?
Question:
For sLSTM and mLSTM, the paper introduces a stabilizer state

$ m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t)) $

and then defines stabilized gates

$ i'_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t), \quad f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t). $

The paper claims that replacing $i_t, f_t$ by $i'_t, f'_t$ in the forward pass does not change the network output or the derivatives with respect to parameters. I can believe this is some kind of rescaling trick, but I do not see how the hidden state remains exactly the same rather than only approximately the same numerically.

Could someone show the algebra for why the transformed recurrence is equivalent to the original one? I am especially confused because $m_t$ depends on a non-smooth $\max$, so I would have expected gradient differences.
Answer:
The key point is that the paper is not changing the model, only changing the *representation* of the same recurrence.

For the sLSTM, the unstabilized states are
$$
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \qquad n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,
$$
and the output is
$$
h_t = o_t \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
$$

Now introduce a time-dependent scaling factor $e^{-m_t}$ and define the rescaled states
$$
\bar c_t := e^{-m_t} c_t, \qquad \bar n_t := e^{-m_t} n_t.
$$
This is just a change of variables. If you multiply both numerator and denominator of the hidden state by the same factor, nothing changes:
$$
h_t = o_t \frac{c_t}{n_t} = o_t \frac{e^{-m_t} c_t}{e^{-m_t} n_t} = o_t \frac{\bar c_t}{\bar n_t}.
$$
So as long as the recurrence for $\bar c_t$ and $\bar n_t$ is algebraically equivalent to the original one, the network output is exactly the same.

That is what the stabilized gates do. Using
$$
m_t = \max\bigl(\log(f_t)+m_{t-1},\, \log(i_t)\bigr),
$$
we get
$$
f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t)+m_{t-1}-m_t), \qquad i'_t = \exp(\log(i_t)-m_t).
$$
Then
$$
\bar c_t
= e^{-m_t}(f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t)
= e^{\log(f_t)+m_{t-1}-m_t}\,\bar c_{t-1} + e^{\log(i_t)-m_t} z_t
= f'_t \bar c_{t-1} + i'_t z_t,
$$
and similarly
$$
\bar n_t = f'_t \bar n_{t-1} + i'_t.
$$
So the stabilized recurrence is exactly the original recurrence written in scaled coordinates. The role of $m_t$ is simply to keep the numbers near order $1$, avoiding overflow/underflow when $i_t$ and $f_t$ are exponential gates.

The gradient statement is the same story. Since the stabilized system computes exactly the same function of the parameters as the unstabilized one, the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters are the same wherever the function is differentiable. The fact that $m_t$ uses a $\max$ does not change that; it only means the representation is piecewise-defined. At points where the two branches of the max tie, the derivative is not unique, but that is a measure-zero situation and the same issue exists in the original piecewise formulation. In practice, autodiff follows one branch consistently.

So the intuition is: $m_t$ is not an extra modeling choice, it is a numerically stable renormalization of the hidden state and normalizer. Because the same scale factor is applied to both, the ratio $c_t/n_t$ is unchanged, and therefore the forward output is unchanged exactly, not approximately.

### Why does mLSTM use a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ instead of just increasing the width of a standard LSTM cell?
Question:
The paper argues that standard LSTMs have limited storage capacity because information must be compressed into scalar cell states, and mLSTM addresses this by introducing a matrix memory with update

$ \mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top $.

I am confused about why this is qualitatively different from simply giving an ordinary LSTM many more hidden units. A wide LSTM also has a vector-valued state, so in some sense it already stores many numbers.

Is the point that the outer-product update stores pairwise key-value associations explicitly, whereas a standard LSTM would have to learn to simulate that with fixed parameters? Or is there a stronger statement about representational capacity or retrieval accuracy here?

I am looking for a conceptual explanation of what the matrix memory can do that a larger conventional LSTM state cannot do as naturally.
Answer:
A wider conventional LSTM does give you more numbers, but that is not the same thing as giving the model an explicit associative memory.

In a standard LSTM, the cell state is still just a vector of scalar slots, and each slot is updated independently by something like $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$. If you make the LSTM wider, you get more slots, but you do not automatically get a mechanism that says “store this key together with this value, and later retrieve the matching value when I see a similar query.” The network could in principle learn to approximate such behavior, but it would have to implement it indirectly through its fixed parameters and the recurrent dynamics. That is a much harder problem than having the association written into the state itself.

That is exactly what the mLSTM is trying to buy with the matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. The update

$$
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top
$$

is a fast-weight or covariance-style update: it stores the outer product of a key $\mathbf{k}_t$ and a value $\mathbf{v}_t$. Then retrieval is done by multiplying by a query $\mathbf{q}_t$, so the model can recover something like “the value associated with the current query.” In other words, the matrix explicitly represents pairwise key-value associations, rather than compressing everything into a one-dimensional hidden trajectory. This is why the paper connects mLSTM to bidirectional associative memories and fast weight programmers.

So the qualitative difference is not just “more parameters” or “more hidden units,” but a different storage geometry. A wider LSTM still stores information in a distributed vector state; an mLSTM stores information in a form that is already organized for associative lookup. That gives it a much better inductive bias for tasks like associative recall and rare-token prediction, which is exactly the kind of limitation the paper says vanilla LSTMs have.

There is also a stronger efficiency point in the paper. A large enough standard LSTM might be able to emulate some of this behavior, but the mLSTM makes the association explicit and thereby improves retrieval accuracy and storage capacity in a structured way. The paper even notes that the covariance update is optimal for maximal separability of retrieved binary vectors, and that greater separability would require limiting retrieval to pairwise interactions, at the cost of quadratic complexity like attention. So the matrix memory is not just “a bigger state”; it is a more expressive memory mechanism with a particular retrieval rule.

In short: widening an LSTM increases capacity in a generic way, while mLSTM changes the type of memory from “many scalars” to “explicit key-value associations.” That is why it can store and retrieve information more naturally, especially for associative memory tasks, and why the paper treats it as a substantive architectural change rather than just a larger hidden state.

### Why is the mLSTM update called a covariance update rule if it looks like an outer product without centering?
Question:
In the mLSTM section, the paper says the memory update uses the covariance update rule

$ \mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top $,

and then mentions that inputs are assumed to be layer-normalized so keys and values have zero mean.

I am confused because in statistics covariance usually involves centering, e.g. something like $(x - \mu_x)(y - \mu_y)^\top$, often with averaging as well. Here I only see a running sum of outer products.

Is the terminology historical from associative memory literature, where "covariance rule" means using outer products under a zero-mean assumption? If so, how important is the zero-mean assumption for the retrieval properties claimed in the paper?
Answer:
Yes — in this paper “covariance update rule” is being used in the older associative-memory / fast-weights sense, not in the strict statistical sense of computing an empirical covariance matrix from centered samples.

The key point is that the mLSTM stores associations with an outer product,
$$
\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
$$
and then retrieves by multiplying the query with this matrix. This is exactly the kind of Hebbian-style, fast-weight update that appears in the classic BAM / fast weights literature cited by the paper. In that literature, “covariance rule” usually means that the weights store correlations between key and value directions, as opposed to a plain “correlation” or “Hebbian” rule that may be biased by nonzero means. So the terminology is historical, but it is not arbitrary: the paper is pointing to the fact that, under the right preprocessing assumptions, the outer product behaves like a covariance-like association operator.

Why does centering matter? In ordinary statistics, covariance is something like
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}\big[(x-\mu_x)(y-\mu_y)^\top\big].
$$
If $\mu_x=\mu_y=0$, this reduces to $\mathbb{E}[xy^\top]$. That is exactly why the paper says that the inputs are layer-normalized before projection to keys and values: the zero-mean assumption makes the raw outer product a reasonable proxy for covariance, because there is no mean term to subtract. Without zero-mean inputs, the memory would also encode mean biases, which creates extra interference between stored items and hurts retrieval.

So the answer is: the paper is not claiming that $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ is a covariance matrix in the textbook statistical sense at each step. Rather, it is using the covariance-update terminology from associative memory theory, where a zero-mean assumption makes the outer product the right object to accumulate.

As for how important the zero-mean assumption is: it is important for the clean retrieval story, but it is not a fragile mathematical identity that must hold exactly. The paper’s retrieval argument is that, with zero-mean keys and values, the stored associations are maximally separable in the sense of Dayan’s result cited there, equivalently giving a better signal-to-noise ratio. Intuitively, when keys and values are centered, irrelevant cross-terms tend to cancel over time, so the query $\mathbf{q}_t$ can recover the intended value with less interference from other stored pairs. If the mean were not near zero, the memory would accumulate a strong common component that acts like structured noise.

So the zero-mean assumption is not just cosmetic; it is what makes the update behave like a covariance rule and supports the retrieval properties the paper emphasizes. But the mechanism itself is still fundamentally an outer-product fast-weight memory.

### What is the role of the denominator $\max\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \}$ in mLSTM retrieval?
Question:
The mLSTM hidden state is computed as

$ \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \}. $

I understand that $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ retrieves a weighted combination of stored values, but I do not understand the exact purpose of dividing by $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ (with absolute value and lower bound 1).

Is this acting like a normalization by the total matching mass, similar to the denominator in attention? If yes, why is the normalizer based on the weighted sum of keys $\mathbf{n}_t$ rather than something like a sum of positive similarities? Also, why is taking $|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|$ theoretically sensible if the sign itself seems meaningful?

I would appreciate an interpretation of this denominator in relation to linear attention or retention.
Answer:
Yes — in the paper this denominator is exactly a normalization term, and it plays the same broad role as the denominator in attention or in linear-attention-style retrieval, but adapted to the way mLSTM stores information.

If you expand the matrix memory update
$$
\mathbf{C}_t=\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top,
$$
then retrieval with a query $\mathbf{q}_t$ gives
$$
\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t
$$
which is a sum of stored values $\mathbf{v}$ weighted by their similarity to $\mathbf{q}_t$, namely by $\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q}_t$, and also by the accumulated gating factors from the forget/input dynamics. So, just like in attention, the raw retrieved vector can grow or shrink depending on how much “matching mass” is present. The normalizer
$$
\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}
$$
corrects for that scale.

The key point is that $\mathbf{n}_t$ is not an arbitrary extra vector: the paper defines it as
$$
\mathbf{n}_t=f_t\mathbf{n}_{t-1}+i_t\mathbf{k}_t,
$$
so it is the running, gate-weighted sum of keys. In other words, $\mathbf{n}_t$ records how much key mass has been stored in the memory. Then $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ acts like the total compatibility between the current query and all stored keys, i.e. the analogue of the attention denominator. This is why the paper says the normalizer state “keeps record of the strength of the gates.”

Why not sum only positive similarities? Because mLSTM is not explicitly computing a softmax over positive attention scores. Its retrieval is linear, based on outer-product memory, so the natural normalization is the accumulated key-query overlap induced by the same state that built the memory. In that sense, $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ is the linear-attention/retention-style analogue of the denominator: it measures the total effective weight of the retrieved content without requiring an explicit nonlinear attention distribution.

The absolute value and the lower bound by $1$ are there for stability, not for semantic modeling. The paper explicitly notes that the dot product can be close to zero, so dividing by it could blow up the hidden state. Taking $|\cdot|$ makes the scale factor nonnegative, and $\max(\cdot,1)$ prevents extremely small denominators. The sign is not meant to be carried by the normalizer; any directional information is already in $\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t$. The denominator’s job is only to keep the retrieval magnitude well-behaved, much like normalization in attention keeps outputs comparable across different amounts of retrieved evidence.

So the short interpretation is: $\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t$ retrieves the content, while $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$ estimates and stabilizes the amount of retrieved mass. This makes mLSTM behave more like a normalized linear memory readout than an unscaled associative sum.

### Why does removing hidden-to-hidden memory mixing make mLSTM parallelizable, but not sLSTM?
Question:
The paper says that mLSTM is fully parallelizable because it abandons memory mixing, while sLSTM is not parallelizable due to hidden-hidden connections. I am trying to understand exactly what recurrence structure prevents parallelization.

In sLSTM, the pre-activations depend on $h_{t-1}$ through terms like $r_z h_{t-1}, r_i h_{t-1}, r_f h_{t-1}, r_o h_{t-1}$. In mLSTM, the gates and projections depend only on $x_t$, and the recurrence is only in the state update itself.

Is the idea that once the per-token quantities $(f_t, i_t, \mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t, \mathbf{q}_t)$ are known independently from all other timesteps, the remaining recurrence can be rewritten as a prefix-scan or chunked parallel computation? If so, could someone explain at a high level what algebraic property makes this possible?
Answer:
Yes — that is essentially the right intuition, and the key point is the difference between a recurrence whose coefficients are known up front versus one where the coefficients themselves depend on the evolving hidden state.

In the paper, sLSTM keeps the classic LSTM-style hidden-to-hidden terms, so quantities like $\tilde{i}_t$, $\tilde{f}_t$, $\tilde{o}_t$, and $\tilde{z}_t$ depend on $h_{t-1}$. That means you cannot determine the gate values at time $t$ without first computing $h_{t-1}$, which itself depends on all earlier steps. So the gates form a genuine causal dependency chain: step $t$ needs the result of step $t-1$, not just the input $x_t$. This is what the paper calls “memory mixing,” and it is exactly what prevents full parallelization.

By contrast, in mLSTM the paper explicitly removes those hidden-hidden connections. The query, key, value, input gate, forget gate, and output gate are all functions of $x_t$ only:

$$
\mathbf{q}_t, \mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t, i_t, f_t, \mathbf{o}_t \;\text{depend only on}\; x_t.
$$

So once you have the whole input sequence, you can compute all per-token quantities independently in parallel. After that, the remaining recurrence is just

$$
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t.
$$

The reason this is parallelizable is that it is an affine recurrence with a very special structure: the update at each step is “multiply the previous state by a scalar, then add a new term.” Such recurrences can be rewritten as a scan/prefix computation because composition is associative. Concretely, if you define the per-step update as the transformation

$$
S \mapsto f_t S + u_t,
$$

then composing two steps gives

$$
S \mapsto f_2(f_1 S + u_1) + u_2 = (f_2 f_1)S + (f_2 u_1 + u_2),
$$

and this composition rule is associative. That is the algebraic property that enables parallel prefix-scan methods.

You can also see the closed form:

$$
\mathbf{C}_t
=
\left(\prod_{j=1}^t f_j\right)\mathbf{C}_0
+
\sum_{j=1}^t
\left(\prod_{\ell=j+1}^t f_\ell\right)
i_j \mathbf{v}_j \mathbf{k}_j^\top,
$$

and similarly for $\mathbf{n}_t$. Once written this way, each position’s contribution is just a gated prefix product times a local update, which is exactly the kind of structure GPUs like to parallelize.

So the short answer is: sLSTM is sequential because the gates depend on the previous hidden state, making the per-step coefficients themselves recurrent. mLSTM removes that dependency, leaving a purely input-driven affine state update. That update has an associative composition law, so it can be implemented with parallel scan/chunking, much like other linear-time recurrent models and parallel prefix algorithms.

### Why are pre up-projection blocks used for mLSTM and post up-projection blocks for sLSTM?
Question:
The architecture section says that xLSTM uses two different residual block styles: sLSTM uses a post up-projection block, while mLSTM uses a pre up-projection block. The motivation given is that mLSTM benefits from operating in a high-dimensional space because its memory capacity becomes larger there.

I am not sure I fully understand this design choice. If higher-dimensional nonlinear summarization is generally better by Cover's theorem, why not use pre up-projection for both sLSTM and mLSTM? Conversely, if post up-projection works well for Transformer FFN-style blocks, why not also use it for mLSTM?

Is this mostly an empirical choice, or is there a more principled reason tied to the different memory structures of scalar versus matrix memory?
Answer:
The paper’s choice is not presented as a purely arbitrary empirical tweak; it is tied to where the recurrent memory does its “thinking.”

The key point is that the authors distinguish between two kinds of residual blocks. In a post up-projection block, the model first processes the sequence in the original model dimension and only then maps the result up and back down. In a pre up-projection block, the input is first lifted to a higher-dimensional space, the nonlinear sequence summarization happens there, and then the result is projected back. The paper explicitly says that an xLSTM block should “non-linearly summarize the past in a high-dimensional space to better separate different histories or contexts,” and then uses Cover’s theorem as the intuition for why this helps. But that intuition matters especially for the mLSTM, because its memory is a matrix memory with a covariance-style update. A matrix memory can store key-value associations more richly when the key/query/value representations themselves live in a larger space, so the authors argue that the memory capacity “becomes larger in the high-dimensional space.”

That is why mLSTM gets the pre up-projection block: the high-dimensional representation is directly feeding the matrix memory, so the memory update and retrieval operate in the richer space where the associative capacity is larger. In other words, the dimension increase is not just for generic nonlinear expressivity; it specifically increases the effective storage and separability of the matrix memory.

For sLSTM, the situation is different. Its memory is scalar, and the paper emphasizes that sLSTM’s strength comes from exponential gating and memory mixing, not from a large associative matrix. Since the memory itself is scalar, there is much less to gain from doing the entire recurrent summarization in a very high-dimensional space. Instead, the authors use the post up-projection style, which is closer to the Transformer FFN pattern: the recurrent core does the sequential summarization in the model space, and then a feedforward-style expansion/refinement is applied after that. This matches the role of sLSTM as a sequence summarizer with memory mixing rather than a high-capacity associative store.

So the distinction is principled in the sense that it reflects the different memory structures. mLSTM benefits from high-dimensional pre-projection because its matrix memory can exploit the larger representational space for storage and retrieval. sLSTM, with scalar memory, is treated more like a recurrent core inside a residual block, and the post up-projection is the more natural place to add extra nonlinear processing without paying the cost of inflating the recurrent memory path.

There is still an empirical component, of course, because architecture choices like this always need validation. But the paper’s justification is not “we tried both and this one worked”; it is that the pre-projection aligns with mLSTM’s need for higher-dimensional associative memory, while the post-projection aligns better with sLSTM’s scalar-memory plus mixing design. In that sense, the choice is tied to the memory mechanism, not just to generic wisdom about “bigger dimensions are better.”

### In what sense are multiple heads and multiple cells equivalent for mLSTM but not for sLSTM?
Question:
The paper states that for mLSTM, multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent because there is no memory mixing, whereas for sLSTM the model can have multiple heads with memory mixing inside each head but not across heads.

I find this statement confusing. In Transformers, heads usually mean separate subspaces with separate projections, while "cells" sounds more like repeated memory units. Why should those become equivalent in mLSTM?

Is the claim that if there is no cross-cell interaction, then splitting one large matrix memory into several independent smaller ones is just a reparameterization of the same computation? If that is right, I would like to see the dimensional argument more explicitly.
Answer:
In the paper’s terminology, the key distinction is not “heads vs. cells” in the Transformer sense, but whether there is any interaction that mixes information across those subunits over time.

For mLSTM, the memory update is a matrix-valued outer-product accumulation,
$$
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
$$
and the paper explicitly says that mLSTM has “no memory mixing.” That means each memory module evolves independently, apart from the shared readout machinery. If you take one large matrix memory and split the channel dimension into several independent blocks, or equivalently instantiate several smaller matrix memories in parallel, you are just partitioning the same computation into disjoint subspaces. There is no term in the recurrence that would couple block $a$ to block $b$, so the result is a direct sum of independent memories. In that situation, “more heads” and “more cells” are just two ways of organizing the same block-diagonal structure. The dimensional argument is exactly that nothing in the update ever creates off-diagonal interaction between the blocks; each block stores its own $(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{v})$-associations and is read out separately.

That is why the paper can say that for mLSTM, “multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent as there is no memory mixing.” Here, “equivalent” should be read as “representationally the same up to reshaping/reindexing of channels,” not as a statement about identical implementation details.

For sLSTM, the situation is different because the paper adds explicit memory mixing through recurrent hidden-to-hidden connections. In the scalar-memory case, the cell update is still
$$
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,
$$
but the gates and input $z_t$ depend on the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$, and the paper emphasizes that with multiple cells, sLSTM allows recurrent weight matrices that mix cell outputs across cells. So if you have multiple cells inside one head, those cells can interact through the recurrent matrices $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{z}}, \mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{i}}, \mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{f}}, \mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{o}}$. But across heads, the paper says there is no such mixing. Hence heads behave like separated groups, while cells within a head can communicate.

So the asymmetry is: in mLSTM, the memory itself is already a non-mixing associative store, so adding heads or cells only changes how you partition a larger independent memory; in sLSTM, the whole point is that the recurrent gating creates cross-cell interaction, and that interaction is present within a head but intentionally absent across heads. That is the “new way of memory mixing” the paper is referring to.

If you want a one-line intuition: for mLSTM, heads/cells are just bookkeeping because the dynamics are block-independent; for sLSTM, the bookkeeping matters because the recurrence couples cells inside a head, so the partition affects the computation.

### Why does the paper claim that models without memory mixing cannot solve tasks like parity?
Question:
In the synthetic experiments, the authors say that Transformers and State Space Models without memory mixing cannot solve regular grammar tasks like parity, and they connect this to state tracking. I thought parity is a very simple regular language, so I am puzzled by the claim that powerful sequence models cannot solve it.

Is the issue not expressivity in the abstract sense, but rather length extrapolation under the specific architectures and training setup? Or do the cited results actually show a stronger theoretical limitation for these model classes when using finite precision or fixed-size continuous states?

I would like help reconciling the intuition that parity only requires one bit of state with the paper's statement that memory mixing is necessary here.
Answer:
The key to reconciling the statement with the fact that parity is “just one bit” is that the paper is not claiming an abstract impossibility in the sense of ordinary automata theory. It is talking about whether these architectures, as used in practice, can learn and extrapolate a *state-tracking algorithm* for formal-language tasks such as parity under finite model size, finite precision, and the training regimes in the experiments.

Parity is indeed a regular language and a deterministic finite automaton with two states can compute it. So in principle, a sequence model with enough carefully used state could represent it. But the paper’s point is that this requires the model to maintain a persistent internal state that is updated step by step according to the current input symbol and the previous state. That is exactly what the authors mean by “memory mixing”: the hidden state at time $t$ must depend nontrivially on the hidden state at time $t-1$, so that the model can implement transitions like “flip the parity bit when you see a $1$.”

In the paper’s terminology, models such as Transformers or State Space Models “without memory mixing” do not have the right kind of hidden-hidden recurrence to do this kind of state tracking robustly. They may aggregate context, but they do not naturally implement the kind of mutable finite-state controller needed for regular-grammar tasks. That is why the paper says they “cannot solve, e.g., regular grammars like the parity task” in their synthetic benchmark: the issue is not that parity is too hard in principle, but that these architectures, as trained and parameterized here, fail to learn the correct algorithm and fail to extrapolate it to longer lengths.

This matches the broader literature the paper cites, where “state tracking” is presented as a distinguishing capability of RNN-style models with recurrent hidden state. The paper’s claim is therefore best read as a statement about *learned, length-extrapolating computational behavior* under practical constraints, not a theorem that parity is outside the expressivity of all continuous-state models. In fact, a fixed-size continuous state with infinite precision can often encode surprisingly much, but that is not the regime these experiments are testing. Finite precision and finite training typically matter a lot, and the paper is arguing that memory mixing gives the model the right inductive bias to use its state as an actual automaton state rather than as a passive summary.

So the short version is: parity is simple as a language, but hard for these architectures to *realize as a stable, length-generalizing state transition rule* unless they have memory mixing. That is why the authors connect it to state tracking rather than to “expressivity” in the purely abstract sense.

### How should I interpret the claim that xLSTM has linear computation but mLSTM uses a $d \times d$ memory?
Question:
The paper says that xLSTM has linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length, but it also says that mLSTM has expensive $d \times d$ matrix memory and update operations.

I am confused about what complexity is actually being claimed. If the sequence length is $T$ and the memory dimension is $d$, is the intended cost something like $O(T d^2)$ for mLSTM versus $O(T^2 d)$ for standard attention? If so, then xLSTM is linear only in $T$, not necessarily efficient in hidden size.

Would it be fair to summarize the tradeoff as "better asymptotics in context length, but heavier per-token state updates"? I want to make sure I am not misreading the computational claims.
Answer:
Yes, that is basically the right way to read it, with one important nuance: the paper is making a claim about **dependence on sequence length** $T$, not a claim that the per-token cost is cheap in the hidden dimension $d$.

What the authors mean by “linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to the sequence length” is that xLSTM processes a sequence by a recurrent update, so it does not need to revisit all previous tokens the way standard self-attention does. In a Transformer, a length-$T$ sequence typically incurs $O(T^2)$ attention work because every token can attend to every earlier token. In xLSTM, each token is processed once, so the total cost grows linearly in $T$. That is the sense in which the model is “linear.”

For mLSTM specifically, the paper is explicit that the memory state is a matrix $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and that the update is
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top.
\]
That means the *state size* is $O(d^2)$, and the update/retrieval involves matrix operations of that scale. So if you look at one step of mLSTM, it is indeed much heavier in hidden size than a scalar-state LSTM, and much heavier than a linear-state model with $O(d)$ state. The paper’s own “Memory and Speed Considerations” section says exactly that the matrix memory is “computationally expensive through its $d \times d$ matrix memory and $d \times d$ update,” while also emphasizing that this cost is independent of sequence length and can be parallelized on GPUs.

So your summary is essentially fair if phrased carefully: xLSTM improves asymptotics in context length, but mLSTM pays a higher per-token cost because its memory is matrix-valued. In big-$O$ terms, a rough way to think about mLSTM is “linear in $T$, but with a substantial $d$-dependent cost per step,” whereas standard attention is “quadratic in $T$.” Whether you write that per-step cost as $O(d^2)$, $O(d^2)$ plus projection costs, or something more implementation-specific depends on which parts of the block you include, but the paper’s main point is not the exact arithmetic count. It is that mLSTM trades increased representational capacity and constant-in-$T$ memory for more expensive matrix operations.

So, in Stack Exchange terms: the claim is not “xLSTM is cheap.” It is “xLSTM avoids the quadratic blowup in sequence length that standard attention has.” And for mLSTM, the relevant tradeoff is indeed “better scaling with context length, at the price of a heavier matrix-valued state update.”

### Why does xLSTM[1:0] sometimes outperform xLSTM[7:1] on PALOMA even though the paper motivates adding sLSTM blocks?
Question:
The paper motivates combining mLSTM and sLSTM so that matrix memory improves capacity while sLSTM contributes memory mixing and state tracking. However, later results say that xLSTM[1:0] performs better than xLSTM[7:1] on PALOMA language tasks, even though xLSTM[7:1] includes some sLSTM blocks.

Does this mean the sLSTM blocks mainly help on specific algorithmic or state-tracking tasks but can hurt pure language modeling on some distributions? Or is it more likely that the mixture ratio and placement of sLSTM blocks were not yet optimized?

I am trying to understand whether the paper's message is "hybrid is best in general" or rather "mLSTM is strongest for language modeling, while sLSTM adds capabilities on particular tasks."
Answer:
Not really a contradiction. The paper is actually quite careful about separating two claims: that sLSTM adds a useful capability, and that this capability always improves every language-modeling benchmark. It does not claim the latter.

The key point is that the two variants are designed for somewhat different roles. The paper presents sLSTM as the component that introduces exponential gating plus memory mixing, and explicitly says this is what helps with “state tracking problems” and similar tasks where the model must revise or maintain discrete internal state. In contrast, mLSTM is the part meant to increase storage capacity via the matrix memory, and the paper emphasizes that this is especially important for language modeling and associative recall. So the intuition is not “sLSTM always helps language modeling,” but rather “sLSTM gives the model an extra ability that may matter on some tasks, while mLSTM gives stronger storage.”

That is exactly consistent with the empirical results you quoted. On PALOMA, the paper reports that $x\mathrm{LSTM}[1\!:\!0]$ performs better than $x\mathrm{LSTM}[7\!:\!1]$. Since PALOMA is a suite of next-token prediction benchmarks across many text domains, it is entirely plausible that the additional sLSTM blocks do not help there, and may even slightly hurt under the particular architecture/training setup used. The paper itself gives a strong clue for this interpretation in the limitations section: it says they “did neither fully optimize the architecture nor the hyperparameters, especially for larger xLSTM architectures,” and that “an extensive optimization process is needed for xLSTM to reach its full potential.” So the ratio and placement of sLSTM blocks were not presented as fully tuned.

At the same time, the paper does not say sLSTM is useless for language modeling. In the 300B-token experiments, both $x\mathrm{LSTM}[1\!:\!0]$ and $x\mathrm{LSTM}[7\!:\!1]$ are reported as the best models on validation perplexity for all sizes. So the hybrid can be competitive overall, but the PALOMA result suggests that the benefit is not uniform across all language distributions and metrics. A reasonable reading is therefore: mLSTM is the stronger default for pure next-token prediction, while sLSTM adds modeling power for tasks that require memory mixing or explicit state tracking, and its advantage may only show up on certain distributions or when the hybrid is better tuned.

So the paper’s message is closer to “the hybrid architecture broadens the model’s capabilities, and xLSTM as a family is strong for language modeling,” rather than “adding some sLSTM blocks monotonically improves every language benchmark.” The PALOMA result is best understood as an ablation outcome, not as a refutation of the motivation for sLSTM.
