# LSTMs Are Back? What xLSTM Means for the Future of Language Models

For a while, it looked like the story of language models was settled. LSTMs dominated early sequence modeling, then Transformers arrived with self-attention, scaling, and parallelism, and the rest of the field followed. But the paper *xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory* asks a useful question: what if the problem was not LSTMs themselves, but the specific form they were stuck with?

The authors’ answer is xLSTM, a modernized version of LSTM designed to address the main weaknesses of the original architecture. Their claim is bold but carefully scoped: with the right changes, LSTMs can once again compete with Transformers and state space models on language modeling tasks, including at large scale.

## Why LSTMs Fell Behind

The original LSTM was built to solve one of the classic problems in recurrent neural networks: vanishing gradients. Its key idea was simple and powerful. A memory cell could carry information forward through time with gated updates, letting the model decide what to keep, what to forget, and what to output.

That design worked well for a long time. LSTMs were used in text generation, translation, image captioning, handwriting generation, reinforcement learning, and more. But they had three major limitations that became more obvious as language models got bigger.

First, standard LSTMs are not good at revising storage decisions. Once they store something, it is hard to update that decision when later context suggests something more relevant. Second, their memory is compressed into scalar cell states, which limits storage capacity. Third, they are difficult to parallelize because recurrent hidden-to-hidden connections force sequential computation.

Transformers solved a different set of problems. Self-attention scales well, parallelizes well, and provides direct access to past tokens. That is why they took over large language modeling.

## What xLSTM Changes

xLSTM keeps the core LSTM idea, but modifies it in two major ways.

The first change is exponential gating. Instead of the usual sigmoid-only gating behavior, xLSTM uses exponential gates together with normalization and stabilization. The goal is to make storage decisions more flexible, especially when the model needs to revise what it has remembered.

The second change is the memory structure. xLSTM introduces two variants:

- **sLSTM**, which keeps a scalar memory but adds new gating and memory mixing behavior.
- **mLSTM**, which uses a matrix memory and a covariance-style update rule, making it more expressive and fully parallelizable.

These are then combined into xLSTM blocks and stacked into full xLSTM architectures, using the same kind of residual backbone structure that modern deep models rely on.

## Two Flavors: sLSTM and mLSTM

The paper’s design is easiest to understand if we separate the two variants.

### sLSTM: Better at Revising Decisions

sLSTM is the version aimed at fixing the “can’t revise old memories well” problem. It uses exponential input gates and stabilized forgetting. The normalization mechanism is important here: if gates grow too large, the model can become numerically unstable, so the authors introduce a stabilizer state to keep the dynamics under control.

A key idea in sLSTM is **memory mixing**. Like the original LSTM, it can have multiple memory cells, with recurrent connections between hidden states and gates. This means sLSTM still has the sequential flavor of recurrent models, but with a more flexible gating system.

### mLSTM: Bigger, Richer Memory

mLSTM tackles the storage-capacity problem. Instead of a scalar memory, it uses a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This lets it store and retrieve key-value pairs using a covariance-style update:

$$\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top.$$

This is especially useful for associative recall, where the model must remember a value attached to a key and later retrieve it using a query. Because mLSTM has no memory mixing, it can be reformulated in a parallel version, which makes it better suited to modern GPU execution.

In short: sLSTM improves decision-making over time, while mLSTM improves storage capacity and parallelism.

## Why This Is Interesting

The paper’s broader argument is not just that xLSTM is a clever architectural tweak. It is that LSTMs may have been underexplored in the Transformer era because their traditional form had real limitations, not because the idea of recurrent gated memory was inherently weak.

That matters because LSTMs have properties that Transformers do not naturally have:

- linear computation in sequence length,
- constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length,
- strong sequence tracking behavior.

The authors argue that if you combine those benefits with modern scaling techniques, exponential gating, and richer memory, you might recover a model family that is both practical and competitive.

## What the Experiments Show

The paper tests xLSTM in several settings, from synthetic tasks to language modeling at scale.

On synthetic tasks, xLSTM performs well on problems designed to test state tracking, associative recall, and long-context processing. This is important because these tasks target exactly the weaknesses the paper claims to fix.

On language modeling, the authors train models on 15B tokens from SlimPajama and compare xLSTM to Transformers, state space models, and other recurrent methods. In that setup, xLSTM achieves the best validation perplexity among the methods they compare.

They also run larger experiments with 300B tokens and model sizes up to 1.3B parameters. In those experiments:

- xLSTM maintains low perplexity when extrapolating to longer contexts,
- xLSTM performs best on validation perplexity for all tested model sizes,
- xLSTM is competitive or best on most downstream reasoning tasks,
- xLSTM performs strongly across 571 PALOMA text domains,
- scaling-law trends suggest xLSTM should remain competitive as models get larger.

The paper also reports generation-time behavior. As expected, xLSTM and other recurrent models scale linearly with sequence length, while Transformer decoding is quadratic in the way it is measured here. Because xLSTM has constant memory with respect to sequence length, it can support larger batch sizes and achieves high throughput in their experiments.

## The Main Takeaway

The big message of the paper is not that Transformers are obsolete. It is that LSTM-style recurrence still has room to grow, especially if you modernize it aggressively.

xLSTM is an attempt to bring back the best parts of LSTMs while removing the bottlenecks that limited them:

- exponential gating helps with revising memory,
- scalar and matrix memory variants expand what can be stored,
- a matrix-based variant enables parallel computation,
- residual stacking makes the architecture fit modern deep learning practice.

The result is a model family that, in the authors’ experiments, performs favorably against contemporary Transformers and state space models.

## What to Watch Going Forward

The paper is careful about its limitations. The authors note that sLSTM is not fully parallelizable, mLSTM is computationally heavier because of its matrix memory, and the current CUDA kernels are not fully optimized. They also say that further tuning and optimization are likely needed.

That means xLSTM is not the end of the story. But it does suggest that the recurrent side of sequence modeling is not done yet.

If Transformers defined the first wave of large language models, xLSTM is a reminder that architectural ideas from earlier eras can still matter, especially when updated with modern training and scaling techniques. The field may not be choosing between “old” recurrent networks and “new” Transformers after all. It may be learning how to combine the best ideas from both.

And that makes xLSTM worth paying attention to.



# The Three Problems with Classic LSTMs—and How xLSTM Fixes Them

Long Short-Term Memory networks, or LSTMs, were one of the original success stories of sequence modeling. They solved the vanishing-gradient problem with a simple but powerful idea: keep a memory cell, and control what gets written, forgotten, and read through gates.

For a long time, that was enough to power strong results in language, speech, time series, and reinforcement learning. But Transformers changed the field by scaling much better and making training more parallelizable. The xLSTM paper asks a natural question: what happens if we take the core LSTM idea, modernize it, and scale it up to today’s model sizes?

The answer starts with three limitations of classic LSTMs.

## 1. Classic LSTMs struggle to revise memories

The original LSTM cell is designed to preserve information over time. That is a strength, but it can also become a weakness. Once the model stores something, changing that decision later can be difficult.

The paper illustrates this with a nearest-neighbor search task. The model sees a reference vector and then scans a sequence to find the most similar one. If a later vector turns out to be a better match, a standard LSTM may have trouble replacing the earlier memory with the better candidate.

xLSTM addresses this with **exponential gating**. Instead of using only sigmoid gates, xLSTM allows input and forget gates to be exponential, combined with normalization and stabilization. The key idea is that the model can make sharper updates to memory, which helps it revise past storage decisions when new evidence arrives.

In the paper’s formulation, this is done in the new **sLSTM** variant. It keeps the familiar LSTM-style recurrence, but changes the gating mechanism and adds a normalizer and stabilizer state to keep the exponentials under control.

## 2. Classic LSTMs have limited storage capacity

A standard LSTM stores information in a scalar cell state. That is compact, but also restrictive. When a task requires remembering many associations, compressing everything into scalar memory can become a bottleneck.

The paper tests this with a multi-query associative recall task. Here, the model must memorize many key-value pairs and later retrieve the right value from a query. As the number of pairs grows, the pressure on memory increases.

xLSTM’s answer is the **mLSTM**, which replaces the scalar cell with a **matrix memory**. Instead of storing one compressed state, it stores richer associations using a covariance-style update:
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top.
\]
Here, a key-value pair is written into memory as an outer product. Later, retrieval happens with a query vector and matrix multiplication.

This gives the model a much larger storage space than a scalar cell. The paper also introduces a normalizer state to track the strength of stored associations and stabilize retrieval.

In plain terms, mLSTM is trying to do for recurrent memory what attention does for associative retrieval, but with a recurrent update rule and linear memory in sequence length.

## 3. Classic LSTMs are hard to parallelize

The third limitation is the one that hurt classic LSTMs most in the Transformer era: they are inherently sequential. Each step depends on the previous hidden state, which makes large-scale training and inference harder to parallelize.

This is especially important for modern language modeling, where efficiency matters as much as accuracy.

xLSTM splits the design into two variants:

- **sLSTM**, which keeps memory mixing through recurrent hidden-hidden connections.
- **mLSTM**, which removes memory mixing and uses a matrix memory that can be processed in a fully parallelizable way.

This tradeoff matters. sLSTM improves the model’s ability to track state, but it remains sequential. mLSTM, on the other hand, is designed to run efficiently on GPUs using standard matrix operations.

The architecture choice around these blocks is also important. The paper places them inside **residual blocks** and stacks those blocks into full xLSTM architectures, following the same general recipe used by modern LLMs.

## How the pieces fit together

xLSTM is not just “an LSTM with bigger layers.” It is a structured redesign with two main upgrades:

### Exponential gating
This gives the model stronger control over memory updates and helps it revise past decisions. Because exponentials can become unstable, the paper adds normalization and stabilization so the computation stays well-behaved.

### New memory structures
The original scalar memory is extended in two directions:

- **sLSTM** keeps scalar memory but adds new gating and memory mixing.
- **mLSTM** uses matrix memory for richer storage and retrieval.

The result is a family of recurrent blocks that can be stacked like modern deep networks.

## What the experiments show

The paper tests xLSTM on synthetic tasks, long-context benchmarks, and language modeling at scale.

A few trends stand out:

- On tasks that require **state tracking**, xLSTM’s memory mixing helps.
- On associative recall tasks, **mLSTM** gives much stronger memory capacity than classic LSTMs.
- On language modeling, xLSTM performs favorably compared with Transformers, State Space Models, and other recurrent models, especially when scaled up.

The paper also reports strong scaling behavior, suggesting that larger xLSTM models continue to improve in a way that is competitive with current large language model families.

## The big picture

The core message of the paper is not that LSTMs were wrong. It is that the original LSTM idea was only a starting point.

xLSTM keeps the useful parts of LSTMs:
the memory cell, the gating, and the recurrent structure.

Then it fixes the three main bottlenecks:

- **Revision of memories** through exponential gating
- **Storage capacity** through matrix memory
- **Parallelization** through a parallelizable mLSTM design

That combination is what makes xLSTM interesting. It is a serious attempt to bring recurrent modeling back into the LLM era, not by competing with Transformers on their own terms, but by modernizing the LSTM family for large-scale language modeling.

If the paper is right, LSTMs still had more to give. xLSTM is the proof of concept.



# Exponential Gating Explained: The Secret Sauce Behind xLSTM

LSTMs have always had one big idea going for them: gating. Instead of blindly overwriting memory at every step, they decide what to keep, what to forget, and what to expose. That simple mechanism is a major reason LSTMs were so effective for sequence modeling.

The xLSTM paper keeps that core idea, but makes a crucial change: it replaces standard sigmoid-style gating with **exponential gating**, then adds **normalization** and **stabilization** so the model stays well-behaved. This turns out to matter a lot. One of the paper’s main claims is that this change helps the model **revise earlier storage decisions** and track important information more reliably over long sequences.

## Why ordinary gating is not enough

In a vanilla LSTM, the cell state is updated with something like

$$
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,
$$

where the forget gate $f_t$ decides how much of the old memory to keep and the input gate $i_t$ decides how much new information to write.

This works well, but it has a limitation: once the model stores something, its ability to later revise that decision is constrained by the usual sigmoid gates. In the paper, this shows up clearly in tasks like nearest-neighbor search over a sequence. If a better match appears later, a standard LSTM can struggle to correct itself and replace the earlier memory with the more relevant one.

That is exactly the kind of problem exponential gating is designed to improve.

## What changes with exponential gating

In xLSTM, the paper keeps the same basic memory update structure, but changes the gate activations. Instead of only using sigmoid gates, the input gate is made exponential, and the forget gate can also be exponential.

Conceptually, this means the gates are no longer just soft switches between 0 and 1. They become multiplicative weights that can express stronger relative preferences.

That matters because the model is no longer limited to “keep a little” or “keep a lot” in a bounded sigmoid range. It can more flexibly scale updates, which gives it more room to revise storage decisions when new evidence arrives.

A useful intuition is to think of memory writing less like flipping a switch and more like adjusting the strength of an edit. If the model sees a new token that is much more relevant than what it stored before, exponential gating gives it a stronger mechanism for making that correction.

## The normalization trick: keeping the math under control

Exponential functions can get large very quickly. That is great for expressiveness, but dangerous for stability. If left unchecked, values can overflow or become numerically hard to manage.

The paper solves this with a **normalizer state**. For the scalar sLSTM, the model tracks a second state:

$$
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t.
$$

The hidden state is then computed as

$$
h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t, \quad \tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
$$

So instead of using raw accumulated memory directly, the model divides by a running normalization term. Intuitively, this makes the cell behave more like a weighted average than an ever-growing accumulator.

That is the key trick: the model can use exponential gates without letting the memory explode.

## Stabilization: making exponential gates practical

Normalization alone is not enough. The paper also introduces a stabilization mechanism based on an auxiliary state $m_t$:

$$
m_t = \max\big(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t)\big).
$$

The gates are then rescaled into stabilized versions:

$$
i'_t = \exp(\log(i_t) - m_t), \quad
f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t).
$$

The important point is not the bookkeeping itself, but the effect. This rescaling lets the model work with exponential gates while avoiding numerical blow-ups. The paper notes that replacing the original gates with the stabilized versions does not change the network output or the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters.

In other words, the stabilization is not a hack that changes the model’s meaning. It is a mathematically equivalent way to make the computation safe.

## Why this helps the model revise memory

The real payoff of exponential gating is better control over memory updates over time.

In a standard gated recurrent model, memory is updated incrementally, but the influence of past decisions can be hard to adjust sharply. If the model has stored something early in the sequence, it may be slow to “realize” that later evidence should matter more.

Exponential gating helps because it can express stronger relative weighting between competing pieces of information. When a better match appears later in the sequence, the model has more flexibility to shift its memory toward that newer signal.

The paper demonstrates this on a nearest-neighbor search task: the model scans a sequence for the most similar vector and must output its associated value at the end. Standard LSTM struggles with the need to revise earlier storage decisions, while xLSTM is designed specifically to handle this kind of update better.

## Why this is not just a small implementation detail

At first glance, changing sigmoid gates to exponential gates might sound like a minor tweak. It is not.

In xLSTM, exponential gating is one of two central ideas, along with modified memory structure. The authors argue that gating is not just about preventing vanishing gradients. It is also about giving the model a better mechanism for selecting, revising, and stabilizing what it remembers.

This matters especially for language modeling, where the model often has to track long-range dependencies, entities, and context that can change as new tokens arrive. If the model can revise its internal state more effectively, it can keep the right information around for longer.

## The bigger picture

The paper’s broader claim is that xLSTM, with exponential gating plus normalization and stabilization, can compete favorably with modern Transformers and State Space Models. The gating change is not the only ingredient, but it is a major one.

What makes the approach interesting is that it preserves the spirit of LSTM while addressing one of its core weaknesses. Instead of treating memory as a fixed additive trace, xLSTM makes memory more adaptive. It can store, revise, and normalize its internal state in a way that is better suited for large-scale sequence modeling.

## Takeaway

If you remember one thing, it is this: **exponential gating gives LSTMs a more flexible way to manage memory, and normalization plus stabilization make that flexibility usable in practice**.

That combination is one of the reasons xLSTM can better handle tasks that require revising old decisions and tracking important information across long sequences. It is a good example of a classic idea being modernized rather than replaced outright.

Sometimes the most powerful upgrade is not inventing a completely new architecture, but making a familiar mechanism smarter. Exponential gating is exactly that kind of upgrade.



# From Scalar Memory to Matrix Memory: Why mLSTM Thinks More Like Retrieval

The original LSTM was built around a simple and powerful idea: carry information forward in a memory cell, update it gradually, and use gates to control what gets written, forgotten, and exposed. That design helped recurrent models survive long sequences, but it also came with a clear bottleneck: the memory cell is essentially scalar. If you want to store richer associations, you have to compress them into a small state.

The mLSTM variant in *xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory* tackles that limitation directly. Instead of storing a single value per memory channel, it replaces the scalar cell state with a matrix memory. That one change moves the model from “compress and remember” toward something closer to retrieval: store a key-value association now, and reconstruct the value later when a matching query appears.

## Why scalar memory runs out of room

In a standard LSTM, the memory update is built around a scalar cell state $c_t$. The model decides what to keep with a forget gate, what to add with an input gate, and what to expose with an output gate. This works well for summarizing a sequence, but it has limited capacity. A single scalar state can only retain so much structured information before different facts start interfering with one another.

That becomes especially visible on tasks that depend on rare details or exact associations. In the paper, the authors point to rare token prediction on Wikitext-103 and to associative recall tasks as examples where limited storage hurts. If a model needs to remember a specific key-value pair buried in a long sequence, scalar memory is often too compressed to preserve the full relation cleanly.

## The mLSTM idea: store associations, not just summaries

mLSTM expands the memory cell from a scalar to a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This is not just a bigger box for more information. It changes the type of information the model can store.

At each step, mLSTM forms a key $\mathbf{k}_t$, a value $\mathbf{v}_t$, and later uses a query $\mathbf{q}_t$ to retrieve information. This is a familiar pattern if you have seen attention mechanisms: keys identify what to look for, values hold the content, and queries ask for the matching content. mLSTM borrows that retrieval flavor, but it does so inside a recurrent memory structure.

The core update is:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top
\]

This is a covariance-style update. The outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ writes a key-value association directly into memory. Later, the model retrieves with matrix multiplication:

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}
\]

So instead of asking, “What single summary should I keep?”, mLSTM asks, “What association should I write down so I can recover it when the right query appears?”

## A retrieval mechanism with LSTM-style gating

mLSTM does not abandon the LSTM recipe. It keeps the familiar input, forget, and output gates, but adapts them to the matrix-memory setting.

The forget gate acts like a decay rate on the memory matrix, and the input gate acts like a learning rate for writing new associations. The output gate still controls how much of the retrieved content is exposed.

This matters because the model is not simply accumulating key-value pairs forever. It is still selective. It can retain useful associations and downweight older ones, which is important when the sequence contains many unrelated facts or when the relevant pattern changes over time.

The paper also uses a normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$, which tracks the strength of the stored keys over time. This stabilizes retrieval by scaling the readout based on how strongly the memory was written.

## Why matrix memory helps with rare tokens

Rare token prediction is a good stress test for memory capacity. Common tokens are easier because the model sees them often and can rely on broad statistical patterns. Rare tokens are harder because the model has fewer chances to learn and reuse them.

The paper reports that LSTM performs worse on rare tokens, and connects that to its limited storage capacity. A scalar cell state has to compress too much, which makes it hard to preserve the exact information needed for low-frequency events.

mLSTM’s matrix memory helps because it can store more structured relationships. Rather than squeezing everything into one stream of numbers, it can represent interactions between a key and its value. That gives it a better chance of keeping the information needed to predict uncommon tokens correctly.

## Why associative recall is a natural fit

The Multi-Query Associative Recall task is almost a direct match for mLSTM’s design. In this setup, the model sees a sequence of random key-value pairs and later has to retrieve the right value when given a query. The challenge scales up with more pairs and longer contexts.

This is exactly the kind of problem where retrieval matters more than compression. You want the model to remember many distinct associations without them blending together.

According to the paper, xLSTM variants with mLSTM perform especially well on this task. In particular, xLSTM[1:1] outperforms other non-Transformer models, and the results suggest that the matrix memory is doing what it was designed to do: preserve and retrieve specific associations at scale.

## Linear sequence complexity, without losing the retrieval benefit

One of the most interesting parts of mLSTM is that it keeps linear complexity in the sequence length. That is a major practical difference from attention, whose full pairwise interactions typically scale quadratically with context length.

mLSTM gets retrieval-like behavior without paying the full attention cost over the sequence. The memory matrix itself is expensive, since it is $d \times d$, but the update and retrieval are standard matrix operations and can be parallelized on GPUs. The paper emphasizes that this gives xLSTM constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length, which makes it attractive for long-context settings and edge-style deployments.

So mLSTM is not attention, but it borrows the useful part of attention: explicit key-value storage and retrieval. It does this while staying in the recurrent family and keeping sequence-time complexity linear.

## Where mLSTM fits in the xLSTM design

The paper presents xLSTM as a family with two main extensions:

- sLSTM, which adds exponential gating and new memory mixing on top of scalar memory
- mLSTM, which replaces scalar memory with a matrix and uses a covariance-style update rule

mLSTM is the version that most clearly resembles retrieval systems. It is built for richer storage, and it is fully parallelizable because it avoids hidden-to-hidden memory mixing. That makes it a strong candidate when the task depends on remembering many distinct associations over long sequences.

The authors also note that multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent for mLSTM, since there is no memory mixing. That keeps the design relatively clean: the model scales by increasing the representational space of the memory, not by adding more sequential dependencies.

## The big picture

The move from scalar memory to matrix memory changes the role of recurrent state. In a classic LSTM, memory is mainly a compressed summary. In mLSTM, memory becomes a structured store for associations. That makes it feel much closer to retrieval systems, especially attention, while preserving the linear-time advantages of recurrence.

That combination seems to be the core appeal of mLSTM. It is expressive enough to handle associative recall and rare-token prediction better than scalar-memory designs, but still efficient enough to avoid the sequence-length cost of attention.

In the broader xLSTM story, mLSTM is the part that says: if the problem is remembering relationships, maybe the memory should look like a relationship. A matrix is a much better fit than a single scalar when what you really need is key-value retrieval.



# sLSTM vs. mLSTM: Two New Kinds of Memory for Long-Context AI

Long Short-Term Memory networks, or LSTMs, were designed to solve one of the classic problems in recurrent neural networks: how to remember useful information over long sequences without gradients disappearing. The xLSTM paper revisits that idea with a modern twist. Instead of treating LSTM as a fixed legacy architecture, it introduces two new memory mechanisms: sLSTM and mLSTM.

These two variants solve different bottlenecks. sLSTM focuses on **state tracking** and **memory mixing**, while mLSTM focuses on **storage capacity** and **parallelizable retrieval**. Together, they form the core of xLSTM.

## Why standard LSTMs fall short

The paper points to three main limitations of classic LSTMs. First, they struggle to **revise earlier storage decisions** when new evidence arrives. Second, they compress information into scalar memory cells, which limits how much they can store. Third, their recurrent hidden-to-hidden connections force sequential processing, making them hard to parallelize at scale.

xLSTM addresses these issues by changing both the gating mechanism and the memory structure.

## sLSTM: better at state tracking and revising decisions

The sLSTM is the closer descendant of the original LSTM. It keeps the familiar scalar memory update, but changes the gates to use **exponential gating** instead of only sigmoid gating. That matters because exponential gates can make the model much better at revising earlier choices when later tokens provide stronger evidence.

To make exponential gates stable, the paper adds a **normalizer state** and a **stabilizer state**. The normalizer keeps track of the accumulated gate strength, so the model can divide the cell state by this normalization term before producing the hidden state. This allows the model to use exponential gates without numerical overflow.

In simplified terms, sLSTM is designed to do what many sequence tasks actually require: keep track of which state you are in, update that state when the input changes, and mix information across memory cells. That makes it especially relevant for **state tracking problems**, such as formal language tasks where the model must remember and update structured context over time.

The paper also notes that sLSTM can have multiple memory cells and multiple heads, with memory mixing within each head. This introduces a new kind of memory mixing that is more expressive than the original scalar-cell LSTM setup.

## mLSTM: bigger memory and parallelization

If sLSTM is about tracking state, mLSTM is about **storing more information**.

Instead of a scalar cell state, mLSTM uses a **matrix memory** $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This lets it store key-value associations using a covariance-style update rule:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top
\]

Here, the model stores a key $\mathbf{k}_t$ and a value $\mathbf{v}_t$, and later retrieves the value using a query $\mathbf{q}_t$. This is conceptually similar to associative memory: write a pair now, recover the value later when the right query appears.

This structure gives mLSTM two important advantages.

First, it has **much higher storage capacity** than a scalar memory cell. That is especially useful for tasks like associative recall and rare-token prediction, where the model must preserve many distinct pieces of information.

Second, because mLSTM does not use hidden-to-hidden memory mixing, its recurrence can be reformulated in a **parallel version**. That makes it much more GPU-friendly than a standard sequential RNN. In practice, this is a big deal: the paper emphasizes that mLSTM is the xLSTM variant that can be parallelized efficiently.

## The key tradeoff: expressivity versus parallelism

The difference between sLSTM and mLSTM is not just about implementation. It reflects two different design goals.

sLSTM is the better choice when the model needs to **track latent state changes**, revise earlier decisions, or mix information across memory cells. This makes it strong on tasks that require sequential reasoning over structure, not just storage.

mLSTM is the better choice when the model needs to **store and retrieve many associations** and do so efficiently on hardware. Its matrix memory provides richer storage, and its lack of memory mixing allows parallel computation.

So the tradeoff looks like this:

- **sLSTM**: better for state tracking, adaptive memory updates, and memory mixing.
- **mLSTM**: better for storage capacity, associative recall, and parallel execution.

## Why combining them works

xLSTM does not pick one of these ideas and discard the other. Instead, it stacks them together in residual blocks. The paper uses architectures like xLSTM[1:1] or xLSTM[7:1], which indicate the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks.

This combination matters because the two variants complement each other. mLSTM can handle large-scale associative storage, while sLSTM adds the ability to mix and revise state in ways that help with harder sequence tasks. The result is a model family that performs strongly on both synthetic long-context tasks and language modeling benchmarks.

On the paper’s synthetic experiments, xLSTM variants show strong results on formal language tasks, associative recall, and the Long Range Arena. On large-scale language modeling, xLSTM outperforms the compared methods in validation perplexity on the 15B-token SlimPajama setup, and also performs strongly after scaling to 300B tokens.

## Takeaway

The simplest way to think about xLSTM is this: it upgrades LSTM memory in two different directions.

sLSTM makes memory more flexible and state-aware through exponential gating and memory mixing. mLSTM makes memory larger and more parallelizable through matrix-based associative storage.

Together, they give xLSTM a more modern memory system for long-context AI. That is the paper’s central message: LSTMs are not just old recurrent models. With the right changes, they can still compete with contemporary Transformer and state-space approaches at scale.



# Can xLSTM Beat Transformers on Long Context? What the Results Actually Show

The xLSTM paper starts from a simple question: if we scale LSTMs up to modern large-model sizes and fix some of their known weaknesses, can they compete with Transformers and State Space Models in language modeling?

The experimental results suggest the answer is yes. More specifically, xLSTM does not just remain viable at scale; in several settings it performs better than the comparison models the authors test, including Transformer baselines like Llama and recurrent or state-space alternatives like Mamba and RWKV.

## The short version

The main takeaway from the experiments is that xLSTM is competitive across multiple regimes:

- On synthetic tasks, it handles state tracking and associative memory better than several non-Transformer baselines.
- On standard language modeling with 15B training tokens, xLSTM achieves the best validation perplexity among the compared methods.
- On larger-scale training with 300B tokens, xLSTM remains strong on long-context extrapolation, downstream tasks, and broad domain evaluation.
- Scaling-law plots suggest the advantage does not disappear as models get larger.

In other words, the paper’s evidence supports the idea that a modernized LSTM can be more than a curiosity. It can act as a serious language model backbone.

## Why the authors think xLSTM matters

The paper’s core claim is not just that xLSTM is another recurrent model. It is designed to address three LSTM limitations that matter for language modeling.

First, standard LSTMs struggle to revise earlier storage decisions. The authors target this with exponential gating. Second, standard LSTMs compress memory into scalar cells, which limits how much information they can retain. xLSTM’s mLSTM variant replaces scalar memory with matrix memory. Third, classic recurrence is hard to parallelize, so xLSTM introduces a split between a parallelizable matrix-memory version and a sequential scalar-memory version.

The experiments are meant to test whether these changes actually translate into better model behavior, not just cleaner theory.

## Synthetic tasks: does xLSTM fix the weaknesses it was designed to fix?

The paper begins with tasks chosen to probe specific capabilities.

On formal-language tasks, the authors use these tests to examine whether xLSTM’s exponential gating and memory mixing help with state tracking. The result is that xLSTM can solve problems that models without memory mixing struggle with. The paper specifically notes that Transformers and State Space Models without memory mixing cannot solve some regular grammar tasks, such as parity, while xLSTM can.

That matters because these tasks are meant to test whether a model can keep track of latent state over long sequences. The result is not just that xLSTM fits training data better, but that it behaves more like a model with stronger sequence-tracking ability.

On the Multi-Query Associative Recall task, the question is different: can the model store and retrieve many key-value pairs? Here the matrix-memory version matters. The paper reports that xLSTM, especially the mixed setup xLSTM[1:1], performs best among the non-Transformer models. Importantly, the scalar-memory block does not reduce performance; it appears to complement the matrix-memory block.

On Long Range Arena, xLSTM again shows strong performance across tasks, suggesting that the architecture handles long-context problems reliably rather than excelling on only one benchmark.

## Language modeling on 15B tokens: xLSTM comes out on top

The first large language modeling comparison is trained on 15B tokens from SlimPajama. The model family list is broad: GPT-3-style Transformers, Llama, H3, Mamba, RWKV variants, GLA, HGRN, RetNet, Hyena, and xLSTM variants.

The headline result is simple: xLSTM gets the best validation perplexity among the compared methods.

That is the most direct practical signal in the paper. Perplexity is not the whole story, but for next-token prediction it is the standard metric, and lower is better. In this experiment, xLSTM is not merely close to Transformer performance. It surpasses all the listed baselines.

The ablation study is also informative. The authors gradually morph a vanilla LSTM into xLSTM and find that the performance gains come from both of the new ideas:

- exponential gating
- matrix memory

This is important because it suggests the improvements are not due to one isolated trick. The gains appear to depend on the architectural changes working together.

## What happens at 300B tokens?

The paper’s larger-scale experiment is the one that most directly addresses the “can it stay competitive as models grow?” question.

The authors train xLSTM, RWKV-4, Llama, and Mamba on 300B tokens from SlimPajama across model sizes from 125M to 1.3B parameters. They then test:

- long-context extrapolation
- validation perplexity
- downstream reasoning tasks
- PALOMA domain-level language modeling
- scaling behavior

### Long-context extrapolation

This is one of the clearest places where xLSTM stands out. The models are trained with context length 2048 and tested up to 16384. The paper reports that xLSTM maintains low perplexity on longer contexts, while the comparison models degrade more noticeably.

For users who care about long-document generation or context retention, this is a meaningful result. It suggests xLSTM is not just fitting short contexts well during training; it generalizes better when asked to work beyond the training window.

### Validation perplexity and downstream tasks

On the SlimPajama validation set, xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] are the best models across all tested sizes. On downstream tasks, xLSTM is the best method on the vast majority of tasks and model sizes, with Mamba occasionally winning on ARC.

This is a useful reminder that no model wins every benchmark. But the overall pattern is strong: xLSTM is broadly competitive and often best-in-class in this setup.

### PALOMA domain benchmarks

The PALOMA results are especially interesting because they cover 571 text domains, ranging from mainstream text sources to niche communities.

Here the paper reports that xLSTM[1:0] outperforms:

- Mamba on 568 of 571 domains
- Llama on 486 of 571 domains
- RWKV-4 on 570 of 571 domains

That is a very strong result. It suggests xLSTM is not just good at the average benchmark setting, but also robust across a wide variety of text domains.

## What the scaling laws suggest

One of the most important questions for any new architecture is whether its advantage persists as you scale model size and data. The paper tackles this with power-law scaling plots.

The result is encouraging for xLSTM. The models show similar scaling behavior, but xLSTM has a better offset than the other baselines. In practical terms, that means the curve is shifted in xLSTM’s favor across model sizes. The authors interpret this as evidence that larger xLSTM models should continue to remain competitive, and possibly increasingly so.

This does not prove xLSTM will dominate at very large scales, but it does show the trend is not obviously saturating against it. In a field where scaling behavior often matters as much as architecture, that is a meaningful signal.

## Speed and memory: why recurrence still matters

The paper also makes an efficiency argument. xLSTM has linear computation and constant memory with respect to sequence length, unlike Transformers, whose cost grows quadratically with context length in standard attention setups.

That shows up in generation benchmarks. The paper reports linear scaling for xLSTM and other recurrent models, compared with quadratic scaling for Llama. Because xLSTM uses constant memory, it can also support larger batch sizes and achieves high decoding throughput.

There is a tradeoff, though. The matrix-memory version is computationally expensive, and the current CUDA kernels are not fully optimized. The authors are explicit about this. So the practical speed advantage is not “xLSTM is cheaper than everything else,” but rather that it has a favorable asymptotic profile and promising throughput characteristics.

## So, can xLSTM beat Transformers on long context?

Based on the paper’s experiments, the most accurate answer is:

Yes, xLSTM can be highly competitive with Transformers on long-context language modeling, and in the reported experiments it often performs better on perplexity, extrapolation, and broad-domain evaluation.

That said, the result is more nuanced than “xLSTM beats Transformers everywhere.” The paper shows a strong set of advantages in the tested regimes, especially on long-context extrapolation and validation perplexity, but it also acknowledges that the implementation is not fully optimized and that more tuning could further change the picture.

The broader conclusion is that the LSTM family is not obsolete. With exponential gating and richer memory structures, xLSTM shows that recurrent models can still scale into the LLM era and remain serious contenders.

## Bottom line

If you want the practical takeaway, it is this:

xLSTM is not presented as a nostalgic revival of old recurrent networks. It is a modernized sequence model that, in the authors’ experiments, competes very well with Transformers and State Space Models at language modeling scale.

The strongest evidence comes from three places:

- better perplexity on large-scale training runs,
- strong long-context extrapolation,
- and favorable scaling behavior.

For readers asking whether recurrence still has a future in large language modeling, this paper’s answer is clearly yes.
