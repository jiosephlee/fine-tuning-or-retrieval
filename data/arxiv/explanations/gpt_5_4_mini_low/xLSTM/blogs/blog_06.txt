\title{Blogs about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

# Can xLSTM Beat Transformers on Long Context? What the Results Actually Show

The xLSTM paper starts from a simple question: if we scale LSTMs up to modern large-model sizes and fix some of their known weaknesses, can they compete with Transformers and State Space Models in language modeling?

The experimental results suggest the answer is yes. More specifically, xLSTM does not just remain viable at scale; in several settings it performs better than the comparison models the authors test, including Transformer baselines like Llama and recurrent or state-space alternatives like Mamba and RWKV.

## The short version

The main takeaway from the experiments is that xLSTM is competitive across multiple regimes:

- On synthetic tasks, it handles state tracking and associative memory better than several non-Transformer baselines.
- On standard language modeling with 15B training tokens, xLSTM achieves the best validation perplexity among the compared methods.
- On larger-scale training with 300B tokens, xLSTM remains strong on long-context extrapolation, downstream tasks, and broad domain evaluation.
- Scaling-law plots suggest the advantage does not disappear as models get larger.

In other words, the paper’s evidence supports the idea that a modernized LSTM can be more than a curiosity. It can act as a serious language model backbone.

## Why the authors think xLSTM matters

The paper’s core claim is not just that xLSTM is another recurrent model. It is designed to address three LSTM limitations that matter for language modeling.

First, standard LSTMs struggle to revise earlier storage decisions. The authors target this with exponential gating. Second, standard LSTMs compress memory into scalar cells, which limits how much information they can retain. xLSTM’s mLSTM variant replaces scalar memory with matrix memory. Third, classic recurrence is hard to parallelize, so xLSTM introduces a split between a parallelizable matrix-memory version and a sequential scalar-memory version.

The experiments are meant to test whether these changes actually translate into better model behavior, not just cleaner theory.

## Synthetic tasks: does xLSTM fix the weaknesses it was designed to fix?

The paper begins with tasks chosen to probe specific capabilities.

On formal-language tasks, the authors use these tests to examine whether xLSTM’s exponential gating and memory mixing help with state tracking. The result is that xLSTM can solve problems that models without memory mixing struggle with. The paper specifically notes that Transformers and State Space Models without memory mixing cannot solve some regular grammar tasks, such as parity, while xLSTM can.

That matters because these tasks are meant to test whether a model can keep track of latent state over long sequences. The result is not just that xLSTM fits training data better, but that it behaves more like a model with stronger sequence-tracking ability.

On the Multi-Query Associative Recall task, the question is different: can the model store and retrieve many key-value pairs? Here the matrix-memory version matters. The paper reports that xLSTM, especially the mixed setup xLSTM[1:1], performs best among the non-Transformer models. Importantly, the scalar-memory block does not reduce performance; it appears to complement the matrix-memory block.

On Long Range Arena, xLSTM again shows strong performance across tasks, suggesting that the architecture handles long-context problems reliably rather than excelling on only one benchmark.

## Language modeling on 15B tokens: xLSTM comes out on top

The first large language modeling comparison is trained on 15B tokens from SlimPajama. The model family list is broad: GPT-3-style Transformers, Llama, H3, Mamba, RWKV variants, GLA, HGRN, RetNet, Hyena, and xLSTM variants.

The headline result is simple: xLSTM gets the best validation perplexity among the compared methods.

That is the most direct practical signal in the paper. Perplexity is not the whole story, but for next-token prediction it is the standard metric, and lower is better. In this experiment, xLSTM is not merely close to Transformer performance. It surpasses all the listed baselines.

The ablation study is also informative. The authors gradually morph a vanilla LSTM into xLSTM and find that the performance gains come from both of the new ideas:

- exponential gating
- matrix memory

This is important because it suggests the improvements are not due to one isolated trick. The gains appear to depend on the architectural changes working together.

## What happens at 300B tokens?

The paper’s larger-scale experiment is the one that most directly addresses the “can it stay competitive as models grow?” question.

The authors train xLSTM, RWKV-4, Llama, and Mamba on 300B tokens from SlimPajama across model sizes from 125M to 1.3B parameters. They then test:

- long-context extrapolation
- validation perplexity
- downstream reasoning tasks
- PALOMA domain-level language modeling
- scaling behavior

### Long-context extrapolation

This is one of the clearest places where xLSTM stands out. The models are trained with context length 2048 and tested up to 16384. The paper reports that xLSTM maintains low perplexity on longer contexts, while the comparison models degrade more noticeably.

For users who care about long-document generation or context retention, this is a meaningful result. It suggests xLSTM is not just fitting short contexts well during training; it generalizes better when asked to work beyond the training window.

### Validation perplexity and downstream tasks

On the SlimPajama validation set, xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] are the best models across all tested sizes. On downstream tasks, xLSTM is the best method on the vast majority of tasks and model sizes, with Mamba occasionally winning on ARC.

This is a useful reminder that no model wins every benchmark. But the overall pattern is strong: xLSTM is broadly competitive and often best-in-class in this setup.

### PALOMA domain benchmarks

The PALOMA results are especially interesting because they cover 571 text domains, ranging from mainstream text sources to niche communities.

Here the paper reports that xLSTM[1:0] outperforms:

- Mamba on 568 of 571 domains
- Llama on 486 of 571 domains
- RWKV-4 on 570 of 571 domains

That is a very strong result. It suggests xLSTM is not just good at the average benchmark setting, but also robust across a wide variety of text domains.

## What the scaling laws suggest

One of the most important questions for any new architecture is whether its advantage persists as you scale model size and data. The paper tackles this with power-law scaling plots.

The result is encouraging for xLSTM. The models show similar scaling behavior, but xLSTM has a better offset than the other baselines. In practical terms, that means the curve is shifted in xLSTM’s favor across model sizes. The authors interpret this as evidence that larger xLSTM models should continue to remain competitive, and possibly increasingly so.

This does not prove xLSTM will dominate at very large scales, but it does show the trend is not obviously saturating against it. In a field where scaling behavior often matters as much as architecture, that is a meaningful signal.

## Speed and memory: why recurrence still matters

The paper also makes an efficiency argument. xLSTM has linear computation and constant memory with respect to sequence length, unlike Transformers, whose cost grows quadratically with context length in standard attention setups.

That shows up in generation benchmarks. The paper reports linear scaling for xLSTM and other recurrent models, compared with quadratic scaling for Llama. Because xLSTM uses constant memory, it can also support larger batch sizes and achieves high decoding throughput.

There is a tradeoff, though. The matrix-memory version is computationally expensive, and the current CUDA kernels are not fully optimized. The authors are explicit about this. So the practical speed advantage is not “xLSTM is cheaper than everything else,” but rather that it has a favorable asymptotic profile and promising throughput characteristics.

## So, can xLSTM beat Transformers on long context?

Based on the paper’s experiments, the most accurate answer is:

Yes, xLSTM can be highly competitive with Transformers on long-context language modeling, and in the reported experiments it often performs better on perplexity, extrapolation, and broad-domain evaluation.

That said, the result is more nuanced than “xLSTM beats Transformers everywhere.” The paper shows a strong set of advantages in the tested regimes, especially on long-context extrapolation and validation perplexity, but it also acknowledges that the implementation is not fully optimized and that more tuning could further change the picture.

The broader conclusion is that the LSTM family is not obsolete. With exponential gating and richer memory structures, xLSTM shows that recurrent models can still scale into the LLM era and remain serious contenders.

## Bottom line

If you want the practical takeaway, it is this:

xLSTM is not presented as a nostalgic revival of old recurrent networks. It is a modernized sequence model that, in the authors’ experiments, competes very well with Transformers and State Space Models at language modeling scale.

The strongest evidence comes from three places:

- better perplexity on large-scale training runs,
- strong long-context extrapolation,
- and favorable scaling behavior.

For readers asking whether recurrence still has a future in large language modeling, this paper’s answer is clearly yes.
