\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Why Parameter-Efficient Finetuning Matters

# Foundation models and the cost of full retraining

Modern foundation models are powerful because they can be pretrained on large-scale data and then reused across many tasks. The paper uses ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion as examples of systems that demonstrate remarkable generalization ability. At the same time, the scale that makes these models so capable also makes them extremely expensive to train. The paper points out that the performance of these models has risen dramatically, but so has the number of parameters, with GPT-3 cited as having around $175$ billion parameters. Training a model of that scale from scratch is prohibitively costly for most researchers and organizations.

This cost creates a practical bottleneck for progress. If every new downstream task required retraining a huge foundation model from the beginning, then the benefits of these pretrained systems would be difficult to realize in practice. The paper’s basic premise is that broad progress now depends less on building entirely new foundation models and more on adapting existing ones efficiently. In other words, the central issue is not only how to pretrain powerful models, but how to reuse them effectively without paying the full cost again.

# Downstream adaptation as a central challenge in modern machine learning

Because foundation models are so expensive to train from scratch, the paper frames downstream adaptation as one of the major challenges in modern machine learning. A pretrained model already contains broad knowledge, but that knowledge has to be transferred to specific tasks or application domains. The problem is therefore not merely to obtain a good pretrained model, but to adapt it in a way that preserves useful pretrained structure while still allowing the model to specialize.

The paper emphasizes that adaptation should be efficient both in training and in deployment. This is important because downstream users often want to modify a large model for a particular task without making the whole system cumbersome or expensive to use afterward. The paper’s broader motivation is that any successful adaptation method must manage this trade-off: it must be expressive enough to fit the new task, but constrained enough to remain efficient and preserve the value of pretraining.

# Three major adaptation paradigms: model finetuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning

The paper organizes efficient adaptation into three major paradigms. The first is model finetuning, where the original architecture remains unchanged and only a subset of the model parameters is updated. The second is adapter tuning, where additional trainable parameters are inserted into the model. The third is prompt tuning, where trainable prefix tokens are attached to the input.

These three families differ in how they introduce task-specific flexibility. In model finetuning, the pretrained network itself is directly modified, but typically only partially. In adapter tuning, the model’s original parameters are preserved and new modules are added to capture downstream information. In prompt tuning, the model is left structurally intact and the adaptation happens through learned input-side prompts. The paper presents these as the main routes for parameter-efficient task adaptation, and it situates its own contribution within model finetuning.

Among these approaches, the paper argues that model finetuning is especially attractive. It is simple, because it does not require adding new modules or redesigning the input format. It is also effective, and importantly, it introduces no inference latency. That distinction matters: methods that add adapters or prompt-related computation can increase the cost of running the model after training, whereas model finetuning keeps inference as straightforward as possible.

# Why model finetuning is attractive: simplicity and no additional inference latency

A major reason model finetuning remains appealing is that it changes the model in place. The architecture stays the same, so the adapted model can be used just like the pretrained one. This makes it conceptually and operationally simpler than methods that introduce extra components. The paper treats this simplicity as a practical advantage, especially when adapting large foundation models.

The paper also stresses that model finetuning does not add inference latency. This is a key benefit in real deployments. Once training is finished, the finetuned weights are simply part of the base model, so there is no need to carry extra adapter modules or additional prompt-processing steps. In a setting where speed and efficiency matter, preserving the original inference pathway is highly valuable.

At the same time, the paper makes clear that finetuning is not automatically efficient. If a large portion of the model is updated freely, the process can become expensive and may even damage the pretrained knowledge that made the model useful in the first place. This is why parameter-efficient forms of finetuning are important: they seek to retain the simplicity of finetuning while reducing the number of trainable parameters and controlling how the model changes.

# The central question of the paper: how to make orthogonal finetuning more parameter-efficient

The paper’s central question is how to make orthogonal finetuning more parameter-efficient. This question arises because orthogonal finetuning, or OFT, is appealing as a principled finetuning method, but it can still require a fairly large number of trainable parameters. OFT works by transforming neurons in a layer with the same orthogonal matrix, which helps preserve pairwise angles and therefore preserve pretrained structure. However, orthogonal matrices are high-dimensional objects, so learning them directly can still be costly.

The original OFT addresses this partially by imposing a block-diagonal structure on the orthogonal matrix. This reduces the number of parameters, but it also introduces a fixed sparsity pattern and a restricted inductive bias. The paper highlights two concerns with that approach. First, the block-diagonal structure reduces expressiveness, because the orthogonal transformation is applied independently within separate blocks. Second, the choice of sparsity pattern is somewhat arbitrary: the paper notes that it is not clear how to find a “good” block structure, and the resulting matrix cannot approximate classic linear transforms such as those associated with butterfly-style factorizations.

The paper’s response is to ask whether one can construct a dense orthogonal matrix without losing parameter efficiency. That is the key problem BOFT is designed to solve. Rather than relying on one large orthogonal matrix or on block-diagonal sparsity, the paper proposes composing a dense orthogonal matrix from multiple sparse orthogonal matrices. This leads to Orthogonal Butterfly, or BOFT, which uses butterfly factorization to achieve a more efficient orthogonal parameterization. In the paper’s framing, this is not just a technical trick but a more general way to think about orthogonal finetuning: it aims to keep the benefits of orthogonal transformations while making them much cheaper to train.

2. Background on Parameter-Efficient Finetuning Methods

# Overview of PEFT methods in NLP, vision, and generative modeling

Parameter-efficient finetuning, often abbreviated as PEFT, addresses a basic practical problem that arises once foundation models become very large: how can we adapt a pretrained model to a new task without retraining all of its parameters from scratch? The paper places this problem in the context of modern foundation models such as large language models, vision models, and text-to-image diffusion models. These models are powerful because they exhibit strong generalization, but their size makes full retraining expensive and often impractical. PEFT methods therefore aim to preserve most of the pretrained model while modifying only a small number of parameters.

The paper identifies three broad PEFT strategies. The first is model finetuning, where the architecture remains unchanged and only a subset of the original parameters is updated. The second is adapter tuning, where new trainable modules are inserted into the pretrained network. The third is prompt tuning, where additional trainable tokens are attached to the input. The paper emphasizes model finetuning as the most direct and latency-free of the three, since it does not require extra modules at inference time. This makes it especially appealing when one wants to preserve the pretrained architecture exactly while still adapting it to a downstream task.

Within this landscape, the paper argues that the most relevant PEFT methods for its purposes are reparameterization-based methods. These methods do not simply decide which existing weights to update; instead, they rewrite the way the new weights are represented. That idea is central to both LoRA and OFT, the two main methods discussed before BOFT is introduced. Understanding these methods is essential because BOFT is presented as a new parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning method that builds directly on OFT and is conceptually contrasted with LoRA.

# Reparameterization-based finetuning as a design pattern

Reparameterization-based finetuning is best understood as a way of changing the form of the weight update rather than changing the architecture itself. The pretrained weight matrix is kept as the starting point, but the finetuned weight is expressed through a structured transformation. This design pattern is attractive because it can preserve useful properties of the pretrained model while still allowing task-specific adaptation.

The paper presents reparameterization as especially relevant because structured weight updates can impose useful constraints that preserve pretrained knowledge. In other words, the model is not allowed to move arbitrarily far from the original solution. This is important because foundation models already contain broad semantic or visual knowledge, and finetuning should ideally adjust that knowledge rather than destroy it. Reparameterization methods therefore try to limit the hypothesis space in a principled way.

The paper contrasts two main reparameterization philosophies. One is additive, as in low-rank updates, where a new term is added to the pretrained weight matrix. The other is multiplicative, as in orthogonal finetuning, where the pretrained matrix is transformed by a learned orthogonal matrix. These two viewpoints lead to different notions of efficiency and regularization. Additive methods often reduce parameters by restricting the rank of the update, while multiplicative orthogonal methods preserve geometric relationships in the weights. The paper’s broader argument is that BOFT inherits the benefits of orthogonal finetuning while improving its parameter efficiency through factorization.

# LoRA: additive low-rank updates to pretrained weights

LoRA, or Low-Rank Adaptation, is presented in the paper as the canonical example of additive reparameterization-based PEFT. The core idea is simple: rather than updating the pretrained weight matrix directly, LoRA adds a low-rank matrix product to it. This gives the model a small number of trainable parameters while still allowing a meaningful change to the original weights.

The paper describes LoRA as updating a pretrained weight matrix by adding the product of two low-rank matrices. Because the rank of this update is restricted, LoRA achieves parameter efficiency by forcing the adaptation to lie in a low-dimensional subspace. This is what makes it effective: the model can still adapt to the task, but only through a compact update. The paper notes that LoRA has become very popular because of its simplicity and strong performance, especially in natural language tasks.

Another important aspect of LoRA in the paper is initialization and regularization. The low-rank weights are zero-initialized so that the model begins exactly at the pretrained solution. This mirrors the broader finetuning principle of starting close to the pretrained model. The paper also notes that LoRA naturally supports dropout on the low-rank update, which can help prevent overfitting. This becomes relevant later because BOFT uses a multiplicative update, so conventional dropout does not transfer directly and must be redesigned.

# Dynamic and adaptive low-rank methods

The paper does not treat LoRA as the only low-rank method, but as the starting point for a family of related approaches. One limitation of standard LoRA is that it uses a fixed rank across layers. In practice, different layers may need different amounts of adaptation, so a uniform rank allocation may not be ideal. To address this, several methods dynamically adjust the rank so that the parameter budget is distributed more effectively across layers.

The paper mentions adaptive and dynamic rank methods as follow-ups to LoRA that try to make low-rank adaptation more flexible. The central idea is that some layers may benefit from more expressive updates while others can remain more constrained. By changing the rank during finetuning or across layers, these methods attempt to balance performance with parameter cost more intelligently than a fixed-rank design.

These methods are important in the paper’s comparison because they illustrate a general theme in PEFT: efficiency is not just about minimizing the number of trainable parameters, but about allocating them well. BOFT is introduced in this same spirit. Rather than choosing a low-rank additive update, BOFT seeks a more parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterization. The paper positions BOFT as a different route to compactness, one based on sparse orthogonal factorization rather than low-rank addition.

# Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): multiplicative orthogonal updates

Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, is the key predecessor to BOFT in the paper. OFT modifies a pretrained linear layer by multiplying its weight matrix by a learned orthogonal matrix. Concretely, if the pretrained layer uses a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, OFT replaces it with $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal. This means that the finetuning update is multiplicative rather than additive.

The paper explains the motivation for this design through geometry. Orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles, so if the neurons in a layer are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix, the relational structure among them is preserved. This is important because the pretrained weights encode useful semantic information, and OFT aims to keep that information intact while still allowing adaptation. In the paper’s framing, orthogonal finetuning is a more principled alternative to simply using a small learning rate to keep the model close to its pretrained state.

OFT also includes a mechanism for guaranteeing orthogonality during optimization. The paper states that a Cayley parameterization is used, with $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. This ensures that the learned transformation remains orthogonal throughout finetuning. To reduce the parameter count, OFT uses a block-diagonal structure, in which the orthogonal matrix is broken into smaller orthogonal blocks. The paper notes that this improves efficiency, but it also introduces a fixed sparsity pattern and a blockwise independence assumption.

That blockwise assumption is exactly where the paper sees a limitation. Although the block-diagonal structure reduces parameters, it constrains the form of the orthogonal transformation in a way that may be too rigid. The blocks are transformed independently, and the sparsity pattern is fixed in advance. The paper argues that this can reduce expressiveness and make it difficult to approximate classic linear transforms. BOFT is introduced as an answer to this problem: it preserves the orthogonal finetuning idea but replaces the block-diagonal structure with a more expressive butterfly-based factorization.

# Contrasting low-rank structure and sparse structure as two routes to parameter efficiency

A central comparative idea in the paper is that both low-rank matrices and sparse matrices are major structured matrix families used for parameter efficiency. LoRA uses low-rank structure, while OFT and BOFT use sparse structure. These are different mechanisms for reducing the effective number of trainable parameters, but they share the same goal: make adaptation compact without retraining the full model.

The paper emphasizes that sparsity appears in two different ways. In OFT, sparsity is introduced through a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix. In BOFT, sparsity is introduced through butterfly factorization, where a dense orthogonal matrix is composed from multiple sparse orthogonal matrices. Both approaches limit the number of free parameters, but they do so with different inductive biases. The block-diagonal structure isolates groups of dimensions from one another, while the butterfly structure allows information to mix across dimensions in a more distributed way.

This comparison is important because it highlights why parameter efficiency is not only about the number of parameters, but also about the structure of those parameters. A low-rank update and a sparse orthogonal update can have similar parameter budgets yet behave very differently. The paper’s argument is that the butterfly structure offers a better compromise: it stays parameter-efficient, but it supports denser connectivity and a smoother path toward full orthogonal expressiveness than the block-diagonal design.

# Trade-offs among expressivity, parameter count, regularization, and training stability

The paper repeatedly returns to the idea that good finetuning is a balance among several competing goals. A method should use few trainable parameters, but it should also remain expressive enough to adapt to the downstream task. At the same time, it should regularize the adaptation so the model does not overfit or lose the useful properties of pretraining. Training stability is another important concern, especially when adapting large models.

In the paper’s discussion of OFT and BOFT, orthogonality plays a major role in stability. Because multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, orthogonal finetuning maintains a favorable spectral property of the original model. The paper connects this to known benefits for generalization and stable training. This helps explain why OFT can outperform low-rank methods in some settings despite its larger parameter count.

However, expressivity alone is not enough. The paper explicitly notes that full finetuning has maximal expressiveness but can still yield unsatisfactory performance. This is a key insight: finetuning benefits from constraints. A method that is too flexible may overfit or move too far from the pretrained solution. A method that is too restrictive may fail to adapt well. The paper positions BOFT as a way to enlarge the parameter space while still keeping structural priors that act as regularization.

This is where the contrast between block-diagonal sparsity and butterfly sparsity becomes conceptually important. The block-diagonal structure in OFT is very restrictive and imposes a rigid partition of the dimensions. The butterfly structure is still structured, but it is more connected and can recover many classic linear transforms. The paper therefore treats BOFT as a middle ground: it is more expressive than the block-diagonal OFT, but still sufficiently structured to remain parameter-efficient and to impose a beneficial inductive bias.

Together, these trade-offs form the background needed to understand why BOFT is introduced. The paper’s central claim is not simply that fewer parameters are better, but that the right kind of structure can make a finetuning method both efficient and effective.

Orthogonal Finetuning: Principles and Limitations

# Preserving pretrained knowledge through constrained finetuning

Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, is motivated by a central problem in adapting foundation models: we want to specialize a pretrained model for a downstream task without destroying the knowledge already stored in its weights. The paper frames this as a need for finetuning methods that keep the pretrained model and the adapted model “similar” in a principled way. In standard finetuning, this is often approximated by using a small learning rate, which encourages the weights to move only a little. OFT takes a more structured approach. Rather than merely hoping that the weights stay close in a Euclidean sense, it preserves a particular geometric property of the weight matrices: the pairwise angles between neurons.

This idea is important because the paper views pretrained representations as carrying semantic knowledge through their relational structure. If the adaptation step changes the geometry too aggressively, then that pretrained structure may be lost. OFT therefore constrains the update so that the transformation applied to a layer is orthogonal. In the paper’s framing, this preserves the relational information encoded in the weight matrix while still allowing the model to adapt to a new task. The result is a finetuning method that aims to be stable and generalizable, rather than simply flexible.

# Neuron geometry and the preservation of pairwise angles

The geometric intuition behind OFT is that neurons in a layer are not just individual parameters; their relative arrangement matters. The paper emphasizes pairwise angles between neurons as the key quantity to preserve. If two neurons have a particular angle in the pretrained model, then an orthogonal transformation keeps that angle unchanged. This matters because orthogonal transformations preserve inner products and therefore preserve the geometry of the space in which the neurons live.

The paper connects this to prior work on hyperspherical energy and orthogonal representations, arguing that preserving angles helps preserve the “semantic knowledge” learned during pretraining. In other words, rather than altering the representation space arbitrarily, OFT changes it in a way that maintains the underlying geometric relationships. This gives a more principled interpretation of finetuning than a simple small-step update: the model is not just moving slightly, it is moving in a structure-preserving way.

# OFT as multiplying pretrained weights by a learnable orthogonal matrix

In the paper’s formulation, OFT adapts a pretrained linear layer by introducing a learnable orthogonal matrix. If the pretrained weight matrix is $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and uses it to transform the weights. This makes the adapted model different from the pretrained one, but only through an orthogonal rotation-like transformation.

This is the fundamental design choice that distinguishes OFT from additive methods such as LoRA. LoRA updates the pretrained weights by adding a low-rank correction. OFT instead updates the weights multiplicatively. The paper presents this as a way to maintain closer structural continuity with the pretrained model, because the transformation is constrained to lie in the orthogonal group. The orthogonality requirement is what makes the method geometrically meaningful and what gives it its stability properties.

# Forward-pass formulation: replacing $W^0$ with $R W^0$

The paper writes the forward pass of a pretrained linear layer as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input and $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the output. Under OFT, this becomes

$$
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
$$

This is a simple but powerful replacement: the pretrained weight matrix is left-multiplied by a learnable orthogonal transformation. The paper’s viewpoint is that the orthogonal matrix acts on the neurons in the same layer, transforming them while preserving geometric relations. Because the architecture of the model is unchanged, this is still model finetuning rather than adapter tuning or prompt tuning.

This formulation is also attractive because it is conceptually clean. The pretrained weights remain the foundation, and adaptation is encoded by a transformation applied to them. The paper treats this as a general framework for orthogonal finetuning, with OFT as the original version and BOFT later introduced as a more parameter-efficient version.

# Identity initialization and its role in safe adaptation

A practical concern in finetuning is how to start the optimization. The paper emphasizes that finetuning methods usually begin with the exact pretrained model so that the adapted model does not deviate too much at the beginning. In OFT, this is done by initializing $\bm{R}$ as the identity matrix.

This identity initialization is important because it means the model initially behaves exactly like the pretrained model. The finetuning process then learns deviations from that starting point, rather than beginning from a random transformation. The paper presents this as analogous to the zero initialization commonly used in LoRA. The intuition is that if adaptation begins from the pretrained solution itself, then optimization is safer and less likely to disrupt useful pretrained behavior early in training.

# Cayley parameterization for enforcing orthogonality

Since $\bm{R}$ must remain orthogonal throughout training, OFT needs a parameterization that guarantees orthogonality rather than merely encouraging it. The paper uses Cayley parameterization for this purpose. Specifically, it writes

$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
$$

where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, meaning $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. This construction ensures that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal as long as the parameterization is valid.

The reason this matters is that optimization should not drift outside the orthogonal group. If $\bm{R}$ were only approximately orthogonal, the geometric guarantees motivating OFT would be weakened. Cayley parameterization gives a concrete way to represent an orthogonal matrix through unconstrained optimization over a skew-symmetric matrix. In the paper, this is part of the practical machinery that makes OFT workable as a finetuning method.

# Block-diagonal OFT for reducing parameter count

A full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix has a large number of degrees of freedom, so OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure to reduce the parameter count. The paper writes the orthogonal matrix as

$$
\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r),
$$

where each block $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is orthogonal and $br = d$. By using smaller blocks, OFT reduces the number of trainable parameters substantially.

This is the main practical compromise in OFT. Instead of learning one large dense orthogonal matrix, the method learns several smaller orthogonal matrices applied to disjoint groups of dimensions. The paper notes that this improves parameter efficiency, but it comes at the cost of a rigid structural assumption. The finer the block partition, the fewer parameters need to be learned, but the more constrained the transformation becomes.

# Why block-diagonal structure is restrictive

The paper is explicit that the block-diagonal structure is not just a technical simplification; it imposes a particular inductive assumption. It assumes that the dimensions of a neuron can be divided into groups based on their indices, and that dimensions in different groups should be transformed separately. The authors argue that this division is not naturally justified. There is no obvious reason why dimensions should be separated according to their index order, yet the block-diagonal structure forces exactly that.

This restriction also affects expressiveness. Because different blocks do not interact, the transformation cannot mix information across the whole layer in the way a dense orthogonal matrix can. The paper uses this as a key criticism of the original OFT design: while it is parameter-efficient, it reduces the flexibility of the transformation and may therefore limit what the adapted model can represent.

# Fixed sparsity patterns and their undesirable inductive biases

Another limitation of block-diagonal OFT is that its sparsity pattern is fixed. The zeros occur in predetermined locations, and the transformation acts independently within each block. The paper argues that this fixed sparsity can introduce undesirable inductive biases because it hard-codes a particular pattern of interaction between dimensions.

This matters because the effective model class is no longer the full orthogonal group but a smaller family of block-structured orthogonal matrices. The paper suggests that such a rigid pattern may reduce expressiveness and, more importantly, makes it unclear how to choose a good sparsity pattern in the first place. The block-diagonal structure saves parameters, but it also imposes a specific organization on the model that may not align with the geometry of the task.

The paper further points out that the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot approximate classic linear transforms in the way more flexible structured matrices can. This comparison is used to motivate the search for a denser but still parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterization.

# Why dense orthogonal transformations are desirable but expensive

The paper argues that dense orthogonal matrices are desirable because they avoid the arbitrary partitioning induced by block-diagonal structure. A dense orthogonal transformation can mix information across all dimensions in a layer, which makes it much more expressive. This is especially appealing if we want a principled finetuning method that preserves geometric structure without imposing unnatural separations between dimensions.

The problem is cost. A $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is too expensive for large models. This is the central tension the paper addresses: how can we obtain the benefits of dense orthogonal transformations while keeping the parameter count low?

This limitation is what motivates the move beyond original OFT. The paper’s answer is to search for a factorized representation that still yields a dense orthogonal matrix but with far fewer trainable parameters. That broader idea leads to the butterfly-based parameterization introduced later in the paper. Here, however, the key point is that original OFT sits between two extremes: it is more principled than unconstrained finetuning, but its block-diagonal approximation is still too restrictive, while a full dense orthogonal matrix is too expensive.

An Information Transmission View of Matrix Factorization

# From dense matrices to connectivity patterns

A central idea in the paper is to stop thinking of an orthogonal matrix only as a numerical object and instead view it as a pattern of connectivity. In ordinary linear algebra, a dense matrix is one whose entries are mostly nonzero, so every output coordinate can in principle depend on every input coordinate. The paper turns this into a graph-theoretic intuition: a dense square matrix of size $d \times d$ can be interpreted as a dense connectivity pattern between $d$ source nodes and $d$ receiver nodes.

This shift in viewpoint is especially useful for orthogonal finetuning, because the paper is not trying to learn an arbitrary dense matrix for its own sake. It is trying to construct a dense orthogonal matrix with as few trainable parameters as possible. If we only look at the matrix entries, it is hard to reason about how a sparse factorization might still generate a dense result. By viewing each nonzero as a possible connection, the question becomes more intuitive: how can we wire together a sequence of sparse transformations so that information can still travel from every input dimension to every output dimension?

# Interpreting nonzero matrix entries as channels of information flow

The paper’s information transmission view assigns a precise meaning to zero and nonzero entries. If $R_{ij}=0$, then information from the $j$-th node cannot flow to the $i$-th node through the matrix $\bm{R}$. If $R_{ij}\neq 0$, then such information can be transmitted. This is not meant as a literal communication system, but as a mental model for understanding how linear transformations propagate signal across dimensions.

This interpretation becomes clearer when $\bm{R}$ is used in the forward pass of a layer. In OFT and BOFT, the pretrained weight matrix is transformed by left-multiplying an orthogonal matrix, so the resulting layer can be seen as taking the original neuron representations and redistributing them. Under the transmission view, every nonzero entry in the transformation matrix represents a possible pathway by which one component of the original representation can influence another. The point of the factorization is therefore not only to reduce parameter count, but also to organize these pathways in a way that still permits full communication across the layer.

# Factorizing a matrix as sequential information exchange across levels

The paper considers a matrix factorization of the form $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots \bm{B}_1$. Instead of thinking of this as a single matrix being written as a product, the paper interprets it as a sequence of information exchanges. Each factor $\bm{B}_i$ is one stage of transmission, and information passes through these stages one after another. In that sense, factorization is not just an algebraic decomposition; it is a staged communication process.

This sequential interpretation matters because each factor can be sparse even if the product is dense. A sparse factor does not need to connect every input to every output directly. It only needs to exchange information locally with the next stage. Over multiple stages, these local exchanges can accumulate into global connectivity. The paper uses this idea to motivate why composing sparse matrices can still produce a dense orthogonal transformation, provided the pattern of sparse connections is chosen carefully.

# Grid-structured graphs induced by matrix products

To formalize the transmission view, the paper unrolls the product $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\cdots \bm{B}_1$ into a grid-structured graph with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes. Each column corresponds to one level of the factorization, starting from the input level and ending at the output level. Each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ is then viewed as the connectivity between level $i$ and level $i+1$.

In this graph, a directed edge is allowed only between adjacent levels. The $(j_1,j_2)$ entry of $\bm{B}_i$ indicates whether there is a directed edge from node $j_2$ in level $i$ to node $j_1$ in level $i+1$. A zero entry means no edge; a nonzero entry means a connection exists. This makes the product structure concrete: multiplying matrices corresponds to propagating information through a layered network, where each layer implements one round of message passing.

The grid picture is useful because it exposes the structural constraints of factorization. A matrix product is dense only if the graph contains enough routes from the first level to the last level. If some nodes cannot reach others through the intermediate layers, then the final matrix will necessarily contain zeros in the corresponding positions. So the graph is not merely a visualization; it is a way to reason about which factorizations can possibly produce dense matrices.

# How matrix multiplication unrolls into a transmission network

The paper illustrates this with an example such as $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_5\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$. In the unrolled graph, each factor $\bm{B}_i$ becomes the connectivity matrix between the $i$-th and $(i+1)$-th levels. Following the product from right to left corresponds to information moving from the first level toward the last. The rightmost factor acts first on the input, then the next factor, and so on until the final output is reached.

This is important because the paper does not treat sparsity as a property of the final matrix alone. Instead, it tracks how sparsity in each factor affects whether a path exists through the network. For example, if one considers a shorter product such as $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$, the paper notes that some source nodes may fail to reach certain receiver nodes. In the example discussed, information from node $1$ cannot be transmitted to node $3$, which corresponds to a zero entry in the final matrix. Thus the final sparsity pattern can be understood by tracing paths through the layered graph.

This unrolling also highlights the repeated role of adjacency. Since each factor only connects neighboring levels, the question becomes whether a chain of local connections can assemble into global communication. That is the basic combinatorial challenge the paper aims to solve.

# Dense connectivity as the requirement that every source can reach every target

The paper’s main criterion for a factorization to generate a dense matrix is dense connectivity: every node in the first level must be able to transmit information to every node in the last level. In other words, for the final matrix $\bm{R}$ to be dense, there must be at least one path from every source node to every target node in the layered graph.

This is the graph-theoretic restatement of density. Instead of asking whether every entry of $\bm{R}$ is nonzero, one asks whether the network supports a path from every input coordinate to every output coordinate. The condition is intuitive: if some source cannot reach some target, then the corresponding matrix entry must be zero. Conversely, if the graph is richly connected enough, then the factorization can in principle produce a dense result.

The paper emphasizes that this requirement is not enough by itself. One also wants the factorization to use as few effective trainable edges as possible. This leads to a second desideratum: minimum free edges. The challenge is therefore to balance two goals at once. The graph must be connected enough to allow dense communication, but sparse enough to keep the parameter count low.

# Understanding sparsity patterns through paths in the graph

A major advantage of the information transmission view is that sparsity patterns can be understood through the existence or absence of paths. If a particular pair of source and target nodes has no path through the graph, then the final matrix entry must be zero. The paper uses this to explain why some sparse structures, such as the block-diagonal structure used in original OFT, cannot naturally produce a dense matrix.

The layered graph makes this limitation visible. Block-diagonal sparsity effectively isolates groups of dimensions from one another, so information is transformed separately within blocks. That means the graph breaks into disconnected or weakly connected regions, which can limit the set of reachable input-output pairs. By contrast, a factorization pattern with better global mixing can still remain sparse at each stage while allowing information to spread across all dimensions after multiple layers.

The paper also notes that orthogonality adds further constraints to these paths. The factors are not arbitrary sparse matrices; they must be orthogonal. This introduces dependencies among edges, so the allowed patterns are more restricted than in an unconstrained sparse network. The information transmission framework is useful precisely because it abstracts away from the exact values of the matrix entries and focuses instead on the nonzero structure, which is what matters for reasoning about connectivity under the orthogonality constraint.

# Why this perspective is useful for orthogonal finetuning

The reason this framework matters is that orthogonal finetuning is about preserving useful pretrained structure while adapting the model efficiently. In the paper, orthogonal matrices are attractive because they preserve pairwise angles and maintain the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. But a full dense orthogonal matrix has too many parameters to be practical. The paper’s conceptual move is to ask how to keep the benefits of orthogonality while reducing the parameter burden.

The information transmission view provides a principled way to search for such parameterizations. Once dense orthogonal matrix construction is seen as a communication problem on a graph, the task becomes one of designing a sparse network that still supports full source-to-target reachability. This opens the door to structured factorizations, especially the butterfly structure, which the paper adopts because it can achieve dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges.

This perspective is valuable not only for deriving BOFT, but also for understanding why BOFT is meant to improve over block-diagonal OFT. Block-diagonal sparsity is a simple way to save parameters, but it hard-codes a rigid partition of dimensions. The graph view shows why this may be undesirable: it imposes a particular connectivity pattern that may be too restrictive and may not approximate the kinds of dense linear transformations that arise naturally in practice. BOFT instead uses a factorization pattern that still controls parameter count but allows information to circulate more globally through the matrix product.

In short, the information transmission view turns the problem of learning a dense orthogonal matrix into a question about graph connectivity. That reframing is the conceptual foundation for the paper’s butterfly-based orthogonal finetuning method.

5. Design Goals for Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Factorizations

# Dense connectivity across the factorization graph

The paper’s information transmission view turns the problem of constructing a dense orthogonal matrix into a graph problem. Instead of thinking only in terms of matrix entries, we interpret a factorization $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots \bm{B}_1$ as a sequence of information exchanges across $m+1$ layers of nodes. In this picture, each factor $\bm{B}_i$ connects the nodes at level $i$ to the nodes at level $i+1$, and the product is dense only if information from every source node in the first level can eventually reach every receiver node in the last level.

This leads directly to the first desideratum: the factorization graph must have dense connectivity. Dense connectivity means that, for every node in the first level, there is at least one path to every node in the final level. If this condition fails, then some entries of the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ must remain zero, and the matrix cannot be dense. In other words, even if each factor is individually sparse, the factorization as a whole must still allow all-to-all communication after composition.

The intuition is that sparsity is only useful if it is arranged so that information can still propagate broadly through the network of factors. A sparse pattern that blocks communication between some input and output coordinates defeats the purpose of constructing a dense orthogonal matrix. The paper emphasizes that dense connectivity is not just a desirable property in an abstract sense; it is the structural condition that makes sparse factorization compatible with expressiveness.

# Minimum free edges for parameter efficiency

The second desideratum is to use as few free edges as possible while still maintaining dense connectivity and orthogonality. Here “free edges” correspond to trainable nonzero entries in the factorization graph. The goal is not merely to achieve density, but to do so with minimal parameter count. This is the central efficiency problem: we want a dense orthogonal matrix without paying the full $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameter cost of an unconstrained dense matrix.

The paper frames this as a trade-off between connectivity and economy. A factorization with too many edges may be expressive, but it sacrifices parameter efficiency. A factorization with too few edges may be efficient, but it may fail to connect the graph densely. The challenge is therefore to find a sparse pattern that is still sufficiently expressive to generate dense orthogonal matrices.

This perspective also clarifies why matrix factorization is attractive in the first place. By composing several sparse matrices, we can reduce the number of trainable parameters in exchange for additional computation during training. That is, instead of storing a large dense orthogonal matrix directly, we store a structured product of simpler factors. The paper’s goal is to make this trade-off as favorable as possible.

# Why orthogonality changes how we count effective parameters

A subtle point in the paper is that orthogonality changes what counts as a trainable parameter. In an ordinary dense matrix, one might count every nonzero entry as a degree of freedom. But orthogonal matrices are constrained by the condition $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I}$. This means the entries are not independent: the choice of some nonzero entries restricts the others.

Because of this, the paper distinguishes between edges that are structurally necessary and edges that are actually trainable. Some nonzero entries are required simply to satisfy orthogonality, but they do not represent additional freedom in the model. The paper notes, for example, that for a $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix with exactly $d$ nonzero entries, those entries can only be $\pm 1$. Such entries are necessary for the matrix to be full-rank and orthogonal, but they do not provide meaningful trainable flexibility.

This is why the paper does not equate “number of nonzero entries” with “number of effective parameters” in a naive way. Orthogonality reduces the number of independent choices available, and therefore the efficiency analysis must account for these constraints. The information transmission framework helps by focusing on the pattern of nonzeros and how they enable communication, rather than on the exact numerical values themselves.

# Full-rank requirements and bijective connections between adjacent levels

A key structural requirement in the paper is that each factor matrix must be full-rank. Since orthogonality implies full-rank, every factor in the product must satisfy this condition. In the graph interpretation, this means that the connections between adjacent levels cannot be arbitrarily sparse; they must be sufficient to support a bijection between the nodes on one level and the nodes on the next.

The paper explains that for a $d\times d$ orthogonal factor, we need $d$ edges between adjacent levels to create a bijection between the $d$ nodes on one level and the $d$ nodes on the next. These $d$ edges are not considered trainable freedom in the same way as additional edges, because they are already required by the orthogonality constraint. They are part of the unavoidable structure needed for a valid orthogonal transformation.

This requirement is important because it shows that orthogonality is not simply a regularizer layered on top of sparsity. It actively shapes the graph. Even before we ask how to make the matrix dense, we must ensure each factor is structurally capable of being orthogonal. The adjacent-level connections therefore serve as the basic backbone of the factorization graph.

# How orthogonality induces dependencies among nonzero entries

Orthogonality introduces dependencies among entries, and the paper highlights that this can affect the sparsity pattern itself. A zero in one position may force another zero elsewhere because the rows and columns must remain mutually orthonormal. The paper gives the simple example of a $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix: if one entry is zero, another entry may be forced to be zero as well. This shows that one cannot treat each nonzero location independently when orthogonality is imposed.

This dependency matters because it complicates the design of sparse patterns. In a general sparse matrix, one might freely choose a pattern of nonzeros and then fit their values. In an orthogonal matrix, however, the pattern and the values interact more tightly. Some patterns are simply incompatible with orthogonality, while others severely limit how much freedom remains after the constraints are enforced.

The paper’s framework is particularly useful here because it abstracts away from the exact values and concentrates on whether the nonzero pattern can support information flow. Since orthogonality can add or remove effective edges through its constraints, the graph view helps organize the design problem in a way that value-level reasoning alone cannot.

# Why some edges are structurally necessary but not trainable

The paper repeatedly distinguishes between edges that are necessary for the matrix to exist and edges that contribute trainable parameters. This distinction is crucial for understanding parameter efficiency in orthogonal factorization.

Structurally necessary edges are those required for full-rank orthogonality. For example, the $d$ connections between adjacent levels are needed so that each factor can act as a valid orthogonal transformation. But these edges do not automatically represent extra freedom. They are part of the minimum structure that any orthogonal factorization must carry. The paper emphasizes that these should not be counted as trainable edges in the efficiency analysis.

Trainable edges, by contrast, are the additional degrees of freedom beyond this minimum structure. The goal is to minimize these while preserving dense connectivity. This is why the paper speaks of “minimum free edges”: the useful measure of efficiency is not the total number of occupied positions, but the number of positions that actually introduce extra parameter freedom beyond the orthogonality baseline.

This distinction is one of the subtle but important conceptual contributions of the paper. It explains why a factorization can be sparse without being parameter-efficient in the relevant sense, and why a careful accounting of orthogonality is needed when designing structured finetuning methods.

# Comparing naive dense parameterization with sparse factorized alternatives

A naive dense parameterization of a $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix would require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. The paper uses this as the baseline against which sparse factorized designs are compared. In the information transmission view, such a matrix corresponds to a fully connected bipartite graph between the input and output levels, which is expressive but expensive.

The paper’s main idea is to replace this single dense matrix with a product of multiple sparse matrices. This trades space for computation: the factorization must be multiplied repeatedly during training, but it can drastically reduce the number of trainable parameters. The information transmission framework makes this trade-off visible by turning the matrix into a graph whose edges can be counted and organized.

The paper also gives an example where a factorization uses fewer edges than a single dense orthogonal matrix. This illustrates that factorization can be beneficial even before introducing the butterfly structure. But the key question is not merely whether a factorization uses fewer edges than the dense case; it is whether it can do so while still maintaining the dense connectivity needed for expressiveness and the orthogonality needed for stable finetuning.

# Why the original OFT block-diagonal graph fails to achieve dense connectivity

The original Orthogonal Finetuning method, OFT, uses a block-diagonal orthogonal structure to reduce the number of trainable parameters. While this makes OFT more efficient than a fully dense orthogonal parameterization, the paper argues that this structure has a fundamental limitation: it cannot construct a dense matrix in the same way a more globally connected factorization can.

In the graph interpretation, a block-diagonal matrix breaks the nodes into separate groups that are transformed independently. This means that information cannot freely move between blocks. As a result, the factorization lacks dense connectivity. Some source nodes can only reach a restricted subset of receiver nodes, which means the resulting matrix cannot approximate the kind of dense mixing desired for a general orthogonal transformation.

The paper points out that this block structure introduces a fixed sparsity pattern, and that the pattern itself creates an inductive bias. While this may reduce parameters, it also reduces expressiveness and makes the choice of sparsity pattern a difficult design problem. In particular, the block-diagonal structure cannot recover classic linear transforms in the way the butterfly structure can. This is one of the main reasons the paper seeks a denser yet still parameter-efficient alternative.

In summary, the original OFT graph succeeds in sparsity but fails in global connectivity. The paper’s information transmission perspective makes this failure easy to see: independent blocks impede communication across the full set of nodes, so the factorization cannot realize the dense mixing that motivates orthogonal finetuning in the first place.

Butterfly Structures and the Fast Fourier Transform Connection

# The Cooley-Tukey FFT and Recursive Information Mixing

The paper introduces butterfly structures through the Cooley-Tukey algorithm for the fast Fourier transform, or FFT. The key idea is that the FFT does not compute an $N$-point discrete Fourier transform in one dense step. Instead, it recursively breaks the problem into two smaller $\frac{N}{2}$-point transforms. This recursive splitting is important because it reveals a pattern of information mixing: local components are combined, then recombined across levels, until information from one part of the input has been propagated throughout the whole computation.

This recursive view is exactly what makes the FFT visually and conceptually resemble a butterfly. At each stage, values are paired and mixed, and then those mixed values are paired again in a later stage. The paper emphasizes that this process is not just a computational trick for Fourier transforms. It also provides a way to think about how information can be transmitted efficiently through a structured sequence of sparse transformations. In the context of orthogonal finetuning, this matters because the goal is to build a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of simpler matrices while keeping the number of trainable parameters small.

The paper uses this analogy to motivate an information transmission perspective. In the FFT, a small local change in one domain can influence a wide range of outputs in another domain. Likewise, in the matrix factorization problem studied in the paper, the authors want a structure in which information from any source dimension can eventually reach every target dimension. The butterfly pattern from the Cooley-Tukey algorithm is appealing because it naturally supports this kind of recursive, global mixing.

# Butterfly Graphs as Efficient Communication Topologies

The paper treats the butterfly structure not only as an algorithmic pattern but also as a graph topology. In this interpretation, nodes represent dimensions or intermediate states, and edges represent allowed information flow. A butterfly graph is a way to wire these nodes so that information can move efficiently from the input level to the output level through a sequence of sparse connections.

This viewpoint is especially helpful because the paper reformulates dense matrix generation as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph. If a matrix is dense, then every source dimension should be able to influence every output dimension. In graph terms, this means every node in the first level should have a path to every node in the last level. The butterfly topology is attractive because it achieves this kind of connectivity using a carefully designed pattern of sparse inter-level connections, rather than by connecting every node to every other node directly.

The paper also notes that butterfly networks have a history as communication topologies in computer networking. Their relevance is not accidental: they are designed for efficient information exchange. That same property makes them a good fit for the factorization problem in BOFT, where the authors want a sparse pattern that still supports dense connectivity after matrix multiplication. The butterfly graph thus becomes a natural candidate because it offers an organized way to route information across levels while preserving parameter efficiency.

# How Butterfly Networks Achieve Dense Information Exchange with Few Edges

A central point in the paper is that a dense orthogonal matrix does not need to be represented directly as one full dense matrix. Instead, it can be realized as a product of several sparse matrices. When these sparse matrices are arranged according to a butterfly pattern, information can be propagated from all input nodes to all output nodes through a sequence of local exchanges.

The paper’s graphical framework makes this intuition precise. Each factor matrix in the product corresponds to edges between adjacent levels in a layered graph. Dense connectivity means that every node in the first layer must be able to reach every node in the final layer through a path that follows the directed edges induced by the factors. The butterfly structure is powerful because it creates these paths using a recursively organized set of sparse connections. In other words, it does not rely on a single all-to-all connection pattern. It gradually spreads information across the graph.

This is why butterfly networks are such a good match for the paper’s orthogonal factorization problem. The authors are not merely looking for sparsity; they are looking for sparsity that still supports dense communication after composition. The butterfly layout is able to do this efficiently because it repeatedly mixes pairs or blocks of coordinates in a structured hierarchy. That recursive mixing makes it possible for information that starts in one coordinate to eventually influence many others, which is exactly what is needed to generate a dense orthogonal transformation.

# Complexity Intuition: $O(d \log d)$ Versus $O(d^2)$

The paper contrasts two broad ways of parameterizing a $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix. A fully dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is expensive when $d$ is large. By contrast, the butterfly factorization uses a product of only $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries. This gives an overall parameter count of $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$, which is a major reduction.

The intuition behind this saving is that the butterfly construction trades space for repeated computation. Rather than storing one large dense transformation, it stores several sparse transformations and multiplies them in sequence. That multiplication has to be performed at training time, so the cost is not free, but the gain is that the number of trainable parameters is dramatically smaller. The paper frames this as a principled way to reduce parameters while preserving the ability to form dense transformations.

The paper also uses the information transmission viewpoint to explain why this is possible. A dense matrix corresponds to a dense communication pattern, but dense communication does not necessarily require dense storage. If the communication can be routed through a structured sequence of sparse layers, then the same overall effect can be obtained more efficiently. The butterfly construction is valuable because it gives a concrete topology that realizes this idea and does so with the favorable $O(d \log d)$ scaling.

# Prior Uses of Butterfly Matrices in Numerical Linear Algebra and Machine Learning

Butterfly matrices are not introduced in the paper as a brand-new mathematical object. Rather, the paper situates them within a broader set of prior applications. They have been used to parameterize orthogonal matrices, to avoid pivoting in Gaussian elimination, and to improve efficiency in numerical settings. They have also appeared in recurrent neural networks to help stabilize training, and in kernel approximation.

In machine learning, the paper points out that butterfly parameterizations have been used to learn fast linear transforms and to enable efficient training of neural networks. Other work has also employed butterfly structures for fast matrix-vector multiplication, data-sparse matrix approximation, and network transmission problems. This prior literature matters because it shows that butterfly patterns are not merely a theoretical curiosity; they are a versatile structured representation that repeatedly appears in contexts where efficient linear transformations are needed.

The paper’s contribution is different from these earlier uses. It does not use butterfly matrices simply as a general-purpose efficient matrix family. Instead, it adapts the butterfly idea specifically to orthogonal finetuning. That means the butterfly structure is being used to design a parameter-efficient way to learn orthogonal transformations that are applied to pretrained layers. This is a more specialized use than earlier butterfly-based work, and it is motivated by the need to preserve pretrained knowledge while adapting to downstream tasks.

# Why Butterfly Factorization Is Promising for Orthogonal Finetuning

The reason butterfly factorization is promising for orthogonal finetuning comes from the combination of three properties emphasized in the paper: dense connectivity, parameter efficiency, and orthogonality. Orthogonal finetuning aims to transform pretrained weights in a way that preserves relational structure, especially pairwise angles between neurons. To do that, the learned transformation must stay orthogonal. But if one tries to learn a fully dense orthogonal matrix directly, the number of parameters becomes large. Butterfly factorization offers a way around this by expressing a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of sparse orthogonal factors.

This is especially attractive in the paper’s framework because the sparse factors are not arbitrary. Their sparsity pattern follows the butterfly topology, which provides a strong inductive bias. The paper argues that this is beneficial because the butterfly pattern is aligned with many classic fast linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier transform, the discrete cosine transform, and the Hadamard transform. In that sense, butterfly factorization sits closer to familiar, well-structured transformations than the block-diagonal pattern used in the original OFT. That makes it a more natural candidate for a finetuning method that should be expressive but not overly unconstrained.

The paper also emphasizes that butterfly factorization improves the balance between expressivity and regularity. A dense orthogonal matrix is highly expressive, but full freedom is not always desirable in finetuning. The butterfly structure gives a smaller hypothesis class inside the orthogonal group, which can help generalization and reduce overfitting. At the same time, it remains flexible enough to recover many useful transforms and to form dense orthogonal matrices when needed. This is why the butterfly viewpoint is central to BOFT: it provides a principled, structured, and efficient way to extend orthogonal finetuning beyond the limitations of block-diagonal designs.

7. Orthogonal Parameterization via Butterfly Factorization

# Definition of the butterfly factor

The technical starting point of BOFT is the idea of a butterfly factor, which is the simplest building block used to assemble a structured orthogonal matrix. In the paper, a butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is defined for a size $k$ that is at least $2$ and a power of $2$. It has a $2 \times 2$ block form, where each block is diagonal:

\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k},
\]

with each $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. This construction is important because it encodes a sparse pattern while still allowing structured mixing between entries. Instead of learning a fully dense $k \times k$ matrix, the method learns only the diagonal entries of these blocks, which drastically reduces the number of free parameters.

The paper’s motivation for introducing this factor is that a dense orthogonal matrix is too expensive to parameterize directly, but a butterfly factor gives a way to build dense behavior from sparse pieces. Each butterfly factor acts like a localized exchange mechanism: information from one part of a vector can be routed to another part through a limited set of trainable connections. That idea becomes the foundation for the full butterfly matrix used in BOFT.

# Recursive construction of butterfly matrices

A single butterfly factor is only the local ingredient. To obtain a full-sized matrix of dimension $d$, the paper defines a butterfly matrix recursively, with $d=2^N$. The butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is built from smaller butterfly matrices and a sequence of structured sparse factors:

\[
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(d/2) & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(d/2)
\end{bmatrix}
=
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d/2)\cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2).
\]

The recursive view is central because it shows how a dense matrix can emerge from repeated combinations of sparse components. At each stage, smaller subproblems are combined into a larger matrix, much like the divide-and-conquer structure of the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform. The paper emphasizes that this recursive organization is not just a computational trick; it is a parameterization strategy that makes dense orthogonal matrices possible without paying the full $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ cost of a dense parameterization.

This recursion also makes the butterfly structure highly regular. Each level of the recursion introduces a new sparse pattern, and the composition of these patterns creates a matrix with dense effective connectivity. In other words, although each factor is sparse, the product has the ability to transmit information across all coordinates.

# Butterfly components as block-diagonal assemblies of butterfly factors

To connect the butterfly factor to the full BOFT parameterization, the paper introduces the notion of a butterfly component. A butterfly component is a block-diagonal matrix formed by repeating butterfly factors along the diagonal:

\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\text{diag}(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\cdots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)),
\]

where each $\bm{B}^F_i(k)$ is a butterfly factor of size $k$. Thus, the full matrix is partitioned into equally sized blocks, and each block is itself a butterfly factor. This makes the component sparse, structured, and modular.

The paper uses this block-diagonal assembly as the bridge between the abstract butterfly factor and the concrete orthogonal parameterization. At the smallest scale, each butterfly component can be viewed as a collection of small local transformations. At larger scales, the different components are stacked together in a recursive fashion, allowing the model to create a much richer transformation than a single block-diagonal matrix could provide.

This distinction matters because BOFT is not merely a single sparse orthogonal matrix. It is a product of such components, each one carrying a carefully designed sparsity structure. The architecture is built so that the sparsity pattern changes across levels, enabling dense communication across the full dimension while keeping the number of trainable parameters small.

# From butterfly matrices to orthogonal butterfly matrices

The paper’s main goal in this section is to turn the butterfly structure into an orthogonal parameterization. The key requirement is that every factor in the product must itself be orthogonal. If this can be done, then their product will also be orthogonal, which preserves the geometric properties that motivated OFT in the first place.

The authors first examine the simplest butterfly component, $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$, whose blocks are $2 \times 2$. This case is convenient because orthogonality can be enforced directly at the block level. Once the smallest component is orthogonal, the remaining butterfly components can be treated as permutations of its non-zero pattern. This means the same orthogonal construction can be reused across the different levels of the butterfly matrix.

The outcome is an efficient orthogonal parameterization built from many small orthogonal pieces. Rather than learning one large orthogonal matrix, BOFT learns a sequence of structured orthogonal components whose product forms the final transformation. This is the core idea that reduces parameter count while preserving orthogonality.

# Ensuring orthogonality of each component

Orthogonality is not automatic in a sparse factorization. The paper is careful to point out that each butterfly component must be explicitly parameterized so that it remains orthogonal during finetuning. This is essential because the central promise of OFT and BOFT is the preservation of the relational structure of neurons, especially their pairwise angles.

For the base case $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$, orthogonality is enforced by parameterizing each $2 \times 2$ block using either a Cayley transform or an equivalent $2$-dimensional rotation. The paper follows the same orthogonality-preserving philosophy used in OFT: the orthogonal matrix is learned through a parameterization that guarantees orthogonality by construction, rather than by adding a penalty or projecting after the fact.

This design is important for training stability. Since the learned matrix stays on the orthogonal manifold, the method preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weights when left-multiplying by the learned orthogonal factor. The paper treats this as a key reason why orthogonal finetuning can be stable and can generalize well.

# Using Cayley transforms or 2D rotations inside small blocks

The paper specifically notes that the orthogonality of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ can be guaranteed using the Cayley transform, just as in the original OFT formulation. In the paper’s notation, the orthogonal matrix is obtained from a skew-symmetric parameter matrix, with the skew-symmetric part initialized at zero so that the resulting transformation begins as the identity.

The appeal of this approach is that it gives a smooth and principled way to parameterize small orthogonal blocks. In the $2 \times 2$ case, the same role can also be played by a standard rotation. Either way, the point is that each small block can be represented with very few effective parameters while staying exactly orthogonal.

This local orthogonal parameterization is the atomic unit from which BOFT is built. Because the larger butterfly components are organized as permutations of these small blocks, once the orthogonality of the base blocks is ensured, the orthogonality of the entire construction can be extended across the full matrix.

# Permutation structure across butterfly components

A crucial observation in the paper is that the non-zero pattern of every butterfly component can be viewed as a permutation of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$. This is what makes the butterfly construction powerful. Rather than inventing a new orthogonal matrix for each level, BOFT reuses the same local orthogonal pattern and rearranges it systematically across layers.

This permutation structure is what allows the butterfly matrix to spread information globally. In the induced graph interpretation used earlier in the paper, each successive component connects different nodes in a way that ensures all input coordinates can eventually influence all output coordinates. The sparse factors are therefore not isolated blocks; they are arranged to create long-range communication through repeated structured mixing.

The paper emphasizes that this is one reason the butterfly structure is preferable to a simple block-diagonal pattern. A block-diagonal matrix isolates groups of coordinates, while a butterfly component permutes interactions across levels. As a result, the butterfly structure supports dense effective connectivity without giving up the sparse parameterization.

# Generalizing to block butterfly components

The paper does not stop at scalar butterfly factors. It generalizes the construction to block butterfly components, denoted $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$, where each entry in the butterfly factor is replaced by a $b \times b$ matrix. This makes the parameterization more flexible and allows BOFT to interpolate between different levels of structural granularity.

In the block version, the smallest nontrivial component is $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, which is now composed of $2b \times 2b$ blocks. The paper explains that this component can again be made orthogonal by parameterizing each $2b \times 2b$ block orthogonally. Once that base case is handled, the higher-level block butterfly components inherit the same sparsity pattern up to a block-wise permutation.

This block generalization is important because it gives BOFT a family of parameterizations rather than a single fixed one. Different choices of the block size $b$ and the number of factors $m$ produce different trade-offs between expressivity and parameter efficiency, all within the same butterfly framework.

# Orthogonality of $2b \times 2b$ blocks

The paper’s block butterfly construction relies on the fact that if the fundamental $2b \times 2b$ blocks are orthogonal, then the larger structured components can be made orthogonal as well. This is the block-level analogue of the $2 \times 2$ case. The authors use this to define a block butterfly component whose non-zero pattern is easy to manage and whose orthogonality is preserved by design.

This step matters because it lets BOFT scale up the same mechanism from scalar entries to larger blocks without losing the benefits of the butterfly design. The block structure gives the method more expressive power than the scalar version, while still retaining the recursive sparsity pattern that keeps parameterization efficient.

The paper frames this as a way to build an orthogonal matrix out of sparse components that are themselves orthogonal. Since the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, the entire construction remains valid as a finetuning transformation.

# Constructing the final orthogonal matrix as a product of orthogonal butterfly components

The final BOFT parameterization is obtained by multiplying a sequence of orthogonal butterfly components. In the paper’s notation, the learned orthogonal matrix is

\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]

with each factor satisfying

\[
\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}=
\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top=\bm{I}_d.
\]

This is the core orthogonal butterfly matrix used in BOFT. It is not a single large unconstrained orthogonal matrix, but a product of structured components whose combined effect can still be dense. The paper’s notation also makes clear how BOFT interpolates between different cases: when $m=1$, it reduces to a block-diagonal OFT-style structure; when the factors are arranged in the full butterfly depth, it can produce a dense orthogonal matrix.

The construction achieves the paper’s main objective in this technical section: a parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterization that preserves the desirable properties of orthogonal finetuning while dramatically reducing the number of trainable parameters. The final matrix is still orthogonal, still compatible with the finetuning framework of OFT, but much more economical because its degrees of freedom are distributed across a sequence of sparse butterfly components rather than concentrated in one dense matrix.

8. The BOFT Method: Formulation, Special Cases, and Complexity

# BOFT forward-pass equation

BOFT, short for Orthogonal Butterfly, is a finetuning method in which the pretrained linear layer is modified by left-multiplying it with a learnable orthogonal matrix. If the frozen pretrained weight matrix is $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ and the input is $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, then the original forward pass is

\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
\]

BOFT replaces this with

\[
\bm{z} = \big(\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x},
\]

where $\bm{R}(m,b) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix constructed from butterfly components. This means BOFT does not change the layer architecture itself; instead, it changes the effective weights by applying a structured orthogonal transformation before the frozen weights are used in the forward pass.

The key idea is that $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is not represented as a single dense orthogonal matrix. Instead, it is built from a product of sparse orthogonal factors. This is what makes BOFT parameter-efficient while still preserving the orthogonality constraint.

# Notation for $\bm{R}(m,b)$ and stacked butterfly components

The BOFT orthogonal matrix is written as a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components:

\[
\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}.
\]

Each factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component. The paper uses the notation $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2^{m-i+1})$ and abbreviates it as $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ for convenience. Every factor in the product must itself be orthogonal, so each one satisfies

\[
\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)} = \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)} \big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top = \bm{I}_d,
\qquad \forall j \in [1,m].
\]

The paper builds these components recursively from butterfly matrices. At the smallest scale, the butterfly factor is defined as a $k \times k$ matrix of the form

\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]

where each $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. The full butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ is then built recursively from smaller butterfly matrices and butterfly components. This recursive structure is what gives BOFT its compact parameterization.

The paper also generalizes this construction to block butterfly components, where the scalar entries in the butterfly factors are replaced by $b \times b$ blocks. This generalization is important because it creates a family of models indexed by the depth parameter $m$ and block size $b$.

# BOFT as a generalized orthogonal finetuning framework

BOFT is presented as a generalized orthogonal finetuning framework because it includes the original OFT as a special case while also allowing denser and more expressive orthogonal parameterizations. The point of the framework is to interpolate between two extremes: highly structured, parameter-efficient orthogonal transformations and fully dense orthogonal transformations.

The original OFT uses a single orthogonal matrix, often with a block-diagonal structure for parameter efficiency. BOFT replaces that single factor with a product of multiple sparse orthogonal butterfly components. As a result, BOFT can represent more expressive orthogonal transformations without requiring a full $d \times d$ dense parameterization.

The paper emphasizes that BOFT is not merely a heuristic modification. It is a structured orthogonal parameterization that subsumes OFT and provides a broader family of models. In this sense, BOFT is a general framework for orthogonal finetuning rather than just a specific architecture.

# Special case: BOFT$(1,b)$ as block-diagonal OFT

When the number of butterfly factors is $m=1$, BOFT reduces to a block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning method. In the paper’s notation, BOFT$(1,b)$ becomes the block-diagonal OFT variant with block size $b$.

This special case is important because it shows that BOFT contains the original parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning idea as a limiting instance. With only one factor, there is no deep butterfly composition; the orthogonal matrix is constrained to the block structure used in OFT. Thus, BOFT does not abandon OFT, but rather extends it.

The paper also notes that BOFT$(1,b)$ corresponds to the block-diagonal structure described in the original OFT work. In other words, if one uses only a single orthogonal block structure, BOFT is exactly the same as block-diagonal OFT.

# Special case: BOFT$(1,d)$ as full dense OFT

Another key special case occurs when $m=1$ and $b=d$. In that setting, BOFT$(1,d)$ reduces to the original OFT with an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix.

This case represents the opposite end of the spectrum from the block-diagonal version. When the block size grows to the full dimension, the orthogonal matrix is no longer sparsely structured in the same way; it becomes a dense orthogonal transformation. Therefore, BOFT naturally includes both the highly structured and the fully dense versions of OFT within one formulation.

This inclusion is conceptually important because it shows that BOFT can be viewed as a continuum between regularity and expressivity. Small block sizes yield stronger structural constraints and fewer parameters, while larger block sizes move toward the full orthogonal group.

# Dense orthogonal construction with logarithmic depth

A major advantage of BOFT is that it can build dense orthogonal matrices using only logarithmic depth in the butterfly case. The paper states that BOFT$(\log \frac{2d}{b}, b)$ uses the block butterfly matrix $\bm{B}^{b/2}(d)$ as $\bm{R}$ and yields a dense orthogonal matrix.

The intuition is that repeated butterfly factorization allows information to propagate across all dimensions through multiple sparse steps. Rather than using one dense orthogonal matrix directly, BOFT composes many sparse orthogonal components so that the final product becomes dense. This is what makes the method parameter-efficient without sacrificing the ability to express rich transformations.

When the butterfly matrix itself is used, meaning $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, BOFT achieves the advertised $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ scaling. This is the central efficiency result of the method. The paper’s construction shows that dense orthogonal behavior does not require dense parameterization.

# Effective trainable parameter count: $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$

For a linear layer of size $d \times n$, the paper gives the effective trainable parameter count of BOFT$(m,b)$ as

\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm.
\]

This formula quantifies the benefit of factorization. The number of trainable parameters depends linearly on the dimension $d$, the butterfly depth $m$, and the block size $b$. The factor $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)$ reflects the internal structure of the orthogonal blocks used in the butterfly components.

This count is much smaller than the $d^2$ parameters needed for a full dense orthogonal matrix when $d$ is large. It is also a useful way to compare different BOFT settings, since changing $m$ or $b$ directly changes the parameter budget.

# When BOFT achieves $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ complexity

BOFT reaches $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ complexity in the butterfly setting, namely when the factorization uses $m=\log d$ and $b=2$. In that case, the parameter count becomes proportional to $d \log d$, which is substantially smaller than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ cost of a dense orthogonal matrix.

This is the regime that most clearly demonstrates the value of the butterfly construction. The product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices can still yield a dense orthogonal transformation, but the number of trainable parameters grows only quasi-linearly in $d$ rather than quadratically.

The paper contrasts this with the original OFT formulation. The dense orthogonal version of OFT requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, while the block-diagonal OFT has complexity on the order of $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, depending on the block size. BOFT achieves a more favorable trade-off because it can produce dense orthogonal matrices without forcing the block size all the way up to $d$.

# Comparison of parameter complexity: full OFT, block-diagonal OFT, and BOFT

The paper positions BOFT as a middle ground between two existing extremes. Full OFT, with an unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix, is expressive but expensive, using $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. Block-diagonal OFT is more efficient, but it imposes a fixed sparsity pattern and reduces expressiveness.

BOFT improves on both by using butterfly factorization. Like block-diagonal OFT, it exploits structure to reduce the number of effective parameters. But unlike block-diagonal OFT, its structure is not confined to independent blocks. Instead, the repeated butterfly composition allows information to flow globally across the matrix, making dense orthogonal matrices possible with far fewer parameters.

The paper’s comparison shows that original OFT must effectively set the block size to $b=d$ to obtain a dense orthogonal matrix. BOFT, by contrast, can achieve density for a range of settings, including the butterfly case. This makes BOFT a more flexible and general orthogonal finetuning scheme.

# Trading off depth, block size, expressivity, and efficiency

A central theme of BOFT is the trade-off between depth, block size, expressivity, and efficiency. Increasing the depth $m$ generally allows the product of more sparse orthogonal components, which can increase expressivity. At the same time, the block size $b$ controls the granularity of each component, and smaller blocks lead to fewer trainable parameters.

The paper’s discussion of approximation experiments supports this trade-off. BOFT with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ is generally more parameter-efficient. In the reported simulations, configurations with more butterfly depth often achieve similar approximation errors using fewer parameters than shallow block-diagonal settings with larger block sizes.

This is the main design principle behind BOFT. Instead of relying on one large orthogonal matrix or a fixed block-diagonal sparsity pattern, BOFT uses a factorized butterfly structure to navigate between regularity and expressivity. The result is a family of orthogonal finetuning methods that can be tuned to fit different parameter budgets while preserving the structural advantages of orthogonal updates.

9. Practical Training Details for BOFT

# Identity initialization of butterfly components

A central practical design choice in BOFT is to start finetuning from the pretrained model as closely as possible. BOFT does this by initializing all butterfly components to identity matrices. In other words, at the beginning of finetuning, the orthogonal matrix $R(m,b)$ is exactly the identity, so the pretrained model is unchanged. This is important because BOFT is intended as a finetuning method, not as a full replacement of the pretrained representation. If the model begins far away from the pretrained solution, then the adaptation process may destroy useful knowledge learned during pretraining before it has a chance to specialize to the downstream task.

This identity initialization is particularly natural in BOFT because the orthogonal transform is applied multiplicatively to the pretrained weight matrix. The forward pass uses the form $(R(m,b)W^0)^\top x$, so if $R(m,b)=I$, then the model behaves exactly like the original pretrained linear layer. That makes the initialization both simple and stable: the network starts from the pretrained function and learns only deviations from it.

# Why initialization at the pretrained model matters in finetuning

The paper emphasizes that finetuning methods usually aim to remain close to the pretrained solution at the start of adaptation. This is a general principle behind many parameter-efficient methods. In the BOFT setting, starting exactly from the pretrained model helps preserve the semantic knowledge encoded in the original weights while allowing the orthogonal transformation to gradually adapt those weights to the target task.

This is especially relevant because BOFT is designed around orthogonal transformations, which preserve the pairwise angles between neurons and help maintain the geometry of the pretrained representation. If training begins with a large perturbation, then that carefully preserved structure would be immediately disrupted. Identity initialization avoids that problem by ensuring that the first step of adaptation is not a change in the model’s behavior, but merely the beginning of a controlled transformation.

# The role of zero-skew initialization under Cayley parameterization

BOFT uses Cayley parameterization to maintain orthogonality during training. In the paper, the orthogonal matrix is written in terms of a skew-symmetric matrix, and the identity initialization is implemented by initializing that skew-symmetric matrix to zero. Because the Cayley transform maps a zero skew-symmetric matrix to the identity, this gives a convenient way to initialize each orthogonal butterfly component exactly at identity.

This zero-skew initialization is not just a technical detail. It is the mechanism that ensures the orthogonal factors begin from a neutral point. Since the learned matrix is orthogonal throughout training, starting with zero skew-symmetry means the parameters initially encode no rotation or mixing beyond the pretrained layer. The resulting optimization path is therefore anchored at the pretrained model, which is consistent with the finetuning objective.

# Why conventional dropout works naturally for additive methods like LoRA

The paper contrasts BOFT with additive methods such as LoRA. In LoRA, the weight update is added to the pretrained matrix as a low-rank term. Because this update is additive, conventional dropout can be applied naturally to the low-rank branch. Dropping parts of the additive update simply means that some contributions to the weight change are temporarily suppressed during training, while the pretrained weights remain intact.

This fits the standard interpretation of dropout as a regularizer on learned activations or learned updates. Since the pretrained model is not being multiplicatively altered, the dropout mechanism can operate on the adaptation branch without interfering with the base model’s identity. That is why the usual dropout layer is compatible with LoRA’s update structure.

# Why multiplicative updates require a different regularization strategy

BOFT differs from LoRA in a crucial way: its update is multiplicative rather than additive. The pretrained weight matrix is transformed by left-multiplication with an orthogonal matrix, rather than being incremented by a separate low-rank correction. Because of this, the standard dropout mechanism is not directly suitable. Dropping units in a multiplicative transform is not the same as dropping additive residuals, since every part of the transformation contributes to the final structure of the orthogonal matrix.

The paper therefore argues that BOFT needs a dropout strategy designed for its factorized orthogonal parameterization. The goal is still the same as in other regularization schemes: to reduce overfitting and improve robustness. But the mechanism must respect the multiplicative, structured form of the update.

# Multiplicative dropout in BOFT

To address this, BOFT introduces multiplicative dropout. Rather than dropping activations or additive update terms, the method operates directly on the butterfly factors that compose the orthogonal matrix. Because the overall orthogonal transform is built as a product of multiple butterfly components, and each component can be viewed in terms of $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal blocks after suitable permutation, dropout can be applied at the level of these structured components.

The paper describes this procedure as first randomly selecting a fraction $p_1$ of the butterfly components, and then selecting a fraction $p_2$ of the diagonal blocks within each chosen component. The selected blocks are replaced with identity matrices. This preserves the overall multiplicative structure while weakening part of the learned transformation during training.

This is why the dropout is described as multiplicative: instead of removing an additive contribution, it neutralizes parts of the multiplicative chain by turning them into identity transformations. The result is still a valid orthogonal-style update, but one that is partially masked during training.

# Randomly dropping butterfly components and diagonal blocks by replacing them with identity

The mechanics of multiplicative dropout are straightforward once the butterfly parameterization is understood. BOFT’s orthogonal matrix is assembled from several butterfly components. Each component contains structured blocks, and these blocks are the natural units for regularization. During training, BOFT randomly chooses some components and some blocks inside them, and replaces those blocks with identity matrices.

Replacing a block with identity means that, for that part of the transformation, the input passes through unchanged. In effect, the model is forced not to depend too heavily on any single butterfly component or block. Because the dropout is applied stochastically, different subsets of the structure are active across training iterations, which encourages the model to learn transformations that do not rely on one fragile configuration of the orthogonal factors.

# Expected effects on overfitting and robustness

The paper’s motivation for multiplicative dropout is the same as for dropout in general: it is intended to reduce overfitting. By randomly disabling parts of the learned butterfly structure during training, the optimization process is encouraged to distribute information more evenly across the available factors. This can help prevent the model from becoming overly specialized to narrow patterns in the training data.

In addition, because the method perturbs the multiplicative path through the orthogonal factors, it may improve robustness in a way that is compatible with BOFT’s structured finetuning philosophy. The paper frames this as part of the practical design that makes BOFT usable: it is not enough to have a parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterization, but one must also make training stable and resistant to overfitting. Multiplicative dropout is the paper’s solution to that issue.

# Practical integration into pretrained linear layers

In practice, BOFT is inserted into pretrained linear layers by replacing the original weight matrix with its orthogonally transformed version. The layer’s computation changes from using $W^0$ directly to using $(R(m,b)W^0)$, where $R(m,b)$ is the learned product of butterfly components. Because the initialization is identity, the layer initially behaves exactly like the pretrained linear layer, and the adaptation begins with no disruption to the original model.

This makes BOFT easy to integrate into existing pretrained architectures. The orthogonal factors are trained as part of the finetuning process, while the pretrained layer remains the anchor of the computation. After finetuning, the learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the pretrained model, so there is no additional inference latency. Thus, the practical training recipe combines identity initialization, Cayley-based orthogonality maintenance, and multiplicative dropout into a finetuning method that is both structured and deployable.

10. Expressivity, Approximation Power, and Theoretical Insights

#
In BOFT, expressivity refers to how rich the set of orthogonal transformations is that the method can represent through its parameterization. This matters because BOFT does not learn an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix directly; instead, it learns a structured product of sparse orthogonal factors. The central question is therefore not simply whether the representation is orthogonal, but how much of the orthogonal group it can cover while still using few trainable parameters. In the paper’s framing, a more expressive parameterization can approximate a wider range of target transformations, while a more constrained one may act as a stronger regularizer. BOFT is designed to sit in the middle of this tension: it is more structured than a fully dense orthogonal matrix, but less restrictive than the block-diagonal form used in OFT.

The paper emphasizes that expressivity should be understood together with parameter efficiency. A method that is too expressive may require too many parameters or may overfit, while a method that is too constrained may fail to capture the transformation needed for downstream adaptation. BOFT’s butterfly factorization is intended to provide a structured subset of the orthogonal group that is still rich enough to be useful in practice. This is why the paper repeatedly contrasts BOFT with the block-diagonal structure of OFT: block-diagonal orthogonal matrices are parameter-efficient, but their sparsity pattern imposes a rigid separation of dimensions that can limit what transformations are possible.

#
To study expressivity empirically, the paper considers the problem of approximating a random dense orthogonal matrix of size $512 \times 512$. This serves as a proxy for asking how well BOFT can represent a generic orthogonal transformation. The approximation error is measured as a function of the number of effective trainable parameters, and the results are averaged over $10$ random seeds. The paper uses this experiment to compare different BOFT settings with different block sizes and factorization depths.

The reported trend is that BOFT generally achieves better parameter efficiency than OFT. In particular, the paper notes that BOFT with a smaller block size and a larger number of butterfly factors is typically more efficient. For example, BOFT$(6,4)$ uses much fewer parameters but reaches a similar approximation error to BOFT$(2,16)$. Likewise, BOFT$(9,2)$ is described as more expressive than BOFT$(1,16)$ while using far fewer parameters. These examples illustrate the main empirical lesson: once the butterfly structure is used, the depth of the factorization can increase the reach of the parameterization without requiring a proportional increase in trainable weights.

The key takeaway is not that one particular setting is always best, but that the butterfly factorization produces a favorable curve between approximation power and parameter count. In the paper’s view, the butterfly structure gives a more structured subset of orthogonal matrices than block-diagonal OFT, and this structure is what makes the method both compact and capable of approximating dense orthogonal transformations.

#
The paper explains the empirical efficiency of BOFT using the interaction between block size and factorization depth. Smaller blocks reduce the number of trainable parameters per factor, while deeper compositions allow information to propagate more globally across the matrix. This is consistent with the earlier information-transmission picture: a single sparse factor can only move information locally according to its connectivity pattern, but a product of several such factors can propagate information across many paths. As a result, increasing the depth $m$ gives the model more opportunities to mix dimensions, even when each factor is individually sparse.

This explains why deeper butterfly compositions can improve efficiency. The factorization spreads the representational burden across multiple sparse orthogonal components rather than concentrating it in one large matrix. In the paper’s formulation, BOFT$(m,b)$ uses $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters for a linear layer of size $d \times n$. When the butterfly matrix is used, with $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$, this becomes $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. By contrast, a full orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. Thus, deeper compositions improve expressivity while still keeping the cost subquadratic.

The paper also points out that the original OFT needs the block size to grow to $d$ in order to produce a dense orthogonal matrix, whereas BOFT can obtain density with any block size by composing enough butterfly components. This is an important distinction: in OFT, a smaller block size makes the matrix more efficient but also more structurally limited, while in BOFT the factorization depth helps recover global mixing even when the blocks remain small.

#
The central theoretical claim of the paper is that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. The theorem states that, for the butterfly matrix to approximate all orthogonal matrices of size $d$, one can multiply butterfly matrices of the form
\[
\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}_{d-1,2}^\top(d)\cdots \bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}_{1,2}^\top(d),
\]
where each $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$ is itself a butterfly matrix. The point of this result is that compositions of butterfly matrices and their transposes can generate a much richer family of orthogonal transformations than a single block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with the same block size.

The theorem does not claim that every setting of BOFT will represent the full orthogonal group. Rather, it establishes that the butterfly-based parameterization has a greater representational potential than OFT at matched block size. In other words, the butterfly structure can simulate a broader class of orthogonal transformations even under the same parameter-efficiency constraints. This is the paper’s formal justification for saying that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size.

#
From this theorem, the paper derives a generalized construction of BOFT. The generalized orthogonal matrix is written as
\[
\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)=\bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\cdots \bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2),
\]
where the $\bm{R}_{i,j}^\top(m,b)$ denote orthogonal matrices used in BOFT. This construction is described as a generalized matrix composition and is connected to the kaleidoscope hierarchy. The paper notes that when $m_1 = m_2 = \log d$, $b_1 = b_2 = 2$, and $l = d - 1$, this generalized form can represent the entire orthogonal group.

The importance of this generalized construction is conceptual as much as practical. It shows that the butterfly-based parameterization is not just a one-off trick for parameter reduction, but part of a broader family of orthogonal compositions. The kaleidoscope hierarchy provides a systematic way to enlarge the representational family by stacking structured orthogonal components. BOFT therefore sits inside a more general framework of structured orthogonal design, where different compositions can interpolate between compactness and expressive completeness.

#
A crucial point made in the paper is that maximal expressivity is not always the goal in finetuning. Although a fully expressive model can, in principle, represent many transformations, the paper argues that this does not automatically translate into better downstream performance. The comparison to full finetuning is used to make this point clear: even though full finetuning is universally expressive, it often yields unsatisfactory performance. The implication is that expressivity alone is not enough to explain generalization or adaptation quality.

Instead, the paper frames finetuning as a trade-off between expressivity and regularity. BOFT is designed to enlarge the parameter space while still enforcing a strong structural prior through butterfly factorization and orthogonality. This keeps the hypothesis class smaller than the full orthogonal group, but not so small that the model becomes inflexible. The structural constraint is therefore not just a limitation; it is also a source of regularization.

This is why the paper emphasizes that BOFT introduces an implicit inductive bias. Because the butterfly structure resembles classic fast linear transforms such as the discrete Fourier transform, discrete sine and cosine transforms, and the Hadamard transform, it favors patterns that are already known to support efficient information mixing. The structured sparsity acts as a bias toward transformations that are both expressive and regularized. In the paper’s view, this is a more favorable regime for finetuning than unconstrained expressivity.

#
The paper also connects these ideas to the spectral behavior of the finetuned weights. Since BOFT multiplies the pretrained weights by an orthogonal matrix on the left, the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix is preserved. The paper presents this as a desirable property because preserving the spectral norm is associated with training stability and generalization. This supports the broader argument that orthogonal finetuning, including BOFT, gains usefulness not only from how many transformations it can represent, but also from the kinds of transformations it allows.

Another way the paper interprets BOFT is as learning a bilinear similarity matrix under orthogonality constraints. In this view, BOFT learns transformations of the form $\bm{w}_i^0 \bm{R} \bm{x}$, where $\bm{w}_i^0$ is a column vector of the pretrained weight matrix. This makes the method resemble a structured form of bilinear metric learning. The expressivity question then becomes whether the learned bilinear form can cover enough useful similarities without becoming too unconstrained. BOFT’s answer is to use butterfly structure to achieve a balanced middle ground.

#
Overall, the paper’s theoretical message is that BOFT succeeds because it makes a careful trade-off. The butterfly factorization increases expressivity relative to OFT at the same block size, and the theorem and generalized construction show that this family can become very rich when composed appropriately. At the same time, the structure is not maximally free, and that limitation is part of the method’s value. The paper argues that this controlled expressivity is exactly what is needed in parameter-efficient finetuning: enough flexibility to adapt, but enough regularity to preserve the benefits of pretraining and support generalization.

11. Spectral Stability, Geometry, and Learning Interpretation

# Orthogonal transformations and preservation of singular values

A central mathematical fact behind Orthogonal Finetuning, and therefore BOFT, is that orthogonal transformations preserve the singular values of a matrix when applied in the way used in the paper. BOFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and applies it to the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by left multiplication, producing the finetuned weight matrix $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, it does not distort the intrinsic scale structure encoded by the singular values of $\bm{W}^0$.

This matters because singular values summarize important global properties of a matrix. In particular, the largest singular value is the spectral norm. The paper emphasizes that preserving this quantity is a key advantage of orthogonal finetuning. If $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ is the singular value decomposition of the pretrained matrix, then applying $\bm{R}$ on the left gives $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Since $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is still orthogonal, the singular value structure encoded by $\bm{\Sigma}$ remains unchanged. This is the reason the paper says orthogonal finetuning perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix.

The intuition is geometric. Orthogonal transformations rotate or reflect vectors without stretching them. So when BOFT updates a layer through an orthogonal matrix, it changes directions while keeping the overall scale behavior controlled. This is very different from arbitrary updates, which can enlarge or shrink directions unpredictably and thereby alter the layer’s spectral behavior.

# Why BOFT preserves the spectral norm of pretrained weights

The paper’s spectral argument follows directly from the fact that BOFT’s learned transformation is orthogonal. If $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, then the updated weight is $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ remains an orthogonal matrix. Therefore, the singular values in $\bm{\Sigma}$ are unchanged, including the largest one.

This preservation is not just an abstract algebraic property. The paper ties it to training stability and generalization. In contrast to finetuning methods that may alter the spectral norm substantially, orthogonal finetuning keeps the layer’s largest singular value fixed. The paper cites prior spectral norm literature to motivate why this can be beneficial. The core idea is that controlling the spectral norm helps keep optimization more stable and can support better generalization behavior.

BOFT inherits this property exactly because its learned transformation is orthogonal, regardless of whether the orthogonal matrix is parameterized densely, block-diagonally, or through butterfly factorization. The factorization changes parameter efficiency, but not the fundamental spectral guarantee. So BOFT is designed to be parameter-efficient without giving up the spectral regularity that motivates orthogonal finetuning in the first place.

# Comparison with LoRA from a spectral perspective

The paper contrasts BOFT with LoRA by emphasizing the different ways the two methods modify pretrained weights. LoRA adds a low-rank matrix to the pretrained weight matrix, whereas BOFT multiplies the pretrained weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix. This distinction has important spectral consequences.

Because LoRA is additive, it can change the singular values of the pretrained weight matrix in uncontrolled ways. The paper does not present this as inherently bad, but it uses the contrast to highlight a strength of orthogonal finetuning: BOFT preserves the spectral norm exactly. In other words, BOFT modifies the model by rotating the pretrained representation rather than shifting it with an additive perturbation. That means BOFT gives a more structurally constrained update.

The paper also frames this difference in terms of regularity versus expressivity. LoRA gains efficiency through low rank, while BOFT gains efficiency through structured orthogonality. BOFT is not simply a different compression mechanism; it is a different geometric bias on how weights are allowed to move. The preservation of singular values is part of that bias, and the paper argues that this helps explain BOFT’s stable training behavior.

# Connections between spectral control, training stability, and generalization

The paper explicitly links orthogonal finetuning’s spectral preservation to training stability and generalization. The reasoning is that if the spectral norm of a layer remains controlled, then optimization is less likely to become unstable. The paper points to prior work on spectral norm regularization to support this connection. In BOFT, this control is not imposed as a separate penalty; it is built into the parameterization itself.

This is an important theme throughout the paper: BOFT aims to impose a useful structure on the adaptation process rather than merely reducing the number of trainable parameters. The orthogonal constraint acts like a regularizer. It limits how much the pretrained representation can be distorted while still allowing meaningful task adaptation. The paper argues that this structured restriction can improve generalization because it keeps the finetuned model closer to the pretrained model in a principled way.

The relation to the butterfly factorization matters here as well. BOFT is not just orthogonal finetuning; it is a parameter-efficient version of orthogonal finetuning. By using butterfly structure, BOFT preserves the orthogonal geometry while reducing the number of effective parameters. So the spectral control is retained, but the model becomes easier to adapt in practice.

# Pairwise angle preservation and geometric regularity

The geometric motivation for orthogonal finetuning in the paper is preservation of pairwise angles between neurons. Because orthogonal transformations preserve inner products and norms, they preserve angles. This means that the relative geometry of neurons in a layer is maintained while the model is adapted.

The paper presents this as a way to preserve semantic knowledge from pretraining. The pretrained model already contains useful structure, and if finetuning changes pairwise angles too aggressively, it may damage that structure. Orthogonal finetuning instead transforms the neurons in a way that keeps the geometry intact. BOFT inherits this property because its learned transformation is orthogonal.

This idea of geometric regularity is central to the paper’s interpretation of finetuning. Rather than thinking only in terms of parameter updates, BOFT can be understood as a constrained geometric transformation of the pretrained representation. The structured orthogonal update keeps the finetuning process smooth and regular, which the paper argues is favorable for downstream adaptation.

# BOFT as learning a bilinear similarity matrix

The paper gives another useful interpretation of BOFT: it can be written as learning a bilinear similarity. If $\bm{w}_i^0$ is the $i$-th neuron, viewed as a column vector of $\bm{W}^0$, then BOFT effectively learns expressions of the form $\bm{w}_i^0\bm{R}\bm{x}$. Here $\bm{R}$ acts as a similarity matrix between the pretrained neuron representation and the input $\bm{x}$.

This viewpoint turns BOFT into a form of structured similarity learning. Instead of changing the pretrained neurons themselves arbitrarily, BOFT learns how they should interact with the input through a bilinear form. The matrix $\bm{R}$ is not free-form, however; it must remain orthogonal. That orthogonality is the “strong regularity” the paper emphasizes. So BOFT learns a similarity relation under a strict geometric constraint.

This interpretation helps explain why BOFT is not just a parameterization trick. It is a way of learning how pretrained features should be compared to inputs while preserving a controlled geometry. The bilinear view also makes BOFT easier to connect to broader mathematical frameworks involving similarity, distance, and structured matrix forms.

# Connections to distance metric learning

The paper notes that BOFT’s bilinear similarity viewpoint connects naturally to distance metric learning. In metric learning, one aims to learn a representation or transformation under which similarity or distance between examples becomes more meaningful for the task. BOFT’s orthogonal matrix plays a similar role: it transforms the pretrained features so that their interaction with the input reflects downstream task needs.

The connection is especially clear because BOFT learns a matrix that mediates similarity, rather than directly learning a new representation from scratch. The orthogonal constraint means that the learned similarity is not arbitrary; it is disciplined by the geometry of the pretrained space. This is analogous to metric learning settings in which the learned transformation must satisfy certain structural requirements so that distances or similarities remain well behaved.

The paper does not claim that BOFT is a standard metric-learning method, but rather that the bilinear form $\bm{w}_i^0\bm{R}\bm{x}$ makes the relationship conceptually similar. In both cases, the learning problem is about shaping similarity in a structured space. BOFT does so through orthogonal parameterization, which keeps the adaptation regular while still allowing expressive changes.

# Connections to bilinear forms and representation similarity

Finally, the paper situates BOFT in the broader language of bilinear forms. A bilinear form takes two vectors and combines them through a matrix, and BOFT can be read in exactly this way. The pretrained neuron vector and the input vector are related through $\bm{R}$, so the finetuning process becomes a structured bilinear interaction.

This perspective is useful because it highlights what BOFT preserves and what it changes. The pretrained representation is not discarded. Instead, it is reinterpreted through a learned orthogonal bilinear matrix. That means BOFT preserves the pretrained feature geometry while learning a task-specific way to compare representations. This is what the paper means by learning with strong regularity.

The bilinear interpretation also reinforces the paper’s broader message: BOFT is a finetuning method that works by structuring how information flows through pretrained weights. The orthogonal transformation preserves angles and spectral properties, the butterfly factorization makes the parameterization efficient, and the bilinear view explains how the model learns similarity in a controlled way. Taken together, these ideas give BOFT its mathematical character: expressive enough to adapt, but constrained enough to remain stable and regular.

12. Inductive Bias and Generalization in Structured Orthogonal Finetuning

#

One of the central claims of the paper is that BOFT is not just a way to save parameters. The butterfly structure is presented as a source of inductive bias, meaning that it changes the kinds of orthogonal transformations the model is naturally inclined to learn. This matters because parameter efficiency by itself does not guarantee good downstream performance. The paper repeatedly emphasizes that finetuning is a trade-off between expressivity and regularity: the method should be flexible enough to adapt the pretrained model, but constrained enough to preserve useful pretrained knowledge and avoid overfitting.

In this setting, structure is not an accidental side effect. It is part of the mechanism. BOFT restricts the finetuning matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ to a structured subset of the orthogonal group, and that restriction narrows the hypothesis class the optimization process can explore. The paper argues that this narrowing can be beneficial, because it creates a more controlled adaptation space than either a fully unconstrained orthogonal matrix or an arbitrary dense update. In other words, the method is not merely trying to be smaller; it is trying to be smaller in a way that keeps the right kinds of transformations available.

#

A useful way to understand the paper’s argument is through the idea of hypothesis class restriction. When a finetuning method allows a very large family of possible transformations, it becomes easier to fit the training data, but also easier to overfit or disturb the pretrained representation too much. The paper uses this idea to interpret BOFT’s structured orthogonal matrices as a smaller hypothesis class inside the larger orthogonal group. This smaller class is not arbitrary. It is designed so that the model still has enough freedom to adapt, while the parameterization itself imposes a regularizing constraint.

The paper contrasts this with full finetuning, which has universal expressivity but often performs poorly in practice because that much freedom can be harmful for generalization. In this sense, BOFT’s structure is intended to provide a better balance. The butterfly factorization is a way of keeping the transformation rich enough to be useful, while still preventing the learned update from becoming too unconstrained. The paper treats this as a general principle: the best finetuning method is not necessarily the one with the largest space of transformations, but the one whose constrained space is well matched to downstream adaptation.

#

The paper places special emphasis on why a structured subset of the orthogonal group may generalize better. Orthogonal finetuning already has a built-in advantage because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles and preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. BOFT inherits these properties, but adds additional structure through butterfly factorization. The paper suggests that this structure can act as an implicit bias toward useful transformations, rather than just arbitrary ones.

This argument is motivated by the observation that butterfly structures appear in many classic fast linear transforms. Because these transforms have been widely used for efficient information mixing, the authors argue that the butterfly pattern is not an unnatural artifact of the parameterization. Instead, it corresponds to a kind of organized connectivity that already appears in established linear operators. That makes the learned orthogonal matrix more interpretable as a structured transformation rather than a random dense mixing. The hope is that this structured organization helps the finetuned model retain generalization ability when adapting to new tasks.

#

A major reason the paper believes butterfly factorization has favorable inductive bias is that it can recover many classic fast transforms. The paper explicitly notes that butterfly structure, together with permutations, can perfectly recover several well-known linear transforms, including the fast Fourier transform, the Hadamard transform, and related fast transform families. This is important because these transforms are examples of highly structured linear operators that are both efficient and expressive. They are not arbitrary sparse matrices; they are carefully organized compositions of simple interactions.

From the perspective of the paper, this recoverability is evidence that butterfly factorization is a principled structure rather than a purely engineering-driven sparsity pattern. If a parameterization can represent classic fast transforms naturally, then it suggests that the parameterization is aligned with useful linear algebraic structure. The paper treats this as part of the reason BOFT can generalize well: it does not merely make the parameter count smaller, but biases learning toward transformations that resemble efficient, well-behaved linear operators.

#

The paper also highlights the recoverability of Fourier, cosine, sine, and Hadamard-like structure as a contrast to the block-diagonal structure used in the original OFT. Butterfly matrices can approximate or recover these transform-like patterns because their connectivity pattern allows information to be mixed across the whole system through multiple sparse stages. This creates a kind of organized global interaction, rather than confining interactions within isolated groups.

That distinction matters. Fourier-like and Hadamard-like transforms are global in character. They move information across the full vector through layered sparse exchanges. The paper uses this fact to argue that butterfly factorization gives BOFT a more meaningful structural prior than simple local block transformations. The learned update is encouraged to behave like a composition of efficient global mixing operations, which the authors associate with better generalization properties.

#

In contrast, block-diagonal OFT lacks these transform-like inductive biases. The original OFT uses orthogonal blocks that act independently on separate groups of dimensions. While this reduces the parameter count, it also hard-codes a strong separation between groups of coordinates. The paper points out that this means the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot recover classic linear transforms in the way butterfly structure can. Its sparsity pattern is too rigid and too local.

This is one reason the paper views block-diagonal sparsity as a weaker inductive bias. It saves parameters, but it does so by partitioning the space into independent parts whose interaction is limited by construction. That makes the transformation less expressive in a structured sense, and it also makes the sparsity pattern less aligned with known efficient transforms. BOFT, by using butterfly factorization, keeps sparsity but arranges it in a way that supports richer global communication.

#

The paper also frames sparse matrix factorization itself as an implicit regularizer. Because BOFT represents a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices, the optimization is not directly choosing an arbitrary dense transformation. Instead, it must assemble the transformation through a constrained sequence of sparse components. This factorization reduces the effective degrees of freedom and imposes a compositional structure on the learned update.

The authors suggest that this can help generalization because the factorization limits the space of solutions the model can choose from. In this sense, sparsity is not only about compression. It also shapes the learning dynamics and the kinds of solutions that are accessible. The paper connects this to the broader idea of implicit inductive bias: a parameterization can steer learning toward certain kinds of functions even without an explicit regularization term. BOFT’s butterfly factorization is presented as such a steering mechanism.

#

The balance between structure and flexibility is the final theme underlying the paper’s generalization argument. The paper repeatedly warns that more expressivity is not automatically better. A method that can represent the entire orthogonal group is extremely flexible, but that flexibility may not be ideal for finetuning. Instead, BOFT is designed to enlarge the finetuning space in a structured way, so that the model has enough freedom to adapt while still benefiting from regularization through architecture.

This is why the paper sees BOFT as a “smooth interpolation” between the identity matrix and the full orthogonal group. At one extreme, the identity preserves the pretrained model exactly. At the other extreme, a fully unconstrained orthogonal matrix allows broad transformation. BOFT sits between these extremes by using butterfly factorization, which gives a structured and expressive family of orthogonal updates. The authors argue that this middle ground is precisely what makes the method effective: it preserves useful pretrained knowledge, supports adaptation, and introduces an inductive bias that can improve downstream generalization.

13. Empirical Evaluation Across Modalities

# Experimental motivation: testing BOFT beyond its original application domain

The central experimental goal of the paper is to show that Orthogonal Butterfly, or BOFT, is not limited to the narrow setting in which orthogonal finetuning was first introduced. The original orthogonal finetuning method, OFT, had been demonstrated on controllable text-to-image diffusion models, but that alone did not establish it as a broadly useful finetuning paradigm. The authors therefore use their experiments to answer a larger question: if orthogonal transformations are a principled way to preserve pretrained knowledge, and if butterfly factorization makes such transformations parameter-efficient, then can this idea work across different kinds of foundation models?

This broader experimental scope matters because the paper’s main claim is not merely that BOFT is a more compact version of OFT. Rather, the claim is that BOFT is a generic parameter-efficient finetuning method. To support that claim, the authors evaluate BOFT in multiple modalities: computer vision, natural language processing, and text-to-image generation. In other words, the experiments are designed to test whether the same orthogonal finetuning idea can transfer beyond the domain where it was initially proposed.

The paper frames these experiments as a direct test of generality. If BOFT performs well only on diffusion models, then it would be a specialized method. If it performs well on large vision transformers and large language models as well, then it is evidence that the method captures something fundamental about how to adapt foundation models with minimal parameter updates while retaining pretrained knowledge.

# Adapting large vision transformers

One major experimental direction in the paper is the adaptation of large vision transformers. This is important because it moves BOFT into a different architectural family from the diffusion models used in the earlier orthogonal finetuning work. Vision transformers have become a central class of foundation models in computer vision, so showing that BOFT applies to them broadens the method’s relevance considerably.

The experiments on vision transformers are meant to demonstrate that BOFT can serve as a practical finetuning mechanism when the pretrained model is large and the downstream task requires adaptation rather than retraining. The paper emphasizes that BOFT operates by transforming pretrained weights with orthogonal matrices, which means the original architecture is unchanged and inference latency is not introduced by added modules. In the vision setting, this makes BOFT especially appealing as a way to adapt a strong pretrained backbone while keeping the finetuning process compact.

From the paper’s perspective, the vision experiments also help evaluate whether the inductive bias induced by butterfly structure is useful beyond generative tasks. The authors argue that the structure of BOFT helps create a smaller and more regular hypothesis class within the orthogonal group. If this structural bias is beneficial, it should not be tied to one modality. The results on vision transformers are therefore part of the evidence that BOFT is a general-purpose finetuning strategy rather than a task-specific trick.

# Adapting large language models

The paper also applies BOFT to large language models, which is another significant test of generality. Language models differ substantially from both vision transformers and diffusion models in their task structure and training dynamics. If BOFT works in this setting, it suggests that orthogonal finetuning with butterfly factorization is not dependent on the peculiarities of one architecture or objective.

The inclusion of large language models is especially meaningful because parameter-efficient finetuning has been widely studied in natural language processing. In that landscape, methods such as LoRA have become popular due to their simplicity and effectiveness. The paper positions BOFT as a competitor in this space by showing that the same orthogonal reparameterization idea can be made efficient enough to be practical for language adaptation.

The broader significance of the language experiments is that they test whether BOFT can provide a useful compromise between expressivity and regularity in a domain where overfitting and parameter budget are both important concerns. Since BOFT preserves orthogonality while reducing the number of trainable parameters through butterfly factorization, it is intended to retain the stabilization and knowledge-preserving properties of orthogonal finetuning while remaining efficient enough for large-scale language adaptation. The reported experiments are used to support exactly that point.

# Adapting text-to-image diffusion models

Text-to-image diffusion models are the original setting in which orthogonal finetuning had already shown promise, so they serve as both a continuity check and a benchmark for the new method. BOFT is built as a generalization of OFT, and the paper explicitly notes that it subsumes OFT as a special case. Because of that, diffusion-model adaptation is an important place to verify that the more compact parameterization does not sacrifice the strengths of the original orthogonal finetuning approach.

The paper emphasizes that orthogonal finetuning is motivated by the desire to preserve the pretrained model’s relational structure, especially the pairwise angles between neurons. In diffusion models, this motivation had already been shown to yield stable and effective finetuning. BOFT carries that same principle forward while reducing parameter count via butterfly factorization. The diffusion experiments therefore test whether this new parameterization preserves the benefits of orthogonal updates while making them more efficient.

Just as importantly, the diffusion setting allows the paper to show that BOFT is not merely an alternative implementation of OFT. Because BOFT uses a dense orthogonal matrix constructed from sparse orthogonal factors, it provides a more flexible path to parameter efficiency than the original block-diagonal structure. The diffusion experiments help confirm that this flexibility is not only theoretically appealing but also practically effective.

# Comparison against current state-of-the-art PEFT baselines

A major theme of the empirical section is comparison with current state-of-the-art parameter-efficient finetuning methods. The paper repeatedly positions BOFT against widely used PEFT approaches, especially methods based on low-rank reparameterization such as LoRA, as well as the original OFT baseline. This comparison is central because the paper is not just asking whether BOFT works, but whether it is competitive with the best available alternatives.

The paper’s framing makes clear that OFT and LoRA represent two different parameter-efficiency philosophies. LoRA uses additive low-rank updates, while OFT uses multiplicative orthogonal updates. BOFT is meant to preserve the key benefits of OFT while achieving a parameter budget closer to that of other PEFT methods. The reported experiments are used to show that BOFT can outperform current state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin, while maintaining a favorable efficiency profile.

This comparison is also important because it ties the empirical results back to the structural claims of the paper. The authors argue that sparse matrix factorization through butterfly structure introduces an inductive bias that is beneficial for finetuning. The fact that BOFT performs strongly relative to state-of-the-art PEFT baselines is presented as evidence that this bias is not merely a theoretical curiosity, but a practical advantage.

# Evidence for improved parameter efficiency

Parameter efficiency is one of the paper’s main motivations, and the empirical section is meant to support the claim that BOFT achieves it in practice. The theory already shows that butterfly factorization reduces the number of effective trainable parameters from the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ scale of a dense orthogonal matrix to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ in the butterfly case. The experiments then ask whether this reduction translates into better finetuning behavior under realistic model adaptation tasks.

The paper’s results are presented as showing that BOFT substantially improves parameter efficiency relative to OFT while still retaining strong performance. This is not just a matter of having fewer parameters in an abstract sense; it means that BOFT uses a much more compact orthogonal parameterization yet still achieves strong downstream adaptation. In the paper’s terminology, BOFT creates a smoother interpolation between the identity matrix and the full orthogonal group, making it possible to operate in a smaller but still expressive hypothesis class.

The empirical evidence for parameter efficiency is especially compelling because BOFT is evaluated across different modalities. If a parameterization is efficient only in one context, the result would be limited. The fact that BOFT is reported to work well for vision transformers, language models, and diffusion models is used to argue that the method’s efficiency is robust rather than accidental.

# Evidence for stronger generalization

The paper places strong emphasis on generalization, not only performance. This is consistent with the motivation for orthogonal finetuning in the first place: preserving pretrained structure should help downstream adaptation avoid unnecessary distortion of the learned representation. BOFT is designed to maintain that property while adding a structured inductive bias through butterfly factorization.

The authors argue that the butterfly structure should help generalization because it resembles the structure of many classic fast linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier, discrete sine/cosine, and Hadamard transforms. The empirical results are used to support this idea. By outperforming existing PEFT methods across tasks, BOFT suggests that the inductive bias induced by the butterfly parameterization is not harmful, and may in fact be beneficial.

The paper also connects generalization to the idea that BOFT defines a constrained subset of the orthogonal group. This constraint reduces the hypothesis class without abandoning the geometric benefits of orthogonal transformations. In the experiments, this is reflected in the claim that BOFT outperforms current methods while using fewer parameters. That combination is presented as evidence that BOFT improves the balance between expressivity and regularity, which the authors view as central to finetuning generalization.

# Evidence for stable optimization behavior

Stability during finetuning is another property the paper highlights, and the experiments are intended to reinforce that point. The theoretical motivation comes from the fact that orthogonal updates preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, which the paper links to better training stability. BOFT inherits this property from OFT because its learned transformations are still orthogonal.

The empirical section supports this by emphasizing that BOFT not only achieves strong results, but does so with stable optimization behavior. The paper contrasts orthogonal finetuning with methods like LoRA, where the update form is different and does not preserve the same geometric structure. In the BOFT framework, the orthogonality constraint is maintained throughout finetuning, and the model is initialized at the identity so that adaptation begins from the exact pretrained model.

The use of multiplicative dropout is also relevant here. Because BOFT’s update is multiplicative rather than additive, the paper introduces a multiplicative dropout mechanism tailored to its structure. This design choice is part of the broader empirical story: BOFT is not only efficient, but engineered to behave well during training. The reported results are used to support the idea that the method can be optimized stably across tasks.

# How the results support BOFT as a generic model finetuning method

Taken together, the experiments across vision, language, and text-to-image generation support the paper’s main conclusion: BOFT is a generic model finetuning method. This conclusion does not rest on a single benchmark or a single modality. Instead, it comes from the repeated pattern that BOFT remains competitive or superior when applied to different foundation models and different downstream tasks.

The key empirical message is that the same orthogonal butterfly parameterization can adapt diverse pretrained models effectively. That is the strongest possible evidence for generality within the scope of the paper. A method that works only in one domain may reflect quirks of that domain; a method that performs well across domains is more plausibly capturing a more universal finetuning principle. BOFT is presented as such a principle, namely that dense orthogonal updates can be made parameter-efficient through butterfly factorization without losing the geometric benefits of orthogonality.

The experiments therefore validate several of the paper’s theoretical claims at once. They support the idea that a dense orthogonal matrix can be built from sparse orthogonal factors, that this construction can achieve strong expressivity with fewer parameters, and that the resulting inductive bias is useful for downstream adaptation. Most importantly, they show that orthogonal finetuning is not confined to controllable diffusion generation. In the paper’s account, BOFT extends orthogonal finetuning into a broader PEFT framework applicable to modern foundation models across modalities.

14. BOFT in Context: Comparisons to Related Structured Methods

# BOFT versus LoRA: multiplicative orthogonal updates versus additive low-rank updates

A useful way to understand BOFT is to compare it with LoRA, since both methods try to make finetuning more parameter-efficient by restricting the form of the update rather than learning a completely free weight matrix. The paper emphasizes, however, that the two methods impose fundamentally different kinds of structure on the adaptation. LoRA updates a pretrained weight matrix by adding a product of two low-rank matrices. In other words, its adaptation is additive: the model learns a correction term that is inserted into the original weights. BOFT, by contrast, updates the pretrained weight matrix multiplicatively by left-multiplying it with an orthogonal matrix. This means that BOFT does not directly add a new correction matrix to the weight tensor; instead, it transforms the pretrained neurons through an orthogonal change of basis.

This distinction matters because BOFT is motivated by preserving the relational information among neurons, especially the pairwise angles between them. The paper presents orthogonality as a principled way to maintain this structure while adapting to downstream tasks. LoRA’s low-rank prior is efficient and widely used, but it is not designed around preserving angles or spectral properties in the same way. BOFT’s multiplicative orthogonal update ensures that the transformed weights retain the spectral norm of the pretrained matrix, which the paper connects to stability and generalization. So while both methods aim to economize parameters, they do so with different geometric assumptions: LoRA constrains the update to lie in a low-rank additive subspace, whereas BOFT constrains the update to be an orthogonal transformation applied to the pretrained weights.

# BOFT versus OFT: dense factorized orthogonality versus fixed block-diagonal structure

BOFT is easiest to see as a generalization of Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT. OFT already introduced the idea of using an orthogonal matrix to finetune a pretrained layer so that the angle relationships among neurons are preserved. In the original form, however, OFT uses a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix to reduce the number of trainable parameters. The block-diagonal design is parameter-efficient because it splits the dimensions into groups and applies separate orthogonal transformations within each block. This reduces the number of free parameters, but it does so by hard-coding a fixed sparsity pattern.

The paper argues that this fixed block structure is a limitation. A block-diagonal matrix cannot represent a dense orthogonal transformation, and its sparsity pattern imposes an arbitrary division of neuron dimensions by index. BOFT addresses this by replacing the block-diagonal construction with a dense orthogonal matrix built from a product of sparse orthogonal factors. The key idea is not merely to reduce parameters, but to do so while still allowing the overall orthogonal matrix to be dense. This is a major conceptual shift: instead of accepting a limited blockwise transformation, BOFT factorizes the orthogonal matrix in a way that can recover dense connectivity while preserving parameter efficiency.

The paper also makes clear that OFT is subsumed as a special case of BOFT. When the number of butterfly factors is $m=1$, BOFT reduces to the block-diagonal OFT form with the corresponding block size. When BOFT is configured with a sufficiently deep butterfly factorization, it can produce a dense orthogonal matrix, something the original block-diagonal OFT cannot do unless it gives up the parameter savings and moves to the full orthogonal parameterization. This is why BOFT is presented not just as a more efficient version of OFT, but as a broader orthogonal finetuning framework.

# Sparse structure versus low-rank structure as PEFT priors

The paper places BOFT and LoRA into a broader comparison between two major structured priors in parameter-efficient finetuning: sparse structure and low-rank structure. These are both ways of reducing the effective number of trainable parameters, but they organize that reduction differently. Low-rank methods like LoRA constrain the update matrix to lie in a low-dimensional factorized subspace. Sparse methods, by contrast, constrain which entries may be nonzero and therefore limit the active connectivity pattern directly.

BOFT belongs to the sparse-structure family in the sense that it uses sparse matrix factorization to build a dense orthogonal matrix. The paper explicitly contrasts sparse matrices and low-rank matrices as two major structured matrix families that enable parameter efficiency. In BOFT, sparsity is not used to make the final orthogonal matrix sparse; rather, sparse factors are multiplied together to produce a dense transformation with far fewer trainable degrees of freedom than a full dense orthogonal matrix would require. This is an important distinction. The final effect is dense and expressive, but the parametrization is sparse and structured.

The paper’s discussion suggests that the value of this sparse prior is not only in parameter count, but also in inductive bias. Because the butterfly structure can recover classic linear transforms and creates a smoother interpolation between identity-like regularity and dense orthogonal expressivity, it is presented as a structured prior that may generalize better than a less constrained alternative. This is different from the low-rank prior in LoRA, which is effective but does not arise from the same graph-based or information-transmission perspective used to motivate BOFT.

# BOFT versus butterfly-based training from scratch

Butterfly matrices are not new in machine learning, but the paper is careful to distinguish its setting from prior work on butterfly-based training from scratch. Several earlier papers use butterfly parameterizations to train neural networks directly, often because butterfly structures are efficient for representing certain linear transforms or for enabling fast matrix operations. In those settings, the butterfly matrix is usually the primary parameterization of the model component being learned.

BOFT uses butterfly structure differently. It does not propose training a network from scratch with butterfly-parameterized weights. Instead, it uses butterfly factorization to parameterize an orthogonal transformation that adapts a pretrained layer. This means BOFT lives in the finetuning regime, where the model already contains pretrained knowledge that should be preserved as much as possible. The goal is therefore not just efficiency in representation, but efficient adaptation with a controlled deviation from the pretrained weights.

This distinction is central to the paper’s claim of novelty. Butterflies in earlier work are often tied to direct weight learning, whereas BOFT uses butterfly factors to build a layer-shared orthogonal update on top of a frozen pretrained matrix. The paper also notes that another line of butterfly-related work, such as Monarch, considers finetuning by first projecting weights onto a butterfly variant and then optimizing those components. BOFT is positioned as different from that strategy as well, because its adaptation mechanism is not a direct projection of the pretrained weights into a butterfly parameterization.

# BOFT versus direct butterfly reparameterization of weights

The paper’s comparison with direct butterfly reparameterization is especially important because it clarifies what BOFT is not doing. In direct reparameterization approaches, the model’s weights themselves are replaced or expressed in butterfly form. That means the network is trained by treating the butterfly structure as the main representation of the weight matrix. BOFT does not do this. Instead, it uses butterfly factorization only as a way to construct an orthogonal transformation that is applied to the pretrained weights.

This difference changes the meaning of the parameterization. In BOFT, the pretrained weights remain the anchor, and the butterfly components are used to learn how to rotate or transform them in an orthogonality-preserving way. The butterfly structure is therefore serving the role of a compact parametrization of the adaptation operator, not a direct replacement for the original layer weights. Because of this, BOFT preserves the finetuning logic of OFT while improving its parameter efficiency, rather than redefining the model architecture itself in butterfly terms.

The paper’s information-transmission framework also helps explain why this matters. BOFT uses butterfly factors to compose a dense orthogonal matrix through sparse orthogonal components, which makes sense in the finetuning setting because only the transformation needs to be learned. A direct butterfly reparameterization of weights would not be framed this way, and it would not exploit the same interpretation of preserving pretrained structure through an orthogonal update.

# The distinct role of layer-shared orthogonal transformations in BOFT

One of BOFT’s most important differences from other butterfly-based methods is that it applies layer-shared orthogonal transformations. The paper stresses that OFT and BOFT transform the neurons of the same layer with the same orthogonal matrix. This layer-shared design is essential to the method’s interpretation as an orthogonal finetuning strategy, because the transformation is applied to the entire weight matrix as a coherent geometric operation rather than as separate per-neuron or per-connection adjustments.

This layer-sharing is part of why BOFT is connected to preserving pairwise angles and spectral properties. Since the same orthogonal matrix is applied throughout a layer, the pretrained layer is adapted in a way that maintains structured relationships among its neurons. The paper also uses this point to distinguish BOFT from approaches that simply insert butterfly parameterizations into neural networks as generic efficient linear layers. BOFT is not merely an efficient linear transform; it is a finetuning method whose update operator is shared across the layer and whose purpose is to adapt pretrained knowledge while keeping the transformation orthogonal.

The paper’s broader claim is that this layer-shared orthogonal transformation creates a better balance between expressivity and regularity than the original block-diagonal OFT design. By using butterfly factorization, BOFT can obtain dense orthogonal transformations with fewer trainable parameters, yet still preserve the structural prior that makes orthogonal finetuning appealing. This is what makes BOFT genuinely distinct in the landscape of structured PEFT methods: it is not just another butterfly model, and it is not just a compressed version of OFT. It is a layer-shared, orthogonally constrained finetuning method whose butterfly parameterization is specifically designed to make dense, expressive adaptation practical.

15. Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Directions

# Summary of BOFT's Main Conceptual and Empirical Contributions

Orthogonal Butterfly, or BOFT, is presented in the paper as a parameter-efficient way to finetune foundation models by reparameterizing the pretrained weights with structured orthogonal transformations. Its main conceptual contribution is to take the earlier idea of Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, and make it significantly more parameter-efficient by replacing a large dense orthogonal matrix with a product of sparse orthogonal factors arranged in a butterfly structure. This idea is motivated by an information transmission view: instead of thinking only about the algebra of matrix multiplication, the authors interpret the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix as a problem of transmitting information across a graph. Under that perspective, the butterfly topology becomes attractive because it can connect all source and receiver nodes with relatively few edges while still enabling dense connectivity.

BOFT is important not just because it reduces parameter count, but because it retains the core promise of orthogonal finetuning. Like OFT, it modifies pretrained weights multiplicatively using an orthogonal matrix, rather than additively as in low-rank methods such as LoRA. The paper emphasizes that orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles and preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weights, which is part of the reason orthogonal finetuning is expected to be stable and generalize well. BOFT keeps this orthogonal structure while making the parameterization much more compact through butterfly factorization.

Empirically, the paper reports that BOFT works across a broader range of adaptation tasks than the earlier OFT setting for controllable text-to-image generation. The authors apply BOFT to large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models, and they report strong performance relative to state-of-the-art parameter-efficient methods. This breadth matters because it suggests orthogonal finetuning is not limited to one application family. In the paper’s framing, BOFT is therefore not just an optimization trick, but a general finetuning framework.

# Runtime Overhead from Multiplying Multiple Orthogonal Factors During Training

The main limitation acknowledged by the authors is that BOFT introduces some extra training-time computation because the final orthogonal matrix is formed as a product of multiple orthogonal butterfly components. In OFT, the orthogonal matrix is already a learned object, but BOFT factorizes that object into several sparse pieces. This factorization reduces the number of trainable parameters, yet it also means that, during each training iteration, the model must repeatedly compose the factors to obtain the effective orthogonal transformation. The paper explicitly notes that this leads to a slightly larger training runtime overhead than OFT.

This trade-off is central to the paper’s design philosophy. BOFT lowers parameter cost by replacing a single large object with a structured product of smaller ones, but that structural savings does not come for free. The authors frame this as the usual exchange between time and space: fewer parameters are achieved by doing more matrix composition work during training. The limitation is therefore not that BOFT is impractical, but that its efficiency is asymmetric. It is especially efficient in terms of trainable parameters and inference cost, while training itself remains somewhat heavier than a more direct parameterization.

The paper does not present this as a fatal flaw. Instead, it treats the extra runtime as an open engineering issue. Since the multiplication is only needed during training, the authors suggest that a better factorization strategy or a more efficient network topology might reduce this overhead further. This leaves room for future work focused on the computational cost of structured orthogonal parameterizations, not just on their parameter count.

# Why BOFT Adds No Inference Latency After Factor Absorption

A particularly important point in the paper is that BOFT does not impose additional inference latency. This follows from the way the method is used after finetuning. During training, the learned orthogonal factors are maintained as a structured parameterization, but once finetuning is finished, the factors can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix itself. In other words, the learned transformation can be absorbed into the model weights before deployment.

This matters because it preserves one of the practical advantages of model finetuning: at test time, the adapted model behaves like a standard pretrained model with modified weights, not like a system that needs extra adapter modules or prompt tokens. The paper contrasts this with other parameter-efficient approaches where added components may complicate inference. BOFT’s factorization is a training-time representation choice, not a permanent architectural addition.

So although BOFT is more expensive than OFT during training, it retains the inference simplicity of standard finetuning. The learned orthogonal matrices do not need to be applied as separate runtime modules once they are merged into the model. This makes BOFT attractive for applications where deployment efficiency matters as much as adaptation quality.

# Open Question: Can Denser Information Transmission Be Achieved Even More Efficiently?

The paper’s information transmission framework is one of its most interesting conceptual contributions, and it also reveals an open problem. The authors show that the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix can be reinterpreted as transmitting information through a grid-structured graph, where sparse factors correspond to edges between layers. Within this framework, the butterfly topology is a compelling solution because it can achieve dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges.

However, the authors are careful not to claim that butterfly is the best possible topology. They explicitly raise the question of whether more efficient network structures might exist for transmitting information across the graph. In their view, the butterfly network is a strong candidate because it is well studied, expressive, and closely related to fast linear transforms such as the Fourier and Hadamard transforms. But the framework itself is broader than butterfly structure alone. It suggests that one could search for other sparse graph topologies that provide the same dense source-to-sink connectivity with even fewer resources or better training properties.

This is an open problem at the intersection of matrix factorization, graph structure, and model adaptation. The paper does not resolve it, but it identifies it clearly: if the goal is to build dense orthogonal matrices from sparse pieces, then the efficiency of the underlying communication pattern becomes the key design question. BOFT is one solution, not necessarily the final one.

# Potential Inspiration from Computer Networking and Communication Topology Design

To motivate this open direction, the paper points toward computer networking as a source of inspiration. The authors note that network topologies have long been studied for their ability to transmit information efficiently. Butterfly graphs themselves already have a history in this area, and the paper cites work on broadcasting and transmission in such networks. The broader suggestion is that design principles from communication systems may be useful for constructing better sparse factorizations.

This analogy is natural because the information transmission view turns matrix multiplication into a routing problem. Each sparse factor can be seen as a layer in a network, and each nonzero entry as a communication link. From this perspective, a good orthogonal parameterization is not just one with few parameters, but one whose topology efficiently routes information from every input dimension to every output dimension. Computer networking offers a language for thinking about such routing problems, including questions about connectivity, path length, and edge efficiency.

The paper does not develop a full networking theory for orthogonal finetuning, but it clearly suggests that such a theory could guide future structured parameterizations. If one can identify topologies that are better than butterfly graphs for dense communication under orthogonality constraints, then those topologies could yield new finetuning methods with better runtime or even better parameter efficiency. This is part of why the paper’s information transmission framing is so valuable: it opens the door to importing ideas from a much broader field.

# Future Directions in Structured Orthogonal Finetuning

The paper’s discussion implies several future directions for structured orthogonal finetuning. One is to search for alternative factorizations of dense orthogonal matrices that preserve the desirable properties of BOFT while reducing training overhead. Another is to explore whether the trade-off between expressivity and regularity can be tuned more finely through different structured parameterizations. The butterfly structure gives a smoother interpolation than block-diagonal OFT between the identity and full orthogonal transformations, but the paper treats this as part of a broader design space rather than an endpoint.

A second direction is to study the role of inductive bias more carefully. The authors argue that butterfly structure may help generalization because it resembles many classic linear transforms and because sparse factorization imposes a structured hypothesis class. That suggests future work could compare different structured orthogonal families not only by parameter count, but by the kinds of biases they induce in downstream finetuning.

A third direction is to refine the practical mechanics of BOFT itself. The paper introduces multiplicative dropout as a BOFT-specific adaptation of regularization, showing that standard dropout is not directly suited to multiplicative weight updates. This indicates that other training techniques may also need to be redesigned when working with structured orthogonal parameterizations. Future work may therefore involve developing optimization and regularization methods that are specifically tailored to the geometry of orthogonal finetuning.

Finally, the paper’s generalization across vision, language, and diffusion models suggests that structured orthogonal finetuning may be a general adaptation strategy rather than a specialized tool. That raises the possibility of applying similar ideas to other foundation model settings where preserving pretrained knowledge while limiting the number of trainable parameters is important.

# Broader Implications for Parameter-Efficient Adaptation of Foundation Models

BOFT’s broader significance is that it strengthens the case for parameter-efficient finetuning as a general paradigm for adapting foundation models. The paper begins from the practical reality that training large models from scratch is prohibitively expensive, and then argues that downstream adaptation should preserve pretrained knowledge while changing as little as possible. BOFT contributes to this agenda by showing that a carefully designed structured orthogonal update can be both compact and effective.

The paper also highlights that parameter efficiency is not just about reducing the number of trainable weights. It is also about choosing the right structure. LoRA uses low-rank structure, OFT uses block-diagonal orthogonal structure, and BOFT uses butterfly structure. All of these methods constrain the adaptation space in different ways. BOFT’s lesson is that the form of the constraint matters as much as its size: a good structure can preserve expressive power while still enforcing the regularity that supports generalization.

In that sense, the paper points toward a more principled future for adaptation methods. Rather than treating parameter-efficient finetuning as a collection of ad hoc tricks, it proposes that model adaptation can be designed using algebraic structure, graph-theoretic intuition, and knowledge of efficient transforms from other domains. BOFT is one concrete result of that approach, but the deeper message is that the space of efficient finetuning methods is still far from exhausted.
