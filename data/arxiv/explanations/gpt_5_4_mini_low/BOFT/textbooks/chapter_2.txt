\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 2: 2. Background on Parameter-Efficient Finetuning Methods

# Overview of PEFT methods in NLP, vision, and generative modeling

Parameter-efficient finetuning, often abbreviated as PEFT, addresses a basic practical problem that arises once foundation models become very large: how can we adapt a pretrained model to a new task without retraining all of its parameters from scratch? The paper places this problem in the context of modern foundation models such as large language models, vision models, and text-to-image diffusion models. These models are powerful because they exhibit strong generalization, but their size makes full retraining expensive and often impractical. PEFT methods therefore aim to preserve most of the pretrained model while modifying only a small number of parameters.

The paper identifies three broad PEFT strategies. The first is model finetuning, where the architecture remains unchanged and only a subset of the original parameters is updated. The second is adapter tuning, where new trainable modules are inserted into the pretrained network. The third is prompt tuning, where additional trainable tokens are attached to the input. The paper emphasizes model finetuning as the most direct and latency-free of the three, since it does not require extra modules at inference time. This makes it especially appealing when one wants to preserve the pretrained architecture exactly while still adapting it to a downstream task.

Within this landscape, the paper argues that the most relevant PEFT methods for its purposes are reparameterization-based methods. These methods do not simply decide which existing weights to update; instead, they rewrite the way the new weights are represented. That idea is central to both LoRA and OFT, the two main methods discussed before BOFT is introduced. Understanding these methods is essential because BOFT is presented as a new parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning method that builds directly on OFT and is conceptually contrasted with LoRA.

# Reparameterization-based finetuning as a design pattern

Reparameterization-based finetuning is best understood as a way of changing the form of the weight update rather than changing the architecture itself. The pretrained weight matrix is kept as the starting point, but the finetuned weight is expressed through a structured transformation. This design pattern is attractive because it can preserve useful properties of the pretrained model while still allowing task-specific adaptation.

The paper presents reparameterization as especially relevant because structured weight updates can impose useful constraints that preserve pretrained knowledge. In other words, the model is not allowed to move arbitrarily far from the original solution. This is important because foundation models already contain broad semantic or visual knowledge, and finetuning should ideally adjust that knowledge rather than destroy it. Reparameterization methods therefore try to limit the hypothesis space in a principled way.

The paper contrasts two main reparameterization philosophies. One is additive, as in low-rank updates, where a new term is added to the pretrained weight matrix. The other is multiplicative, as in orthogonal finetuning, where the pretrained matrix is transformed by a learned orthogonal matrix. These two viewpoints lead to different notions of efficiency and regularization. Additive methods often reduce parameters by restricting the rank of the update, while multiplicative orthogonal methods preserve geometric relationships in the weights. The paper’s broader argument is that BOFT inherits the benefits of orthogonal finetuning while improving its parameter efficiency through factorization.

# LoRA: additive low-rank updates to pretrained weights

LoRA, or Low-Rank Adaptation, is presented in the paper as the canonical example of additive reparameterization-based PEFT. The core idea is simple: rather than updating the pretrained weight matrix directly, LoRA adds a low-rank matrix product to it. This gives the model a small number of trainable parameters while still allowing a meaningful change to the original weights.

The paper describes LoRA as updating a pretrained weight matrix by adding the product of two low-rank matrices. Because the rank of this update is restricted, LoRA achieves parameter efficiency by forcing the adaptation to lie in a low-dimensional subspace. This is what makes it effective: the model can still adapt to the task, but only through a compact update. The paper notes that LoRA has become very popular because of its simplicity and strong performance, especially in natural language tasks.

Another important aspect of LoRA in the paper is initialization and regularization. The low-rank weights are zero-initialized so that the model begins exactly at the pretrained solution. This mirrors the broader finetuning principle of starting close to the pretrained model. The paper also notes that LoRA naturally supports dropout on the low-rank update, which can help prevent overfitting. This becomes relevant later because BOFT uses a multiplicative update, so conventional dropout does not transfer directly and must be redesigned.

# Dynamic and adaptive low-rank methods

The paper does not treat LoRA as the only low-rank method, but as the starting point for a family of related approaches. One limitation of standard LoRA is that it uses a fixed rank across layers. In practice, different layers may need different amounts of adaptation, so a uniform rank allocation may not be ideal. To address this, several methods dynamically adjust the rank so that the parameter budget is distributed more effectively across layers.

The paper mentions adaptive and dynamic rank methods as follow-ups to LoRA that try to make low-rank adaptation more flexible. The central idea is that some layers may benefit from more expressive updates while others can remain more constrained. By changing the rank during finetuning or across layers, these methods attempt to balance performance with parameter cost more intelligently than a fixed-rank design.

These methods are important in the paper’s comparison because they illustrate a general theme in PEFT: efficiency is not just about minimizing the number of trainable parameters, but about allocating them well. BOFT is introduced in this same spirit. Rather than choosing a low-rank additive update, BOFT seeks a more parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterization. The paper positions BOFT as a different route to compactness, one based on sparse orthogonal factorization rather than low-rank addition.

# Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): multiplicative orthogonal updates

Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, is the key predecessor to BOFT in the paper. OFT modifies a pretrained linear layer by multiplying its weight matrix by a learned orthogonal matrix. Concretely, if the pretrained layer uses a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, OFT replaces it with $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal. This means that the finetuning update is multiplicative rather than additive.

The paper explains the motivation for this design through geometry. Orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles, so if the neurons in a layer are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix, the relational structure among them is preserved. This is important because the pretrained weights encode useful semantic information, and OFT aims to keep that information intact while still allowing adaptation. In the paper’s framing, orthogonal finetuning is a more principled alternative to simply using a small learning rate to keep the model close to its pretrained state.

OFT also includes a mechanism for guaranteeing orthogonality during optimization. The paper states that a Cayley parameterization is used, with $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. This ensures that the learned transformation remains orthogonal throughout finetuning. To reduce the parameter count, OFT uses a block-diagonal structure, in which the orthogonal matrix is broken into smaller orthogonal blocks. The paper notes that this improves efficiency, but it also introduces a fixed sparsity pattern and a blockwise independence assumption.

That blockwise assumption is exactly where the paper sees a limitation. Although the block-diagonal structure reduces parameters, it constrains the form of the orthogonal transformation in a way that may be too rigid. The blocks are transformed independently, and the sparsity pattern is fixed in advance. The paper argues that this can reduce expressiveness and make it difficult to approximate classic linear transforms. BOFT is introduced as an answer to this problem: it preserves the orthogonal finetuning idea but replaces the block-diagonal structure with a more expressive butterfly-based factorization.

# Contrasting low-rank structure and sparse structure as two routes to parameter efficiency

A central comparative idea in the paper is that both low-rank matrices and sparse matrices are major structured matrix families used for parameter efficiency. LoRA uses low-rank structure, while OFT and BOFT use sparse structure. These are different mechanisms for reducing the effective number of trainable parameters, but they share the same goal: make adaptation compact without retraining the full model.

The paper emphasizes that sparsity appears in two different ways. In OFT, sparsity is introduced through a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix. In BOFT, sparsity is introduced through butterfly factorization, where a dense orthogonal matrix is composed from multiple sparse orthogonal matrices. Both approaches limit the number of free parameters, but they do so with different inductive biases. The block-diagonal structure isolates groups of dimensions from one another, while the butterfly structure allows information to mix across dimensions in a more distributed way.

This comparison is important because it highlights why parameter efficiency is not only about the number of parameters, but also about the structure of those parameters. A low-rank update and a sparse orthogonal update can have similar parameter budgets yet behave very differently. The paper’s argument is that the butterfly structure offers a better compromise: it stays parameter-efficient, but it supports denser connectivity and a smoother path toward full orthogonal expressiveness than the block-diagonal design.

# Trade-offs among expressivity, parameter count, regularization, and training stability

The paper repeatedly returns to the idea that good finetuning is a balance among several competing goals. A method should use few trainable parameters, but it should also remain expressive enough to adapt to the downstream task. At the same time, it should regularize the adaptation so the model does not overfit or lose the useful properties of pretraining. Training stability is another important concern, especially when adapting large models.

In the paper’s discussion of OFT and BOFT, orthogonality plays a major role in stability. Because multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, orthogonal finetuning maintains a favorable spectral property of the original model. The paper connects this to known benefits for generalization and stable training. This helps explain why OFT can outperform low-rank methods in some settings despite its larger parameter count.

However, expressivity alone is not enough. The paper explicitly notes that full finetuning has maximal expressiveness but can still yield unsatisfactory performance. This is a key insight: finetuning benefits from constraints. A method that is too flexible may overfit or move too far from the pretrained solution. A method that is too restrictive may fail to adapt well. The paper positions BOFT as a way to enlarge the parameter space while still keeping structural priors that act as regularization.

This is where the contrast between block-diagonal sparsity and butterfly sparsity becomes conceptually important. The block-diagonal structure in OFT is very restrictive and imposes a rigid partition of the dimensions. The butterfly structure is still structured, but it is more connected and can recover many classic linear transforms. The paper therefore treats BOFT as a middle ground: it is more expressive than the block-diagonal OFT, but still sufficiently structured to remain parameter-efficient and to impose a beneficial inductive bias.

Together, these trade-offs form the background needed to understand why BOFT is introduced. The paper’s central claim is not simply that fewer parameters are better, but that the right kind of structure can make a finetuning method both efficient and effective.
