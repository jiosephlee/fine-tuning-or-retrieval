\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 7: 7. Orthogonal Parameterization via Butterfly Factorization

# Definition of the butterfly factor

The technical starting point of BOFT is the idea of a butterfly factor, which is the simplest building block used to assemble a structured orthogonal matrix. In the paper, a butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is defined for a size $k$ that is at least $2$ and a power of $2$. It has a $2 \times 2$ block form, where each block is diagonal:

\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k},
\]

with each $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. This construction is important because it encodes a sparse pattern while still allowing structured mixing between entries. Instead of learning a fully dense $k \times k$ matrix, the method learns only the diagonal entries of these blocks, which drastically reduces the number of free parameters.

The paper’s motivation for introducing this factor is that a dense orthogonal matrix is too expensive to parameterize directly, but a butterfly factor gives a way to build dense behavior from sparse pieces. Each butterfly factor acts like a localized exchange mechanism: information from one part of a vector can be routed to another part through a limited set of trainable connections. That idea becomes the foundation for the full butterfly matrix used in BOFT.

# Recursive construction of butterfly matrices

A single butterfly factor is only the local ingredient. To obtain a full-sized matrix of dimension $d$, the paper defines a butterfly matrix recursively, with $d=2^N$. The butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is built from smaller butterfly matrices and a sequence of structured sparse factors:

\[
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(d/2) & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(d/2)
\end{bmatrix}
=
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d/2)\cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2).
\]

The recursive view is central because it shows how a dense matrix can emerge from repeated combinations of sparse components. At each stage, smaller subproblems are combined into a larger matrix, much like the divide-and-conquer structure of the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform. The paper emphasizes that this recursive organization is not just a computational trick; it is a parameterization strategy that makes dense orthogonal matrices possible without paying the full $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ cost of a dense parameterization.

This recursion also makes the butterfly structure highly regular. Each level of the recursion introduces a new sparse pattern, and the composition of these patterns creates a matrix with dense effective connectivity. In other words, although each factor is sparse, the product has the ability to transmit information across all coordinates.

# Butterfly components as block-diagonal assemblies of butterfly factors

To connect the butterfly factor to the full BOFT parameterization, the paper introduces the notion of a butterfly component. A butterfly component is a block-diagonal matrix formed by repeating butterfly factors along the diagonal:

\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\text{diag}(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\cdots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)),
\]

where each $\bm{B}^F_i(k)$ is a butterfly factor of size $k$. Thus, the full matrix is partitioned into equally sized blocks, and each block is itself a butterfly factor. This makes the component sparse, structured, and modular.

The paper uses this block-diagonal assembly as the bridge between the abstract butterfly factor and the concrete orthogonal parameterization. At the smallest scale, each butterfly component can be viewed as a collection of small local transformations. At larger scales, the different components are stacked together in a recursive fashion, allowing the model to create a much richer transformation than a single block-diagonal matrix could provide.

This distinction matters because BOFT is not merely a single sparse orthogonal matrix. It is a product of such components, each one carrying a carefully designed sparsity structure. The architecture is built so that the sparsity pattern changes across levels, enabling dense communication across the full dimension while keeping the number of trainable parameters small.

# From butterfly matrices to orthogonal butterfly matrices

The paper’s main goal in this section is to turn the butterfly structure into an orthogonal parameterization. The key requirement is that every factor in the product must itself be orthogonal. If this can be done, then their product will also be orthogonal, which preserves the geometric properties that motivated OFT in the first place.

The authors first examine the simplest butterfly component, $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$, whose blocks are $2 \times 2$. This case is convenient because orthogonality can be enforced directly at the block level. Once the smallest component is orthogonal, the remaining butterfly components can be treated as permutations of its non-zero pattern. This means the same orthogonal construction can be reused across the different levels of the butterfly matrix.

The outcome is an efficient orthogonal parameterization built from many small orthogonal pieces. Rather than learning one large orthogonal matrix, BOFT learns a sequence of structured orthogonal components whose product forms the final transformation. This is the core idea that reduces parameter count while preserving orthogonality.

# Ensuring orthogonality of each component

Orthogonality is not automatic in a sparse factorization. The paper is careful to point out that each butterfly component must be explicitly parameterized so that it remains orthogonal during finetuning. This is essential because the central promise of OFT and BOFT is the preservation of the relational structure of neurons, especially their pairwise angles.

For the base case $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$, orthogonality is enforced by parameterizing each $2 \times 2$ block using either a Cayley transform or an equivalent $2$-dimensional rotation. The paper follows the same orthogonality-preserving philosophy used in OFT: the orthogonal matrix is learned through a parameterization that guarantees orthogonality by construction, rather than by adding a penalty or projecting after the fact.

This design is important for training stability. Since the learned matrix stays on the orthogonal manifold, the method preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weights when left-multiplying by the learned orthogonal factor. The paper treats this as a key reason why orthogonal finetuning can be stable and can generalize well.

# Using Cayley transforms or 2D rotations inside small blocks

The paper specifically notes that the orthogonality of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ can be guaranteed using the Cayley transform, just as in the original OFT formulation. In the paper’s notation, the orthogonal matrix is obtained from a skew-symmetric parameter matrix, with the skew-symmetric part initialized at zero so that the resulting transformation begins as the identity.

The appeal of this approach is that it gives a smooth and principled way to parameterize small orthogonal blocks. In the $2 \times 2$ case, the same role can also be played by a standard rotation. Either way, the point is that each small block can be represented with very few effective parameters while staying exactly orthogonal.

This local orthogonal parameterization is the atomic unit from which BOFT is built. Because the larger butterfly components are organized as permutations of these small blocks, once the orthogonality of the base blocks is ensured, the orthogonality of the entire construction can be extended across the full matrix.

# Permutation structure across butterfly components

A crucial observation in the paper is that the non-zero pattern of every butterfly component can be viewed as a permutation of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$. This is what makes the butterfly construction powerful. Rather than inventing a new orthogonal matrix for each level, BOFT reuses the same local orthogonal pattern and rearranges it systematically across layers.

This permutation structure is what allows the butterfly matrix to spread information globally. In the induced graph interpretation used earlier in the paper, each successive component connects different nodes in a way that ensures all input coordinates can eventually influence all output coordinates. The sparse factors are therefore not isolated blocks; they are arranged to create long-range communication through repeated structured mixing.

The paper emphasizes that this is one reason the butterfly structure is preferable to a simple block-diagonal pattern. A block-diagonal matrix isolates groups of coordinates, while a butterfly component permutes interactions across levels. As a result, the butterfly structure supports dense effective connectivity without giving up the sparse parameterization.

# Generalizing to block butterfly components

The paper does not stop at scalar butterfly factors. It generalizes the construction to block butterfly components, denoted $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$, where each entry in the butterfly factor is replaced by a $b \times b$ matrix. This makes the parameterization more flexible and allows BOFT to interpolate between different levels of structural granularity.

In the block version, the smallest nontrivial component is $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, which is now composed of $2b \times 2b$ blocks. The paper explains that this component can again be made orthogonal by parameterizing each $2b \times 2b$ block orthogonally. Once that base case is handled, the higher-level block butterfly components inherit the same sparsity pattern up to a block-wise permutation.

This block generalization is important because it gives BOFT a family of parameterizations rather than a single fixed one. Different choices of the block size $b$ and the number of factors $m$ produce different trade-offs between expressivity and parameter efficiency, all within the same butterfly framework.

# Orthogonality of $2b \times 2b$ blocks

The paper’s block butterfly construction relies on the fact that if the fundamental $2b \times 2b$ blocks are orthogonal, then the larger structured components can be made orthogonal as well. This is the block-level analogue of the $2 \times 2$ case. The authors use this to define a block butterfly component whose non-zero pattern is easy to manage and whose orthogonality is preserved by design.

This step matters because it lets BOFT scale up the same mechanism from scalar entries to larger blocks without losing the benefits of the butterfly design. The block structure gives the method more expressive power than the scalar version, while still retaining the recursive sparsity pattern that keeps parameterization efficient.

The paper frames this as a way to build an orthogonal matrix out of sparse components that are themselves orthogonal. Since the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, the entire construction remains valid as a finetuning transformation.

# Constructing the final orthogonal matrix as a product of orthogonal butterfly components

The final BOFT parameterization is obtained by multiplying a sequence of orthogonal butterfly components. In the paper’s notation, the learned orthogonal matrix is

\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]

with each factor satisfying

\[
\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}=
\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top=\bm{I}_d.
\]

This is the core orthogonal butterfly matrix used in BOFT. It is not a single large unconstrained orthogonal matrix, but a product of structured components whose combined effect can still be dense. The paper’s notation also makes clear how BOFT interpolates between different cases: when $m=1$, it reduces to a block-diagonal OFT-style structure; when the factors are arranged in the full butterfly depth, it can produce a dense orthogonal matrix.

The construction achieves the paper’s main objective in this technical section: a parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterization that preserves the desirable properties of orthogonal finetuning while dramatically reducing the number of trainable parameters. The final matrix is still orthogonal, still compatible with the finetuning framework of OFT, but much more economical because its degrees of freedom are distributed across a sequence of sparse butterfly components rather than concentrated in one dense matrix.
