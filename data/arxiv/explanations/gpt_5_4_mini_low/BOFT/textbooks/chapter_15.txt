\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 15: 15. Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Directions

# Summary of BOFT's Main Conceptual and Empirical Contributions

Orthogonal Butterfly, or BOFT, is presented in the paper as a parameter-efficient way to finetune foundation models by reparameterizing the pretrained weights with structured orthogonal transformations. Its main conceptual contribution is to take the earlier idea of Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, and make it significantly more parameter-efficient by replacing a large dense orthogonal matrix with a product of sparse orthogonal factors arranged in a butterfly structure. This idea is motivated by an information transmission view: instead of thinking only about the algebra of matrix multiplication, the authors interpret the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix as a problem of transmitting information across a graph. Under that perspective, the butterfly topology becomes attractive because it can connect all source and receiver nodes with relatively few edges while still enabling dense connectivity.

BOFT is important not just because it reduces parameter count, but because it retains the core promise of orthogonal finetuning. Like OFT, it modifies pretrained weights multiplicatively using an orthogonal matrix, rather than additively as in low-rank methods such as LoRA. The paper emphasizes that orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles and preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weights, which is part of the reason orthogonal finetuning is expected to be stable and generalize well. BOFT keeps this orthogonal structure while making the parameterization much more compact through butterfly factorization.

Empirically, the paper reports that BOFT works across a broader range of adaptation tasks than the earlier OFT setting for controllable text-to-image generation. The authors apply BOFT to large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models, and they report strong performance relative to state-of-the-art parameter-efficient methods. This breadth matters because it suggests orthogonal finetuning is not limited to one application family. In the paper’s framing, BOFT is therefore not just an optimization trick, but a general finetuning framework.

# Runtime Overhead from Multiplying Multiple Orthogonal Factors During Training

The main limitation acknowledged by the authors is that BOFT introduces some extra training-time computation because the final orthogonal matrix is formed as a product of multiple orthogonal butterfly components. In OFT, the orthogonal matrix is already a learned object, but BOFT factorizes that object into several sparse pieces. This factorization reduces the number of trainable parameters, yet it also means that, during each training iteration, the model must repeatedly compose the factors to obtain the effective orthogonal transformation. The paper explicitly notes that this leads to a slightly larger training runtime overhead than OFT.

This trade-off is central to the paper’s design philosophy. BOFT lowers parameter cost by replacing a single large object with a structured product of smaller ones, but that structural savings does not come for free. The authors frame this as the usual exchange between time and space: fewer parameters are achieved by doing more matrix composition work during training. The limitation is therefore not that BOFT is impractical, but that its efficiency is asymmetric. It is especially efficient in terms of trainable parameters and inference cost, while training itself remains somewhat heavier than a more direct parameterization.

The paper does not present this as a fatal flaw. Instead, it treats the extra runtime as an open engineering issue. Since the multiplication is only needed during training, the authors suggest that a better factorization strategy or a more efficient network topology might reduce this overhead further. This leaves room for future work focused on the computational cost of structured orthogonal parameterizations, not just on their parameter count.

# Why BOFT Adds No Inference Latency After Factor Absorption

A particularly important point in the paper is that BOFT does not impose additional inference latency. This follows from the way the method is used after finetuning. During training, the learned orthogonal factors are maintained as a structured parameterization, but once finetuning is finished, the factors can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix itself. In other words, the learned transformation can be absorbed into the model weights before deployment.

This matters because it preserves one of the practical advantages of model finetuning: at test time, the adapted model behaves like a standard pretrained model with modified weights, not like a system that needs extra adapter modules or prompt tokens. The paper contrasts this with other parameter-efficient approaches where added components may complicate inference. BOFT’s factorization is a training-time representation choice, not a permanent architectural addition.

So although BOFT is more expensive than OFT during training, it retains the inference simplicity of standard finetuning. The learned orthogonal matrices do not need to be applied as separate runtime modules once they are merged into the model. This makes BOFT attractive for applications where deployment efficiency matters as much as adaptation quality.

# Open Question: Can Denser Information Transmission Be Achieved Even More Efficiently?

The paper’s information transmission framework is one of its most interesting conceptual contributions, and it also reveals an open problem. The authors show that the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix can be reinterpreted as transmitting information through a grid-structured graph, where sparse factors correspond to edges between layers. Within this framework, the butterfly topology is a compelling solution because it can achieve dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges.

However, the authors are careful not to claim that butterfly is the best possible topology. They explicitly raise the question of whether more efficient network structures might exist for transmitting information across the graph. In their view, the butterfly network is a strong candidate because it is well studied, expressive, and closely related to fast linear transforms such as the Fourier and Hadamard transforms. But the framework itself is broader than butterfly structure alone. It suggests that one could search for other sparse graph topologies that provide the same dense source-to-sink connectivity with even fewer resources or better training properties.

This is an open problem at the intersection of matrix factorization, graph structure, and model adaptation. The paper does not resolve it, but it identifies it clearly: if the goal is to build dense orthogonal matrices from sparse pieces, then the efficiency of the underlying communication pattern becomes the key design question. BOFT is one solution, not necessarily the final one.

# Potential Inspiration from Computer Networking and Communication Topology Design

To motivate this open direction, the paper points toward computer networking as a source of inspiration. The authors note that network topologies have long been studied for their ability to transmit information efficiently. Butterfly graphs themselves already have a history in this area, and the paper cites work on broadcasting and transmission in such networks. The broader suggestion is that design principles from communication systems may be useful for constructing better sparse factorizations.

This analogy is natural because the information transmission view turns matrix multiplication into a routing problem. Each sparse factor can be seen as a layer in a network, and each nonzero entry as a communication link. From this perspective, a good orthogonal parameterization is not just one with few parameters, but one whose topology efficiently routes information from every input dimension to every output dimension. Computer networking offers a language for thinking about such routing problems, including questions about connectivity, path length, and edge efficiency.

The paper does not develop a full networking theory for orthogonal finetuning, but it clearly suggests that such a theory could guide future structured parameterizations. If one can identify topologies that are better than butterfly graphs for dense communication under orthogonality constraints, then those topologies could yield new finetuning methods with better runtime or even better parameter efficiency. This is part of why the paper’s information transmission framing is so valuable: it opens the door to importing ideas from a much broader field.

# Future Directions in Structured Orthogonal Finetuning

The paper’s discussion implies several future directions for structured orthogonal finetuning. One is to search for alternative factorizations of dense orthogonal matrices that preserve the desirable properties of BOFT while reducing training overhead. Another is to explore whether the trade-off between expressivity and regularity can be tuned more finely through different structured parameterizations. The butterfly structure gives a smoother interpolation than block-diagonal OFT between the identity and full orthogonal transformations, but the paper treats this as part of a broader design space rather than an endpoint.

A second direction is to study the role of inductive bias more carefully. The authors argue that butterfly structure may help generalization because it resembles many classic linear transforms and because sparse factorization imposes a structured hypothesis class. That suggests future work could compare different structured orthogonal families not only by parameter count, but by the kinds of biases they induce in downstream finetuning.

A third direction is to refine the practical mechanics of BOFT itself. The paper introduces multiplicative dropout as a BOFT-specific adaptation of regularization, showing that standard dropout is not directly suited to multiplicative weight updates. This indicates that other training techniques may also need to be redesigned when working with structured orthogonal parameterizations. Future work may therefore involve developing optimization and regularization methods that are specifically tailored to the geometry of orthogonal finetuning.

Finally, the paper’s generalization across vision, language, and diffusion models suggests that structured orthogonal finetuning may be a general adaptation strategy rather than a specialized tool. That raises the possibility of applying similar ideas to other foundation model settings where preserving pretrained knowledge while limiting the number of trainable parameters is important.

# Broader Implications for Parameter-Efficient Adaptation of Foundation Models

BOFT’s broader significance is that it strengthens the case for parameter-efficient finetuning as a general paradigm for adapting foundation models. The paper begins from the practical reality that training large models from scratch is prohibitively expensive, and then argues that downstream adaptation should preserve pretrained knowledge while changing as little as possible. BOFT contributes to this agenda by showing that a carefully designed structured orthogonal update can be both compact and effective.

The paper also highlights that parameter efficiency is not just about reducing the number of trainable weights. It is also about choosing the right structure. LoRA uses low-rank structure, OFT uses block-diagonal orthogonal structure, and BOFT uses butterfly structure. All of these methods constrain the adaptation space in different ways. BOFT’s lesson is that the form of the constraint matters as much as its size: a good structure can preserve expressive power while still enforcing the regularity that supports generalization.

In that sense, the paper points toward a more principled future for adaptation methods. Rather than treating parameter-efficient finetuning as a collection of ad hoc tricks, it proposes that model adaptation can be designed using algebraic structure, graph-theoretic intuition, and knowledge of efficient transforms from other domains. BOFT is one concrete result of that approach, but the deeper message is that the space of efficient finetuning methods is still far from exhausted.
