\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 3: Orthogonal Finetuning: Principles and Limitations

# Preserving pretrained knowledge through constrained finetuning

Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, is motivated by a central problem in adapting foundation models: we want to specialize a pretrained model for a downstream task without destroying the knowledge already stored in its weights. The paper frames this as a need for finetuning methods that keep the pretrained model and the adapted model “similar” in a principled way. In standard finetuning, this is often approximated by using a small learning rate, which encourages the weights to move only a little. OFT takes a more structured approach. Rather than merely hoping that the weights stay close in a Euclidean sense, it preserves a particular geometric property of the weight matrices: the pairwise angles between neurons.

This idea is important because the paper views pretrained representations as carrying semantic knowledge through their relational structure. If the adaptation step changes the geometry too aggressively, then that pretrained structure may be lost. OFT therefore constrains the update so that the transformation applied to a layer is orthogonal. In the paper’s framing, this preserves the relational information encoded in the weight matrix while still allowing the model to adapt to a new task. The result is a finetuning method that aims to be stable and generalizable, rather than simply flexible.

# Neuron geometry and the preservation of pairwise angles

The geometric intuition behind OFT is that neurons in a layer are not just individual parameters; their relative arrangement matters. The paper emphasizes pairwise angles between neurons as the key quantity to preserve. If two neurons have a particular angle in the pretrained model, then an orthogonal transformation keeps that angle unchanged. This matters because orthogonal transformations preserve inner products and therefore preserve the geometry of the space in which the neurons live.

The paper connects this to prior work on hyperspherical energy and orthogonal representations, arguing that preserving angles helps preserve the “semantic knowledge” learned during pretraining. In other words, rather than altering the representation space arbitrarily, OFT changes it in a way that maintains the underlying geometric relationships. This gives a more principled interpretation of finetuning than a simple small-step update: the model is not just moving slightly, it is moving in a structure-preserving way.

# OFT as multiplying pretrained weights by a learnable orthogonal matrix

In the paper’s formulation, OFT adapts a pretrained linear layer by introducing a learnable orthogonal matrix. If the pretrained weight matrix is $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and uses it to transform the weights. This makes the adapted model different from the pretrained one, but only through an orthogonal rotation-like transformation.

This is the fundamental design choice that distinguishes OFT from additive methods such as LoRA. LoRA updates the pretrained weights by adding a low-rank correction. OFT instead updates the weights multiplicatively. The paper presents this as a way to maintain closer structural continuity with the pretrained model, because the transformation is constrained to lie in the orthogonal group. The orthogonality requirement is what makes the method geometrically meaningful and what gives it its stability properties.

# Forward-pass formulation: replacing $W^0$ with $R W^0$

The paper writes the forward pass of a pretrained linear layer as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input and $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the output. Under OFT, this becomes

$$
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
$$

This is a simple but powerful replacement: the pretrained weight matrix is left-multiplied by a learnable orthogonal transformation. The paper’s viewpoint is that the orthogonal matrix acts on the neurons in the same layer, transforming them while preserving geometric relations. Because the architecture of the model is unchanged, this is still model finetuning rather than adapter tuning or prompt tuning.

This formulation is also attractive because it is conceptually clean. The pretrained weights remain the foundation, and adaptation is encoded by a transformation applied to them. The paper treats this as a general framework for orthogonal finetuning, with OFT as the original version and BOFT later introduced as a more parameter-efficient version.

# Identity initialization and its role in safe adaptation

A practical concern in finetuning is how to start the optimization. The paper emphasizes that finetuning methods usually begin with the exact pretrained model so that the adapted model does not deviate too much at the beginning. In OFT, this is done by initializing $\bm{R}$ as the identity matrix.

This identity initialization is important because it means the model initially behaves exactly like the pretrained model. The finetuning process then learns deviations from that starting point, rather than beginning from a random transformation. The paper presents this as analogous to the zero initialization commonly used in LoRA. The intuition is that if adaptation begins from the pretrained solution itself, then optimization is safer and less likely to disrupt useful pretrained behavior early in training.

# Cayley parameterization for enforcing orthogonality

Since $\bm{R}$ must remain orthogonal throughout training, OFT needs a parameterization that guarantees orthogonality rather than merely encouraging it. The paper uses Cayley parameterization for this purpose. Specifically, it writes

$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
$$

where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, meaning $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. This construction ensures that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal as long as the parameterization is valid.

The reason this matters is that optimization should not drift outside the orthogonal group. If $\bm{R}$ were only approximately orthogonal, the geometric guarantees motivating OFT would be weakened. Cayley parameterization gives a concrete way to represent an orthogonal matrix through unconstrained optimization over a skew-symmetric matrix. In the paper, this is part of the practical machinery that makes OFT workable as a finetuning method.

# Block-diagonal OFT for reducing parameter count

A full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix has a large number of degrees of freedom, so OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure to reduce the parameter count. The paper writes the orthogonal matrix as

$$
\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r),
$$

where each block $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is orthogonal and $br = d$. By using smaller blocks, OFT reduces the number of trainable parameters substantially.

This is the main practical compromise in OFT. Instead of learning one large dense orthogonal matrix, the method learns several smaller orthogonal matrices applied to disjoint groups of dimensions. The paper notes that this improves parameter efficiency, but it comes at the cost of a rigid structural assumption. The finer the block partition, the fewer parameters need to be learned, but the more constrained the transformation becomes.

# Why block-diagonal structure is restrictive

The paper is explicit that the block-diagonal structure is not just a technical simplification; it imposes a particular inductive assumption. It assumes that the dimensions of a neuron can be divided into groups based on their indices, and that dimensions in different groups should be transformed separately. The authors argue that this division is not naturally justified. There is no obvious reason why dimensions should be separated according to their index order, yet the block-diagonal structure forces exactly that.

This restriction also affects expressiveness. Because different blocks do not interact, the transformation cannot mix information across the whole layer in the way a dense orthogonal matrix can. The paper uses this as a key criticism of the original OFT design: while it is parameter-efficient, it reduces the flexibility of the transformation and may therefore limit what the adapted model can represent.

# Fixed sparsity patterns and their undesirable inductive biases

Another limitation of block-diagonal OFT is that its sparsity pattern is fixed. The zeros occur in predetermined locations, and the transformation acts independently within each block. The paper argues that this fixed sparsity can introduce undesirable inductive biases because it hard-codes a particular pattern of interaction between dimensions.

This matters because the effective model class is no longer the full orthogonal group but a smaller family of block-structured orthogonal matrices. The paper suggests that such a rigid pattern may reduce expressiveness and, more importantly, makes it unclear how to choose a good sparsity pattern in the first place. The block-diagonal structure saves parameters, but it also imposes a specific organization on the model that may not align with the geometry of the task.

The paper further points out that the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot approximate classic linear transforms in the way more flexible structured matrices can. This comparison is used to motivate the search for a denser but still parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterization.

# Why dense orthogonal transformations are desirable but expensive

The paper argues that dense orthogonal matrices are desirable because they avoid the arbitrary partitioning induced by block-diagonal structure. A dense orthogonal transformation can mix information across all dimensions in a layer, which makes it much more expressive. This is especially appealing if we want a principled finetuning method that preserves geometric structure without imposing unnatural separations between dimensions.

The problem is cost. A $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is too expensive for large models. This is the central tension the paper addresses: how can we obtain the benefits of dense orthogonal transformations while keeping the parameter count low?

This limitation is what motivates the move beyond original OFT. The paper’s answer is to search for a factorized representation that still yields a dense orthogonal matrix but with far fewer trainable parameters. That broader idea leads to the butterfly-based parameterization introduced later in the paper. Here, however, the key point is that original OFT sits between two extremes: it is more principled than unconstrained finetuning, but its block-diagonal approximation is still too restrictive, while a full dense orthogonal matrix is too expensive.
