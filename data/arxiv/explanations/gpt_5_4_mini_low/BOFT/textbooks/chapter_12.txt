\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 12: 12. Inductive Bias and Generalization in Structured Orthogonal Finetuning

#

One of the central claims of the paper is that BOFT is not just a way to save parameters. The butterfly structure is presented as a source of inductive bias, meaning that it changes the kinds of orthogonal transformations the model is naturally inclined to learn. This matters because parameter efficiency by itself does not guarantee good downstream performance. The paper repeatedly emphasizes that finetuning is a trade-off between expressivity and regularity: the method should be flexible enough to adapt the pretrained model, but constrained enough to preserve useful pretrained knowledge and avoid overfitting.

In this setting, structure is not an accidental side effect. It is part of the mechanism. BOFT restricts the finetuning matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ to a structured subset of the orthogonal group, and that restriction narrows the hypothesis class the optimization process can explore. The paper argues that this narrowing can be beneficial, because it creates a more controlled adaptation space than either a fully unconstrained orthogonal matrix or an arbitrary dense update. In other words, the method is not merely trying to be smaller; it is trying to be smaller in a way that keeps the right kinds of transformations available.

#

A useful way to understand the paper’s argument is through the idea of hypothesis class restriction. When a finetuning method allows a very large family of possible transformations, it becomes easier to fit the training data, but also easier to overfit or disturb the pretrained representation too much. The paper uses this idea to interpret BOFT’s structured orthogonal matrices as a smaller hypothesis class inside the larger orthogonal group. This smaller class is not arbitrary. It is designed so that the model still has enough freedom to adapt, while the parameterization itself imposes a regularizing constraint.

The paper contrasts this with full finetuning, which has universal expressivity but often performs poorly in practice because that much freedom can be harmful for generalization. In this sense, BOFT’s structure is intended to provide a better balance. The butterfly factorization is a way of keeping the transformation rich enough to be useful, while still preventing the learned update from becoming too unconstrained. The paper treats this as a general principle: the best finetuning method is not necessarily the one with the largest space of transformations, but the one whose constrained space is well matched to downstream adaptation.

#

The paper places special emphasis on why a structured subset of the orthogonal group may generalize better. Orthogonal finetuning already has a built-in advantage because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles and preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. BOFT inherits these properties, but adds additional structure through butterfly factorization. The paper suggests that this structure can act as an implicit bias toward useful transformations, rather than just arbitrary ones.

This argument is motivated by the observation that butterfly structures appear in many classic fast linear transforms. Because these transforms have been widely used for efficient information mixing, the authors argue that the butterfly pattern is not an unnatural artifact of the parameterization. Instead, it corresponds to a kind of organized connectivity that already appears in established linear operators. That makes the learned orthogonal matrix more interpretable as a structured transformation rather than a random dense mixing. The hope is that this structured organization helps the finetuned model retain generalization ability when adapting to new tasks.

#

A major reason the paper believes butterfly factorization has favorable inductive bias is that it can recover many classic fast transforms. The paper explicitly notes that butterfly structure, together with permutations, can perfectly recover several well-known linear transforms, including the fast Fourier transform, the Hadamard transform, and related fast transform families. This is important because these transforms are examples of highly structured linear operators that are both efficient and expressive. They are not arbitrary sparse matrices; they are carefully organized compositions of simple interactions.

From the perspective of the paper, this recoverability is evidence that butterfly factorization is a principled structure rather than a purely engineering-driven sparsity pattern. If a parameterization can represent classic fast transforms naturally, then it suggests that the parameterization is aligned with useful linear algebraic structure. The paper treats this as part of the reason BOFT can generalize well: it does not merely make the parameter count smaller, but biases learning toward transformations that resemble efficient, well-behaved linear operators.

#

The paper also highlights the recoverability of Fourier, cosine, sine, and Hadamard-like structure as a contrast to the block-diagonal structure used in the original OFT. Butterfly matrices can approximate or recover these transform-like patterns because their connectivity pattern allows information to be mixed across the whole system through multiple sparse stages. This creates a kind of organized global interaction, rather than confining interactions within isolated groups.

That distinction matters. Fourier-like and Hadamard-like transforms are global in character. They move information across the full vector through layered sparse exchanges. The paper uses this fact to argue that butterfly factorization gives BOFT a more meaningful structural prior than simple local block transformations. The learned update is encouraged to behave like a composition of efficient global mixing operations, which the authors associate with better generalization properties.

#

In contrast, block-diagonal OFT lacks these transform-like inductive biases. The original OFT uses orthogonal blocks that act independently on separate groups of dimensions. While this reduces the parameter count, it also hard-codes a strong separation between groups of coordinates. The paper points out that this means the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot recover classic linear transforms in the way butterfly structure can. Its sparsity pattern is too rigid and too local.

This is one reason the paper views block-diagonal sparsity as a weaker inductive bias. It saves parameters, but it does so by partitioning the space into independent parts whose interaction is limited by construction. That makes the transformation less expressive in a structured sense, and it also makes the sparsity pattern less aligned with known efficient transforms. BOFT, by using butterfly factorization, keeps sparsity but arranges it in a way that supports richer global communication.

#

The paper also frames sparse matrix factorization itself as an implicit regularizer. Because BOFT represents a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices, the optimization is not directly choosing an arbitrary dense transformation. Instead, it must assemble the transformation through a constrained sequence of sparse components. This factorization reduces the effective degrees of freedom and imposes a compositional structure on the learned update.

The authors suggest that this can help generalization because the factorization limits the space of solutions the model can choose from. In this sense, sparsity is not only about compression. It also shapes the learning dynamics and the kinds of solutions that are accessible. The paper connects this to the broader idea of implicit inductive bias: a parameterization can steer learning toward certain kinds of functions even without an explicit regularization term. BOFT’s butterfly factorization is presented as such a steering mechanism.

#

The balance between structure and flexibility is the final theme underlying the paper’s generalization argument. The paper repeatedly warns that more expressivity is not automatically better. A method that can represent the entire orthogonal group is extremely flexible, but that flexibility may not be ideal for finetuning. Instead, BOFT is designed to enlarge the finetuning space in a structured way, so that the model has enough freedom to adapt while still benefiting from regularization through architecture.

This is why the paper sees BOFT as a “smooth interpolation” between the identity matrix and the full orthogonal group. At one extreme, the identity preserves the pretrained model exactly. At the other extreme, a fully unconstrained orthogonal matrix allows broad transformation. BOFT sits between these extremes by using butterfly factorization, which gives a structured and expressive family of orthogonal updates. The authors argue that this middle ground is precisely what makes the method effective: it preserves useful pretrained knowledge, supports adaptation, and introduces an inductive bias that can improve downstream generalization.
