\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 9: 9. Practical Training Details for BOFT

# Identity initialization of butterfly components

A central practical design choice in BOFT is to start finetuning from the pretrained model as closely as possible. BOFT does this by initializing all butterfly components to identity matrices. In other words, at the beginning of finetuning, the orthogonal matrix $R(m,b)$ is exactly the identity, so the pretrained model is unchanged. This is important because BOFT is intended as a finetuning method, not as a full replacement of the pretrained representation. If the model begins far away from the pretrained solution, then the adaptation process may destroy useful knowledge learned during pretraining before it has a chance to specialize to the downstream task.

This identity initialization is particularly natural in BOFT because the orthogonal transform is applied multiplicatively to the pretrained weight matrix. The forward pass uses the form $(R(m,b)W^0)^\top x$, so if $R(m,b)=I$, then the model behaves exactly like the original pretrained linear layer. That makes the initialization both simple and stable: the network starts from the pretrained function and learns only deviations from it.

# Why initialization at the pretrained model matters in finetuning

The paper emphasizes that finetuning methods usually aim to remain close to the pretrained solution at the start of adaptation. This is a general principle behind many parameter-efficient methods. In the BOFT setting, starting exactly from the pretrained model helps preserve the semantic knowledge encoded in the original weights while allowing the orthogonal transformation to gradually adapt those weights to the target task.

This is especially relevant because BOFT is designed around orthogonal transformations, which preserve the pairwise angles between neurons and help maintain the geometry of the pretrained representation. If training begins with a large perturbation, then that carefully preserved structure would be immediately disrupted. Identity initialization avoids that problem by ensuring that the first step of adaptation is not a change in the model’s behavior, but merely the beginning of a controlled transformation.

# The role of zero-skew initialization under Cayley parameterization

BOFT uses Cayley parameterization to maintain orthogonality during training. In the paper, the orthogonal matrix is written in terms of a skew-symmetric matrix, and the identity initialization is implemented by initializing that skew-symmetric matrix to zero. Because the Cayley transform maps a zero skew-symmetric matrix to the identity, this gives a convenient way to initialize each orthogonal butterfly component exactly at identity.

This zero-skew initialization is not just a technical detail. It is the mechanism that ensures the orthogonal factors begin from a neutral point. Since the learned matrix is orthogonal throughout training, starting with zero skew-symmetry means the parameters initially encode no rotation or mixing beyond the pretrained layer. The resulting optimization path is therefore anchored at the pretrained model, which is consistent with the finetuning objective.

# Why conventional dropout works naturally for additive methods like LoRA

The paper contrasts BOFT with additive methods such as LoRA. In LoRA, the weight update is added to the pretrained matrix as a low-rank term. Because this update is additive, conventional dropout can be applied naturally to the low-rank branch. Dropping parts of the additive update simply means that some contributions to the weight change are temporarily suppressed during training, while the pretrained weights remain intact.

This fits the standard interpretation of dropout as a regularizer on learned activations or learned updates. Since the pretrained model is not being multiplicatively altered, the dropout mechanism can operate on the adaptation branch without interfering with the base model’s identity. That is why the usual dropout layer is compatible with LoRA’s update structure.

# Why multiplicative updates require a different regularization strategy

BOFT differs from LoRA in a crucial way: its update is multiplicative rather than additive. The pretrained weight matrix is transformed by left-multiplication with an orthogonal matrix, rather than being incremented by a separate low-rank correction. Because of this, the standard dropout mechanism is not directly suitable. Dropping units in a multiplicative transform is not the same as dropping additive residuals, since every part of the transformation contributes to the final structure of the orthogonal matrix.

The paper therefore argues that BOFT needs a dropout strategy designed for its factorized orthogonal parameterization. The goal is still the same as in other regularization schemes: to reduce overfitting and improve robustness. But the mechanism must respect the multiplicative, structured form of the update.

# Multiplicative dropout in BOFT

To address this, BOFT introduces multiplicative dropout. Rather than dropping activations or additive update terms, the method operates directly on the butterfly factors that compose the orthogonal matrix. Because the overall orthogonal transform is built as a product of multiple butterfly components, and each component can be viewed in terms of $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal blocks after suitable permutation, dropout can be applied at the level of these structured components.

The paper describes this procedure as first randomly selecting a fraction $p_1$ of the butterfly components, and then selecting a fraction $p_2$ of the diagonal blocks within each chosen component. The selected blocks are replaced with identity matrices. This preserves the overall multiplicative structure while weakening part of the learned transformation during training.

This is why the dropout is described as multiplicative: instead of removing an additive contribution, it neutralizes parts of the multiplicative chain by turning them into identity transformations. The result is still a valid orthogonal-style update, but one that is partially masked during training.

# Randomly dropping butterfly components and diagonal blocks by replacing them with identity

The mechanics of multiplicative dropout are straightforward once the butterfly parameterization is understood. BOFT’s orthogonal matrix is assembled from several butterfly components. Each component contains structured blocks, and these blocks are the natural units for regularization. During training, BOFT randomly chooses some components and some blocks inside them, and replaces those blocks with identity matrices.

Replacing a block with identity means that, for that part of the transformation, the input passes through unchanged. In effect, the model is forced not to depend too heavily on any single butterfly component or block. Because the dropout is applied stochastically, different subsets of the structure are active across training iterations, which encourages the model to learn transformations that do not rely on one fragile configuration of the orthogonal factors.

# Expected effects on overfitting and robustness

The paper’s motivation for multiplicative dropout is the same as for dropout in general: it is intended to reduce overfitting. By randomly disabling parts of the learned butterfly structure during training, the optimization process is encouraged to distribute information more evenly across the available factors. This can help prevent the model from becoming overly specialized to narrow patterns in the training data.

In addition, because the method perturbs the multiplicative path through the orthogonal factors, it may improve robustness in a way that is compatible with BOFT’s structured finetuning philosophy. The paper frames this as part of the practical design that makes BOFT usable: it is not enough to have a parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterization, but one must also make training stable and resistant to overfitting. Multiplicative dropout is the paper’s solution to that issue.

# Practical integration into pretrained linear layers

In practice, BOFT is inserted into pretrained linear layers by replacing the original weight matrix with its orthogonally transformed version. The layer’s computation changes from using $W^0$ directly to using $(R(m,b)W^0)$, where $R(m,b)$ is the learned product of butterfly components. Because the initialization is identity, the layer initially behaves exactly like the pretrained linear layer, and the adaptation begins with no disruption to the original model.

This makes BOFT easy to integrate into existing pretrained architectures. The orthogonal factors are trained as part of the finetuning process, while the pretrained layer remains the anchor of the computation. After finetuning, the learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the pretrained model, so there is no additional inference latency. Thus, the practical training recipe combines identity initialization, Cayley-based orthogonality maintenance, and multiplicative dropout into a finetuning method that is both structured and deployable.
