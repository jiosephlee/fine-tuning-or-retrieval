\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does left-multiplying a weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix preserve pairwise neuron angles?
Question:
I'm reading the BOFT paper, which builds on Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). The paper says that if a pretrained linear layer has weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ and we finetune it as $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ with an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, then the pairwise angles between neurons are preserved.

I'm confused about what exactly counts as a "neuron" here. Since $\mathbf{W}^0$ is $d \times n$, are the neurons the columns of $\mathbf{W}^0$? If so, why does left multiplication by $\mathbf{R}$ preserve the angles between these columns? Is it simply because inner products and norms are preserved under orthogonal transforms?

I think the claim should be something like
$$
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j \rangle = \langle \mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{w}_j \rangle,
$$
but I want to make sure I am interpreting the setup correctly.
Answer:
Yes — in the paper’s notation, a “neuron” is the column vector of the weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$. They say this explicitly: “a neuron (i.e., column vector of the weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$).” So if $\mathbf{W}^0 = [\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, then the $\mathbf{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are the neurons whose pairwise angles they want to preserve.

Under left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$, each neuron becomes $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i$. The reason angles are preserved is exactly the standard property of orthogonal transforms: they preserve inner products and Euclidean norms. Since the angle $\theta_{ij}$ between two nonzero vectors is defined by
$$
\cos \theta_{ij} = \frac{\langle \mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_j\rangle}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\,\|\mathbf{w}_j\|},
$$
it is enough to check that both numerator and denominator stay the same after applying $\mathbf{R}$. Because $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal, $\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$, so
$$
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j\rangle
= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i)^\top (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j)
= \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j
= \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_j
= \langle \mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_j\rangle.
$$
Likewise,
$$
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i\|^2
= \langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i\rangle
= \langle \mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_i\rangle
= \|\mathbf{w}_i\|^2.
$$
So the cosine of the angle is unchanged, hence the angle itself is unchanged.

So your interpretation is correct: the claim is precisely that the columns of $\mathbf{W}^0$ are transformed by the same orthogonal map $\mathbf{R}$, and orthogonality guarantees pairwise angular preservation. Intuitively, $\mathbf{R}$ can rotate or reflect the whole set of neuron vectors, but it cannot stretch, squash, or skew them relative to each other. That is why OFT/BOFT are presented as preserving the “relational information” in the pretrained layer while still allowing adaptation.

### How is the Cayley parameterization guaranteed to produce an orthogonal matrix in OFT/BOFT?
Question:
The paper uses the Cayley transform
$$
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}
$$
with $\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top$ to ensure that $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal during finetuning.

I don't fully see why skew-symmetry of $\mathbf{Q}$ implies orthogonality of $\mathbf{R}$. Could someone derive
$$
\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}
$$
from this definition?

Also, are there any orthogonal matrices that cannot be represented this way? I vaguely remember that the Cayley transform may miss matrices with eigenvalue $-1$, but I'm not sure whether that matters in practice for BOFT.
Answer:
Yes. The orthogonality proof is a straightforward algebraic consequence of $\mathbf{Q}^\top=-\mathbf{Q}$, together with the fact that the Cayley transform is only defined when $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$ is invertible.

Starting from
$$
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
$$
take the transpose:
$$
\mathbf{R}^\top=\left((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\right)^\top(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^\top
=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}^\top)^{-1}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}^\top).
$$
Using $\mathbf{Q}^\top=-\mathbf{Q}$, this becomes
$$
\mathbf{R}^\top=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}).
$$
Now multiply:
$$
\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}
=
(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}.
$$
Since $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$ and $(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})$ commute with each other because both are polynomials in the same matrix $\mathbf{Q}$, the middle factors cancel:
$$
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}),
$$
so
$$
\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}
=
(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
=
\mathbf{I}.
$$
Thus $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal.

On your second question: yes, the Cayley transform does not represent all orthogonal matrices. As the paper notes, it is used as a convenient way to keep the parameterized matrix orthogonal during training, but it only covers an open subset of the orthogonal group. In particular, matrices with eigenvalue $-1$ are excluded, because the inverse Cayley map
$$
\mathbf{Q}=(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{I})^{-1}
$$
requires $\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{I}$ to be invertible. So if $\mathbf{R}$ has eigenvalue $-1$, this inverse is not defined.

In practice, this limitation is usually not a problem for OFT/BOFT, because the paper initializes at the identity and learns small orthogonal updates by optimization. That means the model stays in the Cayley parameterization’s reachable region, which is sufficient for the intended finetuning regime. Also, BOFT applies Cayley parameterization to the small orthogonal factors, and products of such factors can still yield quite expressive orthogonal transformations even though each factor individually comes from the Cayley chart.

### Why is a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix considered an undesirable inductive bias in OFT?
Question:
The paper argues that the original OFT becomes parameter-efficient by restricting the orthogonal matrix to be block diagonal,
$$
\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1, \dots, \mathbf{R}_r),
$$
where each $\mathbf{R}_i$ is a small orthogonal block. It then says this imposes a questionable assumption that neuron dimensions are split into groups by index and transformed independently.

I'm trying to understand why this is considered especially problematic. Isn't any parameter-efficient structure going to impose some bias anyway? What is special about block-diagonal structure that makes it less desirable than butterfly structure?

More concretely, if the dimensions are permuted before applying the block-diagonal matrix, would that already remove most of the problem, or is the limitation deeper than just the ordering of dimensions?
Answer:
The issue is not just that block-diagonal structure is “a bias” in the abstract; it is that it is a very *hard-coded* and *non-adaptive* bias. In the paper’s own terms, a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix assumes that the dimensions of a neuron are split into groups by index, and that dimensions in different groups should be transformed independently. That is a much stronger assumption than merely encouraging structure.

What makes this undesirable is that it limits the model’s ability to mix information across dimensions. If

$$
\mathbf{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r),
$$

then any output coordinate only depends on input coordinates inside its own block. So the transformation is “local” to each group, and no amount of learning can create direct interaction between different blocks inside that single orthogonal factor. In the paper’s information-transmission view, this means the graph is disconnected across groups: information cannot flow from one block to another. That is why the authors say it cannot construct a dense matrix and cannot recover classic dense linear transforms.

A permutation before applying the block-diagonal matrix does help somewhat, but only cosmetically. If you permute dimensions first, you are just changing which coordinates get grouped together. You are not changing the fundamental constraint that, at that layer, the transformation is still a direct sum of independent subspaces. In other words, a permutation can hide the bias, but it does not remove it: the model still only mixes within fixed-size subsets, just subsets chosen in a different order. The limitation is deeper than indexing; it is the absence of cross-group interaction.

This is exactly where butterfly structure is different. Butterfly factorization uses multiple sparse orthogonal factors with permutations/interleavings across stages, so information can move between groups over successive layers. The paper emphasizes that butterfly matrices can densely connect all input nodes to all output nodes with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters, whereas block-diagonal OFT cannot do that unless the block size is essentially the whole dimension. So the butterfly structure imposes a bias too, but it is a much more flexible one: it preserves parameter efficiency while still allowing global mixing and recovering many standard fast transforms such as the Fourier and Hadamard transforms.

So the short answer is: a block-diagonal matrix is undesirable not because it has bias, but because its bias is too rigid and too local. A permutation changes the assignment of coordinates to blocks, but it does not fundamentally solve the lack of cross-block communication that the paper views as the core limitation.

### In the information transmission view, why does a nonzero entry $R_{ij}$ mean information flows from node $j$ to node $i$?
Question:
The paper interprets factorizing a dense matrix $\mathbf{R}$ as an information transmission problem on a layered graph. It says that if $R_{ij} \neq 0$, then information from source node $j$ can flow to destination node $i$.

I think this comes from viewing matrix multiplication as combining coordinates, but I am struggling to make the graph interpretation precise. If
$$
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m \mathbf{B}_{m-1} \cdots \mathbf{B}_1,
$$
what exactly are the nodes and edges, and how does the existence of a path correspond to a nonzero entry of the product matrix?

Is this statement literally true in general, or only generically? For example, couldn't two different paths produce contributions that cancel out algebraically and still give $R_{ij} = 0$ even though a path exists?
Answer:
In the paper, the graph picture is best understood as a statement about the **support pattern** of the matrices, not as a literal theorem that “a path exists if and only if the entry is nonzero” for every possible choice of numerical values.

If you write
$$
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{B}_m\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf{B}_1,
$$
then the “unrolled” graph has $m+1$ layers of $d$ nodes each. The node $j$ in layer $\ell$ represents the $j$-th coordinate of the intermediate vector after multiplying by $\mathbf{B}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{B}_{\ell-1}$. An entry $(\mathbf{B}_\ell)_{ab}\neq 0$ is drawn as a directed edge from node $b$ in layer $\ell$ to node $a$ in layer $\ell+1$. So the graph is just the matrix multiplication diagram: each sparse factor tells you which coordinates can mix at that stage.

Now expand an entry of the product. For the simplest case $m=2$,
$$
R_{ij}=(\mathbf{B}_2\mathbf{B}_1)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^d (\mathbf{B}_2)_{ik}(\mathbf{B}_1)_{kj}.
$$
Each summand corresponds to a length-2 path $j\to k\to i$. For general $m$, $R_{ij}$ is a sum over all length-$m$ paths from node $j$ in the first layer to node $i$ in the last layer, with each path contributing the product of its edge weights. That is the precise algebraic meaning of “information can flow along a path.”

So the right interpretation is:

- if there is **no path at all** from $j$ to $i$, then $R_{ij}=0$ for purely structural reasons;
- if there is **at least one path**, then $R_{ij}$ is a sum of contributions from those paths, and it may be nonzero or zero depending on the values.

That means your cancellation objection is absolutely valid in general. A path existing is **not sufficient** to guarantee $R_{ij}\neq 0$, because different path contributions can cancel exactly. The paper is aware of this kind of distinction and is using the graph as an **information-transmission intuition for sparsity patterns and connectivity**, not as a universal algebraic equivalence. In other words, the graph tells you what is *possible* structurally; the actual matrix values determine whether the final entry survives cancellation.

So, when the paper says “if $R_{ij}\neq 0$, information from node $j$ can flow to node $i$,” it is using “flow” in the loose sense that there is a nonzero input-output influence in the composed linear map. But the more precise statement is the converse one: a nonzero entry means some combination of paths survives in the sum, while a zero entry may arise either because there is no path or because the path contributions cancel. In practice, for the butterfly-type parameterizations they care about, the goal is to choose sparsity patterns that make dense connectivity structurally possible, not to prove a pathwise equivalence for arbitrary numeric matrices.

### Why are the mandatory $d$ edges in each sparse orthogonal factor counted as 'not trainable'?
Question:
In the paper's information transmission framework, the authors say that for a $d \times d$ orthogonal factor, at least $d$ edges are needed between adjacent levels to make the matrix full rank, and these should not be counted as trainable because if an orthogonal matrix has exactly $d$ nonzero entries then those entries must be $\pm 1$.

I don't quite follow this counting argument. Why does having exactly $d$ nonzero entries force the matrix to be a signed permutation matrix? And how does that justify excluding those edges from the effective parameter count?

Is the idea that the actual degrees of freedom of a sparse orthogonal matrix are much smaller than the number of nonzero entries, because orthogonality creates constraints between them?
Answer:
Yes — that is exactly the right intuition, and the paper is using a very specific fact about orthogonal matrices.

If an orthogonal matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ has only $d$ nonzero entries, then each row and each column must contain exactly one nonzero entry. This is because orthogonality means the rows are orthonormal. In particular, every row must have norm $1$. If a row had either zero nonzeros or more than one nonzero, then with only $d$ total nonzeros spread across $d$ rows, some row would have to be all zeros or some other row would have to have multiple nonzeros. The same applies to columns, since $Q^\top Q = I$.

So the support pattern must be a permutation pattern: one nonzero per row and one nonzero per column. That is already the structure of a permutation matrix, except the nonzero values are not necessarily $1$ yet. Now orthogonality forces the magnitude of each nonzero to be $1$, because each row has exactly one entry and its squared norm must be $1$. Hence every nonzero entry must be either $+1$ or $-1$. Therefore such a matrix is exactly a signed permutation matrix.

That is why the paper says these $d$ edges “should not be counted as trainable.” In the information-transmission view, an edge just means “this entry is structurally present.” But if orthogonality and sparsity together force that entry to be $\pm 1$ and to live in a permutation pattern, then it is not really a free parameter anymore. It is required just to make the factor full rank / orthogonal at all. So those edges are part of the minimal structure, not part of the effective parameter budget.

More generally, your interpretation is correct: orthogonality creates strong dependencies among the nonzero entries, so the number of nonzeros is not the same as the number of degrees of freedom. The paper is explicitly counting only the “free” edges, i.e. those whose values can actually be learned. The mandatory $d$ edges are the baseline needed for a full-rank orthogonal factor, and once you are down to exactly $d$ nonzeros there is no continuous freedom left — only discrete sign choices. That is why they exclude them from the effective trainable count.

### How does the butterfly factorization become dense even though each factor is sparse and block structured?
Question:
The BOFT paper defines a butterfly factor
$$
\mathbf{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2) \\
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}
$$
and then recursively builds a butterfly matrix from products of sparse block-diagonal components.

What I don't understand is how repeated multiplication of these very sparse matrices eventually yields a dense matrix. Is there a simple example for $d=8$ showing how a given input coordinate can influence all output coordinates after $\log d$ butterfly stages?

I can see that each stage mixes pairs or groups of coordinates, but I am having trouble seeing why this specific pattern guarantees full connectivity rather than just a larger sparse pattern.
Answer:
The key point is that the matrix product is dense because sparsity is not preserved under multiplication. An entry of the product is not determined by a single edge; it is a sum over all paths through the intermediate layers. So even if each factor has only $O(d)$ nonzeros, after enough stages the connectivity pattern can “spread” so that every input coordinate has a path to every output coordinate.

This is exactly the graph view used in the paper. Each sparse factor is one layer of a directed graph on $d$ nodes, and multiplying factors means concatenating layers. A nonzero in the final matrix $R_{ij}$ exists whenever there is at least one path from input node $j$ to output node $i$ through the intermediate layers. The butterfly pattern is designed so that after $\log_2 d$ stages, every node can reach every other node.

For $d=8$, the standard butterfly has $3$ stages. Ignoring the actual values and looking only at which coordinates can mix, the stages connect coordinates in increasingly large groups. A typical pattern is:

Stage 1: pairwise mixing within adjacent pairs, such as $(1,2)$, $(3,4)$, $(5,6)$, $(7,8)$.

Stage 2: mixing across pairs inside blocks of size $4$, such as $(1,3)$, $(2,4)$, $(5,7)$, $(6,8)$.

Stage 3: mixing across the two halves of size $4$, such as $(1,5)$, $(2,6)$, $(3,7)$, $(4,8)$.

Now track a single input coordinate, say coordinate $1$. After stage 1, it can influence coordinates $1$ and $2$. After stage 2, those can each influence one coordinate in the first four positions, so coordinate $1$ can now reach all of $\{1,2,3,4\}$. After stage 3, those can reach both halves, so coordinate $1$ can influence all $8$ outputs. The same argument works for any input coordinate by symmetry.

So the butterfly does not become dense because each factor itself becomes dense; rather, the pattern of sparse factors is chosen so that the set of reachable coordinates doubles at each stage. After $\log_2 d$ stages, the reachability set has size $d$, which is why the product can be dense.

A useful intuition is that each stage acts like a controlled “mixing” step that doubles the span of information. This is the same reason the Cooley–Tukey FFT is fast: it reorganizes computation through a butterfly graph so that local pairwise operations eventually produce global coupling. BOFT borrows exactly this idea for orthogonal matrix parameterization.

One small technical caveat: “dense” here means that the factorization pattern allows a dense matrix generically. Of course, if you choose special parameter values, some entries of the product can still cancel to zero. But the butterfly structure itself does not force any such zeros, unlike a block-diagonal factorization, where entire off-block regions can never be reached.

### Why does BOFT($1$, $b$) reduce to block-diagonal OFT, and what exactly do the parameters $m$ and $b$ control?
Question:
The paper says that BOFT($1$, $b$) is the same as the original block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, BOFT($1$, $d$) recovers the full orthogonal OFT, and BOFT($\log \frac{2d}{b}$, $b$) gives a dense orthogonal matrix.

I'm getting lost in the notation here, especially because they define the actual orthogonal matrix using $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$ and then say that "for convenience" BOFT with $\mathbf{R}(m, \frac{b}{2})$ is denoted BOFT($m$, $b$).

Could someone unpack this notation carefully? In particular:

1. What is the difference between the block size in the butterfly components and the $b$ shown in BOFT($m$, $b$)?
2. Why does setting $m=1$ make the whole thing block diagonal?
3. Why is the dense case reached at $m = \log \frac{2d}{b}$ rather than simply $\log d$?
Answer:
The notation is a bit overloaded, but the paper’s intent is actually simple once you separate two different “sizes” that appear in the butterfly construction.

In BOFT, the final orthogonal matrix is written as a product
$$
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
$$
where each factor $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is a block-diagonal matrix built from small butterfly blocks. The key point is that the paper uses $b$ in BOFT$(m,b)$ as the *user-facing block size parameter*, but internally the actual butterfly components are written with block size $\frac{b}{2}$. That is why they say “for convenience, we denote BOFT with $\mathbf{R}(m,\frac{b}{2})$ as BOFT$(m,b)$.” In other words, the symbol $b$ in the BOFT name is not the same as the literal block size of $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b$; it is twice that. This is just a notational shift so that the smallest interesting case is $b=2$, corresponding to $2\times 2$ orthogonal building blocks.

Now to your first question: the block size in the butterfly components is $\frac{b}{2}$, while the $b$ in BOFT$(m,b)$ is the paper’s shorthand for twice that. So BOFT$(m,2)$ means the factors are built from $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{1}_{(d,i)}$, i.e. $1\times 1$ blocks in the internal notation, which is the minimal case. BOFT$(m,4)$ corresponds to internal block size $2$, and so on. The important takeaway is that $b$ controls the size of the local orthogonal mixing inside each butterfly component, while $m$ controls how many such mixing stages you compose.

For the second question, $m=1$ gives block-diagonal OFT because with only one butterfly component there is no “butterfly” inter-block mixing across stages at all. You just have a single structured orthogonal factor, and by the way the paper defines the family, that factor is exactly the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix used in original OFT, with block size $b$. So BOFT$(1,b)$ is just original block-diagonal OFT. Intuitively, the butterfly structure only becomes genuinely more expressive when you stack multiple sparse orthogonal components; one component alone is just a sparse blockwise transformation.

For the dense case, the reason the threshold is $m=\log \frac{2d}{b}$ rather than $\log d$ is that the number of butterfly stages depends on how far the recursive factorization can keep splitting until it reaches the base block size. In the paper, the effective base block size is $\frac{b}{2}$, so the recursion stops when
$$
2^m = \frac{d}{b/2} = \frac{2d}{b}.
$$
Equivalently,
$$
m=\log \frac{2d}{b}.
$$
At that depth, the butterfly factorization has propagated information across the full $d$-dimensional space, giving a dense orthogonal matrix. If you set $b=2$, this becomes $m=\log d$, which is the familiar full butterfly depth. So $\log d$ is only the special case when the internal block size is the minimum one. For larger $b$, you need fewer stages because each stage operates on larger local blocks.

So the short version is: $m$ is the number of butterfly layers, $b$ is the effective block-size parameter in the BOFT naming convention, and dense connectivity happens when the recursive butterfly splitting reaches the base scale, which in the paper’s notation is $m=\log \frac{2d}{b}$.

### Where does the effective parameter count $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ for BOFT come from?
Question:
The paper claims that BOFT($m$, $b$) uses
$$
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm
$$
effective trainable parameters for a linear layer of input dimension $d$.

I tried to derive this but got confused. My rough intuition was that each butterfly stage is composed of many small orthogonal blocks, and each orthogonal block has fewer degrees of freedom than its number of entries. But I cannot recover the exact formula.

Could someone show the counting step by step? For example, how many $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal blocks are there per stage, and how many independent parameters does each contribute under the paper's parameterization?
Answer:
The key is that BOFT does **not** count the raw entries of the block matrices, but the number of **free orthogonal degrees of freedom** in each block.

In the paper, the orthogonal parameterization is built from butterfly components, and each component is a block-permutation of a base component $\tilde{\mathbf B}^b(d,2)$. After a suitable permutation, this base component decomposes into independent orthogonal blocks of size $b\times b$ along the diagonal. So, per stage, you get about $\frac{d}{b}$ independent $b\times b$ orthogonal blocks.

Now recall a standard fact: an orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ has
$$
\frac{b(b-1)}{2}
$$
degrees of freedom, because it lives on the manifold $O(b)$, whose dimension is $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$. This is exactly what the Cayley/skew-symmetric parameterization is exploiting: a skew-symmetric $b\times b$ matrix has $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ independent entries, and those are the effective trainable parameters for one orthogonal block.

So one butterfly stage contributes
$$
\frac{d}{b}\cdot \frac{b(b-1)}{2}
=
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d
$$
effective parameters.

Since $\mathrm{BOFT}(m,b)$ uses $m$ such butterfly stages, the total becomes
$$
m \cdot \frac{1}{2}(b-1)d
=
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm.
$$

If you want to think in terms of the paper’s “$2b\times 2b$ block” phrasing, the important point is that those blocks are still orthogonal blocks with only the orthogonality-compatible degrees of freedom counted; after the blockwise orthogonal parameterization, the number of free parameters per stage reduces to the same $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d$. The raw matrix size is larger, but most entries are not independent once orthogonality is enforced.

So the count is simply:

$$
\text{\# blocks per stage} \times \text{DOF per block} \times \text{\# stages}
=
\frac{d}{b}\cdot \frac{b(b-1)}{2}\cdot m
=
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm.
$$

That is the paper’s effective parameter count.

### Why does left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserve the spectral norm but not necessarily all singular vectors?
Question:
The paper argues that BOFT has better spectral properties than LoRA because if
$$
\mathbf{W}^0 = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top,
$$
then the finetuned weight becomes
$$
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top,
$$
and therefore the spectral norm is unchanged.

I understand that orthogonal left multiplication should preserve singular values, but I want to make sure I see the exact reason. Is it because $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}$ is still orthogonal, so this is another valid SVD with the same diagonal $\mathbf{\Sigma}$?

Also, does this imply that all singular values are preserved, or only the largest one? The paper emphasizes the spectral norm, but it seems like the entire singular value spectrum should remain the same.
Answer:
Yes — for an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$, left-multiplication preserves the entire singular value spectrum, not just the spectral norm.

The cleanest way to see it is exactly through the SVD. If
$$
\mathbf{W}^0=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top,
$$
with $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ orthogonal, then
$$
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top.
$$
Since $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{U}$ are both orthogonal, their product $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}$ is also orthogonal. So this is again an SVD of $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ with exactly the same diagonal matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}$. That means every singular value is unchanged.

An even more invariant way to say this is that singular values are determined by the eigenvalues of
$$
(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)
= (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0
= (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{W}^0.
$$
So $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ and $\mathbf{W}^0$ have the same singular values. This is true for all of them, not only the largest one.

What the paper emphasizes is the spectral norm because that is the quantity most often discussed in optimization and generalization arguments, and preserving it is the headline “spectral property” they want to contrast with LoRA. But mathematically, orthogonal left multiplication preserves the full singular value spectrum.

The subtle point is about singular vectors. The singular values stay the same, but the left singular vectors generally change: $\mathbf{u}_i$ becomes $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}_i$. The right singular vectors $\mathbf{v}_i$ stay the same in this factorization. So if by “preserve singular vectors” you mean the vectors themselves are unchanged, then no. What is preserved is the singular-value structure, while the left singular basis is rotated by $\mathbf{R}$.

So your intuition is right: $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}$ is still orthogonal, and that gives a valid SVD with the same $\mathbf{\Sigma}$. The paper singles out the spectral norm, but the stronger statement is that all singular values are preserved.

### In what sense is BOFT 'more expressive than OFT with the same block size'?
Question:
The paper states a theorem saying that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size, and later says that repeated products of butterfly matrices can even represent the entire orthogonal group.

I'm not sure what "more expressive" means formally here. Does it mean the set of orthogonal matrices representable by OFT is a strict subset of those representable by BOFT when the block size is fixed? If so, is there an intuitive example of a matrix that BOFT can represent but block-diagonal OFT cannot?

I think any block-diagonal OFT matrix cannot mix information across blocks, whereas a butterfly product can, but I'd like to understand whether that is the whole argument or whether there is a deeper approximation-theoretic statement behind the theorem.
Answer:
In the paper, “more expressive” is used in a fairly concrete hypothesis-class sense: for a fixed block size, the set of orthogonal matrices that BOFT can represent is strictly larger than the set that OFT can represent. So yes, the intended meaning is that OFT with that block size is a strict subset of BOFT with the same block size, not just that BOFT is “better at mixing” informally.

The reason is exactly the one you intuited. In original OFT, the orthogonal matrix is block-diagonal, so the transformation is applied independently inside each block. That means information cannot flow across blocks at all, no matter how you choose the parameters. If the block size is $b$, then an OFT matrix has the form $\mathrm{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)$, so entries outside the diagonal blocks are identically zero. Such a matrix can only rotate or reflect within each block; it cannot create any coupling between different blocks.

A butterfly product, on the other hand, is a composition of sparse orthogonal factors whose sparsity pattern permutes and mixes coordinates across levels. Even when each factor is sparse, their product can become dense. That is the key extra expressive power: repeated sparse mixing lets coordinates from one block influence coordinates in another block after several stages. So the theorem is not just saying “BOFT is denser”; it is saying that the family of matrices obtainable by butterfly factorization contains strictly more orthogonal transformations than a single block-diagonal orthogonal factor of the same block size.

An intuitive example is any orthogonal matrix that performs a permutation or rotation mixing coordinates from different OFT blocks. A block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ cannot represent, say, a simple swap between coordinate $1$ and coordinate $b+1$ if those lie in different blocks, because that would require nonzero entries outside the block diagonal. But a butterfly product can realize such cross-block mixing, and in fact the paper points out that the butterfly structure can recover classic fast transforms like the FFT, Hadamard, and related cosine/sine transforms, which are dense and globally mixing. Those are precisely the kinds of matrices the block-diagonal structure cannot recover.

So the deeper statement is both structural and approximation-theoretic. Structurally, the representable set is larger. Approximation-wise, BOFT can approximate or exactly realize many dense orthogonal matrices that OFT cannot even express at all under the same block-size constraint. The theorem in the paper is mainly about expressivity in this representability sense; the later remark about products of butterfly matrices representing the entire orthogonal group is a stronger universality statement obtained by stacking enough butterfly factors.

### Why might a more expressive finetuning family perform worse, even if it can represent the optimum better?
Question:
One point in the BOFT paper surprised me: after arguing that BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT, the authors also say that more expressivity does not always help in finetuning, and mention that full finetuning can perform worse despite being the most expressive.

I understand this loosely as a regularization/generalization trade-off, but I am struggling to connect that idea specifically to orthogonal finetuning. If BOFT can approximate a larger subset of the orthogonal group, why wouldn't optimization just find a good solution inside that set?

Is the claim mainly about overfitting on small downstream datasets, or also about optimization stability? In other words, what is the concrete mechanism by which restricting the update to a structured orthogonal family can outperform a less restricted one?
Answer:
The paper’s point is not simply “a bigger parameterization is worse because it overfits,” although that is part of the story. The more precise claim is that in finetuning, especially with limited downstream data, you usually do not want the largest possible hypothesis class; you want a class that is expressive enough to solve the task but still constrained enough to preserve useful pretrained structure. BOFT is designed to sit in that middle ground.

In the paper’s language, full finetuning is the most expressive possible orthogonal update, but “better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance in finetuning.” The reason is that the pretrained model already contains a lot of reusable structure, and finetuning is supposed to adapt it rather than relearn everything. A very flexible update family can move the weights in many directions that are not actually helpful for the downstream task, especially when the dataset is small or noisy. That is the usual generalization-side explanation: a larger family can fit spurious patterns more easily. BOFT deliberately restricts the update to a structured subset of the orthogonal group, which acts as an inductive bias.

But there is also a stability angle that is specific to orthogonal finetuning. The paper emphasizes that orthogonal updates preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, which is one reason OFT and BOFT are more stable than additive methods like LoRA. So the issue is not just “can the optimizer find a good point,” but also “what kind of optimization landscape and weight dynamics does the parameterization induce?” A structured orthogonal family can make training easier because the update stays close to the pretrained geometry: it preserves angles, preserves the spectral norm, and limits the ways the representation can drift. That tends to help both convergence and generalization.

This is why the paper contrasts BOFT with both block-diagonal OFT and full orthogonal finetuning. BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT, but still not as unconstrained as a full orthogonal matrix. That intermediate level of freedom is exactly the intended benefit: the butterfly structure can represent many useful dense transforms and has an inductive bias similar to classical fast transforms like the Fourier or Hadamard transforms, while still being parameter-efficient. In other words, the restriction is not an arbitrary handicap; it is a way of shrinking the hypothesis class to something that is more compatible with the pretrained model and the downstream data regime.

So the concrete mechanism is a combination of regularization and optimization geometry. The structured orthogonal family reduces degrees of freedom, which lowers the risk of fitting task-irrelevant directions, and it preserves the orthogonal/spectral properties that make finetuning stable. BOFT is meant to find a better expressivity-regularity trade-off, not simply the largest possible set of reachable weights.

### How should I interpret BOFT as learning a bilinear similarity $\mathbf{w}_i^{0\top} \mathbf{R} \mathbf{x}$?
Question:
The paper says that BOFT can be written as learning a bilinear similarity, where the score for neuron $i$ is something like
$$
\mathbf{w}_i^{0\top} \mathbf{R} \mathbf{x}
$$
and that this connects BOFT to distance metric learning and bilinear forms.

I'm not sure how useful this interpretation is supposed to be. Is the point simply that instead of changing each neuron vector directly, BOFT learns a shared transformation of the feature space through a bilinear form?

If so, how is this different in spirit from learning a Mahalanobis-like metric, except with the strong constraint that $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal rather than positive semidefinite? I'm looking for an intuitive explanation of why this viewpoint matters for understanding BOFT.
Answer:
The bilinear form interpretation is mainly a way to see what BOFT is *doing geometrically* to the pretrained layer, not just a different algebraic rewrite.

In the paper, a pretrained linear layer is updated as $(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}$, so the score for neuron $i$ is
$$
\mathbf{w}_i^{0\top}\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{x}
$$
up to the paper’s convention of whether $\mathbf{R}$ is written on the left or absorbed into the transpose. The key point is that the same learned transformation $\mathbf{R}$ is applied across all neurons in that layer. So yes, one intuitive reading is that BOFT does not “move each neuron independently”; it learns a shared change of coordinates in feature space, and then every pretrained neuron is compared to the transformed input through that shared map. That is why the authors connect it to bilinear similarity: the score is linear in $\mathbf{w}_i^0$ and linear in $\mathbf{x}$, with $\mathbf{R}$ mediating the interaction between them.

But the Mahalanobis analogy is only partly right. A Mahalanobis metric typically looks like $x^\top M x$ with $M \succeq 0$, so it defines a symmetric distance geometry. BOFT is not trying to learn a distance on inputs in that sense. It is learning an orthogonal operator $\mathbf{R}$ that preserves angles and spectral norm, and then using it to reorient the pretrained weights. Orthogonality is the central regularizer here: it preserves pairwise angles between neurons, which the paper argues helps retain pretrained structure and stabilize finetuning. Positive semidefiniteness would be the wrong constraint for that goal, because PSD is about distances/inner products being nonnegative and symmetric, whereas orthogonality is about rigid rotations/reflections that do not distort geometry.

So the viewpoint matters because it clarifies the tradeoff BOFT is making. BOFT is not adding many free degrees of freedom to individual weights; it is restricting adaptation to a structured family of global, geometry-preserving transforms. The butterfly factorization then makes that global orthogonal transform parameter-efficient. In other words, the bilinear form is useful because it highlights that BOFT learns a *shared geometric transformation* of the pretrained representation, rather than a full unconstrained metric or a per-neuron rewrite. That is exactly why the paper ties it to generalization: the orthogonal constraint preserves the pretrained model’s relational structure while still allowing a nontrivial reorientation of the feature space.
