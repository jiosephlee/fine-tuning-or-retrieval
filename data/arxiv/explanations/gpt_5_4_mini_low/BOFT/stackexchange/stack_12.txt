\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How should I interpret BOFT as learning a bilinear similarity $\mathbf{w}_i^{0\top} \mathbf{R} \mathbf{x}$?
Question:
The paper says that BOFT can be written as learning a bilinear similarity, where the score for neuron $i$ is something like
$$
\mathbf{w}_i^{0\top} \mathbf{R} \mathbf{x}
$$
and that this connects BOFT to distance metric learning and bilinear forms.

I'm not sure how useful this interpretation is supposed to be. Is the point simply that instead of changing each neuron vector directly, BOFT learns a shared transformation of the feature space through a bilinear form?

If so, how is this different in spirit from learning a Mahalanobis-like metric, except with the strong constraint that $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal rather than positive semidefinite? I'm looking for an intuitive explanation of why this viewpoint matters for understanding BOFT.
Answer:
The bilinear form interpretation is mainly a way to see what BOFT is *doing geometrically* to the pretrained layer, not just a different algebraic rewrite.

In the paper, a pretrained linear layer is updated as $(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}$, so the score for neuron $i$ is
$$
\mathbf{w}_i^{0\top}\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{x}
$$
up to the paper’s convention of whether $\mathbf{R}$ is written on the left or absorbed into the transpose. The key point is that the same learned transformation $\mathbf{R}$ is applied across all neurons in that layer. So yes, one intuitive reading is that BOFT does not “move each neuron independently”; it learns a shared change of coordinates in feature space, and then every pretrained neuron is compared to the transformed input through that shared map. That is why the authors connect it to bilinear similarity: the score is linear in $\mathbf{w}_i^0$ and linear in $\mathbf{x}$, with $\mathbf{R}$ mediating the interaction between them.

But the Mahalanobis analogy is only partly right. A Mahalanobis metric typically looks like $x^\top M x$ with $M \succeq 0$, so it defines a symmetric distance geometry. BOFT is not trying to learn a distance on inputs in that sense. It is learning an orthogonal operator $\mathbf{R}$ that preserves angles and spectral norm, and then using it to reorient the pretrained weights. Orthogonality is the central regularizer here: it preserves pairwise angles between neurons, which the paper argues helps retain pretrained structure and stabilize finetuning. Positive semidefiniteness would be the wrong constraint for that goal, because PSD is about distances/inner products being nonnegative and symmetric, whereas orthogonality is about rigid rotations/reflections that do not distort geometry.

So the viewpoint matters because it clarifies the tradeoff BOFT is making. BOFT is not adding many free degrees of freedom to individual weights; it is restricting adaptation to a structured family of global, geometry-preserving transforms. The butterfly factorization then makes that global orthogonal transform parameter-efficient. In other words, the bilinear form is useful because it highlights that BOFT learns a *shared geometric transformation* of the pretrained representation, rather than a full unconstrained metric or a per-neuron rewrite. That is exactly why the paper ties it to generalization: the orthogonal constraint preserves the pretrained model’s relational structure while still allowing a nontrivial reorientation of the feature space.
