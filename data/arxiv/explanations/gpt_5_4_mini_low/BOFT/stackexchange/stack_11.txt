\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why might a more expressive finetuning family perform worse, even if it can represent the optimum better?
Question:
One point in the BOFT paper surprised me: after arguing that BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT, the authors also say that more expressivity does not always help in finetuning, and mention that full finetuning can perform worse despite being the most expressive.

I understand this loosely as a regularization/generalization trade-off, but I am struggling to connect that idea specifically to orthogonal finetuning. If BOFT can approximate a larger subset of the orthogonal group, why wouldn't optimization just find a good solution inside that set?

Is the claim mainly about overfitting on small downstream datasets, or also about optimization stability? In other words, what is the concrete mechanism by which restricting the update to a structured orthogonal family can outperform a less restricted one?
Answer:
The paper’s point is not simply “a bigger parameterization is worse because it overfits,” although that is part of the story. The more precise claim is that in finetuning, especially with limited downstream data, you usually do not want the largest possible hypothesis class; you want a class that is expressive enough to solve the task but still constrained enough to preserve useful pretrained structure. BOFT is designed to sit in that middle ground.

In the paper’s language, full finetuning is the most expressive possible orthogonal update, but “better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance in finetuning.” The reason is that the pretrained model already contains a lot of reusable structure, and finetuning is supposed to adapt it rather than relearn everything. A very flexible update family can move the weights in many directions that are not actually helpful for the downstream task, especially when the dataset is small or noisy. That is the usual generalization-side explanation: a larger family can fit spurious patterns more easily. BOFT deliberately restricts the update to a structured subset of the orthogonal group, which acts as an inductive bias.

But there is also a stability angle that is specific to orthogonal finetuning. The paper emphasizes that orthogonal updates preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, which is one reason OFT and BOFT are more stable than additive methods like LoRA. So the issue is not just “can the optimizer find a good point,” but also “what kind of optimization landscape and weight dynamics does the parameterization induce?” A structured orthogonal family can make training easier because the update stays close to the pretrained geometry: it preserves angles, preserves the spectral norm, and limits the ways the representation can drift. That tends to help both convergence and generalization.

This is why the paper contrasts BOFT with both block-diagonal OFT and full orthogonal finetuning. BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT, but still not as unconstrained as a full orthogonal matrix. That intermediate level of freedom is exactly the intended benefit: the butterfly structure can represent many useful dense transforms and has an inductive bias similar to classical fast transforms like the Fourier or Hadamard transforms, while still being parameter-efficient. In other words, the restriction is not an arbitrary handicap; it is a way of shrinking the hypothesis class to something that is more compatible with the pretrained model and the downstream data regime.

So the concrete mechanism is a combination of regularization and optimization geometry. The structured orthogonal family reduces degrees of freedom, which lowers the risk of fitting task-irrelevant directions, and it preserves the orthogonal/spectral properties that make finetuning stable. BOFT is meant to find a better expressivity-regularity trade-off, not simply the largest possible set of reachable weights.
