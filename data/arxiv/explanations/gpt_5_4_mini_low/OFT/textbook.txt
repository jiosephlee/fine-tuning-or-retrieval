\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 1: Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

# Overview of modern text-to-image diffusion models and their capabilities

Recent text-to-image diffusion models have made it possible to generate high-fidelity, photorealistic images from natural language prompts. The paper places this progress in the context of models such as GLIDE, Imagen, Stable Diffusion, and DALL-E2, which represent the state of the art in text-guided image synthesis. These systems learn to translate text into images through diffusion-based generative modeling, and in doing so they have become remarkably strong at producing images that are both realistic and semantically aligned with the prompt.

The important point emphasized in the paper is that these models already have strong generative ability before any downstream adaptation takes place. They can synthesize diverse, visually convincing outputs, and their learned representations encode a great deal of semantic structure. This pretrained capability is the foundation that later finetuning methods must preserve. In other words, the challenge is not to build a generator from scratch, but to adapt a powerful pretrained generator to new control objectives without damaging what it already knows.

# Why text prompts alone are often insufficient for precise image control

Although text-to-image diffusion models are impressive, the paper argues that text guidance alone is often not enough for fine-grained control. A text prompt can describe a scene or concept, but many details remain ambiguous. For example, a prompt may be too underspecified to determine the exact appearance of a particular subject, its pose, or the spatial arrangement of objects in the scene. As a result, even highly capable models may generate outputs that are semantically plausible but not sufficiently precise for downstream uses.

This limitation motivates the broader problem addressed in the paper: how to guide these models more effectively. The authors focus on scenarios where additional control is needed beyond what language alone can express. Their central concern is that while the pretrained model can already generate good images, adapting it to respect extra constraints should not come at the cost of losing the model’s original diversity and realism. Thus, the issue is not simply adding control, but adding control while preserving the model’s generative competence.

# Subject-driven generation: generating a specific subject in new contexts from a few reference images

One of the two downstream tasks studied in the paper is subject-driven generation. In this setting, the goal is to generate images of a particular subject, using only a few reference images of that subject together with a text prompt. The text prompt describes the new context, while the reference images provide identity information about the subject. The task is to place the same subject into new scenes or situations without altering its distinctive visual characteristics.

The paper highlights that this task is difficult because it requires the model to preserve subject-specific details while also following the prompt. The model must remember the identity of the subject from a very small number of examples and then reproduce that identity consistently across new generations. This is precisely the kind of adaptation that can easily break a pretrained model if finetuning is not handled carefully. If the update is too aggressive, the model may forget the subject identity or lose the broader semantic structure that makes the original generator effective.

DreamBooth is cited as the representative framework for this task, and the paper adopts its general setting. In this setup, finetuning is used to make the text-to-image model respond to a customized token representing the subject. The paper’s contribution is not to redefine the task, but to propose a different way of finetuning the pretrained model so that the subject can be learned while the pretrained generative behavior remains stable.

# Controllable generation: conditioning on extra signals such as edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks

The second main task is controllable generation, in which the model receives an additional control signal alongside the text prompt. The paper gives examples such as canny edges, segmentation maps, and landmarks. These control signals specify structural or geometric information that should be reflected in the generated image. The text prompt still provides semantic context, but the extra signal acts as a stronger source of guidance for the image layout or shape.

This task is meant to address the same weakness of text-only control from a different angle. Instead of asking the prompt to describe everything, the model is given an explicit visual or spatial constraint. For example, edges may define the rough outline of the scene, segmentation maps may define object regions, and landmarks may define facial geometry. The challenge is to integrate this control information into a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model without making the system unstable or overly specialized.

The paper follows the task settings of ControlNet and T2I-Adapter, which also adapt pretrained diffusion models to accept extra control inputs. What matters in the paper’s framing is that controllable generation is another form of downstream adaptation where the pretrained model’s structure must be preserved even as new conditional behavior is learned. The paper aims to make this adaptation more stable, more parameter-efficient, and more faithful to the original generative prior.

# The core challenge of finetuning without destroying pretrained semantics

At the heart of the paper is a central problem: how to finetune a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model for downstream control without losing the semantic knowledge encoded during pretraining. The authors emphasize that this is not merely a matter of getting the new task to work. A finetuning method must also preserve the model’s high-fidelity generation, diversity, and semantic understanding.

This is a delicate balance. If finetuning is too weak, the model may not adapt enough to the new task. If it is too strong, it can damage the pretrained generator. The paper describes this as a trade-off between flexibility and regularity. Flexibility is needed to learn the new downstream behavior, but regularity is needed to prevent collapse or semantic drift. The paper’s main argument is that successful finetuning should be measured not only by how well it solves the downstream task, but also by how well it maintains the pretrained model’s generative structure.

The paper also argues that existing finetuning methods lack a principled way to quantify how much of the pretrained semantic ability has been preserved. This makes it hard to choose hyperparameters such as the learning rate or the number of training epochs. The paper’s response to this difficulty is to focus on a more structural notion of preservation, one that looks at relationships among neurons rather than only at Euclidean distance between weights.

# Desiderata for finetuning: training efficiency and preservation of generalizable generation

The paper identifies two main desiderata for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models. The first is training efficiency. Since these models are large, it is desirable to adapt them with fewer trainable parameters and fewer training epochs. This matters both practically and computationally, especially when only a small amount of task-specific data is available.

The second desideratum is generalizability preservation. A good finetuning method should maintain the high-fidelity and diverse image generation abilities of the pretrained model. In the paper’s view, this is essential because the model’s pretrained semantics are what make it broadly useful in the first place. A downstream adaptation should refine the model’s behavior without erasing the knowledge that it already contains.

These two goals are not automatically aligned. A method may be efficient but damaging, or conservative but too limited to adapt properly. The paper frames its contribution as a way to reach a better balance. The proposed method is designed to be efficient in parameter usage and optimization, while also protecting the structure that supports good image synthesis.

# Limitations of naive full finetuning and standard small-learning-rate updates

The paper contrasts its approach with standard finetuning, in which model weights are updated using gradient descent with a small learning rate. This conventional strategy implicitly encourages the finetuned model to stay close to the pretrained model in Euclidean distance. The underlying assumption is that a small weight change should correspond to limited loss of pretrained capability.

The paper argues that this assumption is incomplete. Euclidean closeness between weight matrices does not necessarily capture how much semantic structure has been preserved. Two models may be close in Euclidean norm yet differ significantly in the relations among their neurons, and those relations may matter greatly for the model’s behavior. As a result, simply shrinking the learning rate does not provide a principled guarantee that pretrained generation will remain intact.

The paper also notes that full finetuning can be too flexible for these large models. With many parameters being updated directly, the model can easily drift away from the pretrained solution and lose its generative strengths. This is why the paper seeks a more structured approach to adaptation, one that controls how the weights change rather than merely how much they change in Euclidean terms.

# Why parameter-efficient adaptation methods are attractive for diffusion models

Because full finetuning is costly and potentially destructive, the paper discusses parameter-efficient adaptation as an attractive alternative. Methods such as LoRA and adapter-style approaches reduce the number of trainable parameters while still enabling task-specific adaptation. The appeal of these methods is that they are easier to train, cheaper to store, and often safer than unrestricted finetuning.

In the paper’s framing, parameter efficiency is not only about reducing compute. It is also about making finetuning more stable. A smaller, more structured set of trainable components can restrict the model’s movement away from the pretrained solution. This makes the adaptation problem better behaved and can reduce the risk of collapsing the pretrained semantic space.

However, the paper also makes clear that parameter efficiency alone is not enough. A method can be compact and still fail to preserve the right properties of the pretrained model. The paper therefore seeks an adaptation mechanism that is not just small, but structurally aligned with what should be preserved. This is where orthogonal transformations enter the story.

# The paper’s central thesis: preserve semantic structure during finetuning through orthogonal transformations

The central thesis of the paper is that finetuning should preserve the semantic structure of the pretrained model by preserving the pairwise angular relationships among neurons. The authors use hyperspherical energy as the structural quantity that captures these relationships. Their key insight is that if the pairwise similarity structure of neurons is preserved, then the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability is more likely to remain intact.

This leads to Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT. The idea is to finetune a pretrained layer by applying a shared orthogonal transformation to its neurons. Because orthogonal transformations preserve angles, they also preserve the hyperspherical energy of the layer. The paper argues that this property is crucial: rather than merely keeping the finetuned weights close in Euclidean distance, OFT preserves a geometric structure that better reflects the model’s semantic organization.

The paper further proposes a constrained variant, COFT, which explicitly limits how far the orthogonal transformation can move from the identity. This adds another layer of stability by ensuring that the finetuned model remains within a controlled neighborhood of the pretrained one. Together, OFT and COFT embody the paper’s main response to the finetuning problem: adapt the model by changing its coordinates in an orthogonal way, so that semantics are modified enough to support the downstream task but not so much that the pretrained generator is destroyed.

In this way, the paper reframes diffusion-model adaptation as a problem of preserving structure under transformation. The motivation for controlling text-to-image models is therefore not just to make them obey extra instructions, but to do so while respecting the geometric organization that makes pretrained diffusion models powerful in the first place.

Chapter 2: Background on Diffusion Models, Adaptation, and Related Tasks

# Brief review of diffusion and latent diffusion models

Text-to-image diffusion models are a class of generative models that create images by gradually denoising a sample until a coherent image emerges. The paper places its method in the context of recent diffusion-based text-to-image systems, which have become the dominant approach for high-fidelity generation. These models are learned from paired text-image data and use the text prompt as a guide during the generation process.

The paper also refers to the broader diffusion literature, including denoising diffusion models and score-based models, as the foundation behind these systems. The key idea is that the model learns a reverse process that turns noise into an image, and the text condition helps steer that process toward an image matching the prompt. In practice, this gives diffusion models strong flexibility and visual quality, but it does not fully solve the problem of precise control, especially when the prompt alone is ambiguous.

A central distinction in the paper is between pixel-space and latent-space diffusion models. Pixel-space models operate directly on image pixels, while latent-space models operate in a compressed representation. This distinction matters because the paper uses Stable Diffusion as its pretrained backbone, which is a latent diffusion model. The text-to-image adaptation problems studied in the paper are therefore framed around improving control while preserving the pretrained generative abilities of a latent diffusion backbone.

# Stable Diffusion as the pretrained backbone used in the paper

The paper uses Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained text-to-image model in its experiments. Stable Diffusion belongs to the family of latent-space diffusion models, meaning that generation is performed in a latent representation rather than directly in pixel space. This is one reason it is a practical backbone for large-scale text-to-image generation: it combines strong image quality with greater efficiency than operating directly in pixel space.

In the paper’s experimental setup, Stable Diffusion is the model being adapted to downstream tasks such as subject-driven generation and controllable generation. The motivation is not to train a text-to-image system from scratch, but rather to preserve the model’s pretrained semantic and visual knowledge while steering it toward a new capability. That is why the paper treats Stable Diffusion as a foundation model whose generative behavior should remain intact during finetuning.

The paper emphasizes that finetuning should not destroy the pretrained model’s ability to produce high-fidelity and diverse images. This is especially important for Stable Diffusion because it already has strong generative performance, and the goal of adaptation is to extend its usefulness rather than replace its learned image prior. The whole OFT framework is built around this premise.

# Pixel-space versus latent-space text-to-image diffusion models

The paper briefly contrasts pixel-space and latent-space text-to-image diffusion systems. Pixel-space methods, such as GLIDE and Imagen, train diffusion models directly on image pixels. These approaches are conceptually straightforward because the model learns to generate the final image representation directly. However, they can be computationally heavy because pixel-level generation is expensive.

Latent-space models, such as Stable Diffusion and DALL-E 2, instead work in a compressed representation. Stable Diffusion uses VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook for the latent space, while DALL-E 2 uses CLIP to build a joint latent embedding space for text and images. The paper treats these latent-space models as part of the recent advance that has made text-to-image generation more scalable and practical.

This distinction matters for the paper because the methods studied are applied to latent diffusion backbones, specifically Stable Diffusion. The finetuning strategy must therefore work well within the architecture and representation of a latent-space diffusion model. OFT is presented as model-agnostic in principle, but the paper evaluates it within this latent diffusion setting.

# Subject-driven generation methods before OFT

Subject-driven generation is the task of generating images of a specific subject given only a few example images and a text prompt. The paper describes this as one of the key downstream tasks that motivates the need for careful finetuning. The core challenge is to preserve the identity of the subject while allowing the model to place that subject into new contexts described by text.

Before OFT, several methods had been explored for this task. Some approaches use masks or inversion techniques to preserve the subject while editing the surrounding context. Others attempt local or global editing without masks. The paper notes that these methods often struggle to preserve identity-related details well enough. In other words, they can modify the scene, but they may fail to maintain the uniqueness of the subject.

The paper also mentions methods such as Pivotal Tuning, which finetunes a generator around an inverted latent code while regularizing identity preservation, and personalized face-prior approaches that learn from a small set of face images. Another instance-based method can generate variations of an object, but may lose instance-specific details. These methods reflect the same underlying problem: how to adapt a powerful generative model to a specific subject without losing the pretrained model’s broader ability to generate realistic images.

# DreamBooth and its prior-preservation finetuning framework

DreamBooth is the main subject-driven generation baseline discussed in the paper and the framework that OFT adapts. In DreamBooth, the model is finetuned using a customized token that refers to the subject, along with a small number of subject images. The goal is to bind the subject identity to that token so the model can generate the subject in new contexts.

The paper explains that DreamBooth uses a reconstruction loss together with a class-specific prior preservation loss. The prior preservation term is important because it is meant to prevent the model from forgetting the general category knowledge associated with the subject. For example, if the subject is a specific dog, the model should not stop knowing what dogs look like in general while learning the particular dog instance.

Despite this design, DreamBooth is still a form of naive finetuning, and the paper treats it as vulnerable to instability and degradation of the pretrained model’s generative performance. In the paper’s results, DreamBooth can preserve subject identity well in some cases, but it may fail to follow the prompt accurately or eventually produce collapsed images when finetuning continues too long. This is exactly the kind of limitation OFT is designed to address.

# Controllable generation methods before OFT

Controllable generation is the task of conditioning image generation on additional signals beyond text, such as canny edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks. The paper positions this as the second major downstream task motivating its method. The goal is to make the diffusion model respect an explicit control input while still generating realistic images consistent with the text prompt.

Before OFT, controllable generation was already studied in earlier image-to-image and conditional generation work. The paper mentions that image-to-image translation had traditionally been addressed with conditional GANs, and that diffusion models had also been applied to image-to-image translation. More recently, the paper highlights ControlNet and T2I-Adapter as strong methods for adapting pretrained diffusion models to control signals.

The main challenge in controllable generation is similar to subject-driven generation: the model must be adapted without losing the pretrained generative prior. However, here the finetuning must also make the model sensitive to the control input. The paper’s message is that this balance is difficult, and that standard finetuning approaches do not provide a principled way to preserve the original generative structure while adding control.

# ControlNet and T2I-Adapter as strong controllable generation baselines

ControlNet is one of the strongest controllable generation methods discussed in the paper. It adapts a pretrained diffusion model to additional control signals by finetuning and integrating a control branch. The paper treats it as a major baseline because it has strong practical performance and is specifically designed for controlled generation from signals such as edges, segmentation maps, and poses.

T2I-Adapter is another related method that finetunes a pretrained diffusion model to improve controllability. Like ControlNet, it aims to inject control information into the generation process, but the paper positions it as a competing baseline in the same family of controllable diffusion adaptation methods. Both methods represent the broader trend of extending pretrained diffusion models rather than training a new one from scratch.

The paper’s experiments compare OFT to both ControlNet and T2I-Adapter on multiple tasks, including canny edge to image, segmentation map to image, and landmark to face generation. These comparisons are important because they establish the setting in which OFT is evaluated: not against weak baselines, but against some of the strongest existing controllable generation methods.

# Parameter-efficient finetuning in large pretrained models

A major theme of the paper is parameter-efficient finetuning. Since text-to-image diffusion models are large, updating all parameters during downstream adaptation is often expensive and can destabilize the pretrained behavior. The paper therefore frames finetuning as a trade-off between flexibility and stability.

The paper notes that one common strategy is to update neuron weights with a small learning rate. This can reduce the distance from the pretrained model, but the paper argues that Euclidean closeness alone is not enough to guarantee preservation of semantic behavior. Another strategy is to add a small reparameterized component to the model, which can reduce the number of trainable parameters. However, the paper argues that existing strategies still lack a principled way to preserve the pretrained model’s generative structure.

This is where OFT enters the discussion. The paper’s motivation is that finetuning should not only be cheap in parameters and training steps, but should also preserve the semantic and structural information encoded in the pretrained weights. The background on parameter-efficient finetuning is therefore essential for understanding why the paper introduces an orthogonal transformation-based update rather than standard additive finetuning.

# LoRA: low-rank additive updates and their intuition

LoRA is the most relevant parameter-efficient baseline in the paper’s discussion. The paper describes LoRA as introducing a low-rank additive weight update during finetuning. Instead of changing the full weight matrix directly, LoRA learns a small low-rank correction, which reduces the number of trainable parameters.

The intuition behind LoRA is that the adaptation needed for a downstream task may live in a low-dimensional subspace, so it is not necessary to update every parameter. This makes LoRA attractive because it can be efficient while still providing useful adaptation. The paper also notes that LoRA is commonly applied to attention weights in transformer-based models, and this is the comparison point used when OFT is applied to transformer layers.

At the same time, the paper emphasizes that LoRA is still an additive update, and therefore it does not explicitly constrain how the pretrained structure is preserved beyond the low-rank assumption. The paper argues that this makes LoRA less stable in some finetuning settings, especially when strong preservation of the pretrained semantic structure is important. In the experiments, LoRA sometimes fails to preserve subject identity or achieve strong control as effectively as OFT.

# How OFT differs from LoRA, adapters, and full finetuning

OFT differs from LoRA in both mechanism and philosophy. Rather than adding a low-rank correction to the pretrained weights, OFT multiplies the pretrained weight matrix by a learned orthogonal matrix. This means OFT performs a multiplicative transformation that preserves pairwise angular relationships among neurons in the same layer. The paper presents this as a more structural way to adapt a pretrained model while keeping its hyperspherical energy unchanged.

Compared with adapters, OFT is also different in where the adaptation lives. Adapter-style methods insert extra trainable components, whereas OFT modifies the existing weights through an orthogonal transform. The paper’s argument is that this lets OFT adapt the model without introducing additional inference overhead, because the learned transformation can be absorbed into the weight matrix at test time.

Compared with full finetuning, OFT is much more constrained. Full finetuning updates the model parameters directly and thus can easily distort the pretrained representation if the learning rate or training duration is not carefully controlled. The paper argues that OFT offers a better balance between flexibility and regularity by preserving the pairwise geometry of neuron directions. This is the core background idea behind the paper’s method: instead of changing the model arbitrarily, adapt it by rotating or reflecting the pretrained neuron space in a structured way.

Chapter 3: Why Semantics May Live in Angles, Not Magnitudes

# Neuron direction versus neuron magnitude in neural representations

The paper’s central geometric claim is that, for the purpose of preserving and modifying semantics in text-to-image diffusion models, the direction of a neuron vector matters more than its magnitude. In other words, what a neuron “points toward” in representation space can carry the most important information, while its length is comparatively less informative. This idea is not presented as a universal law for all neural networks, but as a working hypothesis supported by the paper’s intuition and experiments.

This distinction matters because finetuning is not simply about making a model produce different outputs. In subject-driven generation and controllable generation, the goal is to alter the model’s behavior in a targeted way without erasing the knowledge acquired during pretraining. If semantic information is mainly encoded in angular relationships among neurons, then changing those angles may be the most direct way to adapt the model while leaving much of the pretrained structure intact. By contrast, changing magnitudes alone would be a weaker or less meaningful way to move the model’s semantics.

The paper argues that this direction-based view is especially relevant when thinking about the preservation of pretrained generative ability. Standard finetuning methods often implicitly assume that staying close in Euclidean distance to the pretrained weights is enough to preserve behavior. The authors argue that this is too coarse, because two models can be close in Euclidean norm while still differing substantially in the relational geometry of their neurons. The paper therefore shifts attention from raw weight difference to angular structure.

# Angular similarity as a semantic signal in representation learning

The paper builds its intuition on the observation that angular similarity often captures semantic similarity well in representation learning. The idea is simple: if two feature vectors point in similar directions, then they are often representing related content. In the context of neural networks, this means that cosine-like relationships between neurons can encode meaningful structure beyond what is visible from magnitudes alone.

This is why the paper introduces hyperspherical energy as a structural measure. Hyperspherical energy summarizes pairwise hyperspherical similarity, such as cosine similarity, across neurons in the same layer. Rather than looking at each neuron independently, it examines the network as a geometric configuration on a unit hypersphere. The more uniform or structured this configuration is, the more it reflects the relational organization of the layer.

The authors argue that a good finetuned model should preserve the pretrained model’s hyperspherical energy. That is, even if the model is adapted to a new task, the pairwise angular relationships among neurons should not be destroyed. This is the key semantic-preservation principle that motivates Orthogonal Finetuning. The intuition is not merely that angles are “important,” but that preserving pairwise angular structure preserves the pretrained model’s semantic organization.

# Hyperspherical embeddings and normalized neurons

To make angular structure precise, the paper considers normalized neurons lying on a unit hypersphere. If a neuron vector $\bm{w}_i$ is normalized to $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$, then its magnitude is removed and only its direction remains. Once all neurons are normalized in this way, the geometry of the layer is described by how these unit vectors relate to one another.

This hyperspherical view is what allows the paper to define hyperspherical energy. For a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}$, the hyperspherical energy is the sum over pairwise distances between normalized neurons, written in the paper as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
This quantity measures the relational arrangement of neurons on the hypersphere. Importantly, because the neurons are normalized, the measure emphasizes angular geometry rather than raw scale.

The paper’s core hypothesis is that this hyperspherical arrangement contains semantic information that should be preserved during finetuning. That is why the authors do not treat finetuning as merely optimizing weights, but as carefully modifying a geometry. Their method seeks transformations that change the model enough to support a downstream task while keeping this hyperspherical structure intact.

# SphereNet and related evidence that direction can encode essential information

The paper cites prior work suggesting that neuron direction can fully or nearly fully capture important information. In particular, it refers to SphereNet, which shows that training a neural network with all neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere can achieve comparable capacity and even better generalizability. The significance of this result, as used in the paper, is that it suggests the direction of neurons can be sufficient for representing useful information.

The paper also points to empirical work on angular feature differences, noting that angular similarity has been found to characterize semantic gaps well. These observations support the broader claim that angular relationships are not just a mathematical convenience, but a meaningful feature of learned representations. In the paper’s framing, this provides a strong rationale for building a finetuning method around angle-preserving transformations.

The authors use these references to justify a key design principle: if semantics are encoded primarily by direction, then a finetuning method should allow directions to change in a controlled way, rather than letting arbitrary weight updates disrupt them. This is exactly what orthogonal transformations accomplish, because they preserve pairwise angles while still changing the coordinate representation.

# The toy autoencoder experiment from the paper

To strengthen the argument that direction matters more than magnitude, the paper includes a toy experiment using a standard convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images. This experiment is meant to be intuitive rather than definitive, but it plays an important role in motivating the main method.

The setup is as follows. During training, the autoencoder is built in the standard way using the inner product between the convolution kernel $\bm{w}$ and the input patch $\bm{x}$. The output of a convolutional filter is the usual inner product, and the model is trained with this standard mechanism. Then, at test time, the authors compare three ways of generating the feature map: the ordinary inner product used during training, magnitude information alone, and angular information alone.

The purpose of this comparison is to ask which part of the neuron representation actually carries the semantic content needed to reconstruct the input image. The experiment is not about text-to-image diffusion directly, but about whether angular structure can retain enough information to reproduce meaningful visual content.

# Inner product, magnitude-only, and angle-only feature map generation

In the toy experiment, the authors specifically compare three feature-map constructions. First is the standard inner product, which is the normal convolutional response. Second is a magnitude-only representation. Third is an angle-only representation, obtained through cosine-style information that isolates direction.

The contrast between these three choices is the heart of the experiment. The magnitude-only version tests whether length alone is enough to recover the image semantics. The angle-only version tests whether direction alone is enough. Since the network was trained using ordinary inner products, the experiment also shows that angular information is not merely a training artifact introduced by a cosine-based objective; rather, it appears after ordinary training as an informative representation that can be extracted and used.

This makes the result more compelling for the paper’s purposes. If a standard network trained with ordinary inner products still stores essential information in angles, then finetuning methods that preserve angular relationships may protect the most important part of the model’s semantics. That is the exact logic the paper uses to motivate Orthogonal Finetuning.

# Empirical finding: angular information nearly preserves image semantics while magnitude does not

The key empirical observation from the toy autoencoder is that angular information can almost perfectly recover the input images, while magnitude information alone contains little useful semantic signal. In the paper’s interpretation, this strongly suggests that the important content is encoded in the directions of the neurons, not their lengths.

This finding is especially important because it gives an intuitive picture of why orthogonal transformations are suitable for finetuning. If the semantics of an image are tied to angular structure, then a finetuning method that preserves angles has a better chance of preserving meaning. Conversely, a method that changes angular relationships too freely may damage the semantics that were learned during pretraining.

The paper uses this result to justify why changing neuron directions is the right lever for adapting diffusion models. Subject-driven generation and controllable generation both require semantic modification. The experiment suggests that if one wants to alter semantics while retaining core visual knowledge, one should intervene in angular geometry rather than indiscriminately perturbing all weights.

# The analogy to Fourier phase and magnitude

The paper further explains its intuition through an analogy with the 2D Fourier transform. In Fourier analysis, an image can be decomposed into magnitude and phase. The paper emphasizes that phase, which corresponds to angular information between input and basis, preserves much of the semantic structure. Indeed, the authors note that an image can often be reconstructed with its phase spectrum even if the magnitude spectrum is replaced by something random, without losing the image’s semantics.

This analogy is not meant to be a literal equivalence between neural representations and Fourier coefficients. Instead, it is used to support the broader conceptual point that angle-like information often carries the content that humans perceive as semantic structure. Just as phase is crucial in Fourier-based image reconstruction, neuron directions may be crucial in neural representations of visual meaning.

The analogy helps explain why orthogonal transformations are natural. An orthogonal transformation changes the coordinate basis while preserving angles. In the paper’s view, this resembles changing how the model expresses its knowledge without destroying the relational structure that encodes semantics.

# Why changing neuron directions is a natural way to change generated image semantics

The paper argues that if semantic information is stored largely in directions, then changing neuron directions is a natural way to alter what the model generates. This is especially relevant for downstream text-to-image tasks. In subject-driven generation, the model must produce the same subject in a new context. In controllable generation, it must obey an additional signal like edges or segmentation while still producing realistic images. Both tasks require semantic adaptation rather than mere low-level adjustment.

Orthogonal Finetuning is designed around this premise. Instead of directly updating weights in an unconstrained manner, OFT learns a layer-shared orthogonal transformation that rotates or reflects the neurons in a controlled way. Because the pairwise angles are preserved, the model can shift its representation while keeping the internal semantic geometry intact. The paper presents this as a way to adjust the model’s canonical coordinate system rather than replace its learned knowledge.

This is why the paper says that OFT can be understood as operating on a smooth hypersphere manifold. The finetuning process is not a free-form perturbation of parameters; it is a structured geometric modification. That geometric restriction is the source of both semantic preservation and stable optimization.

# The risk of unconstrained directional changes during finetuning

Although changing directions is the right kind of operation, the paper also stresses that uncontrolled directional changes are dangerous. If neuron directions are altered too freely, the pretrained semantic structure can be destroyed. This is the main risk that motivates the orthogonality constraint.

The paper makes this point in two ways. First, it observes that simply applying a regularizer to keep hyperspherical energy unchanged does not guarantee that the energy difference will be minimized effectively. Second, it notes that methods with too much flexibility can cause instability, model collapse, or loss of pretrained performance. In other words, direction matters, but direction alone is not enough; the change must be constrained.

That is why orthogonality is central. An orthogonal transformation preserves pairwise hyperspherical similarity exactly, which means it preserves the internal relational structure among neurons. At the same time, it still allows the representation to move enough to support downstream adaptation. The paper’s message is that finetuning should have enough freedom to change semantics, but not so much freedom that it breaks the geometry that made the pretrained model useful in the first place.

In this sense, the paper’s geometric intuition is the foundation of the whole OFT framework. Semantics live in angles, but angles must be changed carefully. Orthogonal transformations provide exactly that kind of controlled change.

Chapter 4: Hyperspherical Energy as a Structural Preservation Principle

# The Need for a Structural Metric Beyond Euclidean Distance to Pretrained Weights

A central problem in finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models is preserving what the pretrained model already knows while adapting it to a new downstream task. The paper argues that simply keeping the finetuned weights close to the pretrained weights in Euclidean distance is not enough to guarantee this preservation. Although a small parameter change is often treated as a sign that the model has not drifted too far, the authors emphasize that Euclidean distance alone does not fully capture whether the semantic structure of the network has been preserved.

This is an important distinction. Two models can differ only slightly in Euclidean norm and yet behave very differently in terms of the images they generate. Conversely, a larger parameter movement may still preserve the relational structure that matters for semantics. The paper therefore motivates a more structural measure, one that does not just ask how far the weights moved, but asks whether the internal relationships among neurons have been maintained. This is the conceptual opening for hyperspherical energy.

# Hyperspherical Similarity and Pairwise Angular Relationships

The paper builds its structural measure from hyperspherical similarity, with cosine similarity as the main example. The key idea is that semantic information is often encoded more strongly in the directions of feature vectors than in their magnitudes. When neurons are normalized onto the unit hypersphere, the geometry becomes angular: two neurons are similar if they point in similar directions, and dissimilar if their directions differ substantially.

This angular view is consistent with the paper’s discussion of empirical work showing that hyperspherical similarity can encode semantic information well. The intuition is that angles between neurons reflect relational information inside a layer. Rather than treating each neuron as an isolated parameter vector, the paper treats a layer as a collection of vectors whose pairwise angles jointly describe the layer’s structure. In this sense, angular relationships become a compact way to represent how the neurons in a layer are organized relative to one another.

# Definition of Hyperspherical Energy for a Layer of Neurons

To formalize this structural view, the paper defines hyperspherical energy for a layer as the sum of pairwise hyperspherical similarities among the normalized neurons in that layer. For a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, each neuron vector is normalized as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy is then defined as

\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]

The paper presents this quantity as a summary of the pairwise relational structure among neurons on the unit hypersphere. Because the normalized vectors lie on the sphere, the energy is controlled by their angular arrangement. The more the neurons cluster or diverge in their spherical configuration, the more the hyperspherical energy changes. Thus, rather than measuring each weight independently, hyperspherical energy measures the geometry of the entire layer.

# Interpreting Hyperspherical Energy as Neuron Uniformity and Relational Structure

The paper emphasizes that hyperspherical energy captures the level of neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere. If neurons are arranged in a way that makes them more uniformly distributed, the hyperspherical energy is lower; if they are more clustered or structured differently, the energy reflects that change. This is why the authors view it as a meaningful descriptor of the internal organization of a layer.

What matters here is that the energy is not merely a scalar statistic with no interpretation. It is a proxy for how the neurons relate to one another. In the paper’s framing, the pretraining stage creates a particular relational structure among neurons, and that structure is tied to the model’s generative capability. Preserving the energy therefore means preserving a key part of that structure. The model is not only about the values of the weights, but about how those weights are arranged relative to each other.

# Why Preserving Pairwise Neuron Relations May Preserve Pretrained Semantics

The paper’s main hypothesis is that preserving pairwise neuron relations helps preserve pretrained semantics. This claim is grounded in the idea that the angles between neurons store important information about what the network has learned. If the relative angular geometry of a layer remains the same, then the pretrained model’s semantic organization is more likely to remain intact, even if the model is being adapted to a downstream task.

This hypothesis is also supported by the paper’s broader intuition that changing neuron directions is the key to semantically modifying generated images. The authors argue that because semantic information is tied to angular relationships, a finetuning method that changes directions in a controlled way can modify behavior without erasing the pretrained model’s knowledge. Hyperspherical energy becomes the structural quantity that allows this idea to be stated precisely. Preserving it means keeping the pairwise angular scaffold of the layer stable.

# Formal Expression of Hyperspherical Energy for Normalized Neurons

The paper uses a layer of normalized neurons to make the structural preservation objective explicit. In a fully connected layer, the original pretrained weights are denoted by $\bm{W}^0$, and the finetuned weights are denoted by $\bm{W}$. The objective is to keep the hyperspherical energy of the finetuned layer as close as possible to that of the pretrained layer.

This leads to the formal objective

\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\|
\]

which the paper writes equivalently as

\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \bigg{\|} \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}-\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i-\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|^{-1}\bigg{\|}.
\]

This formulation makes the preservation goal concrete. The finetuned model is encouraged to match the pretrained model not in raw Euclidean weight space, but in the structural geometry induced by the normalized neurons. The paper treats the minimum value of this objective as zero, which is achieved when the finetuned layer differs from the pretrained one only by a rotation or reflection.

# The Finetuning Objective: Minimizing Hyperspherical Energy Difference from the Pretrained Model

The paper’s finetuning principle is therefore to minimize the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned model and the pretrained model. This is the key structural preservation objective underlying Orthogonal Finetuning. Rather than directly constraining every parameter to stay near its original value, the method constrains the layer’s pairwise spherical geometry to remain unchanged.

This is a more principled target because it directly reflects the structure the paper believes is important for semantic preservation. The objective does not merely penalize movement; it penalizes movement that changes the layer’s internal angular organization. In this way, the finetuning task is reframed as finding a new parameterization that supports the downstream task while keeping the pretrained relational pattern intact.

# Why Regularizing Hyperspherical Energy Directly Is Difficult

The paper notes that a naive way to use hyperspherical energy would be to add a regularizer encouraging the energy to remain unchanged during finetuning. However, this direct regularization approach does not guarantee that the energy difference will actually be well minimized. In other words, simply penalizing energy drift is not enough to ensure that the pretrained structure is preserved in a controlled and reliable way.

This difficulty is one reason the paper moves from a generic regularization idea to an invariance-based design. Instead of trying to force the energy to stay constant through a loss term alone, the authors look for transformations that preserve the structural quantity by construction. That search leads to orthogonal transformations, which have the right invariance property for pairwise hyperspherical similarity.

# Invariance of Pairwise Angular Relationships Under Shared Orthogonal Transformation

The crucial observation in the paper is that pairwise hyperspherical similarity is provably preserved if the same orthogonal transformation is applied to all neurons in a layer. This means that if a layer’s neurons are jointly transformed by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, then their pairwise angles remain unchanged. Since hyperspherical energy is built from those pairwise relationships, the energy is preserved as well.

The paper interprets this as a structural invariance: the layer can be re-expressed in a different coordinate system without changing the internal angular relations among its neurons. In geometric terms, an orthogonal transformation rotates or reflects the entire configuration of neurons while preserving distances and angles on the sphere. Because the pairwise arrangement remains the same, the hyperspherical energy remains the same.

This invariance is the technical foundation of the method. It gives a way to adapt the network while guaranteeing that the specific structure captured by hyperspherical energy is not destroyed.

# How This Invariance Leads to a Principled Finetuning Design

The invariance property of hyperspherical energy motivates Orthogonal Finetuning directly. Instead of finetuning arbitrary weights, the paper proposes to learn a layer-shared orthogonal transformation for the neurons in each layer. In effect, the finetuning method adjusts the canonical coordinate system of the layer while preserving its pairwise angular structure.

This design achieves two goals at once. First, it allows the model to change in a meaningful way for the downstream task, since orthogonal transformations can alter the neuron directions that encode semantics. Second, it guarantees that the hyperspherical energy stays unchanged, preserving the pretrained relational structure that the paper associates with semantic generation ability. The result is a finetuning strategy that is not based on a heuristic penalty, but on a structural invariance built into the parameterization itself.

The paper’s core insight is that semantic preservation is better understood as preserving geometry than preserving raw Euclidean distance. Hyperspherical energy gives a way to measure that geometry, and orthogonal transformations give a way to preserve it exactly while still allowing adaptation. That combination is what makes hyperspherical energy the structural principle behind the method.

Chapter 5: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Core Method

# Conventional finetuning as implicit minimization of deviation from pretrained weights

The paper begins from the standard intuition behind finetuning large pretrained models: if a model already knows how to generate good images, then adapting it to a new task should not move it too far away from its pretrained state. In ordinary gradient-based finetuning, this idea is enforced only indirectly. One uses a small learning rate, and as a result the updated model tends to stay relatively close to the original pretrained model. In the paper’s formulation, this amounts to implicitly trying to minimize the distance between the finetuned weights $\bm{M}$ and the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$, that is, encouraging $\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0\|$ to remain small.

This is a useful heuristic, but the paper emphasizes that it is not a principled guarantee of preserving the model’s generative behavior. A small Euclidean change does not necessarily mean that the internal structure responsible for semantics has been preserved. For large text-to-image diffusion models, this matters a great deal: if finetuning changes the wrong internal relationships, the model can lose its ability to generate realistic and diverse images even if the weight change appears small. The motivation for OFT is therefore to find a more structural way to constrain finetuning, one that better reflects how neural knowledge is stored.

# LoRA as a low-rank additive update constraint

The paper contrasts OFT with LoRA, which is one of the most relevant parameter-efficient finetuning methods. LoRA also tries to keep finetuning lightweight, but it does so by constraining the update to be low-rank. In the paper’s discussion, LoRA adds a low-rank matrix to the pretrained weights, and this low-rank structure limits how the model can change during adaptation. If the pretrained weights are $\bm{M}^0$, then LoRA effectively learns an update $\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0$ whose rank is kept small.

The key difference is conceptual as much as mathematical. LoRA controls the size and structure of the additive change, whereas the paper argues that this does not directly preserve the semantic structure of the pretrained model. A low-rank update can still alter important relationships among neurons in a way that harms the original generative ability. So although LoRA is parameter-efficient and often effective, the paper treats it as lacking a direct mechanism for preserving the internal geometry that matters for text-to-image generation.

# OFT as a multiplicative update that preserves pairwise neuron angles

OFT takes a different route. Instead of learning a small additive change to the weights, it learns a multiplicative orthogonal transformation applied to the pretrained weights. In the simplest fully connected-layer view, the pretrained weight matrix is transformed by a learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, so that the finetuned weights become $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This is a multiplicative update rather than an additive one.

The reason this is important is that orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles between vectors. Since the paper argues that the angular relationships among neurons encode key semantic information, preserving those angles helps preserve the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability. In the language of the paper, OFT aims to keep the hyperspherical energy unchanged. This makes OFT fundamentally different from methods that merely restrict the size of the update: it preserves a geometric property of the neuron set itself.

# Fully connected layer notation and forward pass under OFT

To make the method concrete, the paper first describes a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. Each column $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is treated as a neuron. Given an input vector $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, the standard forward pass is $\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top \bm{x}$. This is the baseline layer computation before finetuning.

Under OFT, the pretrained matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is transformed by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$, producing $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. The forward pass becomes
$$
\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},
$$
with the orthogonality constraint
$$
\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}.
$$
The paper presents this as the basic OFT formulation. It is important that the transformation is applied on the left, because this corresponds to rotating or reflecting the neuron vectors in the layer while preserving their pairwise geometric relations.

# Learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix for each finetuned layer

Rather than learning a separate transformation for each neuron, OFT learns one orthogonal matrix shared across the whole layer. This is why the paper describes OFT as using a “layer-shared orthogonal transformation.” The idea is that a single structured transformation can adapt the entire layer while preserving its relational geometry.

This shared transformation is also what makes OFT efficient and stable. It avoids unconstrained changes to individual neurons, which could easily distort the pretrained structure. Instead, the whole layer is adjusted in a coordinated way. The paper emphasizes that this is not just a parameter-saving trick; it is the mechanism that preserves the semantic structure encoded in the layer.

# Rotation and reflection as semantic-preserving transformations on neuron sets

The paper notes that if $\bm{W}$ and $\bm{W}^0$ differ only by an orthogonal matrix, then they differ by either a rotation or a reflection, depending on the determinant of $\bm{R}$ being $1$ or $-1$. This is a crucial point because it gives an intuitive picture of what OFT is doing. The neurons are not being arbitrarily rearranged; they are being moved in a way that preserves distances and angles on the unit sphere.

The authors use this to justify why orthogonal transformations are appropriate for finetuning. A rotation or reflection changes the orientation of the coordinate system, but not the intrinsic geometry of the vector set. In that sense, OFT changes how the model represents the same underlying semantic structure, rather than destroying that structure and relearning it from scratch.

# Proof intuition: orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles and hyperspherical energy

The paper’s central theoretical claim is that OFT preserves hyperspherical energy. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}$, define normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy is
$$
\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
$$
This quantity summarizes the pairwise relational structure among neurons on the unit hypersphere.

The intuition is straightforward: orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and angles. If $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, then applying $\bm{R}$ to every neuron in the layer preserves the normalized pairwise distances between neurons, and thus preserves the hyperspherical energy. The paper uses this invariance to argue that OFT keeps the internal semantic structure of the pretrained model intact. In contrast to trying to regularize energy directly, OFT uses a transformation class for which this preservation happens by construction.

# Identity initialization so finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained model

A practical issue in finetuning is that the adaptation procedure should begin exactly from the pretrained network, not from a perturbed version of it. OFT handles this by initializing the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. If $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, then the finetuned weights are initially
$$
\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=\bm{W}^0.
$$
So the model starts from the pretrained state and only gradually learns a transformation away from it.

This mirrors the logic of zero initialization in other parameter-efficient methods, but here the identity initialization is tied to the orthogonal structure. It is not merely a convenient starting point; it ensures that the OFT model initially behaves exactly like the pretrained model and then adapts through controlled geometric transformations.

# Why OFT can be interpreted as changing the coordinate system of neurons

The paper explicitly interprets OFT as changing the canonical coordinate system for the neurons in a layer. This interpretation is helpful because it clarifies why orthogonal transformations are natural. If the neurons are viewed as vectors in a coordinate system, then multiplying by an orthogonal matrix changes the basis orientation without changing the geometric relationships among the vectors.

This is why the paper views OFT as semantically meaningful but structurally conservative. It adapts the layer by re-expressing its neurons in a different orthogonal coordinate frame. The model can therefore shift its representation in a way that supports a new downstream task, while still keeping the pairwise angular structure that the paper associates with pretrained knowledge.

# The role of orthogonality as a regularizer balancing flexibility and stability

A major theme of the paper is the trade-off between flexibility and stability. Standard finetuning is flexible, but it can easily destabilize the pretrained model and lead to collapse or loss of semantic fidelity. OFT is designed to sit in between extremes: it is flexible enough to adapt the model to subject-driven generation and controllable generation, but regularized enough to preserve the pretrained structure.

Orthogonality is the key regularizer. By restricting updates to orthogonal transformations, OFT constrains the finetuning process to a family of transformations that cannot arbitrarily distort the geometry of the layer. The paper argues that this regularity is precisely what enables stable convergence and good preservation of generative ability. In this sense, OFT is not just a different parameterization; it is a principled way to control how much the model can move while still allowing it to adapt.

Overall, the core insight of OFT is that the semantic information in a text-to-image diffusion model is closely tied to angular relationships among neurons. By learning layer-shared orthogonal transformations, OFT adapts pretrained weights in a way that preserves these relationships, maintains hyperspherical energy, and balances adaptation with stability.

Chapter 6: Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization

# Why Direct Orthogonalization Methods Such as Gram-Schmidt Are Costly

Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, relies on learning orthogonal transformations of neural weights. The basic requirement is simple: we want the transformation matrix to remain orthogonal during training, so that the pairwise angles between neurons are preserved. In principle, one could try to enforce this by using a standard orthogonalization procedure such as the Gram-Schmidt method. However, the paper emphasizes that this approach is too expensive for practical finetuning of large text-to-image diffusion models.

The issue is not that Gram-Schmidt is mathematically invalid; rather, it is computationally cumbersome when used repeatedly during optimization. Finetuning requires many gradient updates, and if orthogonality must be restored by an expensive procedure at every step, the method becomes impractical. The paper therefore seeks a differentiable and efficient way to parameterize orthogonal matrices directly, instead of projecting onto the orthogonal group using a costly routine.

This motivation is central to the practical design of OFT. The main idea of OFT is conceptually elegant, but to make it usable in large models, the orthogonality constraint must be maintained in a way that is compatible with gradient-based learning. That is the role played by the Cayley parameterization.

# Cayley Parameterization of Orthogonal Matrices

To efficiently represent an orthogonal transformation, the paper adopts Cayley parameterization. This is a way of generating an orthogonal matrix from another matrix that is easier to optimize. Instead of learning an unconstrained square matrix and then orthogonalizing it afterward, OFT learns an intermediate parameter that is mapped into an orthogonal matrix by a fixed formula.

The key construction is that the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is defined as

$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
$$

where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix. This means that the optimization happens over $\bm{Q}$, while $\bm{R}$ is always produced as an orthogonal matrix by the Cayley transform. The paper notes that this is a much more efficient alternative to generic orthogonalization procedures.

The advantage of this parameterization is that it is differentiable. That matters because OFT is trained by gradient descent. Since the orthogonal matrix is expressed as a differentiable function of $\bm{Q}$, standard backpropagation can be used. This makes Cayley parameterization a natural fit for finetuning large diffusion models.

# Skew-Symmetric Matrices and the Construction $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$

The Cayley transform used in OFT requires $\bm{Q}$ to be skew-symmetric, meaning

$$
\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top.
$$

This structural condition is important because it is what enables the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ to be orthogonal. The paper uses this fact to ensure that the learned transformation preserves the hyperspherical structure of the neurons.

The form

$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}
$$

is the heart of the efficient parameterization. Rather than directly learning $\bm{R}$ under an orthogonality constraint, OFT learns $\bm{Q}$ and then constructs $\bm{R}$ through this formula. The matrix inverse appears in the transform, which is why the method is still somewhat more involved than an unconstrained parameterization, but it is far more manageable than repeatedly orthogonalizing by brute force.

The paper also notes that this form gives a useful identity initialization. If $\bm{Q}$ is initialized as the zero matrix, then $\bm{R}$ becomes the identity matrix. This means OFT begins exactly at the pretrained model and then gradually learns a transformation away from it, which is important for stable finetuning.

# Orthogonality Guarantees Under Cayley Parameterization

A major reason for using the Cayley transform is that it guarantees orthogonality by construction. Since the learned matrix $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, the resulting $\bm{R}$ satisfies the orthogonality condition

$$
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}.
$$

This property is not merely a technical detail. It is the core mechanism by which OFT preserves the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model. Because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles, the neuron geometry within each layer is maintained even as the model is adapted to a downstream task.

The paper makes clear that this is what distinguishes OFT from standard finetuning. In ordinary finetuning, the model can drift in ways that damage the pretrained semantic structure. In OFT, the transformation is constrained so that this structural information is not destroyed. The orthogonality guarantee is therefore the mathematical foundation for the method’s preservation behavior.

# Restriction to Determinant $1$ and Why It Is Acceptable in Practice

The paper points out one limitation of the Cayley parameterization: it can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$. In other words, it covers the special orthogonal group rather than the full orthogonal group. This means that transformations corresponding to determinant $-1$ are not represented by the Cayley transform.

Even though this is a restriction, the paper reports that it does not affect performance in practice. The method still works well for the finetuning tasks studied, and the determinant-$1$ limitation is not presented as an obstacle to the empirical success of OFT. In the context of their experiments, the expressiveness of the parameterization remains sufficient.

This is a good example of the paper’s practical perspective. The goal is not to represent every possible orthogonal transformation in the most general abstract sense, but to find a parameterization that is efficient, differentiable, and effective for text-to-image finetuning. The special orthogonal subgroup appears to be enough for that purpose.

# Computational and Memory Costs of Full Orthogonal Matrices

Even with Cayley parameterization, a full orthogonal matrix can be expensive when the layer dimension $d$ is large. A full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix has a large number of free parameters, and learning such a matrix for many layers in a diffusion model would be costly in both computation and memory.

The paper quantifies this cost by noting that an orthogonal matrix of size $d \times d$ has $d(d-1)/2$ parameters, which leads to a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. For large neural layers, this quadratic scaling is a serious issue. It means the number of trainable parameters grows rapidly as the width of the layer increases.

This motivates the paper’s next design choice: if the full orthogonal matrix is too expensive, can we structure it so that it is still orthogonal but uses far fewer parameters? The answer is to impose a block-diagonal form.

# Block-Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices as a Parameter-Efficient Design

To reduce the parameter cost, the paper proposes a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix. Instead of learning one large dense orthogonal matrix, OFT splits it into $r$ smaller orthogonal blocks:

$$
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r),
$$

where each block $\bm{R}_i$ is itself orthogonal. This structure preserves orthogonality while dramatically reducing the number of trainable parameters.

The intuition is that each block only operates on a subset of the neuron dimension. This makes the transformation more parameter-efficient and also introduces a useful regularization effect. The paper frames this as a practical compromise between flexibility and simplicity. The full dense matrix is highly flexible but expensive, while the block-diagonal structure is cheaper and still preserves the essential orthogonal property.

Importantly, block-diagonal structure does not weaken the key guarantee of OFT. Because each block is orthogonal, the whole matrix remains orthogonal. Therefore, hyperspherical energy is still preserved even after the parameter reduction.

# Controlling the Number of Blocks with the Hyperparameter $r$

The number of blocks is controlled by the hyperparameter $r$. When $r=1$, the block-diagonal matrix reduces to the standard full orthogonal matrix. As $r$ increases, the matrix is split into more blocks, and each block becomes smaller. This lowers the number of parameters and makes the method more efficient.

The paper gives the complexity of an $r$-block orthogonal matrix as $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. This is a direct reduction from the full-matrix cost of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. By adjusting $r$, the user can choose a trade-off between expressiveness and efficiency. More blocks mean fewer parameters, while fewer blocks mean a more flexible transformation.

The paper also notes that the block structure can be made even more restrictive by sharing blocks across positions, which further reduces the parameter count. So $r$ is not only a design parameter for efficiency, but also a way to tune how strongly the orthogonal transform is regularized.

# Complexity Reduction from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r)$

The main computational benefit of the block-diagonal design is that it reduces the number of trainable parameters from quadratic in $d$ to quadratic in $d$ divided by the number of blocks. For a full orthogonal matrix, the parameter count is $d(d-1)/2$, which scales like $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. For the $r$-block version, the parameter count becomes $d(d/r - 1)/2$, which scales like $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$.

This reduction is significant because the diffusion model layers OFT targets can be wide. A quadratic parameterization is manageable only if the layers are small, but text-to-image diffusion models are large-scale systems. The block-diagonal strategy therefore makes OFT much more practical.

At the same time, the method remains faithful to the central goal of OFT. The blocks still define an orthogonal transformation, so the layer’s hyperspherical energy is preserved. The paper emphasizes that this is an important advantage over methods that reduce parameter count but do not provide such a structural guarantee.

# Optional Block Sharing and Further Reduction to $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r^2)$

The paper goes one step further by allowing the blocks to be shared. That is, it is possible to set

$$
\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j, \quad \forall i \neq j.
$$

This optional sharing reduces the parameter count even more, down to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. The intuition is that if different blocks use the same transformation, then fewer unique parameters are needed to describe the whole orthogonal matrix.

This is a particularly efficient design, but the paper treats it as an optional enhancement rather than the central mechanism. The essential point is that the orthogonality is still preserved, so the guarantee about hyperspherical energy remains intact. The block-sharing trick changes parameter efficiency, not the fundamental mathematical property of the transform.

This design choice shows how OFT balances multiple goals at once: maintain orthogonality, preserve pretrained structure, and keep the number of trainable parameters small enough to make finetuning practical.

# Comparison of OFT and LoRA Parameter Counts

The paper explicitly compares OFT with LoRA in terms of parameter efficiency. LoRA uses a low-rank update and has trainable parameter count $r'(d+n)$ for a weight matrix of size $d \times n$. OFT, by contrast, uses orthogonal transformations and its parameter count depends on the block structure. The paper argues that when the block size is chosen to scale with dimension, OFT can be very parameter-efficient.

The comparison is not just about absolute count, but also about how the parameters are structured. LoRA reduces parameters by exploiting low rank in an additive update. OFT reduces parameters by exploiting orthogonal structure and block sparsity in a multiplicative transform. These are different forms of compression, and the paper presents OFT as a distinct route to parameter efficiency.

The paper further notes that while LoRA has a low-rank update, OFT can also be interpreted as a kind of low-rank update in some cases, since

$$
\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0.
$$

However, the key difference is that OFT constrains the transform itself to be orthogonal, rather than directly assuming a low-rank additive correction.

# Concrete Example Comparing Trainable Parameter Numbers for OFT and LoRA

To make the comparison concrete, the paper considers a weight matrix of size $128 \times 128$. In this example, LoRA with rank parameter $r' = 8$ has $2{,}048$ trainable parameters. OFT with block parameter $r = 8$ and without block sharing has $960$ trainable parameters.

This example illustrates the practical advantage of the proposed design. Even with a structurally constrained orthogonal transform, OFT can use fewer trainable parameters than LoRA in the setting reported by the paper. The point is not merely that OFT is mathematically elegant, but that it can be more efficient in actual model finetuning.

The example also reinforces a broader lesson from the chapter: efficient parameterization is not only about reducing size, but about doing so while preserving the transformations that matter for semantic fidelity. OFT’s orthogonal parameterization is efficient precisely because it is designed around the geometry of neuron relationships, rather than treating finetuning as an unconstrained weight update.

Chapter 7: Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) and Re-scaled OFT

# Motivation for adding an explicit neighborhood constraint around pretrained weights

The paper’s original Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, is built around a central idea: preserve the hyperspherical energy of each weight matrix while still allowing the model to adapt to a downstream task. This already gives a strong form of structural preservation, because orthogonal transformations keep pairwise angles between neurons unchanged. However, the authors observe that preserving this angular structure alone is not always enough to guarantee the best finetuning stability. In standard finetuning, one often relies on a small learning rate to keep the model close to its pretrained parameters, but that closeness is only implicit. COFT makes this intuition explicit by adding a direct constraint on how far the transformed weights may move from the pretrained ones.

The motivation is therefore not to replace OFT, but to strengthen it. OFT preserves the semantic structure encoded by pairwise neuron angles, while COFT additionally limits the amount of deviation from the pretrained model. In the paper’s view, this is a natural way to balance flexibility and regularity. A model should be able to change enough to support subject-driven or controllable generation, but not so much that it destroys the pretrained generative behavior. COFT is introduced as a more stable version of OFT precisely because it formalizes that trade-off.

# COFT forward pass and the deviation constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$

COFT keeps the same basic transformation as OFT. For a pretrained fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, the finetuned layer is written as $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, so the forward pass becomes
\[
\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top \bm{x}=(\bm{R}\cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
subject to the orthogonality condition
\[
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}
\]
and an additional deviation constraint
\[
\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon.
\]

This second constraint is the defining difference between COFT and standard OFT. The identity matrix $\bm{I}$ corresponds to doing nothing at all to the pretrained model. So bounding $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|$ means restricting the learned orthogonal transform to remain within a controlled neighborhood of the original coordinates. In the paper’s interpretation, this is an explicit version of the small-deviation principle that many finetuning methods only encourage indirectly. Instead of hoping that gradient descent with a small learning rate will stay nearby, COFT enforces proximity as part of the formulation itself.

The result is a method that still changes neuron directions through an orthogonal transformation, but does so in a way that is more conservative than OFT. Because the transformation remains orthogonal, hyperspherical energy is still preserved. Because the deviation from identity is bounded, the model is less likely to drift too far from the pretrained representation. The paper argues that this added restriction improves finetuning stability.

# Interpreting COFT as combining structural preservation with bounded movement

COFT can be understood as having two simultaneous goals. The first is structural preservation: the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ ensures that the pairwise angles among neurons in a layer stay intact, so the hyperspherical energy remains unchanged. The second is bounded movement: the condition $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ ensures that this structure-preserving transformation does not become arbitrarily large.

This interpretation matters because the paper treats finetuning as a trade-off between flexibility and regularity. Purely flexible finetuning can adapt strongly, but it may collapse the pretrained semantics. Purely regularized finetuning can preserve the original model, but it may fail to adapt enough to the downstream task. COFT is designed to occupy the middle ground. It keeps the orthogonal structure that preserves the model’s relational geometry, while also limiting how much the orthogonal map can move away from identity.

The paper notes that this explicit deviation constraint is usually only implicit in existing finetuning methods. In standard approaches, a small learning rate is often used with the hope that the model remains near the pretrained point. COFT makes that hope formal. This is one of the reasons the authors present COFT as more stable than OFT, even though both are rooted in the same orthogonal framework.

# Approximating the Cayley transform near identity using the Neumann series

To enforce orthogonality efficiently, the paper uses Cayley parameterization. In this formulation,
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, meaning $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. This guarantees that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal. The challenge in COFT is that the additional constraint on $\bm{R}$ is not easy to impose directly through this parameterization.

The paper handles this by looking at the Cayley transform near identity. Using the Neumann series, it approximates the inverse term and obtains
\[
\bm{R}\approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2),
\]
assuming the Neumann series converges in operator norm. This approximation shows that when $\bm{Q}$ is small, $\bm{R}$ stays close to $\bm{I}$. That observation is the key to moving the deviation constraint from the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to the skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$.

In other words, the paper uses the local behavior of the Cayley transform to connect the geometric condition on $\bm{R}$ to a simpler condition on its unconstrained parameter. This is an important step because it turns the difficult problem of constraining an orthogonal matrix into a more manageable problem on the underlying skew-symmetric matrix.

# Moving the deviation constraint from $\bm{R}$ to $\bm{Q}$

After the Neumann approximation, the paper argues that the constraint on $\bm{R}$ can be translated into a constraint on $\bm{Q}$. Instead of directly enforcing
\[
\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon,
\]
COFT uses the equivalent-style constraint
\[
\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\leq \epsilon',
\]
where $\bm{0}$ is the zero matrix and $\epsilon'$ is a hyperparameter related to $\epsilon$.

The intuition is straightforward. If $\bm{Q}$ remains small, then the Cayley transform produces an orthogonal matrix that is close to identity. So bounding $\bm{Q}$ near zero becomes a proxy for bounding how far $\bm{R}$ moves away from the pretrained setting. This is not presented as an exact algebraic identity in the paper, but as a practical way to implement the deviation constraint in the parameter space that directly defines the orthogonal transform.

This move is central to COFT because it makes the method implementable. Orthogonality is still guaranteed through Cayley parameterization, but proximity to the pretrained model is controlled in the space of the parameter that drives the transform. That gives COFT its stability-oriented character.

# Projected gradient descent to enforce the constraint on $\bm{Q}$

Once the constraint has been moved to $\bm{Q}$, the paper proposes enforcing it with projected gradient descent. This is a natural choice because the feasible set is now described by a norm bound on $\bm{Q}$. During optimization, the parameters can be updated by gradient descent and then projected back into the allowed neighborhood if necessary.

The paper does not emphasize the projection as a complicated new optimization scheme. Rather, it presents it as a practical way to keep the learned transform within the prescribed radius around the identity. Since the main finetuning variable is the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, projection can be applied directly to that variable to maintain the $\epsilon'$-deviation constraint.

This approach matches the overall philosophy of COFT. The method is not trying to maximize freedom; instead, it is trying to preserve a useful amount of freedom while constraining the transform enough to avoid instability. Projected gradient descent serves that purpose by ensuring the model does not wander too far during training.

# Zero initialization of $\bm{Q}$ for identity initialization of $\bm{R}$

The paper wants COFT to begin finetuning exactly from the pretrained model. To achieve this, it initializes $\bm{Q}$ as the zero matrix. Under the Cayley parameterization, this gives
\[
\bm{R}=\bm{I}
\]
at the start of training. That means the model initially behaves exactly like the pretrained diffusion model, and all adaptation happens gradually through optimization.

This identity initialization is important for two reasons. First, it makes COFT consistent with the broader finetuning principle of starting from the pretrained solution rather than from a random perturbation. Second, it helps stabilize training because the model does not begin from an arbitrary transformed coordinate system. Instead, it starts from the exact pretrained state and learns a constrained orthogonal deviation from there.

The paper presents this as the analog of the identity initialization used in OFT more generally, but COFT sharpens the idea by coupling identity initialization with an explicit neighborhood constraint. The result is a particularly conservative and stable starting point for downstream adaptation.

# How $\epsilon$ controls the flexibility-regularity trade-off

The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ plays a central role in COFT. It determines how much the orthogonal transform is allowed to move away from identity, and therefore how much the finetuned model can deviate from the pretrained one. A larger $\epsilon$ gives the model more flexibility. A smaller $\epsilon$ makes COFT more regularized and more similar to the original pretrained model.

The paper describes this as a direct control over the flexibility-regularity trade-off. When $\epsilon$ is large, COFT behaves more like OFT, because the orthogonal transform has more room to adapt. When $\epsilon$ is small, the finetuned model stays close to the pretrained diffusion model and changes more conservatively. This makes $\epsilon$ an interpretable knob for controlling the degree of adaptation.

The authors also note that COFT becomes easier to tune because this explicit constraint helps stabilize the training dynamics. In their discussion, smaller $\epsilon$ values make the model increasingly resemble the pretrained model, while larger values allow the model to move further toward the behavior of OFT. So $\epsilon$ does not merely regulate optimization; it directly controls the amount of representational change that COFT can express.

# Empirical intuition for why COFT improves stability over OFT

The paper’s empirical intuition is that OFT already preserves a crucial semantic structure, but COFT goes one step further by preventing excessive movement. In their experiments, they observe that COFT yields even better finetuning stability than OFT. This is consistent with the idea that preserving hyperspherical energy alone may still leave enough freedom for the model to drift, whereas explicitly restricting the deviation from the pretrained model helps keep training under control.

The paper illustrates this effect through examples showing how $\epsilon$ affects performance. The main message is that the deviation constraint is not merely a technical detail; it materially shapes how the finetuned model behaves. A stronger constraint keeps the model closer to the pretrained distribution and reduces instability. A looser constraint allows more adaptation, but with behavior that approaches standard OFT.

This is why COFT is framed as a stability-oriented extension of OFT. OFT already offers a principled structural guarantee, but COFT adds the practical safeguard that many downstream tasks need. The empirical claim is not that COFT always maximizes freedom, but that it better balances freedom and preservation, which leads to smoother and more predictable finetuning.

# Re-scaled OFT: adding a learnable positive diagonal scaling matrix

The paper also proposes a separate extension called re-scaled OFT. Unlike COFT, which is designed to make finetuning more conservative, re-scaled OFT is designed to give the model a bit more flexibility. It does this by introducing a learnable diagonal matrix of positive scaling coefficients:
\[
\bm{D}=\text{diag}(s_1,\cdots,s_n),
\]
with all $s_i>0$. The forward pass becomes
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]
where both $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{D}$ are learnable.

This modification retains the orthogonal transformation on the weight matrix while also letting the model rescale neuron magnitudes. In the paper’s terminology, it is a simple way to add magnitude flexibility on top of the angular adaptation already provided by OFT. The authors note that this increases flexibility with only a negligible number of extra parameters.

The paper also includes an erratum noting that the original implementation is correct even though the NeurIPS camera-ready version mistakenly wrote the forward pass in a different order. The intended formulation is the one above, with $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$.

# Why scaling neuron magnitudes does not change hyperspherical energy

The reason re-scaled OFT can add magnitude flexibility without breaking the main geometric idea is that hyperspherical energy is computed after normalization. In the paper’s definition, the normalized neuron is
\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}.
\]
Since normalization removes scale, multiplying neurons by positive factors does not change their directions on the unit sphere. Therefore, the pairwise angular relationships, and thus the hyperspherical energy, remain unchanged.

This is an important point because it means the re-scaling operation does not interfere with the core preservation property of OFT. The orthogonal transform still preserves pairwise angles, and the diagonal scaling only changes the magnitudes before normalization. So the model gains an additional degree of freedom without sacrificing the structural invariant that OFT is built to preserve.

In the logic of the paper, this makes re-scaled OFT a natural extension of the original method. If angular change alone is not enough to capture all useful adaptations, scaling provides an extra handle while leaving hyperspherical energy intact. The method therefore expands flexibility without abandoning the geometric foundation.

# Benefits and limitations of adding magnitude flexibility

The main benefit of re-scaled OFT is that it can improve flexibility while keeping the essential orthogonal structure. The paper says that this variant further improves OFT and that, in practice, it is generally better than the original version. It is especially attractive because it adds only a small number of parameters and does not alter the central preservation mechanism.

At the same time, the paper chooses to focus experimentally on the original OFT rather than the re-scaled version, precisely to demonstrate the effectiveness of orthogonal transformation alone. This tells us something important about the authors’ perspective: the core contribution is the angular, structure-preserving transformation, and re-scaling is an optional enhancement rather than the main idea.

So the limitation is not that re-scaled OFT is ineffective, but that it adds another layer of complexity to the model. The paper presents it as a flexible extension rather than the default method. In the broader story of the chapter, this makes re-scaled OFT the counterpart to COFT: COFT adds explicit regularity and stability, while re-scaled OFT adds modest extra flexibility. Together, they show how the original OFT framework can be adapted in two complementary directions depending on whether one wants more stability or more expressive power.

Chapter 8: Broader Interpretation and Theoretical Insights

# Architecture-Agnostic Nature of OFT

One of the paper’s central claims is that Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, is not tied to a single model family. In principle, it can be applied to any neural network, because its basic operation is simple: it learns an orthogonal transformation on the neurons in a layer. The paper emphasizes this point because the method is intended as a general finetuning strategy for text-to-image diffusion models, not a task-specific trick. The core object OFT manipulates is a weight matrix, and the core constraint is that the learned transformation remains orthogonal. Since many neural architectures are built from weight matrices, the same idea can be transferred across settings.

That said, the paper is careful about how OFT is used in practice. To compare fairly against LoRA, the experiments only apply OFT to the same kinds of layers where LoRA is typically used. This matters because otherwise one method might appear stronger simply because it is modifying more of the network. The paper’s comparison is therefore designed to isolate the difference between the adaptation mechanisms themselves: OFT’s orthogonal multiplicative update versus LoRA’s low-rank additive update.

# Applying OFT to Transformer Attention Layers for Fair Comparison with LoRA

For Transformer-based models, the paper notes that LoRA is usually applied to the attention weights. Because of that, the authors restrict OFT to finetune attention weights as well when they want a direct comparison. This is an important experimental design choice. If OFT were applied more broadly than LoRA, the comparison would not be clean, since the methods would not be modifying the same parts of the model.

This also shows how OFT fits naturally into Transformer architectures. Attention layers are just weight matrices, so they can be transformed with the same orthogonal parameterization described in the paper. The main point is not that OFT depends on attention specifically, but that attention is a standard and meaningful place to test the method in a fair way against existing parameter-efficient finetuning methods.

# Applying OFT to Convolutional Layers and Channel-Wise Interpretations

The paper also argues that OFT is well suited for convolutional layers. This is significant because it suggests that the method is not restricted to fully connected layers or attention layers. Convolution layers have a structured weight tensor, but the paper interprets them in a way that still supports orthogonal finetuning. The block-diagonal structure used in OFT becomes especially meaningful here, because it can be aligned with the channel structure of convolutions.

The paper does not present OFT on convolutions as a separate, different method. Rather, it presents the same orthogonal idea in a form that respects the organization of convolutional parameters. This reinforces the broader message that OFT is architecture-agnostic: the method is defined by the transformation it applies, not by a specific model component.

# Depth-Wise and Shared-Channel Interpretations of Block-Diagonal OFT in Convolutions

The block-diagonal parameterization of OFT has an especially intuitive interpretation when applied to convolution layers. When the number of blocks matches the number of input channels, each block transforms one neuron channel independently. The paper likens this to depth-wise convolution, because each channel gets its own transformation rather than mixing all channels together at once.

At the other extreme, if all blocks are shared, then the same orthogonal matrix is applied across channels. In that case, OFT acts like a channel-shared orthogonal transformation. These two interpretations show that the block structure is not only a computational trick, but also a way of controlling the granularity of adaptation. More blocks means more flexibility and less coupling; more sharing means stronger regularization and fewer parameters. The paper uses this block structure as part of its broader argument that OFT balances flexibility with regularity.

# Rewriting OFT as an Additive Update: $W = W^0 + (R - I)W^0$

Although OFT is conceptually a multiplicative method, the paper shows that it can be rewritten in a form that resembles an additive update. If the pretrained weight matrix is $\bm{W}^0$ and the orthogonal matrix is $\bm{R}$, then the finetuned weights are $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This can be expanded as
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0.
\]
This rewriting is useful because it helps relate OFT to other finetuning methods. It makes clear that OFT does change the pretrained weights, but does so through a structured transform rather than by directly learning an unconstrained additive perturbation.

The paper uses this identity to explain why OFT still behaves like a parameter-efficient update. Even though the transformation matrix $\bm{R}$ is full-rank, the effective change to the pretrained weights may still be limited depending on the structure of $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$. This is part of the paper’s deeper comparison with LoRA.

# When OFT Induces an Effectively Low-Rank Update

The paper points out that if $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is low-rank, then the update $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ is also effectively low-rank. This is an interesting bridge between OFT and LoRA. LoRA is explicitly built around a low-rank update, while OFT is built around an orthogonal transform. But the paper shows that OFT can, in certain situations, produce an update that behaves somewhat similarly to a low-rank modification.

This does not mean the two methods are the same. The key distinction is that OFT does not start by assuming low rank. Instead, it starts from an orthogonality constraint and uses block structure to reduce parameter count. So while an OFT update may be low-rank in effect, the mechanism is different: it is governed by geometric preservation rather than by explicit rank restriction.

# OFT versus LoRA: Sparsity-Structured Multiplicative Adaptation versus Low-Rank Additive Adaptation

The paper draws a sharp conceptual contrast between OFT and LoRA. LoRA updates weights additively by learning a low-rank correction. OFT, by contrast, applies a multiplicative orthogonal transformation to the pretrained weights. The paper describes this as exploiting sparsity structure rather than low-rank structure. In OFT, the block-diagonal organization reduces the number of free parameters while preserving orthogonality. So the reduction in complexity comes from how the transformation is structured, not from assuming that the update matrix itself is low-rank.

This difference matters because the two methods embody different intuitions about preservation. LoRA tries to keep the pretrained model from moving too far by limiting the rank of the update. OFT tries to preserve the relational geometry of neurons by preserving pairwise angles, and therefore hyperspherical energy. The paper’s argument is that this geometric preservation is more directly tied to maintaining the semantic generation ability of the pretrained diffusion model.

# Why OFT May Converge Faster by Directly Changing Neuron Directions

The paper offers a simple intuition for why OFT can converge faster than methods like standard finetuning or LoRA. It argues that changing neuron directions is a direct way to modify semantic information. In the paper’s toy experiment, angular information was enough to reconstruct the input images, while magnitude information was not especially useful. From this, the authors infer that the semantic content of the network is strongly tied to the directions of the neurons.

If semantic information is carried primarily by direction, then a method that directly adjusts directions should be efficient for downstream adaptation. That is exactly what OFT does. Rather than slowly nudging weights in arbitrary directions, it learns an orthogonal transformation that rotates or reflects the neurons in a controlled way. This gives the method a more targeted update mechanism, which the paper connects to faster convergence in practice.

# Optimization over a Smooth Hypersphere-Related Manifold

Another reason the paper gives for OFT’s convergence behavior is geometric. By constraining the transformation to be orthogonal, OFT effectively optimizes on a smooth manifold associated with the hypersphere. The paper describes this as giving a better optimization landscape. The intuition is that orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles and keep the geometry of the neuron set well behaved.

This geometric perspective helps explain why OFT is stable. A finetuning method that preserves the spherical structure of neurons avoids abrupt distortions in the representation geometry. The paper ties this to the observation that orthogonal transformation can be a sweet spot between flexibility and regularity. It is flexible enough to adapt to the downstream task, but structured enough to avoid destabilizing the pretrained model.

# Why Minimizing Hyperspherical Energy Itself Is Not the Goal in Finetuning

The paper makes a subtle but important distinction: preserving hyperspherical energy is the goal, not minimizing it. This differs from prior work on orthogonal over-parameterized training for classification, where low hyperspherical energy is beneficial because it encourages more uniform neuron spacing. In finetuning, however, the pretrained model already contains useful semantic structure, and the objective is to retain that structure while adapting to a new task.

If finetuning were to minimize hyperspherical energy, it might distort or destroy the semantic organization learned during pretraining. The paper explicitly warns against this. Instead, OFT aims to keep the hyperspherical energy unchanged relative to the pretrained model. In other words, it tries to preserve the intrinsic relational structure of the original model, rather than impose a new uniform geometry that may not be appropriate for generation.

# Trade-off Between Flexibility and Regularity Across Full Finetuning, LoRA, OFT, and COFT

The paper repeatedly returns to a central trade-off: finetuning must be flexible enough to adapt the model, but regular enough to preserve pretrained knowledge. Standard finetuning is highly flexible, because it allows direct weight updates with few explicit restrictions. But the paper argues that this flexibility comes at the cost of instability and possible model collapse. LoRA adds a rank constraint to reduce update freedom, improving parameter efficiency and often stability, but it still does not explicitly preserve the neuron geometry that the paper considers important.

OFT is presented as a better balance. By preserving pairwise angles through orthogonal transformations, it regularizes the update while still allowing meaningful adaptation. COFT goes one step further by explicitly constraining the deviation from the pretrained model, not just preserving hyperspherical energy. This makes COFT even more stable, at the expense of some flexibility. The paper’s broader interpretation is that these methods form a spectrum: standard finetuning is the most flexible, LoRA is more constrained, OFT preserves geometric structure, and COFT adds an additional neighborhood constraint for extra stability.

# No Additional Inference Overhead After Merging OFT Transforms into Pretrained Weights

A practical advantage of OFT emphasized in the paper is that it does not introduce any extra inference-time cost. During training, OFT learns the orthogonal transformation matrix, but at inference time the learned transform can simply be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix. The resulting model uses the merged weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, so the forward pass is just that of an ordinary pretrained model with updated weights.

This is an important deployment advantage. Methods like ControlNet require extra components at inference, whereas OFT does not. The paper frames this as a major strength of the method: it improves controllability and finetuning stability while keeping inference efficient. In the broader interpretation of the paper, this makes OFT not just mathematically elegant, but also practical for real use.

Chapter 9: Experimental Design and Evaluation Methodology

# Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained base model

The paper evaluates Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, on top of a pretrained text-to-image diffusion backbone, and the specific base model used in the experiments is Stable Diffusion v1.5. This choice matters because the paper is not proposing a new image generator from scratch; instead, it studies how to adapt an already powerful pretrained diffusion model to downstream control tasks while keeping its original generative ability intact. Using a strong pretrained base model makes the finetuning problem meaningful: the central question is whether a method can improve controllability without damaging the image quality, semantic fidelity, and diversity that the pretrained model already possesses.

Because the paper focuses on finetuning rather than pretraining, the pretrained model serves as the fixed starting point for all methods being compared. OFT, COFT, DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, and T2I-Adapter are all evaluated in relation to this shared backbone, which allows the authors to isolate the effect of the finetuning strategy itself. In other words, the experiments are designed to answer whether orthogonal finetuning offers an advantage over other adaptation methods when applied to the same underlying diffusion model.

# Fair comparison principles across DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, OFT, and COFT

A major concern in experimental design is fairness. The paper explicitly attempts to make comparisons among methods meaningful by keeping the underlying pretrained model and task settings aligned as much as possible. For subject-driven generation, the baselines are DreamBooth and LoRA, and for controllable generation, the baselines are ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. OFT and COFT are evaluated under the same general task formulations as these existing approaches, so the results reflect differences in finetuning strategy rather than differences in task definition.

The paper also emphasizes that fairness extends to the layer choice used for finetuning. Since LoRA is typically applied only to certain layers, OFT and COFT are restricted to the same layers in the comparison to LoRA. This matters because finetuning more layers would increase capacity and parameter count, making the comparison harder to interpret. By matching the target layers, the paper ensures that OFT is not benefiting merely from a broader intervention in the network.

Another fairness principle is that the methods are compared using the same task-specific losses when appropriate. In the subject-driven generation experiments, the paper states that all methods adopt the same loss function as in DreamBooth. This means the comparison focuses on the effect of how the model is updated, rather than on differences in the learning objective. In the controllable generation setting, the comparison follows the established task setup used by ControlNet and T2I-Adapter, again making the experimental setting consistent with prior work.

# Matching OFT and LoRA on the same target layers

The paper makes a deliberate effort to compare OFT to LoRA under comparable parameterization conditions. This is important because LoRA and OFT are both designed as efficient finetuning methods, but they do so in fundamentally different ways. LoRA introduces additive low-rank updates, while OFT introduces multiplicative orthogonal transformations. To avoid giving one method an unfair structural advantage, the authors apply OFT or COFT to the same layers where LoRA is used.

This design choice has a concrete methodological consequence: the comparison is not about whether one method can finetune more of the network than the other, but whether a particular kind of constrained update is more effective than another under similar scope. Since both methods are intended to reduce the cost of adaptation, matching the finetuning location makes the comparison especially relevant for practitioners who want efficient downstream adaptation.

The paper also notes that OFT and COFT use a similar number of trainable parameters to LoRA in the controllable generation experiments, while still converging faster and producing stronger control. That result would be hard to interpret without a careful control over the layers being adapted. By keeping the comparison anchored to the same target layers, the paper strengthens the claim that the benefits of OFT come from its orthogonal update structure rather than from simply using more trainable capacity.

# Subject-driven generation training setup following DreamBooth

For subject-driven generation, the paper follows the DreamBooth setup. The goal here is to personalize the diffusion model so it can generate images of a specific subject from only a few example images, while still obeying a text prompt that places that subject into new contexts. The paper uses DreamBooth and LoRA as baselines, and OFT and COFT are evaluated under the same general training formulation.

Crucially, the paper states that all methods adopt the same loss function as DreamBooth. This keeps the subject-driven task definition stable across methods. The key question becomes whether orthogonal finetuning can achieve better subject preservation and prompt following than standard finetuning or low-rank adaptation, not whether one method is solving a different optimization problem. In this setting, the paper studies both qualitative outputs and quantitative scores that measure how well the generated images preserve the subject identity while aligning with the prompt.

The training protocol is also used to study stability and convergence. The paper compares how quickly the methods adapt and whether their performance remains stable as the number of iterations increases. This is especially relevant for a method like OFT, because the authors argue that orthogonal transformation should preserve pretrained structure more reliably than naive finetuning. The subject-driven experiments are therefore not only about final performance, but also about how predictable and robust the finetuning process is.

# Controllable generation setup following ControlNet and T2I-Adapter

For controllable generation, the paper follows the general setting of ControlNet and T2I-Adapter. The task is to condition image generation on an additional control signal, such as an edge map, segmentation map, or facial landmarks, along with a text prompt. The paper compares OFT and COFT against ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA in this setting.

The experimental design uses the same pretrained Stable Diffusion v1.5 backbone, but the methods are adapted to accept and obey control inputs. The paper highlights that OFT and COFT are applied in a way that does not add test-time computational overhead, unlike ControlNet, which introduces an additional network path. This makes the evaluation not only about accuracy of control but also about efficiency and deployability.

The controllable generation experiments are structured to test both convergence behavior and final control quality. This is why the paper reports not just final images, but also performance across epochs. The authors want to understand whether a method can learn the control mapping smoothly and efficiently, and whether it can preserve fidelity while doing so. The setup thus reflects the central theme of the paper: the finetuning method should be stable, parameter-efficient, and faithful to the pretrained model.

# Datasets used for controllable generation: COCO, ADE20K, and CelebA-HQ

The paper evaluates controllable generation on three datasets, each representing a different type of control problem. For canny edge to image, or C2I, the paper uses the COCO dataset. For segmentation map to image, or S2I, it uses ADE20K. For landmark to face, or L2F, it uses CelebA-HQ. These choices create a diverse evaluation suite, covering object-level scenes, semantic layout generation, and face synthesis from structural landmarks.

This dataset selection is important because controllability can look very different depending on the type of signal being provided. Edge maps capture coarse outlines, segmentation maps encode semantic regions, and landmarks constrain facial geometry. By testing on all three, the paper shows whether OFT is robust across multiple forms of control rather than tuned to a single representation. The diversity of datasets therefore supports a broader claim about the method’s generality.

The paper also mentions that it can perform well on additional control tasks beyond the three main ones, including dense pose to human body, sketch to image, and depth to image, which are discussed in the appendices. Even so, the core experimental methodology in the main paper is built around COCO, ADE20K, and CelebA-HQ, because these provide clear and standard benchmarks for different control modalities.

# Training duration and epoch settings across tasks

Training duration is an important part of the paper’s experimental methodology because the authors want to compare both sample efficiency and convergence speed. For controllable generation, all methods are used to finetune Stable Diffusion v1.5 for 20 epochs on the three benchmark datasets. This provides a shared training budget that makes the comparison across methods more interpretable.

The paper pays particular attention to how performance changes over the course of training. In the subject-driven generation experiments, it compares behavior after 400 iterations and after 2000 iterations, showing that OFT and COFT remain stable while some baselines degrade or collapse. This suggests that the number of training steps is not merely a bookkeeping detail, but a crucial aspect of evaluating finetuning methods. A method that performs well only at a narrow training duration is less practical than one that remains robust across a wider range.

One of the paper’s claims is that OFT is less sensitive to the exact number of finetuning iterations. This matters for experimental design because it reduces the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning. In practice, a method with stable convergence is easier to use because the user does not have to find a delicate stopping point. The training protocols in the paper are therefore chosen not just to measure final quality, but also to reveal whether the method is easy to optimize.

# Subject fidelity metrics: DINO and CLIP-I

To measure how well a method preserves the identity of the subject in subject-driven generation, the paper uses DINO and CLIP-I. Both metrics compare generated images against real reference images of the same subject, but they do so using different pretrained visual representations. CLIP-I computes the average pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between generated and real images. DINO is similar, except that it uses ViT S/16 DINO embeddings.

These metrics are designed to capture subject fidelity, meaning how faithfully the generated image preserves identity-related details from the input images. In the context of DreamBooth-style personalization, this is one of the main objectives. If a method generates visually pleasing images but fails to preserve the subject, then it does not solve the personalization problem. The paper therefore uses DINO and CLIP-I as direct indicators of whether the learned finetuning procedure retains the subject’s appearance.

The reason for using both metrics is that no single embedding space is guaranteed to fully characterize identity fidelity. By reporting results in both DINO and CLIP-I, the paper obtains a more robust assessment of subject preservation. The results show that OFT and COFT improve these metrics substantially, supporting the claim that orthogonal finetuning better retains the subject-specific information encoded by the pretrained model.

# Text alignment metric: CLIP-T

While subject preservation is important, the generated image also needs to match the text prompt. The paper uses CLIP-T to evaluate this aspect. CLIP-T is defined as the average cosine similarity between the CLIP embeddings of the text prompt and the generated image. In other words, it measures whether the model is not only remembering the subject, but also placing it in the intended textual context.

This metric is especially relevant in subject-driven generation because there is often a tension between identity preservation and prompt adherence. A method may overfit to the subject and ignore the prompt, or it may obey the prompt but distort the subject identity. CLIP-T helps quantify the second half of that trade-off. By including it alongside DINO and CLIP-I, the paper assesses both subject fidelity and prompt fidelity in a balanced way.

The paper reports that OFT and COFT achieve slightly better or comparable CLIP-T performance relative to baselines while improving subject fidelity. This is an important result because it suggests that the method does not improve identity preservation by sacrificing textual controllability. Instead, the evaluation indicates that orthogonal finetuning can support both goals at once.

# Diversity metric: LPIPS

To measure sample diversity, the paper uses LPIPS. In the subject-driven generation setting, diversity matters because a useful personalization method should generate multiple distinct images of the same subject rather than collapsing to nearly identical outputs. LPIPS captures perceptual variation between generated images, so it serves as a proxy for whether the method produces a range of plausible samples.

The paper evaluates average LPIPS cosine similarity between generated images of the same subject with the same text prompt. This gives a sense of how diverse the outputs are when the model is asked to generate multiple images under the same condition. A method with low diversity may be overly constrained, while a method with higher diversity can better exploit the generative capability of the pretrained diffusion model.

By reporting LPIPS together with subject fidelity and prompt alignment metrics, the paper avoids a one-sided evaluation. A finetuning method could potentially score well on identity preservation by producing nearly identical outputs, but that would not be desirable. Including diversity ensures that OFT and COFT are not simply memorizing the subject in a rigid way, but are preserving the model’s ability to generate varied images.

# Why multiple random seeds are needed for robust comparison

The paper repeatedly finetunes the same pretrained model with 30 different random seeds for the subject-driven generation evaluation. This is a methodological choice intended to reduce the influence of randomness. Finetuning neural networks can vary depending on initialization, data sampling, or optimization noise, so a single run may not reflect the true behavior of a method.

Using multiple seeds makes the comparison more reliable. It helps distinguish a method that genuinely converges well from one that only appears strong in a lucky run. In a setting like subject-driven generation, where the training data is extremely limited, this is especially important because the model can be sensitive to small perturbations. By averaging over many seeds, the paper provides a more robust estimate of each method’s typical performance.

The repeated-seed protocol also aligns with the paper’s emphasis on stability. Since OFT and COFT are claimed to be more stable than standard finetuning, it is appropriate to evaluate them under conditions where randomness could otherwise obscure the result. The multiple-seed design therefore strengthens the credibility of the reported improvements.

# Motivation for introducing a new control consistency metric

For controllable generation, the paper argues that standard image quality metrics are not enough to assess whether a model truly follows the control input. This motivates the introduction of a new control consistency metric. The key idea is to estimate the control signal from the generated image and then compare that estimated signal with the original control signal.

This is a more direct way to evaluate controllability than simply inspecting the image or relying only on human judgment. If the model is supposed to follow an edge map, segmentation map, or landmark configuration, then the generated image should reflect that structure in a recoverable way. A control consistency metric checks exactly that by asking whether the intended control information can be recovered from the output.

The paper presents this as a novel contribution because it provides a task-specific and measurable notion of control quality. In controllable generation, a visually appealing image is not enough if it violates the conditioning signal. The new metric aims to capture the degree to which the model respects the control specification, which is central to the paper’s notion of successful finetuning.

# Estimating control signals from generated images for evaluation

The control consistency metric works by using an estimator appropriate to the task to infer the control signal from the generated image, and then comparing that inferred signal to the original input control signal. The exact measure depends on the control task. For the C2I task, the paper reports IoU and F1 score. For the S2I task, it reports mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy. For the L2F task, it reports the mean $\ell_2$ distance between control landmarks and predicted landmarks.

This design reflects the different types of structure encoded by each control signal. For segmentation-based tasks, overlap and classification-style metrics are natural, since the output should preserve region labels and spatial layout. For landmark-based face generation, geometric distance is more appropriate, since the task is about matching facial keypoint configuration. The evaluation is therefore tailored to the semantics of each control modality rather than forcing all tasks into a single metric.

By estimating the control signal from the generated image, the paper obtains a practical test of whether the model internalized the control condition. This is stronger than simply checking whether an image looks plausible, because a plausible image may still ignore the conditioning input. The control consistency framework thus helps validate the paper’s central claim that OFT and COFT produce images that are not only realistic but also accurately aligned with the intended control signals.

Chapter 10: Subject-Driven Generation Results

# Problem setup for subject-driven generation

Subject-driven generation asks a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to produce images of a specific subject from only a few reference images, while still following a text prompt that places that subject into new contexts. In the paper, this task is treated as one of the two main downstream applications for finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models. The central challenge is not only to learn the subject identity from a tiny set of images, but also to preserve the model’s pretrained ability to generate high-fidelity and semantically rich images.

The paper emphasizes that this is a difficult balancing act. A finetuning method must adapt the model enough to bind the subject identity to a learned token, yet it must avoid destroying the pretrained generative structure. For subject-driven generation, this means that a good method should both preserve identity-specific details and respond correctly to the surrounding text prompt. The paper frames this as a test of finetuning stability, sample efficiency, and preservation of pretrained semantic knowledge.

# Using the DreamBooth loss for all compared methods

For the subject-driven generation experiments, all compared methods use the same loss function as DreamBooth. This is important because it isolates the effect of the finetuning strategy itself rather than differences in the objective. In other words, the comparison is not about whether one method has access to a better training signal, but whether OFT and COFT can make better use of the same subject-driven generation setup.

The paper follows the DreamBooth framework and uses a customized token together with a few subject images. DreamBooth itself uses a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior preservation loss, and OFT adopts this same framework. The main difference is that DreamBooth performs standard finetuning with a small learning rate, whereas OFT replaces naive weight updates with orthogonal transformations of the pretrained weights. COFT is the constrained version of this idea, adding an explicit deviation constraint to improve stability.

# Baselines: DreamBooth and LoRA

The subject-driven generation experiments compare OFT and COFT against DreamBooth and LoRA. DreamBooth is the natural baseline because the paper’s subject-driven setup follows that framework directly. LoRA is included because it is a widely used parameter-efficient finetuning method and is especially relevant for comparison with OFT in terms of training efficiency.

The paper positions DreamBooth and LoRA as two different ways of adapting a pretrained diffusion model without full retraining. DreamBooth updates the model in the standard way using a small learning rate, while LoRA adds a low-rank update to the weights. Both are meant to preserve the pretrained model’s generative behavior, but the paper argues that neither method can guarantee semantic preservation. OFT is introduced as a more principled alternative because it preserves hyperspherical energy by construction, and this turns out to matter for keeping the pretrained model’s semantic structure intact.

# Convergence speed of OFT and COFT versus competing methods

A major result in the paper is that OFT and COFT converge faster than DreamBooth and LoRA. The experiments show that after 400 iterations, both DreamBooth and the OFT variants can achieve good control, but LoRA already begins to fail at preserving the subject identity. This indicates that LoRA, despite being parameter-efficient, is less reliable at quickly adapting the model to the subject-driven task.

The advantage becomes even clearer after longer finetuning. At 2000 iterations, DreamBooth starts producing collapsed images, and LoRA fails to preserve the intended appearance, such as generating yellow fur instead of a yellow shirt in the example discussed in the paper. By contrast, both OFT and COFT remain stable and continue to generate consistent results. The paper interprets this as evidence that OFT provides fast yet stable convergence, which is especially valuable because it reduces the need to carefully tune the exact number of training iterations for each subject.

# Training stability across short and long finetuning runs

Stability is one of the paper’s central claims, and the subject-driven experiments strongly support it. The results show that OFT and COFT remain reliable over both short and long finetuning schedules. They can produce good subject preservation early in training and continue to do so without degrading into collapsed or inconsistent generations.

This behavior matters because finetuning large diffusion models can be highly sensitive to optimization settings. The paper stresses that stable training dynamics make a method easier to use in practice, since users do not need to constantly search for the exact stopping point. In this regard, OFT and especially COFT are presented as practical methods rather than merely mathematically elegant ones. Their training curves are smoother and less fragile than those of the baselines.

# Model collapse behavior in DreamBooth under longer finetuning

The paper specifically notes that DreamBooth begins to generate collapsed images after longer training. This is an important failure mode because it shows that naive finetuning can gradually harm the pretrained generative ability even if it appears to work earlier in training. In the subject-driven setting, collapse means that the model loses the ability to produce coherent or faithful outputs as training continues.

This result supports the paper’s broader argument that preserving the pretrained structure matters. DreamBooth may initially capture the subject identity, but continued training can damage the model’s generative quality. The paper uses this to motivate OFT’s structural constraint: rather than merely making the parameter update small, OFT preserves the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, which the authors argue are crucial for retaining semantic knowledge.

# Identity preservation failures observed in LoRA

LoRA is also shown to struggle with identity preservation in the subject-driven generation experiments. The paper reports that after 400 iterations, LoRA fails to preserve the subject identity, and in the qualitative examples it can completely lose the identity of the subject in certain cases, such as the bowl example. This is a key weakness because subject-driven generation is fundamentally about keeping the same subject recognizable across new contexts.

The paper uses this failure to highlight a limitation of low-rank additive updates. Although LoRA is efficient, the paper argues that its update mechanism does not directly preserve the structural relationships in the pretrained weights that encode semantic information. OFT, in contrast, changes the neuron directions through orthogonal transformation while preserving hyperspherical energy, which the authors find to be more compatible with subject preservation.

# Why OFT is less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations

One of the most practically useful findings in the paper is that OFT is relatively insensitive to the number of finetuning iterations. The authors explicitly note that this reduces the trouble of tuning the iteration number for different subjects. In practice, this means that one can use a relatively large number of iterations without carefully searching for the best stopping point, because OFT remains stable across a wide range of training durations.

The paper connects this behavior to the fact that OFT updates neuron directions in a controlled way. Instead of making unconstrained changes to the model, OFT preserves the pairwise angles among neurons and therefore retains the pretrained semantic structure. COFT goes one step further by adding an explicit deviation constraint, which makes its behavior even more predictable. As a result, both methods are described as easier to tune than standard finetuning or LoRA.

# Quantitative results on DINO and CLIP-I subject fidelity

To evaluate subject fidelity quantitatively, the paper uses DINO and CLIP-I. DINO measures similarity using ViT S/16 DINO embeddings, while CLIP-I computes the average pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between generated and real images. These metrics are intended to capture how well the generated images preserve the identity-related details of the subject.

The paper reports that both COFT and OFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA by a considerable margin on DINO and CLIP-I. This means that the subject appearance in the generated images is more faithfully preserved when using orthogonal finetuning. The improvements are not marginal; the paper describes them as substantial, indicating that the structural constraint used by OFT translates into better identity retention in practice.

# Quantitative results on CLIP-T prompt fidelity

The paper also evaluates prompt fidelity using CLIP-T, which measures the cosine similarity between the CLIP embeddings of the text prompt and the generated image. This metric asks whether the finetuned model still follows the user’s prompt after learning the subject identity. In subject-driven generation, this is especially important because the subject must be placed into different contexts rather than merely copied.

According to the paper, OFT and COFT achieve slightly better or comparable prompt fidelity relative to the baselines. This is notable because a method could in principle improve identity preservation while harming prompt compliance. The results show that OFT does not force this trade-off; it can strengthen subject retention without losing the ability to track the text prompt. The qualitative examples later reinforce this point by showing that OFT better respects the requested scene or context.

# Quantitative results on LPIPS diversity

The paper evaluates diversity using LPIPS, computed as an average cosine similarity between generated images of the same subject under the same text prompt. This helps determine whether the model can generate varied outputs rather than reproducing nearly identical images each time. In a subject-driven setting, diversity matters because a useful model should produce multiple plausible images of the subject in different configurations, not just a single memorized pose.

The paper reports that OFT and COFT achieve slightly better or comparable diversity compared with DreamBooth and LoRA. This suggests that improving fidelity does not come at the cost of collapsing variety. Instead, OFT appears to preserve enough of the pretrained distribution to support multiple valid generations while still holding onto the subject identity.

# Effect of repeated trials with many random seeds

To reduce randomness, the paper repeats finetuning on the same pretrained model with 30 different random seeds. This is an important experimental detail because finetuning results can vary significantly depending on initialization, optimization noise, and data sampling. By averaging over many trials, the authors aim to show that the observed improvements are not accidental.

The repeated trials support the claim that OFT is consistently better, not just occasionally lucky. The paper reports that the methods are compared under multiple random seeds and that OFT’s superior convergence and final performance persist across these runs. This strengthens the argument that orthogonal finetuning is genuinely more stable and reliable than standard finetuning or LoRA.

# Qualitative examples showing improved identity retention

The paper provides qualitative examples to complement the quantitative metrics. In these examples, OFT and COFT are shown to preserve the semantic identity of the subject very well. The generated images remain recognizably tied to the reference subject while also adapting to the new prompt. This is the type of outcome that subject-driven generation is supposed to deliver, and the paper uses these examples to show that the quantitative improvements correspond to visibly better outputs.

In contrast, LoRA often fails to preserve the subject identity. The paper highlights cases where LoRA loses the subject completely, demonstrating that a low-rank update alone is not enough to maintain identity across generated scenes. DreamBooth can preserve identity better than LoRA in some cases, but as the paper notes, it may fail to follow the prompt as accurately. OFT and COFT are presented as better balancing these two goals.

# Qualitative examples showing stronger prompt controllability

The qualitative comparisons also show that OFT and COFT provide stronger prompt controllability. That is, they better reflect the specific scene or context described by the text prompt while still keeping the subject recognizable. This dual success is central to the paper’s claim that orthogonal finetuning improves controllability without sacrificing semantic fidelity.

The paper points out that DreamBooth may preserve the subject but still fail to generate the image according to the prompt, whereas OFT and COFT are more accurate in both respects. The examples suggest that the model can insert the subject into the desired context more reliably when finetuned with orthogonal transformations. This is consistent with the paper’s broader intuition that changing neuron directions, while preserving relational structure, is an effective way to modify semantics without destroying the pretrained model.

# Practical implication: easier hyperparameter tuning for OFT and especially COFT

A final practical takeaway from the subject-driven generation experiments is that OFT makes hyperparameter tuning easier, and COFT makes it even easier. Because OFT is less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations, users do not need to finely calibrate the training length for each subject. This is a major usability advantage over methods that require careful stopping criteria to avoid collapse or overfitting.

COFT improves this further by adding an explicit deviation constraint. The paper notes that with a proper $\epsilon$, both the learning rate and the iteration number become easier to set. In other words, COFT gives the user a more controlled optimization process by limiting how far the finetuned model can move away from the pretrained one. The practical result is a more stable and predictable finetuning pipeline for personalized subject generation.

Chapter 11: Controllable Generation Results

# Problem Setup for Controllable Generation

In the paper, controllable generation is presented as the second major application of Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT. The goal is to adapt a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model so that it can respond not only to a text prompt, but also to an additional control signal. This extra signal is meant to give the user more precise influence over the structure of the generated image than text alone can provide.

The paper frames controllable generation as a finetuning problem: instead of training a diffusion model from scratch, one begins with a pretrained model and adapts it to follow the new control input while trying not to destroy the model’s original image generation ability. This is important because a diffusion model that follows control too aggressively can lose realism, while a model that remains too close to the original may ignore the control signal. The paper’s main claim is that OFT and its constrained variant, COFT, are designed to achieve a better balance between these two goals.

# Control Tasks Studied in the Paper: Canny-to-Image, Segmentation-to-Image, and Landmark-to-Face

The paper evaluates controllable generation on three tasks, each chosen to represent a different kind of structural guidance.

The first is canny edge to image, or C2I, on the COCO dataset. Here the control signal is an edge map, and the model must generate an image whose structure matches those edges. This task tests whether the model can respect a sparse but informative outline of the target image.

The second is segmentation map to image, or S2I, on the ADE20K dataset. In this setting, the control input is a semantic segmentation map. The model must generate a realistic image that matches the layout and category information encoded in the segmentation. This is a stronger test of semantic alignment than edge guidance, because the model must preserve object identities and spatial arrangement across multiple regions.

The third is landmark to face, or L2F, on the CelebA-HQ dataset. In this task, facial landmarks serve as the control signal, and the model is asked to generate a face image that matches the given landmark configuration. This is especially useful for evaluating precise geometric control, because the predicted image must reflect pose and facial structure accurately.

Together, these tasks cover different forms of control: sparse structure, semantic layout, and fine-grained geometry. The paper uses them to show that OFT and COFT are not restricted to one particular kind of conditioning signal.

# Baselines: ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA

To evaluate OFT fairly, the paper compares it against three strong baselines. The first is ControlNet, which is designed specifically to add control signals to a pretrained diffusion model through finetuning. The second is T2I-Adapter, another method for adapting a pretrained diffusion model to extra inputs. The third is LoRA, which is a general parameter-efficient finetuning method and is included as a broader baseline for comparing adaptation strategies.

These baselines represent different philosophies of control. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are specialized for controllable generation, while LoRA is more general-purpose. The paper’s comparisons are meant to show whether OFT’s orthogonal transformation approach can compete with or exceed methods that are already tailored to control tasks.

A point emphasized in the paper is that OFT and COFT are used with a similar number of trainable parameters to LoRA, and far fewer than ControlNet. This makes the comparison important: the paper is not simply showing better results by adding many more parameters, but rather by changing the finetuning mechanism itself.

# Training Controllable Models for 20 Epochs on Each Dataset

For the controllable generation experiments, the paper finetunes Stable Diffusion v1.5 on the three datasets for 20 epochs. This training setup is shared across the methods compared in the paper. The authors note that OFT and COFT are applied in the same layers where LoRA is used, so the comparison remains fair in terms of which parts of the model are being adapted.

The choice of 20 epochs is important because the paper does not only examine final performance; it also studies convergence behavior over the course of training. In particular, the authors use the L2F task to show how quickly each method learns to respect the control signal. This makes controllable generation not just a question of final accuracy, but also one of training stability and sample efficiency.

The paper argues that a good finetuning method should converge with fewer updates and should remain stable as training continues. This matters in practical settings, because a method that requires careful tuning of training duration is harder to use reliably.

# Convergence Study on Landmark-to-Face

The landmark-to-face task is used in the paper as the main setting for studying convergence. The reason is that landmark accuracy offers a direct and interpretable way to measure whether the generated face matches the intended geometry. The authors examine how control error changes as the number of training epochs increases.

The convergence study shows that both OFT and COFT reach strong performance much faster than LoRA. According to the paper, LoRA needs about 20 epochs to match the performance that OFT reaches by epoch 8. This is presented as evidence that orthogonal finetuning is not only effective, but also more sample-efficient and optimization-friendly.

The paper also emphasizes that OFT and COFT are able to use a similar number of trainable parameters to LoRA while converging more quickly. This suggests that the gain is not merely from extra model capacity, but from the structure of the finetuning update itself.

# Landmark Error as a Quantitative Measure of Controllability

For the landmark-to-face task, the paper uses the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the control landmarks and the predicted face landmarks as the quantitative measure of controllability. This metric directly measures how far the generated face’s landmarks are from the target landmark configuration.

This choice is intuitive: if the goal is to generate a face that matches a given pose and shape, then the generated image should produce landmarks close to the input landmarks when they are re-estimated from the output. A smaller mean $\ell_2$ distance therefore means stronger control.

The paper uses this metric both for comparing methods at convergence and for showing how control improves during training. It serves as a concrete example of the paper’s broader strategy: evaluate control by comparing the input control signal to the signal recovered from the generated image.

# Faster Convergence of OFT and COFT Compared with ControlNet and LoRA

One of the central findings in the controllable generation experiments is that OFT and COFT converge faster than the baselines. In the L2F study, both methods reduce landmark error more rapidly than LoRA and ControlNet. The paper highlights this as a major practical advantage, because faster convergence means less training time and less need to fine-tune hyperparameters by trial and error.

The paper also notes that OFT and COFT are more data-efficient. In particular, the authors point out that OFT can converge well with only $5\%$ of the ADE20K dataset. This is presented as evidence that orthogonal finetuning can learn strong controllability even when the available training data is limited.

This faster convergence is part of the paper’s broader claim that OFT provides a better trade-off between flexibility and regularity. The method changes the model enough to learn control, but not so freely that training becomes unstable or inefficient.

# Smooth and Predictable Emergence of Control in OFT and COFT

The paper describes the training dynamics of OFT and COFT as smooth and predictable. In the qualitative convergence results for landmark-to-face, the generated face pose gradually becomes aligned with the control landmarks over training. This gradual improvement is contrasted with methods whose control may appear less stable or less interpretable during optimization.

This smooth emergence of control is important because it makes training easier to manage. If the behavior of the model changes in a gradual way, then the practitioner can more reliably choose when to stop training and how to set hyperparameters such as the learning rate or number of epochs. The paper explicitly argues that this makes OFT and COFT easier to use in practice.

The authors connect this stability to the structure of orthogonal finetuning. Since the learned transformation preserves pairwise angles in the layer, the model is being adapted in a constrained and controlled way rather than through an unrestricted weight update.

# Sudden Convergence Behavior Observed in ControlNet

In contrast to OFT and COFT, the paper reports a sudden convergence pattern for ControlNet in the landmark-to-face study. Specifically, the control quality appears to emerge abruptly after the 8th epoch. The authors relate this to the sudden convergence behavior observed in the original ControlNet paper.

This behavior is not necessarily bad in terms of final performance, but the paper treats it as a disadvantage from the perspective of training predictability. A model that changes abruptly can be harder to tune because small changes in training length may lead to very different outcomes.

The paper uses this comparison to argue that OFT and COFT offer more stable training dynamics. The difference is not just in the final output, but in how the model gets there.

# Data Efficiency Evidence, Including Strong Results with Only 5% of ADE20K

The paper presents data efficiency as one of the strengths of OFT. In the controllable generation experiments, the authors highlight an example from the ADE20K segmentation task showing that OFT can converge well with only $5\%$ of the dataset. This is important because it suggests that the method is able to extract useful control behavior from relatively little supervision.

Data efficiency matters especially in controllable generation, where gathering paired control-image examples can be costly. A method that performs well with fewer samples is more practical and more broadly applicable.

The paper does not present this as a separate algorithmic trick, but as a consequence of the orthogonal finetuning framework’s stability and its ability to preserve pretrained structure while still adapting to the control signal.

# Control Consistency Metric for Canny-to-Image Using IoU and F1

To evaluate controllability more systematically, the paper introduces a control consistency metric. The basic idea is to recover the control signal from the generated image and compare it with the original input control signal. This gives a direct way to assess whether the output truly follows the intended conditioning.

For the canny-to-image task, the paper uses IoU and F1 score. These metrics quantify how well the structure inferred from the generated image matches the original edge map. A high IoU or F1 means that the generated image retains the intended contour structure.

The reason this is useful is that visual quality alone is not enough. A generated image may look realistic while ignoring the input control signal. The consistency metric checks whether the model is actually controllable in the intended sense.

# Control Consistency Metric for Segmentation-to-Image Using Mean IoU and Accuracies

For segmentation-to-image, the paper evaluates control consistency using mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy. Here the task is to see whether the semantic regions in the generated image correspond to the input segmentation map.

This is a natural metric choice because segmentation provides categorical structure. If the output image is faithful to the input control, then a segmentation model should recover regions that match the original categories and layout. Mean IoU measures overlap between predicted and target regions, while the accuracy measures capture how often the recovered labels agree with the input.

The paper emphasizes that this is one of its novel contributions, since the metric provides a more accurate evaluation of control than simply inspecting sample quality. It is designed to measure the actual consistency between the conditioning signal and the generated result.

# Control Consistency Metric for Landmark-to-Face Using Mean $\ell_2$ Landmark Error

For landmark-to-face, the control consistency metric is again the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the input landmarks and the landmarks predicted from the generated image. This is the same metric used in the convergence study, and it functions as a direct measure of how precisely the generated face matches the desired pose and facial geometry.

Because facial landmarks are geometric points, $\ell_2$ distance is a particularly natural measure. Smaller distances indicate that the generated face closely follows the intended landmark arrangement.

The paper uses this metric to support the claim that OFT and COFT provide stronger and more accurate control than the baselines. In other words, the method does not merely create realistic faces; it creates faces that obey the given geometric constraints.

# Quantitative Evidence That OFT and COFT Yield Stronger Final Controllability

The paper reports that OFT and COFT outperform the baselines in the quantitative controllability evaluation across the three tasks. In the C2I task, they achieve better IoU and F1. In the S2I task, they achieve better segmentation agreement metrics. In the L2F task, they achieve lower landmark error.

The authors interpret this as evidence that the orthogonal finetuning framework yields stronger final controllability. Importantly, this improvement is reported even though OFT and COFT use similar or fewer trainable parameters than the competing methods. That makes the result more impressive, because it suggests a better finetuning strategy rather than simply more model capacity.

The paper also notes that the performance of OFT has not saturated, since it continues to improve as the number of trainable parameters increases. This is used to argue that the method has room to scale while maintaining its benefits.

# Qualitative Comparison Across All Three Control Tasks

The paper provides qualitative results showing that OFT and COFT generally produce images that are both realistic and well aligned with the control signal. In the segmentation-to-image task, LoRA is reported to fail completely in following the segmentation map, while ControlNet, OFT, and COFT can generate images consistent with the layout. OFT and COFT are described as producing more vivid details and more reasonable geometric structures than ControlNet, even with fewer parameters.

In the canny-to-image task, OFT and COFT can generate semantically similar images from rough edge maps. The paper contrasts this with weaker results from T2I-Adapter and LoRA. In the landmark-to-face task, OFT produces especially accurate pose control, even for challenging poses.

Across all three tasks, the qualitative results are used to reinforce the same conclusion as the quantitative metrics: OFT and COFT are able to preserve realism while improving adherence to control.

# Why OFT Produces Better Geometry, Fidelity, and Control with Fewer Trainable Parameters

The paper’s explanation for OFT’s performance is rooted in its finetuning mechanism. Rather than directly perturbing the pretrained weights with unrestricted updates, OFT learns an orthogonal transformation of the neurons. This preserves the pairwise angular relationships in each layer, which the paper argues are important for retaining the pretrained model’s semantic structure.

Because the transformation is constrained, OFT can adjust the model in a way that is expressive enough to learn the new control behavior but regular enough to avoid destroying the original generative ability. This is why the paper argues that OFT strikes a better balance between flexibility and stability.

COFT goes a step further by explicitly constraining the deviation from the pretrained model. This additional restriction improves finetuning stability even more. The paper therefore presents OFT and COFT as two closely related ways of achieving controllability, with COFT being the more conservative and stable variant.

# Generalization to Additional Control Tasks Mentioned in the Appendix

Although the main paper focuses on C2I, S2I, and L2F, it also states that OFT performs well on additional control tasks discussed in the appendix. These include dense pose to human body, sketch to image, and depth to image.

The point of mentioning these extra tasks is to show that OFT is not tied to a single kind of control signal. Instead, the method appears to generalize to different forms of structured conditioning. This is consistent with the paper’s broader claim that OFT is a model-agnostic finetuning approach for controllable generation.

Taken together, these results suggest that orthogonal finetuning can be used as a general strategy for adapting text-to-image diffusion models to a variety of downstream control tasks, while maintaining both realism and adherence to the requested structure.

Chapter 12: Practical Lessons, Limitations, and Open Problems

# Summary of the paper’s main empirical and conceptual contributions

The central contribution of the paper is the proposal of Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, as a principled way to adapt large text-to-image diffusion models to downstream tasks without damaging the model’s pretrained generative behavior. The authors focus on two tasks where this trade-off is especially important: subject-driven generation, where a model must preserve the identity of a subject from only a few example images, and controllable generation, where the model must obey extra signals such as edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks. In both settings, the challenge is not simply to make the model learn the new task, but to do so while retaining the high-fidelity and diverse image synthesis capabilities acquired during pretraining.

Conceptually, the paper argues that Euclidean distance between pretrained and finetuned weights is not a sufficiently meaningful measure of preservation. Instead, the authors propose hyperspherical energy as a more structural quantity that captures the pairwise angular relationships among neurons in a layer. Their key insight is that preserving these relationships helps preserve the semantic generation ability of the pretrained model. OFT is designed around this principle: by learning a shared orthogonal transformation for neurons in a layer, it can alter the model while provably preserving hyperspherical energy. They further introduce Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning, or COFT, which adds an explicit deviation constraint to improve stability.

Empirically, the paper reports that OFT and COFT outperform or match the main baselines in both tasks, with particular strengths in convergence speed, training stability, and sample efficiency. In subject-driven generation, the methods preserve identity better than DreamBooth and LoRA while maintaining prompt fidelity. In controllable generation, they achieve stronger control consistency than ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA, often with fewer trainable parameters and smoother convergence. The paper also introduces a new consistency-based evaluation perspective for controllable generation, emphasizing that the control signal should be recoverable from the generated image.

# Why preserving hyperspherical structure is a promising finetuning principle

The paper’s main theoretical intuition is that neuron directions carry semantic information more reliably than neuron magnitudes. This idea is supported by prior observations on angular features and by the authors’ toy autoencoder experiment, where angular information was shown to recover image structure much better than magnitude information. From this viewpoint, finetuning should aim to modify directions carefully, because directions are what encode the semantic relationships that matter for generation.

Hyperspherical energy is used as the quantity that summarizes these angular relationships across a layer. It measures how pairwise normalized neurons relate to each other on the unit hypersphere. The important point is not merely that the weights change little in a numerical sense, but that the relational geometry among neurons remains intact. That is why the authors treat preservation of hyperspherical energy as a proxy for preservation of pretrained semantics. In their framework, a good finetuned model is one whose neuron geometry has been transformed in a way that supports the downstream task without disrupting the pretrained structure.

The orthogonal transformation is the mechanism that makes this principle practical. If all neurons in a layer undergo the same orthogonal map, their pairwise angles are preserved exactly, so hyperspherical energy is unchanged. This gives OFT a built-in structural guarantee rather than an approximate regularization term. In the paper’s argument, this is more robust than simply penalizing weight distance, because a small Euclidean update does not necessarily preserve the underlying semantic geometry. For the types of adaptation studied in the paper, preserving this geometry appears to be a good balance between flexibility and stability.

# When OFT and COFT are preferable to DreamBooth, LoRA, or ControlNet-style adaptation

The paper positions OFT and COFT as especially useful when the downstream task demands both strong adaptation and strong preservation of pretrained generative behavior. In subject-driven generation, this means producing images of a particular subject in new contexts while keeping identity intact. In controllable generation, this means faithfully following a control signal without sacrificing image realism. The experiments suggest that OFT is a good choice when standard finetuning becomes unstable, when LoRA’s adaptation capacity is too limited, or when ControlNet-style methods are effective but comparatively heavy and slower to train.

Compared with DreamBooth, OFT offers more stable finetuning and better consistency over long training runs. The paper reports that DreamBooth can eventually collapse after more iterations, while OFT and COFT remain stable. Compared with LoRA, OFT tends to preserve subject identity and obey control signals better, especially when the task requires the model to adjust semantics without drifting away from the pretrained structure. Compared with ControlNet and T2I-Adapter, OFT can provide competitive or better controllability while using fewer parameters and converging faster, and it does so without adding any test-time computation.

COFT is particularly relevant when training stability is a priority. The paper makes the trade-off explicit by constraining the transformed weights to remain close to the pretrained model in addition to preserving orthogonality. This makes COFT more conservative than OFT, which can be useful when one wants to reduce the risk of over-adaptation. In the paper’s discussion, the main takeaway is that OFT and COFT offer a practical middle ground between the flexibility of standard finetuning and the rigidity of overly restrictive adaptation schemes.

# Deployment advantage: no additional inference-time overhead

One of the most practically important advantages emphasized in the paper is that OFT introduces no additional inference-time overhead. This matters because many finetuning methods improve controllability by adding extra modules or pathways that must be evaluated during generation, which can slow deployment. OFT does not require such additions. At inference time, the learned orthogonal matrix can simply be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix, producing an equivalent transformed weight matrix. As a result, the finetuned model is just as fast to run as the original pretrained model.

This is an important distinction from methods such as ControlNet, where extra computation is introduced by the architecture itself. The paper highlights this as a deployment advantage because it means OFT is not only a training method but also a way to produce a compact final model. For practical use, especially in systems where generation latency matters, this property makes OFT attractive. The model can be adapted to a task and then used normally, without a special inference procedure.

# Scalability challenge of matrix inversion in Cayley parameterization

Although OFT is designed to be efficient in parameter count and stable in optimization, the paper also acknowledges a scalability issue in its orthogonal parameterization. The authors use Cayley parameterization to generate orthogonal matrices because it is differentiable and computationally more practical than classical orthogonalization methods like Gram-Schmidt. However, Cayley parameterization requires a matrix inverse of the form $(I - Q)^{-1}$, where $Q$ is skew-symmetric. This inverse is the source of the scalability concern.

The paper notes that this inverse slightly limits the scalability of OFT, especially as model dimensions grow. Even though the authors mitigate the cost through block-diagonal structure, the underlying inverse still remains a challenge from the perspective of large-scale deployment. This is why the concluding section identifies faster differentiable orthogonal parameterizations as an open problem. In other words, the paper establishes that orthogonal finetuning is a promising principle, but also makes clear that the exact computational realization of orthogonality still has room for improvement.

# How block-diagonal parameterization helps and what biases it introduces

To make OFT more parameter-efficient, the paper introduces a block-diagonal parameterization of the orthogonal matrix. Instead of learning one large dense orthogonal matrix, the transformation is split into smaller orthogonal blocks. This reduces the number of trainable parameters substantially, because each block operates on a lower-dimensional subspace. The authors also describe an optional block-sharing variant, which reduces parameters even further.

The benefit of this design is obvious: it makes OFT more practical for large layers, and it lowers the cost of finetuning while preserving the core orthogonality property. Since orthogonality is retained, the hyperspherical energy preservation guarantee still holds. This is important because it means the efficiency gain does not come at the expense of the central structural principle of the method.

At the same time, the paper notes that the block-diagonal structure introduces bias and limits flexibility. This is a structural restriction on how the transformation can act across neurons, and it may prevent the method from exploring all possible orthogonal transforms. The authors explicitly identify this as a limitation in the conclusion, where they ask how to improve parameter efficiency in a more effective and less biased way. So the block structure is a useful engineering compromise, but not a fully neutral choice.

# Potential compositionality of multiple learned orthogonal transforms

The paper raises an interesting possibility: because orthogonal matrices are closed under multiplication, the orthogonal transforms learned by different OFT finetuning tasks could potentially be composed. This is a natural consequence of the mathematical structure the method uses. If multiple tasks each produce an orthogonal transform, then combining them still yields an orthogonal transform. The authors view this as an intriguing direction because it suggests a way to build composite models from separately learned task adaptations.

The appeal of this idea is that it could make OFT more modular than many other finetuning methods. Rather than relearning a new adaptation from scratch for every task, one could imagine combining learned transforms to accumulate capabilities. The paper does not claim this has been solved, but it flags the possibility as a meaningful future direction. This makes compositionality one of the most interesting conceptual questions raised by the work.

# Open question: whether composed OFT transforms preserve multiple downstream skills

Although orthogonal matrices can be multiplied together and remain orthogonal, the paper explicitly leaves open the question of whether the resulting composed transform would preserve the knowledge associated with all downstream tasks. This is not guaranteed by orthogonality alone. The fact that a matrix is orthogonal means it preserves angles, but it does not by itself prove that the semantic effects of separately learned tasks will coexist well after composition.

This open question is important because it shifts the discussion from geometry to behavior. Even if the mathematical property is preserved, the practical generative skills may interfere with one another. The paper suggests this as an interesting research direction rather than an established result. So while orthogonality offers a promising language for thinking about composition, the actual task-level compatibility of multiple OFT adaptations remains unresolved.

# Open question: improving parameter efficiency without restrictive structural bias

Another open issue is how to improve parameter efficiency without relying so heavily on the block-diagonal design. The paper is clear that this structure is useful, but also that it imposes an additional inductive bias. In effect, the model is asked to adapt within a particular sparse orthogonal family. That helps control the number of parameters, but it may limit the range of transformations available to the finetuning procedure.

This creates a tension that the paper does not fully resolve. On one hand, parameter efficiency is necessary for practical finetuning of large models. On the other hand, overly restrictive structure can reduce flexibility and may hide better solutions. The authors identify this as a major open problem, implying that future work should seek methods that preserve the orthogonal finetuning principle while relaxing the need for such a strong structural bias.

# Open question: better differentiable orthogonal parameterizations for large models

The paper’s use of Cayley parameterization is effective, but it is not the final answer to the problem of orthogonal finetuning. Because Cayley transform involves a matrix inverse, it is not ideal for very large models or very large layers. The authors therefore explicitly point to the need for better differentiable orthogonal parameterizations. Such methods would ideally preserve the core guarantee of OFT while being easier to compute and scale.

This is an important research direction because the value of OFT depends not just on the mathematical idea, but on whether the orthogonal transform can be learned efficiently and stably in practice. A better parameterization could expand the range of layers and model sizes to which the method applies. The paper treats this as a concrete technical challenge rather than a minor implementation detail.

# Broader implications for finetuning large generative models beyond text-to-image diffusion

Although the paper studies text-to-image diffusion models, its core message is broader: finetuning large generative models should be guided by structural properties of the pretrained representation, not only by low-rank updates or small weight changes. The orthogonal finetuning framework is presented as model-agnostic in spirit, and the authors note that it can in principle be applied to different neural architectures. They also discuss its applicability to fully connected and convolutional layers, and mention that it is compatible with the kinds of layers typically finetuned in transformer-based settings.

The larger lesson is that preserving the geometry of internal representations may be a powerful way to adapt pretrained models without destroying their learned capabilities. The paper does not claim that hyperspherical energy is the only possible structural measure, but it provides evidence that it is a useful one for the studied tasks. For students reading research in this area, the broader implication is that effective finetuning may require respecting the pretrained model’s internal organization, not merely changing its parameters in a numerically small way.

In that sense, OFT and COFT are more than task-specific algorithms. They are examples of a general design philosophy for controllable adaptation: keep the structure that encodes pretrained semantics, and introduce task-specific behavior through transformations that are constrained enough to remain stable but flexible enough to be useful. That balance between preservation and adaptation is one of the main ideas this paper contributes to the study of modern generative model finetuning.
