# Teaching AI New Image Tricks Without Making It Forget: A Simple Guide to Orthogonal Finetuning

Text-to-image diffusion models can already turn prompts like “a red fox in a snowy forest” into impressive images. But once you want these models to do something more specific, such as generate a particular subject from a few photos or follow an extra control signal like an edge map or segmentation mask, you usually have to finetune them.

That is where things get tricky. Finetuning often improves the new task, but it can also damage the model’s original ability to generate diverse, high-quality images. In practice, the model may become unstable, overfit, or even collapse. The paper *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning* proposes a simple idea to reduce that problem: instead of freely changing the model weights, update them through orthogonal transformations, which act like controlled rotations and reflections.

## The finetuning problem

The paper focuses on two downstream tasks for text-to-image diffusion models.

In subject-driven generation, the model sees a few images of a specific subject and a text prompt, and it should generate that same subject in new settings. DreamBooth is a well-known example of this kind of setup.

In controllable generation, the model gets an extra input such as canny edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks, and it should follow both that signal and the text prompt. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are representative methods here.

Both tasks need a pretrained diffusion model to adapt without losing what it already knows. Standard finetuning tries to do this by making small weight updates. Low-rank methods like LoRA add a constrained update path. But neither approach gives a strong guarantee that the pretrained generative behavior will stay intact.

The key question in the paper is: what property of the pretrained model should we preserve during finetuning?

## Why angles matter more than raw weight distance

The authors argue that Euclidean distance between weight matrices is not a complete measure of whether a model has preserved its semantics. Instead, they look at the angles between neurons.

Their main structural measure is hyperspherical energy, which depends on pairwise hyperspherical similarity, such as cosine similarity, between neurons in the same layer. Intuitively, this captures how neurons are arranged relative to each other on a unit hypersphere. The paper’s hypothesis is that preserving this arrangement helps preserve the model’s original semantic generation ability.

That leads to a useful observation: if you apply the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer, then pairwise angles are preserved. In other words, the layer is rotated or reflected, but its internal geometry stays the same.

This is the central idea behind Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT.

## Orthogonal Finetuning in one sentence

OFT finetunes a model by learning an orthogonal matrix $R$ for each layer, so the pretrained weights $W^0$ become

\[
W = R W^0, \qquad R^\top R = I.
\]

Because $R$ is orthogonal, the angles between neurons are preserved. That means the model can change how it represents information, while keeping the pairwise geometric structure of the original layer intact.

You can think of it as adjusting the coordinate system of a layer rather than rewriting the whole layer from scratch.

## Why this helps

The intuition comes from a simple semantic idea: directions matter.

The paper gives a toy autoencoder example showing that angular information can recover images much better than magnitude alone. This supports the idea that neuron directions carry a lot of semantic content. If you want to change what a model generates, changing neuron directions can be effective. But if you let those directions drift too freely, you may destroy the pretrained knowledge.

Orthogonal transformations are a sweet spot. They are expressive enough to change the model’s behavior, but structured enough to avoid arbitrary distortion.

## Making OFT practical

A full orthogonal matrix can still be expensive, so the paper uses efficient parameterizations.

One important choice is the Cayley parameterization, which constructs an orthogonal matrix from a skew-symmetric matrix. To reduce parameter cost further, the paper uses a block-diagonal form, splitting the matrix into smaller orthogonal blocks. This makes OFT much more parameter-efficient.

The paper also compares OFT to LoRA in terms of complexity. For a weight matrix of size $d \times d$, OFT can be very compact, especially with block structure, while still preserving the orthogonal constraint exactly.

## COFT: adding a stability constraint

The authors also propose Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning, or COFT. This version adds another constraint: the learned orthogonal transform should not move too far from the identity matrix.

In practice, this means COFT keeps the finetuned model close to the pretrained one while still allowing useful adaptation. The paper finds that this extra constraint can improve stability even further.

So the difference is:

- OFT preserves layer geometry through orthogonality.
- COFT preserves layer geometry and also limits how far the model can drift.

## What about performance?

The paper evaluates OFT on Stable Diffusion v1.5 for both subject-driven generation and controllable generation.

For subject-driven generation, OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on identity preservation metrics like DINO and CLIP-I, while maintaining good prompt fidelity and diversity. The authors also report that OFT converges more stably and is less sensitive to the exact number of training iterations.

For controllable generation, the paper tests tasks such as:

- canny edge to image,
- segmentation map to image,
- landmark to face.

Here too, OFT and COFT show strong control performance, often better than ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA in the reported metrics. The paper also introduces a control consistency metric that checks whether the generated image matches the original control signal when that signal is re-extracted from the output.

A practical advantage is that OFT does not add inference-time overhead. At test time, the learned orthogonal transform is simply folded into the pretrained weights.

## Why this is interesting

OFT is appealing because it replaces a vague idea, “make small updates,” with a more structured principle: preserve the internal angular geometry of the pretrained model.

That gives a cleaner trade-off between flexibility and regularity. The model can learn new tasks, but it is less likely to forget the visual knowledge it already had. The result is a finetuning method that is both efficient and stable.

## Takeaway

The main lesson of the paper is simple: when adapting a text-to-image diffusion model, not all weight changes are equally harmful. If you rotate the layer’s neurons instead of freely reshaping them, you can often teach the model new tricks without making it forget the old ones.

Orthogonal Finetuning uses that idea directly. It preserves pairwise neuron angles, keeps the pretrained structure intact, and delivers strong results on both subject-driven and controllable generation. For a problem where finetuning often destabilizes the model, that is a surprisingly elegant solution.



# Why Angles Matter More Than Weight Size in AI Image Generation

Text-to-image diffusion models can generate remarkably realistic images from prompts, but adapting them to new tasks is tricky. If you finetune them too aggressively, they often lose the broad visual knowledge they learned during pretraining. If you finetune them too conservatively, they fail to learn the new task.

The paper *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning* argues that one reason this balance is so hard is that the usual way of thinking about finetuning is incomplete. We often focus on how much the weights move in Euclidean space, but the paper suggests that what really matters for preserving semantics is something more geometric: the angles between neurons.

## The core idea: semantics live in directions

A neural network layer contains many neurons, each represented by a weight vector. In standard finetuning, we usually think in terms of changing those weights by a small amount. The intuition is that if the new weights stay close to the old ones, the model should keep its original behavior.

But the paper argues that raw distance is not the whole story. In many neural representations, the direction of a vector carries more semantic information than its magnitude. This is consistent with prior work on angular similarity and hyperspherical representations. In other words, the model’s “meaning” is often encoded in how neuron vectors point relative to each other, not just how long they are.

The authors support this with a toy autoencoder experiment. They show that angular information can almost reconstruct the input images, while magnitude alone does not contain useful semantic information. The takeaway is simple: if you want to preserve what a model “knows,” preserving neuron directions may matter more than preserving exact weight values.

## Hyperspherical energy: a way to measure structure

To make this intuition precise, the paper uses a quantity called hyperspherical energy. For a layer of neurons, hyperspherical energy measures the pairwise similarity among normalized weight vectors, using a cosine-like geometry on the unit sphere.

You can think of it as a summary of the layer’s internal arrangement. If the pairwise angles between neurons stay the same, then the layer preserves its relational structure. That structure is important because it captures how neurons are organized relative to one another, which the paper argues is closely tied to semantic behavior.

This is a key shift in perspective. Instead of asking, “How far did the weights move?”, OFT asks, “Did the geometry of the layer change?”

## Why orthogonal transformations?

The paper’s main method, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), is built around a neat mathematical fact: orthogonal transformations preserve angles. If you rotate or reflect all neurons in a layer by the same orthogonal matrix, their pairwise angles stay exactly the same.

That means the hyperspherical energy of the layer is preserved.

This is the central trick. Rather than directly updating every weight vector freely, OFT learns an orthogonal transformation applied to a pretrained layer. In effect, it changes the coordinate system of the layer without distorting the angular relationships inside it.

This is appealing for two reasons. First, it gives a principled way to finetune while preserving structure. Second, it helps explain why the model can adapt to new tasks without completely forgetting its pretrained visual knowledge.

## Why this helps text-to-image models

The paper studies two kinds of downstream tasks for text-to-image diffusion models:

Subject-driven generation means the model must generate a particular subject, such as a specific person or object, from just a few example images plus a text prompt. Controllable generation means the model must follow an extra signal such as edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks.

Both tasks require the model to change behavior in a targeted way, but not to lose its general image-generation ability. That is exactly where preserving neuron geometry helps. If the finetuning process changes the model’s semantic structure too much, the model may forget identity details or fail to follow the input control signal reliably.

By preserving pairwise angular relationships, OFT aims to keep the pretrained model’s semantic foundation intact while still allowing task-specific adaptation.

## Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning adds a safety rail

The paper also proposes Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning, or COFT. This version adds an explicit constraint that keeps the finetuned model close to the pretrained one in a controlled way.

The motivation is practical. Even if orthogonal transformations preserve angular structure, there can still be a trade-off between flexibility and stability. COFT makes that trade-off explicit by limiting how far the orthogonal transformation can deviate from the identity transformation.

In plain terms, OFT says: preserve the geometry. COFT says: preserve the geometry, and don’t move too far away from the original model either.

## Why not just use small learning rates?

A lot of finetuning methods rely on the idea that a small learning rate will keep the model close to its starting point. But the paper argues that closeness in Euclidean distance is not enough to guarantee semantic preservation.

Two models can be close in weight space and still differ in important structural ways. Conversely, a model can undergo a structured transformation that changes the weights substantially while preserving the semantic relationships that matter.

This is why OFT is interesting: it replaces an implicit heuristic with an explicit geometric constraint.

## What the experiments show

The paper tests OFT on subject-driven generation and controllable generation using Stable Diffusion v1.5. Across these tasks, OFT and COFT generally outperform baselines such as DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, and T2I-Adapter in terms of generation quality, convergence speed, and stability.

For subject-driven generation, the method preserves identity better and converges more stably. For controllable generation, it follows the control input more accurately and trains faster. Importantly, OFT does this without adding test-time inference overhead, since the learned orthogonal transformation can be merged into the pretrained weights.

The broader lesson is that preserving structure during finetuning is not just a theoretical preference. It has practical effects on how well the model retains its visual understanding.

## The bigger takeaway

The paper’s main contribution is not just a new finetuning trick. It is a shift in how we think about preserving knowledge in neural networks.

If neuron directions encode much of the semantic content, then finetuning should respect those directions. Hyperspherical energy gives a way to measure that structure, and orthogonal transformations give a way to preserve it while adapting the model.

So the title could be summarized like this: when adapting image generators, the key may not be how much the weights change, but whether the angles between them still make sense.

That is the intuition behind Orthogonal Finetuning, and it helps explain why preserving geometry can preserve generation quality.



# OFT vs LoRA vs DreamBooth vs ControlNet: What’s the Best Way to Customize a Diffusion Model?

Text-to-image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion are powerful, but the real challenge starts when you want to adapt them to a specific task. Maybe you want the model to generate a particular subject from just a few reference images. Or maybe you want it to follow an extra control signal, like edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks.

That is exactly the problem tackled in *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning*. The paper introduces **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, and its constrained variant **COFT**, as a way to adapt pretrained diffusion models while preserving their original generative ability.

The main takeaway is simple: among the methods compared in the paper, OFT is designed to be both **efficient** and **stable**. It uses fewer trainable parameters than full finetuning, preserves the pretrained model’s structure more carefully than standard updates, and converges faster than the baselines in the paper’s experiments.

## Why customization is hard

Finetuning a pretrained diffusion model sounds straightforward: update the weights on your downstream task and stop when the outputs look good. In practice, this is risky. If you update too aggressively, the model may lose the semantic knowledge that made it good in the first place.

The paper focuses on two downstream tasks:

- **Subject-driven generation**: generate a specific subject from a few images and a prompt.
- **Controllable generation**: generate images that follow an extra condition, such as edges, segmentation, or landmarks.

Both tasks need a careful balance between **flexibility** and **preservation**. You want the model to adapt, but not forget.

## The common baselines: what each method is doing

The paper compares OFT against several widely used adaptation methods. Each one takes a different approach to the same problem.

### DreamBooth: direct finetuning with preservation loss

DreamBooth adapts a pretrained diffusion model by finetuning it on a few subject images using a reconstruction loss plus a class-specific prior preservation loss. In the paper’s setting, DreamBooth is used as the main baseline for subject-driven generation.

This approach can preserve identity reasonably well, but it is still standard finetuning. The paper reports that it can become unstable with longer training, and after enough iterations it may produce collapsed images.

### LoRA: low-rank updates

LoRA is a parameter-efficient adaptation method. Instead of updating the full weight matrix, it adds a low-rank update. This makes finetuning cheaper and lighter than full updates.

The paper treats LoRA as the most relevant comparison to OFT because both are parameter-efficient. But they work differently:

- **LoRA** updates weights through a low-rank additive structure.
- **OFT** updates weights through a learned orthogonal transformation.

In the experiments, LoRA often converges more slowly and is less stable than OFT and COFT. In subject-driven generation, the paper also reports that LoRA may fail to preserve subject identity as well as OFT.

### ControlNet: adding a control branch

ControlNet is designed for controllable generation. It adapts a pretrained diffusion model by adding a trainable branch that processes the control signal.

This gives strong control, but it also comes with more parameters and test-time overhead than OFT. The paper emphasizes that OFT does **not** add inference overhead: after finetuning, the learned orthogonal transform can simply be absorbed into the pretrained weights.

### T2I-Adapter: lightweight control adaptation

T2I-Adapter is another method for adding control signals to a pretrained model. Like ControlNet, it is designed for controllable generation rather than subject personalization.

The paper compares OFT against T2I-Adapter in control tasks and finds that OFT achieves stronger controllability with fewer trainable parameters and faster convergence.

## What makes OFT different?

The core idea behind OFT is that it does not try to freely move the pretrained weights around. Instead, it learns a **layer-shared orthogonal transformation** of the neurons.

Why is that useful?

The paper argues that the **angles between neurons** carry important semantic information. OFT preserves those pairwise angles, which means it preserves the model’s **hyperspherical energy**. The authors use this as a structural measure of how much the finetuned model differs from the pretrained one.

In simpler terms: OFT tries to change the model in a way that keeps its internal geometry intact.

That is a very different philosophy from naive finetuning. Rather than pushing weights to new values with a small learning rate and hoping the model stays intact, OFT constrains the update to a transformation that is mathematically structured and stable.

## COFT: OFT with an extra safety rail

The paper also introduces **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)**. COFT adds a radius constraint so that the finetuned model stays close to the pretrained model in addition to preserving orthogonality.

This matters because the paper treats OFT as a good balance between flexibility and stability, while COFT pushes stability even further. In the experiments, COFT often behaves more predictably and is less sensitive to hyperparameter choices.

## How the methods compare in practice

The paper evaluates the methods on both subject-driven and controllable generation using Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the base model.

### Subject-driven generation

For subject-driven generation, the paper compares DreamBooth, LoRA, OFT, and COFT.

The reported results show that:

- **OFT and COFT converge stably**
- **OFT and COFT preserve subject identity better than the baselines**
- **LoRA struggles more with identity preservation**
- **DreamBooth can collapse with longer training**
- **OFT is less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations**

The paper also reports quantitative gains using metrics like DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, and LPIPS. OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on subject fidelity metrics, while keeping prompt fidelity and diversity competitive.

### Controllable generation

For controllable generation, the paper compares OFT and COFT against ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA on three tasks:

- canny edge to image
- segmentation map to image
- landmark to face

Here the paper introduces a **control consistency metric**: generate an image, estimate the control signal from that image, and compare it to the original control signal.

The main findings are:

- **OFT and COFT achieve stronger control consistency**
- **They converge faster than the baselines**
- **They are more stable during training**
- **They use far fewer parameters than ControlNet**
- **They do not add inference overhead**

In the landmark-to-face task, the paper notes that OFT reaches the performance that LoRA only matches after many more epochs. The training dynamics are also smoother than ControlNet’s sudden convergence behavior.

## Why orthogonal updates help

The paper’s intuition is that orthogonal transformations let the model change its internal directions without distorting the geometry that encodes the pretrained semantics.

That matters because, for diffusion models, you often want to modify *what* the model generates without breaking *how* it generates realistic images.

The paper argues that this is why OFT converges faster and remains stable: it gives the model enough freedom to adapt while keeping the pretrained structure intact.

## A practical summary

If you zoom out, the comparison looks like this:

- **DreamBooth**: simple subject personalization, but standard finetuning can become unstable.
- **LoRA**: parameter-efficient and popular, but not always strong enough on identity preservation or control.
- **ControlNet**: excellent for controllable generation, but adds more parameters and test-time overhead.
- **T2I-Adapter**: lightweight control adaptation, but still not as strong as OFT in the paper’s benchmarks.
- **OFT / COFT**: parameter-efficient, stable, fast-converging, and designed to preserve the pretrained model’s semantic structure.

The paper’s central claim is not that OFT replaces every existing method in every setting. Rather, it offers a principled finetuning strategy that works especially well when you care about preserving what the pretrained model already knows.

## Bottom line

If your goal is to customize a diffusion model without destroying its generative ability, the paper makes a strong case for **Orthogonal Finetuning**.

Compared with DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, and T2I-Adapter, OFT stands out because it:

- preserves hyperspherical structure in the weights,
- converges faster,
- is more stable during training,
- preserves subject identity better in subject-driven generation,
- improves controllability in control tasks,
- and adds no extra inference overhead.

COFT goes one step further by explicitly constraining how far the model can drift from the pretrained state.

So if you are looking for the paper’s answer to “what’s the best way to customize a diffusion model?”, the answer is: **adapt it orthogonally, not just aggressively**.



# From Personalized Avatars to Sketch-Guided Images: Where Orthogonal Finetuning Shines

Text-to-image diffusion models are already good at turning prompts into photorealistic images. The harder problem is making them do something specific without breaking what they already know. If you want a model to generate a particular person, object, or scene layout, plain prompt engineering often is not enough.

That is the setting of **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**. The paper proposes a finetuning method for adapting pretrained diffusion models to downstream image-generation tasks while trying to preserve the model’s original generative ability. The key idea is simple: instead of freely changing weights, OFT updates neuron directions through an orthogonal transformation, which keeps the pairwise angular relationships between neurons intact. In practice, that gives a better balance between flexibility and stability.

The paper focuses on two practical use cases:

- **Subject-driven generation**, where the model learns to generate a specific subject from just a few reference images.
- **Controllable generation**, where the model follows extra guidance such as edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks.

These are exactly the kinds of tasks where finetuning is useful, but also where overfinetuning can quickly ruin image quality or identity preservation.

## Why these tasks are hard

A pretrained text-to-image model already knows how to generate a wide range of images. The problem is that downstream tasks often ask it to do something more constrained.

In subject-driven generation, you may want the model to generate the same person, toy, or object in new contexts. The model must preserve identity while still following new text prompts.

In controllable generation, the model must obey an additional signal. For example, a canny edge map may specify coarse shapes, a segmentation map may define scene layout, or facial landmarks may specify pose. The model has to follow that structure and still produce a realistic image.

Naively finetuning the full model can work for a while, but it can also cause drift, instability, or collapse. The paper argues that simply keeping the finetuned weights close to the pretrained weights in Euclidean distance is not enough. Instead, it introduces a more structural constraint based on hyperspherical energy, and uses orthogonal transformations to preserve it.

## The core idea behind OFT

At a high level, OFT changes a layer by multiplying its weights with an orthogonal matrix. Orthogonal transformations preserve angles. The paper connects this to the idea that angular relationships among neurons encode important semantic information.

This matters because the method is designed to modify what the model generates without destroying the structure it learned during pretraining. The paper also introduces **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)**, which adds a radius constraint to keep the finetuned model even closer to the pretrained one.

There is also an efficient parameterization using a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix, so the method remains practical. Importantly, OFT does not add inference-time overhead: after finetuning, the learned orthogonal transformation can simply be folded into the pretrained weights.

## Subject-driven generation: learning a person or object from a few images

One of the most compelling applications in the paper is **subject-driven generation**. The setup is familiar from DreamBooth-style finetuning: give the model a few images of a subject and a text prompt, and ask it to generate new images of that same subject in different scenes or styles.

The challenge is preserving identity. If you finetune too aggressively, the subject may drift. If you finetune too conservatively, the model may ignore the new subject and keep generating something generic.

The paper evaluates OFT on this task using the DreamBooth framework and compares it with DreamBooth and LoRA. The results show two important behaviors.

First, OFT and COFT converge stably. The paper reports that after a relatively small number of iterations, they already achieve strong subject preservation and prompt following. Second, they remain stable even when training continues longer. This is notable because DreamBooth can start producing collapsed images with too much finetuning, and LoRA can fail to preserve the subject identity in some cases.

The paper’s qualitative examples show that OFT can keep the subject recognizable while still placing it in new prompts and contexts. That makes it useful for personalized generation, such as generating a consistent avatar or object across multiple scenes.

Quantitatively, the paper uses metrics such as DINO and CLIP-I for subject fidelity, CLIP-T for text alignment, and LPIPS for diversity. OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on the identity-preservation metrics, while maintaining comparable or better prompt fidelity and diversity.

## Controllable generation: following edges, layouts, and poses

The second major use case is **controllable generation**, where the model receives an extra signal beyond text. The paper evaluates three tasks:

- **Canny edge to image** on COCO.
- **Segmentation map to image** on ADE20K.
- **Landmark to face** on CelebA-HQ.

These tasks are a good stress test for finetuning methods because the model must obey a spatial constraint while keeping the image realistic.

The paper compares OFT and COFT against ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. The results are strong across both convergence and final quality.

On the landmark-to-face task, the paper measures how close the generated face landmarks are to the target landmarks. OFT and COFT converge faster than the baselines, and the training process is smoother. The authors note that ControlNet can sometimes show a more sudden emergence of control later in training, while OFT’s behavior is more gradual and predictable.

For evaluation, the paper also introduces a **control consistency** metric. The idea is to estimate the control signal back from the generated image and compare it with the original input control signal. That gives a more direct way to measure whether the model actually followed the condition. For example:

- For edge-guided generation, they compute IoU and F1 score.
- For segmentation-guided generation, they compute mean IoU and accuracy.
- For landmark-guided generation, they compute landmark distance.

By these measures, OFT and COFT show stronger control than the baselines.

Visually, the paper reports that OFT and COFT can produce realistic images that better match the input structure. In the segmentation task, for example, LoRA can fail to follow the mask, while OFT and COFT generate images that respect the layout. In the edge-guided setting, OFT can infer semantically consistent images from rough edge sketches. In the landmark setting, it produces accurate facial poses even in difficult cases.

## Why orthogonal finetuning helps in practice

The practical value of OFT is not just that it works, but that it works in a controlled way.

The paper’s central claim is that preserving pairwise neuron angles helps keep the pretrained semantic structure intact. That gives OFT a useful trade-off: it is flexible enough to adapt to new tasks, but regularized enough to avoid destroying the original model.

This shows up in three ways.

First, **stability**. OFT and COFT are less prone to collapse than naive finetuning.

Second, **sample efficiency**. The paper reports that OFT can converge with fewer training images and fewer epochs than competing methods.

Third, **deployment efficiency**. Unlike some adapter-style approaches, OFT does not add inference overhead. After finetuning, the modified weights can be used like a standard diffusion model.

## What to take away

If you are trying to adapt a text-to-image model for personalized or controllable generation, OFT is a clean and principled alternative to standard finetuning.

The paper’s main message is that **not all parameter changes are equally safe**. Updating weights while preserving angular structure can maintain the semantic knowledge embedded in a pretrained diffusion model. That makes OFT especially appealing for tasks where you want the model to learn something new without forgetting how to generate high-quality images.

In short, OFT shines in exactly the scenarios that make diffusion finetuning useful:

- generating a consistent subject from a few examples,
- following sketches, edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks,
- and doing all of that without sacrificing the base model’s generative quality.

For personalized avatars and control-guided image synthesis, that is a very practical combination.



# A Smarter Way to Fine-Tune Diffusion Models: Inside COFT and the Stability Problem

Fine-tuning a text-to-image diffusion model sounds straightforward: take a pretrained model, train it a bit more on a downstream task, and get better control. In practice, it is much trickier. If the update is too weak, the model barely learns the new task. If it is too flexible, it can drift away from the pretrained model and lose the rich generation ability that made it useful in the first place.

That trade-off is the motivation behind Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning, or COFT. COFT is the more stable variant of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), introduced in the paper *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning*. The basic idea is simple: preserve the semantic structure of the pretrained model while still allowing enough movement to adapt to a new task.

## Why fine-tuning can be unstable

The paper looks at two downstream settings for text-to-image diffusion models: subject-driven generation and controllable generation.

In subject-driven generation, the model must learn to generate a specific subject from only a few reference images. In controllable generation, the model must follow extra signals such as edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks. Both tasks need adaptation, but they also need the model to remember how to generate high-quality images in the first place.

A common fine-tuning strategy is to update the pretrained weights with a small learning rate. This implicitly tries to keep the new model close to the old one. But the paper argues that simple Euclidean closeness is not enough to capture whether the model’s semantics are preserved. A model can be close in weight space and still lose important structure.

OFT tackles that by preserving pairwise angular relationships between neurons, using an orthogonal transformation. Since orthogonal transformations preserve angles, they preserve the hyperspherical energy of each layer. The paper finds this matters for keeping the pretrained semantic generation ability intact.

But even OFT can still be more flexible than desirable in some settings. That is where COFT comes in.

## What COFT adds on top of OFT

COFT keeps OFT’s orthogonal transformation, but adds an explicit constraint that limits how far the transformed weights can move from the pretrained model.

Formally, COFT uses the same forward pass structure as OFT, where the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is transformed by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$:

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0, \quad \text{s.t. } \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}.
\]

COFT adds one more requirement:

\[
\norm{\bm{R} - \bm{I}} \leq \epsilon.
\]

This extra term keeps the learned transformation within a bounded neighborhood of the identity matrix. In plain terms, COFT says: you may rotate or reflect the weights, but not too much.

That matters because the identity matrix corresponds to the original pretrained model. If the learned orthogonal transform stays close to identity, the fine-tuning process remains anchored to the pretrained behavior. The model can still adapt, but it does so more cautiously.

## Why this helps training stability

The paper’s main point about COFT is not just that it works, but that it makes training easier to manage.

With standard fine-tuning, the learning rate and number of epochs can be very sensitive. Train too long, and the model may collapse or start producing worse outputs. Train too little, and it never really adapts. This is especially frustrating in workflows where the best hyperparameters vary by subject or dataset.

COFT makes the optimization more predictable because the constraint limits the amount of freedom available to the model. The paper shows that this can improve finetuning stability and reduce sensitivity to the training schedule.

There is also a practical side to this. COFT is designed so that the orthogonality constraint can still be handled efficiently using the Cayley parameterization. The additional deviation constraint is enforced by working with the matrix used inside the parameterization, and the paper suggests projecting it during optimization. The result is a method that keeps the mathematical structure of OFT while adding a useful stability guardrail.

## A better balance between flexibility and regularity

The paper frames fine-tuning as a balancing act.

Standard fine-tuning is highly flexible, but that flexibility comes with a cost: the pretrained structure can drift or collapse. OFT already improves this by preserving the angular relationships among neurons. COFT goes one step further by explicitly limiting how far the update can move from the original model.

This is a useful idea because downstream adaptation does not always need maximum freedom. Often, it needs just enough freedom to encode the new task while keeping the original model’s generalization intact.

The paper’s experiments support this intuition. In subject-driven generation, OFT and COFT both converge stably, while DreamBooth and LoRA can become unstable or lose subject fidelity over longer training. In controllable generation, COFT and OFT show strong control performance and better convergence behavior, with COFT offering the extra stability that comes from the deviation constraint.

## Why COFT is easier to use in practice

For real-world workflows, the biggest advantage of COFT may be operational simplicity. If a fine-tuning method is sensitive to the exact number of steps or the learning rate, then every new subject or dataset becomes a tuning problem. That slows down experimentation and makes deployment harder.

COFT reduces that burden by making the training dynamics smoother. The paper reports that with a proper choice of $\epsilon$, both the learning rate and iteration count become easier to set. In other words, the method is less fragile.

That does not mean COFT eliminates hyperparameter choices entirely. It still depends on $\epsilon$, which controls how much the model is allowed to deviate from the pretrained weights. But this parameter is conceptually useful: larger values make COFT behave more like OFT, while smaller values keep the model closer to the original diffusion model.

## The big takeaway

COFT is not a completely different fine-tuning recipe. It is a refinement of OFT that makes the stability constraint explicit.

The underlying message is that successful fine-tuning is not just about giving the model more capacity to learn. It is also about preserving the structure that made the pretrained model useful in the first place. COFT does that by combining two ideas:

- preserve pairwise neuron angles through orthogonal transformations;
- restrict how far those transformations can move from the identity.

That combination gives the model enough room to adapt while keeping it anchored to the pretrained prior. In the paper’s experiments, that translates into more stable training, easier tuning, and strong performance on both subject-driven and controllable generation.

For diffusion model workflows, that is a valuable trade-off: not just better control, but control that is more predictable.
