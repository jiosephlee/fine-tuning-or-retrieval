\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Why Angles Matter More Than Weight Size in AI Image Generation

Text-to-image diffusion models can generate remarkably realistic images from prompts, but adapting them to new tasks is tricky. If you finetune them too aggressively, they often lose the broad visual knowledge they learned during pretraining. If you finetune them too conservatively, they fail to learn the new task.

The paper *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning* argues that one reason this balance is so hard is that the usual way of thinking about finetuning is incomplete. We often focus on how much the weights move in Euclidean space, but the paper suggests that what really matters for preserving semantics is something more geometric: the angles between neurons.

## The core idea: semantics live in directions

A neural network layer contains many neurons, each represented by a weight vector. In standard finetuning, we usually think in terms of changing those weights by a small amount. The intuition is that if the new weights stay close to the old ones, the model should keep its original behavior.

But the paper argues that raw distance is not the whole story. In many neural representations, the direction of a vector carries more semantic information than its magnitude. This is consistent with prior work on angular similarity and hyperspherical representations. In other words, the model’s “meaning” is often encoded in how neuron vectors point relative to each other, not just how long they are.

The authors support this with a toy autoencoder experiment. They show that angular information can almost reconstruct the input images, while magnitude alone does not contain useful semantic information. The takeaway is simple: if you want to preserve what a model “knows,” preserving neuron directions may matter more than preserving exact weight values.

## Hyperspherical energy: a way to measure structure

To make this intuition precise, the paper uses a quantity called hyperspherical energy. For a layer of neurons, hyperspherical energy measures the pairwise similarity among normalized weight vectors, using a cosine-like geometry on the unit sphere.

You can think of it as a summary of the layer’s internal arrangement. If the pairwise angles between neurons stay the same, then the layer preserves its relational structure. That structure is important because it captures how neurons are organized relative to one another, which the paper argues is closely tied to semantic behavior.

This is a key shift in perspective. Instead of asking, “How far did the weights move?”, OFT asks, “Did the geometry of the layer change?”

## Why orthogonal transformations?

The paper’s main method, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), is built around a neat mathematical fact: orthogonal transformations preserve angles. If you rotate or reflect all neurons in a layer by the same orthogonal matrix, their pairwise angles stay exactly the same.

That means the hyperspherical energy of the layer is preserved.

This is the central trick. Rather than directly updating every weight vector freely, OFT learns an orthogonal transformation applied to a pretrained layer. In effect, it changes the coordinate system of the layer without distorting the angular relationships inside it.

This is appealing for two reasons. First, it gives a principled way to finetune while preserving structure. Second, it helps explain why the model can adapt to new tasks without completely forgetting its pretrained visual knowledge.

## Why this helps text-to-image models

The paper studies two kinds of downstream tasks for text-to-image diffusion models:

Subject-driven generation means the model must generate a particular subject, such as a specific person or object, from just a few example images plus a text prompt. Controllable generation means the model must follow an extra signal such as edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks.

Both tasks require the model to change behavior in a targeted way, but not to lose its general image-generation ability. That is exactly where preserving neuron geometry helps. If the finetuning process changes the model’s semantic structure too much, the model may forget identity details or fail to follow the input control signal reliably.

By preserving pairwise angular relationships, OFT aims to keep the pretrained model’s semantic foundation intact while still allowing task-specific adaptation.

## Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning adds a safety rail

The paper also proposes Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning, or COFT. This version adds an explicit constraint that keeps the finetuned model close to the pretrained one in a controlled way.

The motivation is practical. Even if orthogonal transformations preserve angular structure, there can still be a trade-off between flexibility and stability. COFT makes that trade-off explicit by limiting how far the orthogonal transformation can deviate from the identity transformation.

In plain terms, OFT says: preserve the geometry. COFT says: preserve the geometry, and don’t move too far away from the original model either.

## Why not just use small learning rates?

A lot of finetuning methods rely on the idea that a small learning rate will keep the model close to its starting point. But the paper argues that closeness in Euclidean distance is not enough to guarantee semantic preservation.

Two models can be close in weight space and still differ in important structural ways. Conversely, a model can undergo a structured transformation that changes the weights substantially while preserving the semantic relationships that matter.

This is why OFT is interesting: it replaces an implicit heuristic with an explicit geometric constraint.

## What the experiments show

The paper tests OFT on subject-driven generation and controllable generation using Stable Diffusion v1.5. Across these tasks, OFT and COFT generally outperform baselines such as DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, and T2I-Adapter in terms of generation quality, convergence speed, and stability.

For subject-driven generation, the method preserves identity better and converges more stably. For controllable generation, it follows the control input more accurately and trains faster. Importantly, OFT does this without adding test-time inference overhead, since the learned orthogonal transformation can be merged into the pretrained weights.

The broader lesson is that preserving structure during finetuning is not just a theoretical preference. It has practical effects on how well the model retains its visual understanding.

## The bigger takeaway

The paper’s main contribution is not just a new finetuning trick. It is a shift in how we think about preserving knowledge in neural networks.

If neuron directions encode much of the semantic content, then finetuning should respect those directions. Hyperspherical energy gives a way to measure that structure, and orthogonal transformations give a way to preserve it while adapting the model.

So the title could be summarized like this: when adapting image generators, the key may not be how much the weights change, but whether the angles between them still make sense.

That is the intuition behind Orthogonal Finetuning, and it helps explain why preserving geometry can preserve generation quality.
