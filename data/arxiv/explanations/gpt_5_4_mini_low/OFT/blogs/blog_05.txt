\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# A Smarter Way to Fine-Tune Diffusion Models: Inside COFT and the Stability Problem

Fine-tuning a text-to-image diffusion model sounds straightforward: take a pretrained model, train it a bit more on a downstream task, and get better control. In practice, it is much trickier. If the update is too weak, the model barely learns the new task. If it is too flexible, it can drift away from the pretrained model and lose the rich generation ability that made it useful in the first place.

That trade-off is the motivation behind Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning, or COFT. COFT is the more stable variant of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), introduced in the paper *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning*. The basic idea is simple: preserve the semantic structure of the pretrained model while still allowing enough movement to adapt to a new task.

## Why fine-tuning can be unstable

The paper looks at two downstream settings for text-to-image diffusion models: subject-driven generation and controllable generation.

In subject-driven generation, the model must learn to generate a specific subject from only a few reference images. In controllable generation, the model must follow extra signals such as edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks. Both tasks need adaptation, but they also need the model to remember how to generate high-quality images in the first place.

A common fine-tuning strategy is to update the pretrained weights with a small learning rate. This implicitly tries to keep the new model close to the old one. But the paper argues that simple Euclidean closeness is not enough to capture whether the model’s semantics are preserved. A model can be close in weight space and still lose important structure.

OFT tackles that by preserving pairwise angular relationships between neurons, using an orthogonal transformation. Since orthogonal transformations preserve angles, they preserve the hyperspherical energy of each layer. The paper finds this matters for keeping the pretrained semantic generation ability intact.

But even OFT can still be more flexible than desirable in some settings. That is where COFT comes in.

## What COFT adds on top of OFT

COFT keeps OFT’s orthogonal transformation, but adds an explicit constraint that limits how far the transformed weights can move from the pretrained model.

Formally, COFT uses the same forward pass structure as OFT, where the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is transformed by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$:

\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0, \quad \text{s.t. } \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}.
\]

COFT adds one more requirement:

\[
\norm{\bm{R} - \bm{I}} \leq \epsilon.
\]

This extra term keeps the learned transformation within a bounded neighborhood of the identity matrix. In plain terms, COFT says: you may rotate or reflect the weights, but not too much.

That matters because the identity matrix corresponds to the original pretrained model. If the learned orthogonal transform stays close to identity, the fine-tuning process remains anchored to the pretrained behavior. The model can still adapt, but it does so more cautiously.

## Why this helps training stability

The paper’s main point about COFT is not just that it works, but that it makes training easier to manage.

With standard fine-tuning, the learning rate and number of epochs can be very sensitive. Train too long, and the model may collapse or start producing worse outputs. Train too little, and it never really adapts. This is especially frustrating in workflows where the best hyperparameters vary by subject or dataset.

COFT makes the optimization more predictable because the constraint limits the amount of freedom available to the model. The paper shows that this can improve finetuning stability and reduce sensitivity to the training schedule.

There is also a practical side to this. COFT is designed so that the orthogonality constraint can still be handled efficiently using the Cayley parameterization. The additional deviation constraint is enforced by working with the matrix used inside the parameterization, and the paper suggests projecting it during optimization. The result is a method that keeps the mathematical structure of OFT while adding a useful stability guardrail.

## A better balance between flexibility and regularity

The paper frames fine-tuning as a balancing act.

Standard fine-tuning is highly flexible, but that flexibility comes with a cost: the pretrained structure can drift or collapse. OFT already improves this by preserving the angular relationships among neurons. COFT goes one step further by explicitly limiting how far the update can move from the original model.

This is a useful idea because downstream adaptation does not always need maximum freedom. Often, it needs just enough freedom to encode the new task while keeping the original model’s generalization intact.

The paper’s experiments support this intuition. In subject-driven generation, OFT and COFT both converge stably, while DreamBooth and LoRA can become unstable or lose subject fidelity over longer training. In controllable generation, COFT and OFT show strong control performance and better convergence behavior, with COFT offering the extra stability that comes from the deviation constraint.

## Why COFT is easier to use in practice

For real-world workflows, the biggest advantage of COFT may be operational simplicity. If a fine-tuning method is sensitive to the exact number of steps or the learning rate, then every new subject or dataset becomes a tuning problem. That slows down experimentation and makes deployment harder.

COFT reduces that burden by making the training dynamics smoother. The paper reports that with a proper choice of $\epsilon$, both the learning rate and iteration count become easier to set. In other words, the method is less fragile.

That does not mean COFT eliminates hyperparameter choices entirely. It still depends on $\epsilon$, which controls how much the model is allowed to deviate from the pretrained weights. But this parameter is conceptually useful: larger values make COFT behave more like OFT, while smaller values keep the model closer to the original diffusion model.

## The big takeaway

COFT is not a completely different fine-tuning recipe. It is a refinement of OFT that makes the stability constraint explicit.

The underlying message is that successful fine-tuning is not just about giving the model more capacity to learn. It is also about preserving the structure that made the pretrained model useful in the first place. COFT does that by combining two ideas:

- preserve pairwise neuron angles through orthogonal transformations;
- restrict how far those transformations can move from the identity.

That combination gives the model enough room to adapt while keeping it anchored to the pretrained prior. In the paper’s experiments, that translates into more stable training, easier tuning, and strong performance on both subject-driven and controllable generation.

For diffusion model workflows, that is a valuable trade-off: not just better control, but control that is more predictable.
