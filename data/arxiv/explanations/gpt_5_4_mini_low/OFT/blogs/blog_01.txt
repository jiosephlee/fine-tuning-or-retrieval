\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Teaching AI New Image Tricks Without Making It Forget: A Simple Guide to Orthogonal Finetuning

Text-to-image diffusion models can already turn prompts like “a red fox in a snowy forest” into impressive images. But once you want these models to do something more specific, such as generate a particular subject from a few photos or follow an extra control signal like an edge map or segmentation mask, you usually have to finetune them.

That is where things get tricky. Finetuning often improves the new task, but it can also damage the model’s original ability to generate diverse, high-quality images. In practice, the model may become unstable, overfit, or even collapse. The paper *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning* proposes a simple idea to reduce that problem: instead of freely changing the model weights, update them through orthogonal transformations, which act like controlled rotations and reflections.

## The finetuning problem

The paper focuses on two downstream tasks for text-to-image diffusion models.

In subject-driven generation, the model sees a few images of a specific subject and a text prompt, and it should generate that same subject in new settings. DreamBooth is a well-known example of this kind of setup.

In controllable generation, the model gets an extra input such as canny edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks, and it should follow both that signal and the text prompt. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are representative methods here.

Both tasks need a pretrained diffusion model to adapt without losing what it already knows. Standard finetuning tries to do this by making small weight updates. Low-rank methods like LoRA add a constrained update path. But neither approach gives a strong guarantee that the pretrained generative behavior will stay intact.

The key question in the paper is: what property of the pretrained model should we preserve during finetuning?

## Why angles matter more than raw weight distance

The authors argue that Euclidean distance between weight matrices is not a complete measure of whether a model has preserved its semantics. Instead, they look at the angles between neurons.

Their main structural measure is hyperspherical energy, which depends on pairwise hyperspherical similarity, such as cosine similarity, between neurons in the same layer. Intuitively, this captures how neurons are arranged relative to each other on a unit hypersphere. The paper’s hypothesis is that preserving this arrangement helps preserve the model’s original semantic generation ability.

That leads to a useful observation: if you apply the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer, then pairwise angles are preserved. In other words, the layer is rotated or reflected, but its internal geometry stays the same.

This is the central idea behind Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT.

## Orthogonal Finetuning in one sentence

OFT finetunes a model by learning an orthogonal matrix $R$ for each layer, so the pretrained weights $W^0$ become

\[
W = R W^0, \qquad R^\top R = I.
\]

Because $R$ is orthogonal, the angles between neurons are preserved. That means the model can change how it represents information, while keeping the pairwise geometric structure of the original layer intact.

You can think of it as adjusting the coordinate system of a layer rather than rewriting the whole layer from scratch.

## Why this helps

The intuition comes from a simple semantic idea: directions matter.

The paper gives a toy autoencoder example showing that angular information can recover images much better than magnitude alone. This supports the idea that neuron directions carry a lot of semantic content. If you want to change what a model generates, changing neuron directions can be effective. But if you let those directions drift too freely, you may destroy the pretrained knowledge.

Orthogonal transformations are a sweet spot. They are expressive enough to change the model’s behavior, but structured enough to avoid arbitrary distortion.

## Making OFT practical

A full orthogonal matrix can still be expensive, so the paper uses efficient parameterizations.

One important choice is the Cayley parameterization, which constructs an orthogonal matrix from a skew-symmetric matrix. To reduce parameter cost further, the paper uses a block-diagonal form, splitting the matrix into smaller orthogonal blocks. This makes OFT much more parameter-efficient.

The paper also compares OFT to LoRA in terms of complexity. For a weight matrix of size $d \times d$, OFT can be very compact, especially with block structure, while still preserving the orthogonal constraint exactly.

## COFT: adding a stability constraint

The authors also propose Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning, or COFT. This version adds another constraint: the learned orthogonal transform should not move too far from the identity matrix.

In practice, this means COFT keeps the finetuned model close to the pretrained one while still allowing useful adaptation. The paper finds that this extra constraint can improve stability even further.

So the difference is:

- OFT preserves layer geometry through orthogonality.
- COFT preserves layer geometry and also limits how far the model can drift.

## What about performance?

The paper evaluates OFT on Stable Diffusion v1.5 for both subject-driven generation and controllable generation.

For subject-driven generation, OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on identity preservation metrics like DINO and CLIP-I, while maintaining good prompt fidelity and diversity. The authors also report that OFT converges more stably and is less sensitive to the exact number of training iterations.

For controllable generation, the paper tests tasks such as:

- canny edge to image,
- segmentation map to image,
- landmark to face.

Here too, OFT and COFT show strong control performance, often better than ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA in the reported metrics. The paper also introduces a control consistency metric that checks whether the generated image matches the original control signal when that signal is re-extracted from the output.

A practical advantage is that OFT does not add inference-time overhead. At test time, the learned orthogonal transform is simply folded into the pretrained weights.

## Why this is interesting

OFT is appealing because it replaces a vague idea, “make small updates,” with a more structured principle: preserve the internal angular geometry of the pretrained model.

That gives a cleaner trade-off between flexibility and regularity. The model can learn new tasks, but it is less likely to forget the visual knowledge it already had. The result is a finetuning method that is both efficient and stable.

## Takeaway

The main lesson of the paper is simple: when adapting a text-to-image diffusion model, not all weight changes are equally harmful. If you rotate the layer’s neurons instead of freely reshaping them, you can often teach the model new tricks without making it forget the old ones.

Orthogonal Finetuning uses that idea directly. It preserves pairwise neuron angles, keeps the pretrained structure intact, and delivers strong results on both subject-driven and controllable generation. For a problem where finetuning often destabilizes the model, that is a surprisingly elegant solution.
