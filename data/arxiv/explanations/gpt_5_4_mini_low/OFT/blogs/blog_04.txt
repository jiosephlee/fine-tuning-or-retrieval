\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# From Personalized Avatars to Sketch-Guided Images: Where Orthogonal Finetuning Shines

Text-to-image diffusion models are already good at turning prompts into photorealistic images. The harder problem is making them do something specific without breaking what they already know. If you want a model to generate a particular person, object, or scene layout, plain prompt engineering often is not enough.

That is the setting of **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**. The paper proposes a finetuning method for adapting pretrained diffusion models to downstream image-generation tasks while trying to preserve the model’s original generative ability. The key idea is simple: instead of freely changing weights, OFT updates neuron directions through an orthogonal transformation, which keeps the pairwise angular relationships between neurons intact. In practice, that gives a better balance between flexibility and stability.

The paper focuses on two practical use cases:

- **Subject-driven generation**, where the model learns to generate a specific subject from just a few reference images.
- **Controllable generation**, where the model follows extra guidance such as edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks.

These are exactly the kinds of tasks where finetuning is useful, but also where overfinetuning can quickly ruin image quality or identity preservation.

## Why these tasks are hard

A pretrained text-to-image model already knows how to generate a wide range of images. The problem is that downstream tasks often ask it to do something more constrained.

In subject-driven generation, you may want the model to generate the same person, toy, or object in new contexts. The model must preserve identity while still following new text prompts.

In controllable generation, the model must obey an additional signal. For example, a canny edge map may specify coarse shapes, a segmentation map may define scene layout, or facial landmarks may specify pose. The model has to follow that structure and still produce a realistic image.

Naively finetuning the full model can work for a while, but it can also cause drift, instability, or collapse. The paper argues that simply keeping the finetuned weights close to the pretrained weights in Euclidean distance is not enough. Instead, it introduces a more structural constraint based on hyperspherical energy, and uses orthogonal transformations to preserve it.

## The core idea behind OFT

At a high level, OFT changes a layer by multiplying its weights with an orthogonal matrix. Orthogonal transformations preserve angles. The paper connects this to the idea that angular relationships among neurons encode important semantic information.

This matters because the method is designed to modify what the model generates without destroying the structure it learned during pretraining. The paper also introduces **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)**, which adds a radius constraint to keep the finetuned model even closer to the pretrained one.

There is also an efficient parameterization using a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix, so the method remains practical. Importantly, OFT does not add inference-time overhead: after finetuning, the learned orthogonal transformation can simply be folded into the pretrained weights.

## Subject-driven generation: learning a person or object from a few images

One of the most compelling applications in the paper is **subject-driven generation**. The setup is familiar from DreamBooth-style finetuning: give the model a few images of a subject and a text prompt, and ask it to generate new images of that same subject in different scenes or styles.

The challenge is preserving identity. If you finetune too aggressively, the subject may drift. If you finetune too conservatively, the model may ignore the new subject and keep generating something generic.

The paper evaluates OFT on this task using the DreamBooth framework and compares it with DreamBooth and LoRA. The results show two important behaviors.

First, OFT and COFT converge stably. The paper reports that after a relatively small number of iterations, they already achieve strong subject preservation and prompt following. Second, they remain stable even when training continues longer. This is notable because DreamBooth can start producing collapsed images with too much finetuning, and LoRA can fail to preserve the subject identity in some cases.

The paper’s qualitative examples show that OFT can keep the subject recognizable while still placing it in new prompts and contexts. That makes it useful for personalized generation, such as generating a consistent avatar or object across multiple scenes.

Quantitatively, the paper uses metrics such as DINO and CLIP-I for subject fidelity, CLIP-T for text alignment, and LPIPS for diversity. OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on the identity-preservation metrics, while maintaining comparable or better prompt fidelity and diversity.

## Controllable generation: following edges, layouts, and poses

The second major use case is **controllable generation**, where the model receives an extra signal beyond text. The paper evaluates three tasks:

- **Canny edge to image** on COCO.
- **Segmentation map to image** on ADE20K.
- **Landmark to face** on CelebA-HQ.

These tasks are a good stress test for finetuning methods because the model must obey a spatial constraint while keeping the image realistic.

The paper compares OFT and COFT against ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. The results are strong across both convergence and final quality.

On the landmark-to-face task, the paper measures how close the generated face landmarks are to the target landmarks. OFT and COFT converge faster than the baselines, and the training process is smoother. The authors note that ControlNet can sometimes show a more sudden emergence of control later in training, while OFT’s behavior is more gradual and predictable.

For evaluation, the paper also introduces a **control consistency** metric. The idea is to estimate the control signal back from the generated image and compare it with the original input control signal. That gives a more direct way to measure whether the model actually followed the condition. For example:

- For edge-guided generation, they compute IoU and F1 score.
- For segmentation-guided generation, they compute mean IoU and accuracy.
- For landmark-guided generation, they compute landmark distance.

By these measures, OFT and COFT show stronger control than the baselines.

Visually, the paper reports that OFT and COFT can produce realistic images that better match the input structure. In the segmentation task, for example, LoRA can fail to follow the mask, while OFT and COFT generate images that respect the layout. In the edge-guided setting, OFT can infer semantically consistent images from rough edge sketches. In the landmark setting, it produces accurate facial poses even in difficult cases.

## Why orthogonal finetuning helps in practice

The practical value of OFT is not just that it works, but that it works in a controlled way.

The paper’s central claim is that preserving pairwise neuron angles helps keep the pretrained semantic structure intact. That gives OFT a useful trade-off: it is flexible enough to adapt to new tasks, but regularized enough to avoid destroying the original model.

This shows up in three ways.

First, **stability**. OFT and COFT are less prone to collapse than naive finetuning.

Second, **sample efficiency**. The paper reports that OFT can converge with fewer training images and fewer epochs than competing methods.

Third, **deployment efficiency**. Unlike some adapter-style approaches, OFT does not add inference overhead. After finetuning, the modified weights can be used like a standard diffusion model.

## What to take away

If you are trying to adapt a text-to-image model for personalized or controllable generation, OFT is a clean and principled alternative to standard finetuning.

The paper’s main message is that **not all parameter changes are equally safe**. Updating weights while preserving angular structure can maintain the semantic knowledge embedded in a pretrained diffusion model. That makes OFT especially appealing for tasks where you want the model to learn something new without forgetting how to generate high-quality images.

In short, OFT shines in exactly the scenarios that make diffusion finetuning useful:

- generating a consistent subject from a few examples,
- following sketches, edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks,
- and doing all of that without sacrificing the base model’s generative quality.

For personalized avatars and control-guided image synthesis, that is a very practical combination.
