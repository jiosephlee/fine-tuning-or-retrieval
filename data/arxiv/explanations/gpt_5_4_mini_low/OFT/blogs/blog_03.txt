\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# OFT vs LoRA vs DreamBooth vs ControlNet: What’s the Best Way to Customize a Diffusion Model?

Text-to-image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion are powerful, but the real challenge starts when you want to adapt them to a specific task. Maybe you want the model to generate a particular subject from just a few reference images. Or maybe you want it to follow an extra control signal, like edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks.

That is exactly the problem tackled in *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning*. The paper introduces **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, and its constrained variant **COFT**, as a way to adapt pretrained diffusion models while preserving their original generative ability.

The main takeaway is simple: among the methods compared in the paper, OFT is designed to be both **efficient** and **stable**. It uses fewer trainable parameters than full finetuning, preserves the pretrained model’s structure more carefully than standard updates, and converges faster than the baselines in the paper’s experiments.

## Why customization is hard

Finetuning a pretrained diffusion model sounds straightforward: update the weights on your downstream task and stop when the outputs look good. In practice, this is risky. If you update too aggressively, the model may lose the semantic knowledge that made it good in the first place.

The paper focuses on two downstream tasks:

- **Subject-driven generation**: generate a specific subject from a few images and a prompt.
- **Controllable generation**: generate images that follow an extra condition, such as edges, segmentation, or landmarks.

Both tasks need a careful balance between **flexibility** and **preservation**. You want the model to adapt, but not forget.

## The common baselines: what each method is doing

The paper compares OFT against several widely used adaptation methods. Each one takes a different approach to the same problem.

### DreamBooth: direct finetuning with preservation loss

DreamBooth adapts a pretrained diffusion model by finetuning it on a few subject images using a reconstruction loss plus a class-specific prior preservation loss. In the paper’s setting, DreamBooth is used as the main baseline for subject-driven generation.

This approach can preserve identity reasonably well, but it is still standard finetuning. The paper reports that it can become unstable with longer training, and after enough iterations it may produce collapsed images.

### LoRA: low-rank updates

LoRA is a parameter-efficient adaptation method. Instead of updating the full weight matrix, it adds a low-rank update. This makes finetuning cheaper and lighter than full updates.

The paper treats LoRA as the most relevant comparison to OFT because both are parameter-efficient. But they work differently:

- **LoRA** updates weights through a low-rank additive structure.
- **OFT** updates weights through a learned orthogonal transformation.

In the experiments, LoRA often converges more slowly and is less stable than OFT and COFT. In subject-driven generation, the paper also reports that LoRA may fail to preserve subject identity as well as OFT.

### ControlNet: adding a control branch

ControlNet is designed for controllable generation. It adapts a pretrained diffusion model by adding a trainable branch that processes the control signal.

This gives strong control, but it also comes with more parameters and test-time overhead than OFT. The paper emphasizes that OFT does **not** add inference overhead: after finetuning, the learned orthogonal transform can simply be absorbed into the pretrained weights.

### T2I-Adapter: lightweight control adaptation

T2I-Adapter is another method for adding control signals to a pretrained model. Like ControlNet, it is designed for controllable generation rather than subject personalization.

The paper compares OFT against T2I-Adapter in control tasks and finds that OFT achieves stronger controllability with fewer trainable parameters and faster convergence.

## What makes OFT different?

The core idea behind OFT is that it does not try to freely move the pretrained weights around. Instead, it learns a **layer-shared orthogonal transformation** of the neurons.

Why is that useful?

The paper argues that the **angles between neurons** carry important semantic information. OFT preserves those pairwise angles, which means it preserves the model’s **hyperspherical energy**. The authors use this as a structural measure of how much the finetuned model differs from the pretrained one.

In simpler terms: OFT tries to change the model in a way that keeps its internal geometry intact.

That is a very different philosophy from naive finetuning. Rather than pushing weights to new values with a small learning rate and hoping the model stays intact, OFT constrains the update to a transformation that is mathematically structured and stable.

## COFT: OFT with an extra safety rail

The paper also introduces **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)**. COFT adds a radius constraint so that the finetuned model stays close to the pretrained model in addition to preserving orthogonality.

This matters because the paper treats OFT as a good balance between flexibility and stability, while COFT pushes stability even further. In the experiments, COFT often behaves more predictably and is less sensitive to hyperparameter choices.

## How the methods compare in practice

The paper evaluates the methods on both subject-driven and controllable generation using Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the base model.

### Subject-driven generation

For subject-driven generation, the paper compares DreamBooth, LoRA, OFT, and COFT.

The reported results show that:

- **OFT and COFT converge stably**
- **OFT and COFT preserve subject identity better than the baselines**
- **LoRA struggles more with identity preservation**
- **DreamBooth can collapse with longer training**
- **OFT is less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations**

The paper also reports quantitative gains using metrics like DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, and LPIPS. OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on subject fidelity metrics, while keeping prompt fidelity and diversity competitive.

### Controllable generation

For controllable generation, the paper compares OFT and COFT against ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA on three tasks:

- canny edge to image
- segmentation map to image
- landmark to face

Here the paper introduces a **control consistency metric**: generate an image, estimate the control signal from that image, and compare it to the original control signal.

The main findings are:

- **OFT and COFT achieve stronger control consistency**
- **They converge faster than the baselines**
- **They are more stable during training**
- **They use far fewer parameters than ControlNet**
- **They do not add inference overhead**

In the landmark-to-face task, the paper notes that OFT reaches the performance that LoRA only matches after many more epochs. The training dynamics are also smoother than ControlNet’s sudden convergence behavior.

## Why orthogonal updates help

The paper’s intuition is that orthogonal transformations let the model change its internal directions without distorting the geometry that encodes the pretrained semantics.

That matters because, for diffusion models, you often want to modify *what* the model generates without breaking *how* it generates realistic images.

The paper argues that this is why OFT converges faster and remains stable: it gives the model enough freedom to adapt while keeping the pretrained structure intact.

## A practical summary

If you zoom out, the comparison looks like this:

- **DreamBooth**: simple subject personalization, but standard finetuning can become unstable.
- **LoRA**: parameter-efficient and popular, but not always strong enough on identity preservation or control.
- **ControlNet**: excellent for controllable generation, but adds more parameters and test-time overhead.
- **T2I-Adapter**: lightweight control adaptation, but still not as strong as OFT in the paper’s benchmarks.
- **OFT / COFT**: parameter-efficient, stable, fast-converging, and designed to preserve the pretrained model’s semantic structure.

The paper’s central claim is not that OFT replaces every existing method in every setting. Rather, it offers a principled finetuning strategy that works especially well when you care about preserving what the pretrained model already knows.

## Bottom line

If your goal is to customize a diffusion model without destroying its generative ability, the paper makes a strong case for **Orthogonal Finetuning**.

Compared with DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, and T2I-Adapter, OFT stands out because it:

- preserves hyperspherical structure in the weights,
- converges faster,
- is more stable during training,
- preserves subject identity better in subject-driven generation,
- improves controllability in control tasks,
- and adds no extra inference overhead.

COFT goes one step further by explicitly constraining how far the model can drift from the pretrained state.

So if you are looking for the paper’s answer to “what’s the best way to customize a diffusion model?”, the answer is: **adapt it orthogonally, not just aggressively**.
