\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### If OFT uses $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$, isn't that just a change of basis that could be canceled by the previous layer?
Question:
I'm confused about the functional effect of OFT. The paper says that OFT learns a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ and replaces a pretrained weight matrix by

$$
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0.
$$

At first glance, this looks like a rotation or reflection of the input-side coordinates for that layer. But if we only rotate the weights of one layer, is this really adding expressive power, or is it just a basis change that another layer could absorb or cancel?

More concretely, for a single linear layer we have

$$
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{x}.
$$

So it seems like OFT is equivalent to rotating the input activation $\mathbf{x}$ before applying the original weights. Why does that help fine-tune the model for a new task rather than just reparameterize the same computation?

I suspect the answer has to do with the surrounding fixed layers and nonlinearities, but I'd like a clearer explanation.
Answer:
At the level of a single isolated linear map, your intuition is right: if you replace $\mathbf{W}^0$ by $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ with $\mathbf{R}$ orthogonal, then

$$
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{x},
$$

so this is equivalent to applying a rotation/reflection to the input coordinates before the original weight matrix. If the *entire network* were just a stack of linear layers and you rotated every layer consistently, then yes, this would mostly amount to a change of basis and could be canceled out.

But that is not the setting of OFT. The paper is explicit that OFT is a finetuning method for a pretrained diffusion model, where only selected layers are adapted, and the surrounding layers are left fixed. In that situation, the orthogonal transform is not “free” to cancel globally, because the next layer does not automatically receive the inverse transform. The network as a whole is not invariant to rotating one layer’s hidden representation unless the adjacent layers are transformed in a coordinated way, which OFT does not do.

This is exactly why the paper emphasizes that OFT “adjusts the canonical coordinate system for the neurons in the same layer” while preserving pairwise angles. The key point is not that the function of one linear layer changes in an arbitrary way; rather, OFT changes the internal geometry of the representation in a controlled, structure-preserving way. That gives the model a way to move into a better task-specific configuration without badly disrupting the pretrained semantic structure.

The surrounding nonlinearities matter too. In a transformer or diffusion U-Net, the rotated activations pass through nonlinearities, normalization, residual connections, attention, and other fixed components. Once you have those, a basis change at one point in the network is no longer something that can be algebraically “pulled through” and canceled. So even though the local formula looks like a coordinate change, the *global* effect on the network is a genuine functional adaptation.

The paper’s intuition is that this is a good trade-off: an orthogonal transform is flexible enough to change neuron directions, which they argue carries semantic information, but restrictive enough to preserve hyperspherical energy and therefore keep the pretrained generative behavior stable. That is why OFT is not just a reparameterization in practice, even though it has a basis-change interpretation at the level of a single layer.
