\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why does the paper define hyperspherical energy as $\sum_{i \ne j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ instead of directly using cosine similarity?
Question:
The paper motivates OFT using pairwise hyperspherical similarity and says hyperspherical energy characterizes neuron relationships on the unit sphere. But then the actual definition they use is

$$
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i \ne j} \left\| \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j \right\|^{-1}.
$$

I would have expected something more directly based on cosine similarity, like a sum over $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j$ or angular distances.

Is there a specific reason for using inverse Euclidean distance on the unit sphere? Since for unit vectors this is related to the angle anyway, is this just inherited from prior hyperspherical energy literature, or does it have some special optimization or theoretical property here?

I'm trying to understand whether the exact form of the energy matters for OFT, or whether any angle-preserving similarity measure would have led to the same method.
Answer:
The short answer is that the paper is following the standard definition of hyperspherical energy from the cited hypersphere-regularization literature, and on the unit sphere inverse Euclidean distance is just a convenient way to write a function of pairwise angle. It is not meant to be a fundamentally different notion from cosine similarity; it is a particular “repulsive” energy whose preservation is guaranteed by orthogonal transformations.

For two unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j$, the Euclidean distance is determined entirely by their cosine similarity:
\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^2
= 2 - 2\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j
= 2(1-\cos\theta_{ij}).
\]
So
\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}
= \bigl(2-2\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\bigr)^{-1/2}.
\]
That means the energy is still entirely angle-based. Using $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j$ directly would also be angle-based, but the inverse-distance form has a useful interpretation inherited from the hyperspherical energy literature: it acts like a repulsive potential on the sphere, penalizing neurons that become too close much more strongly than neurons that are already far apart. This “spread the points out on the sphere” viewpoint is exactly what hyperspherical energy is designed to capture.

In the context of OFT, the exact functional form is not what drives the method. The key property is that if all neurons in a layer are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$, then pairwise angles, cosine similarities, and Euclidean distances on the unit sphere are all preserved. So the energy
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\ne j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}
\]
is unchanged under $\mathbf{W}\mapsto \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}$. That invariance is what OFT exploits: it can change the coordinate system of the layer without changing the pairwise geometric structure that the paper argues encodes semantic information.

So yes, this is largely inherited from prior hyperspherical energy work, but not arbitrarily so. The inverse-distance form gives a standard, geometry-sensitive energy whose preservation is exactly equivalent to preserving the pairwise spherical structure that OFT cares about. A sum of raw cosine similarities would be related, but it would not be the canonical hyperspherical energy used in that line of work, and the paper’s theoretical and empirical discussion is built around that established notion.
