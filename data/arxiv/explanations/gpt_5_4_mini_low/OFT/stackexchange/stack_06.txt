\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How is the COFT constraint $\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon$ actually enforced when using Cayley parameterization?
Question:
In the constrained version, the paper introduces

$$
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}, \quad \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}, \quad \|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon.
$$

Then it says that with the Cayley transform,

$$
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}, \quad \mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top,
$$

we can use the approximation

$$
\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2)
$$

and therefore move the constraint to something like $\|\mathbf{Q}\| \le \epsilon'$.

This is the part where I get lost. Is this really equivalent, or only approximately equivalent for small $\mathbf{Q}$? If it is only approximate, then what norm are they projecting under in practice, and how much does the actual bound on $\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\|$ depend on higher-order terms?

I'm not sure whether COFT is enforcing a true geometric constraint on $\mathbf{R}$ or just a heuristic proxy through $\mathbf{Q}$.
Answer:
It is only an approximation, not an exact equivalence.

What the paper is doing is the following. In COFT the desired constraint is on the orthogonal matrix itself, namely $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\leq \epsilon$. But because $\mathbf{R}$ is not optimized directly, it is parameterized by a skew-symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ through the Cayley transform
$$
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}, \qquad \mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^\top.
$$
For small $\mathbf{Q}$, the Neumann-series expansion gives
$$
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} \approx \mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}^2+\cdots,
$$
so
$$
\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2).
$$
This is why they say the deviation of $\mathbf{R}$ from identity can be controlled by constraining $\mathbf{Q}$ near $0$. But that is only a first-order approximation: the actual difference is
$$
\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}=2\mathbf{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2),
$$
so the higher-order terms are ignored in the argument that motivates the constraint transfer. There is no exact identity of the form “$\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ if and only if $\|\mathbf{Q}\|\leq \epsilon'$”.

The paper is quite explicit about this being a practical move: it says the constraint on $\mathbf{Q}$ “can be easily enforced by projected gradient descent,” and that $\epsilon'$ is “another error hyperparameter.” That means they are not deriving a mathematically exact bound on $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|$ from a chosen $\epsilon'$, but using $\|\mathbf{Q}\|\leq \epsilon'$ as a proxy constraint that is easier to project onto. In practice, you would project $\mathbf{Q}$ back into a norm ball after each update, while maintaining skew-symmetry. The paper does not specify a more exotic projection; the natural reading is projection in the same matrix norm used in the approximation argument, namely the operator norm they mention when discussing convergence of the Neumann series.

So the right way to think about COFT is: it enforces orthogonality exactly via Cayley parametrization, and it enforces proximity to the pretrained model only approximately by keeping $\mathbf{Q}$ small. The true bound on $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|$ does depend on the neglected higher-order terms, so the guarantee is local and approximate rather than a globally exact geometric constraint. In other words, COFT is a principled heuristic proxy, but a very natural one: small skew-symmetric $\mathbf{Q}$ means a small orthogonal perturbation away from identity.
