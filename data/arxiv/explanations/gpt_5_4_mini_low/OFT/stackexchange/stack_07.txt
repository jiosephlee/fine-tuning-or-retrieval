\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What norm is used in the COFT constraint $\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon$, and how should I interpret it geometrically?
Question:
The paper says COFT constrains the finetuned model to stay within a neighborhood of the pretrained model by requiring

$$
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon.
$$

But I couldn't tell which norm this is supposed to be. Is it the Frobenius norm, spectral norm, or something else?

This seems important, because different norms correspond to very different notions of how far an orthogonal matrix can move from the identity. For example, a small Frobenius norm controls the average amount of rotation across dimensions, while a small spectral norm controls the worst-case direction.

Do the authors specify this somewhere implicitly, and is there an intuitive geometric meaning of $\epsilon$ in terms of rotation angle?
Answer:
In the paper, the norm is not explicitly redefined in the COFT equation, but the surrounding derivation makes it pretty clear that they are thinking in terms of the operator norm, i.e. the spectral norm induced by the Euclidean vector norm.

The key clue is this sentence:

> “under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm”

They then use the approximation
$$
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} \approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2),
$$
and say the constraint $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le \epsilon$ can be moved to an equivalent constraint on $\|\mathbf{Q}\|$. That argument is naturally stated in operator norm, because the Neumann-series convergence condition is $\|\mathbf{Q}\|<1$ in an induced matrix norm, and the spectral norm is the standard choice when one says “operator norm” without further qualification.

So the safest reading is: $\|\cdot\|$ here means the spectral/operator norm, not the Frobenius norm.

Geometrically, this constraint means that the orthogonal transform $\mathbf{R}$ is close to the identity as a linear map, so it cannot rotate any unit vector by too much in the worst case. If $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal, all of its eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, and being close to $\mathbf{I}$ in spectral norm means those eigenvalues stay near $1$, i.e. the principal rotation angles are small. In that sense, $\epsilon$ controls the maximum angular deviation allowed by the finetuning. The paper itself phrases this intuition informally: larger $\epsilon$ makes COFT behave more like unconstrained OFT, while smaller $\epsilon$ keeps the finetuned model closer to the pretrained one.

If you want a more concrete picture, for a pure rotation in a 2D plane by angle $\theta$, the spectral distance to the identity is
$$
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_2 = 2\left|\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\right|,
$$
so small $\epsilon$ corresponds to small rotation angles. In higher dimensions, the same idea applies to the largest principal rotation angle. By contrast, a Frobenius norm bound would measure the total amount of rotation spread across all dimensions, which is a different and less worst-case notion than what the paper’s operator-norm argument suggests.

So, in short: the paper does not spell it out in a standalone definition, but the derivation indicates the spectral/operator norm, and geometrically $\epsilon$ is best understood as a bound on the maximum allowed deviation of the orthogonal transform from the identity, i.e. a bound on how much the layer can rotate its neuron directions.
