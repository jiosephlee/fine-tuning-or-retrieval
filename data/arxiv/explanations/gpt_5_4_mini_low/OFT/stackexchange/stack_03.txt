\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why is preserving pairwise angles between weight vectors supposed to preserve semantic generation ability?
Question:
The key hypothesis of the paper seems to be that preserving hyperspherical energy, or equivalently preserving pairwise angular relationships between neurons in a layer, helps preserve the pretrained model's semantic generation ability.

I understand that angular information can matter, and the paper mentions prior work like SphereNet and a toy autoencoder experiment. But I'm struggling with the jump from "angles can encode semantics" to "preserving pairwise angles among weights should preserve the semantics learned by a diffusion model."

Why should the set of pairwise angles between weight vectors be the right structural quantity here? For example, couldn't two weight matrices have the same pairwise angles among columns but still behave quite differently once they interact with nonlinearities, residual connections, layer norms, and the rest of the diffusion architecture?

Is this claim meant as a formal guarantee about function preservation, or more as an empirical inductive bias that seems to work well?
Answer:
The short answer is that the paper is **not claiming a formal theorem that pairwise angles alone guarantee semantic preservation of the whole diffusion model**. What it does claim is narrower: if, during finetuning, you preserve the **pairwise angular geometry of the neurons within a layer**, then you preserve a structural property that the authors argue is strongly correlated with the model’s pretrained semantic behavior. In other words, this is an **inductive bias**, not a full functional equivalence guarantee.

The reasoning in the paper is roughly this. They treat the columns of a weight matrix as neuron vectors and define hyperspherical energy from pairwise cosine-like relationships among the normalized neurons. If you apply the same orthogonal matrix $R$ to all neurons in a layer, then inner products and angles between those neurons are preserved, because orthogonal maps preserve dot products. So the layer’s neurons are not being “reshaped” arbitrarily; they are being rotated or reflected together as a rigid configuration. The paper’s intuition is that this keeps the pretrained layer’s internal relational structure intact while still allowing the layer to move to a different coordinate system that can better fit the downstream task.

That is why the authors emphasize “adjusting the canonical coordinate system” rather than changing the geometry itself. Their hypothesis is that in a pretrained diffusion model, much of the useful semantic information is carried by these angular relationships, so keeping them fixed should help retain the model’s generative knowledge. This is also why they cite prior work on angular features and their toy autoencoder experiment: those are meant as evidence that direction/angle can encode semantic information better than raw magnitude.

But your concern is exactly the right one: preserving pairwise angles among weights in one layer does **not** by itself imply the whole network function is preserved, because the layer is embedded in a much more complicated architecture with nonlinearities, residual paths, normalization, and cross-layer interactions. The paper does not prove otherwise. In fact, the paper explicitly frames OFT as preserving hyperspherical energy, and says this property is **crucial for preserving semantic generation ability** based on empirical observation. That is an empirical claim about a useful regularization principle, not a mathematical guarantee that two networks with the same layerwise angular structure will generate the same outputs.

So the correct interpretation is: orthogonal finetuning is designed to preserve a **structured similarity** to the pretrained model that is more expressive than small Euclidean weight drift, but less destructive than unrestricted finetuning. The orthogonality constraint gives a provable invariance at the level of pairwise neuron angles, and the authors then show experimentally that this tends to preserve generation quality. That makes it a principled heuristic with strong empirical support, not a strict function-preservation theorem.
