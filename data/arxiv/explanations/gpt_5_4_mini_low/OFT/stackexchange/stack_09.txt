\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### In what sense is OFT more parameter-efficient than LoRA when a full orthogonal matrix seems quadratic in $d$?
Question:
The parameter efficiency discussion confused me a bit. A full orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ has about $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom, which is still quadratic in $d$. LoRA with rank $r'$ has about $r'(d+n)$ parameters.

The paper then argues that with block-diagonal structure, OFT can become very efficient, even giving an example where a $128 \times 128$ layer uses fewer parameters for OFT than LoRA.

What I'm unsure about is whether this is a fair apples-to-apples comparison. OFT's efficiency seems to depend heavily on choosing many blocks, which also imposes a strong structural bias. LoRA's efficiency depends on low rank, which is a different kind of restriction.

Is there a principled way to compare the expressive capacity of block-diagonal OFT with rank-constrained LoRA, or is the comparison purely empirical?
Answer:
The short answer is that the paper is comparing two different kinds of restrictions, so there is no clean theoretical notion of “same expressive capacity” that would make OFT and LoRA directly equivalent. The comparison in the paper is mainly about parameter count and empirical finetuning behavior, not about a proven capacity ordering.

What OFT is counting is the number of free parameters needed to specify an orthogonal transform. A full orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ indeed has about $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom, so by itself it is quadratic. The paper’s efficiency claim comes from not using an unconstrained full matrix in practice, but a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix. If the matrix is split into $r$ blocks, each block lives in dimension about $d/r$, so the total number of degrees of freedom drops to about
\[
r \cdot \frac{(d/r)(d/r-1)}{2} \approx \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{d^2}{r}\right),
\]
and with shared blocks even further. So yes, the efficiency depends heavily on the block structure.

But that is not merely a trick to “cheat” the comparison; it is part of the method’s inductive bias. LoRA and OFT are constraining the update in fundamentally different ways. LoRA adds a low-rank correction to the weight matrix, so the updated weight is of the form
\[
W^0 + \Delta W,\qquad \mathrm{rank}(\Delta W)\le r',
\]
which says the change lies in a low-dimensional additive subspace. OFT instead uses
\[
W = R W^0,\qquad R^\top R = I,
\]
so it is multiplicative and constrained to preserve pairwise angles between neurons. The block-diagonal version just makes that orthogonal transform cheaper to parameterize.

So the fair way to read the paper is: OFT is not claiming to be “more expressive per parameter” in some universal theoretical sense. It is claiming that, for these diffusion finetuning tasks, a structured orthogonal update can preserve pretrained semantics better while using a small number of trainable parameters, and that block-diagonal orthogonal matrices can be made cheaper than the particular LoRA setting they benchmark against. The paper itself is quite explicit that the block structure introduces extra bias and that improving parameter efficiency “in a more effective and less biased way” is an open problem.

So the comparison is partly empirical and partly architectural. There is no principled theorem in the paper that says block-diagonal OFT strictly dominates rank-constrained LoRA in expressive capacity. Instead, the paper argues that the two methods encode different priors, and that OFT’s prior is better matched to preserving the pretrained diffusion model’s structure. That is why the authors emphasize stability, convergence, and preservation of hyperspherical energy rather than a general capacity equivalence.
