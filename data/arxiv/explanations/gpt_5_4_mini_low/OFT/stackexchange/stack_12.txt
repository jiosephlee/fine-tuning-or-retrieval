\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why would minimizing hyperspherical energy be good for classification but bad for finetuning a pretrained diffusion model?
Question:
The paper contrasts OFT with prior orthogonal over-parameterized training work, saying that in classification one may want to minimize hyperspherical energy because uniformly spread neurons can improve generalization, but for finetuning diffusion models this could destroy pretrained semantic structure.

I follow the high-level claim, but I don't quite see the mechanism. If low hyperspherical energy means less redundancy and more uniform neuron directions, why is that beneficial in classification but potentially harmful in generation? Is the idea that a pretrained generative model already contains a highly task-specific geometry that should be preserved rather than "regularized away"?

A more concrete explanation of this difference would help me understand why OFT preserves the original energy instead of trying to optimize it toward some universal optimum.
Answer:
The key difference is that in the two settings, hyperspherical energy is playing two very different roles.

In orthogonal over-parameterized training for classification, the model is being learned from scratch, so there is no pretrained semantic geometry to protect. In that context, the paper you cite from \cite{liu2021orthogonal} uses orthogonal transformations to keep the neurons well spread out on the hypersphere, which corresponds to low hyperspherical energy. Intuitively, that encourages the network to use its representational capacity efficiently: fewer redundant or highly correlated neurons, better-conditioned features, and often better generalization. Since the model is still free to organize itself around the classification task, “making the neurons more uniform” is a useful regularizer rather than a destructive one.

For finetuning a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model, however, the situation is inverted. The pretrained weights already encode a highly structured semantic representation learned from large-scale image-text data. The paper explicitly argues that pairwise neuron angles carry semantic information, and that preserving the hyperspherical energy preserves that relational structure. So here, the goal is not to find a universally “better” geometry, but to stay close to the geometry that already makes the model capable of generating meaningful images. If you push the layer toward a new minimum-energy configuration, you are no longer just removing redundancy; you are actively rearranging the neuron directions that the pretrained model uses to represent concepts, textures, composition, and prompt-image alignment. That can erase or distort the semantic organization the model already has.

So the phrase “minimizing hyperspherical energy” is beneficial in classification because the representation is being built, whereas it can be harmful in diffusion finetuning because the representation is already built and should be preserved. The paper’s intuition is essentially that the pretrained model is not an arbitrary starting point; it is a delicate semantic manifold. OFT therefore does not try to optimize hyperspherical energy toward some new optimum. Instead, it preserves the original hyperspherical energy by restricting updates to orthogonal transformations, since orthogonal maps preserve pairwise angles exactly. This lets the model adapt to the downstream task while keeping the pretrained neuron geometry intact.

That is also why the paper emphasizes preserving energy rather than minimizing it. In finetuning, the objective is a trade-off between flexibility and stability: enough freedom to learn the new control signal or subject, but not so much freedom that the model “forgets” how to generate coherent images. Orthogonal finetuning is a way of changing coordinates without changing the underlying geometry, which is why it fits diffusion finetuning much better than the “spread everything out as much as possible” principle used in classification.
