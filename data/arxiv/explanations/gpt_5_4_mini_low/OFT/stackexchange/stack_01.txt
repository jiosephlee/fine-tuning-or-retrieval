\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why does multiplying a weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix preserve the paper's hyperspherical energy?
Question:
I'm reading the OFT paper and I want to make sure I understand the main claim correctly. For a layer with weights $\mathbf{W}^0 = \{\mathbf{w}_1^0, \dots, \mathbf{w}_n^0\}$, they define hyperspherical energy using normalized neurons, for example

$$
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i \ne j} \left\| \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j \right\|^{-1}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}.
$$

Then they say that if $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ with an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$, the hyperspherical energy is preserved exactly.

I can see informally that orthogonal transforms preserve lengths and angles, but I'm still not fully seeing the exact argument for the normalized neurons in this definition. Is the reason simply that

$$
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{R}\mathbf{b}\| = \|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\|
$$

for all vectors $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}$, so every pairwise term stays the same after normalization? Or is there some more subtle point because the normalization happens before measuring distances?

A step-by-step explanation of why $\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0) = \mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)$ would help.
Answer:
Yes, the reason is essentially the one you wrote, but it helps to unpack why the normalization does not introduce any extra complication.

The key fact is that an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ preserves Euclidean norms and distances. So for any vector $\mathbf{w}$, we have
$$
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}\|=\|\mathbf{w}\|.
$$
This immediately implies that normalization commutes with the orthogonal transform:
$$
\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}
=\frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}\|}
=\frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}
=\mathbf{R}\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}
=\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}.
$$
So although the hyperspherical energy is defined using normalized neurons, after applying $\mathbf{R}$ the normalized version of each neuron is just the old normalized neuron rotated by the same orthogonal transform.

Now look at one pairwise term in the energy:
$$
\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}_i-\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}_j\right\|
=
\left\|\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\right\|.
$$
Because $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal, it preserves distances, so
$$
\left\|\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\right\|
=
\left\|\mathbf{R}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j)\right\|
=
\left\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\right\|.
$$
Therefore each reciprocal distance term is unchanged:
$$
\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}_i-\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}_j\right\|^{-1}
=
\left\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\right\|^{-1}.
$$
Summing over all $i\neq j$ gives
$$
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)=\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0).
$$

So there is no subtle extra issue from the normalization step. In fact, the normalization is exactly what makes the quantity “hyperspherical”: it throws away magnitudes and keeps only directions. Since orthogonal maps preserve both lengths and angles, they preserve the geometry on the unit sphere, and thus preserve the hyperspherical energy exactly.

A slightly more conceptual way to say it is that OFT is not trying to keep the weights numerically close in Euclidean coordinates; it is trying to preserve the relative angular configuration of the neurons. Orthogonal transformations are precisely the transformations that change coordinates without changing that internal geometry. That is why the paper can claim exact preservation of hyperspherical energy when $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ with $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$.
