\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is meant by a 'neuron' in OFT when the layer is written as $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$?
Question:
The paper considers a fully connected layer written as $\mathbf{W} = \{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ and says $\mathbf{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the $i$-th neuron, with output

$$
\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}.
$$

I'm confused about the terminology here. In standard linear algebra for neural nets, I would usually think of each column of $\mathbf{W}$ as the weight vector for one output unit, but calling that entire vector a "neuron" feels a bit unusual.

So in this paper, when they talk about preserving pairwise neuron angles, are they really just talking about preserving the angles between the column vectors of the weight matrix? If so, does OFT preserve relationships among output channels rather than individual scalar activations?

I want to be sure I'm interpreting the geometry correctly before trying to understand the rest of the method.
Answer:
Yes — in this paper a “neuron” means the whole column vector $\mathbf{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ of a fully connected layer, not a single scalar activation.

The paper is using the common matrix convention for a linear layer written as
$$
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}, \qquad \mathbf{W}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
$$
so each column $\mathbf{w}_i$ is the weight vector for the $i$-th output coordinate $z_i$. In other words, one column corresponds to one output unit / output channel in that layer. The paper then treats those column vectors as the “neurons” whose geometry it wants to preserve.

That is exactly why hyperspherical energy is defined from the normalized columns
$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i=\frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|},
$$
and why preserving “pairwise neuron angles” means preserving the angles between these column vectors, equivalently preserving their cosine similarities. Since the pairwise angle between $\mathbf{w}_i$ and $\mathbf{w}_j$ is
$$
\cos \theta_{ij}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_j}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\,\|\mathbf{w}_j\|},
$$
OFT is really preserving the angular relationships among the output units’ weight vectors in the same layer.

So your interpretation is correct: OFT is not talking about individual scalar activations directly, but about the geometry of the layer’s weight vectors, which correspond to output channels. The reason this matters is that, in the authors’ view, these directions encode semantic structure, and an orthogonal transform preserves all pairwise angles among the columns while still allowing the layer to be changed. That is the key invariant OFT exploits.

One small caveat is that this discussion is framed for the fully connected layer as an illustrative example, but the same idea is applied more broadly to the layers they finetune, especially attention weights in diffusion models. The “neuron” terminology is a bit loose, but mathematically it always refers to the layer’s weight vectors, not scalar neuron outputs.
