\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why does the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix still preserve hyperspherical energy if it cannot mix all dimensions?
Question:
To make OFT parameter-efficient, the paper replaces a full orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ with a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix,

$$
\mathbf{R} = \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1, \dots, \mathbf{R}_r).
$$

They also say that despite this restriction, there is "no sacrifice in preserving hyperspherical energy" because the resulting matrix is still orthogonal.

I think I understand the literal statement that any orthogonal matrix preserves pairwise distances and angles, including block-diagonal ones. But I'm confused about the trade-off here. If block-diagonal $\mathbf{R}$ cannot rotate across blocks, isn't the set of reachable finetuned weights much smaller than with a full orthogonal matrix?

So is the claim only that energy preservation is exact, while adaptation flexibility is reduced? If so, how should I think about the role of the block count $r$ beyond just parameter efficiency?
Answer:
Yes — the paper is making two different claims, and it helps to separate them.

The claim about preserving hyperspherical energy is purely an orthogonality claim. Hyperspherical energy is built from pairwise cosine-like relations between normalized neurons, and any orthogonal map $ \mathbf{R} $ preserves inner products and therefore preserves angles and pairwise distances on the unit sphere. So if you replace the full orthogonal matrix by a block-diagonal one like
$$
\mathbf{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r),
$$
it still remains orthogonal, so the energy preservation statement is still exact. The fact that the matrix cannot mix coordinates across different blocks does not matter for that invariant: orthogonality alone is enough.

What you are noticing, however, is the separate issue of expressivity. A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix is a strict subset of all orthogonal matrices, so yes, the set of reachable finetuned weights is smaller. The paper does not deny that; in fact it explicitly frames the block-diagonal form as a parameter-efficiency and regularization device. In their words, the block structure is “a stronger regularization of the orthogonal matrix,” and later they note that this “inevitably introduces additional biases and limits the flexibility.” So the trade-off is exactly what you suspect: energy preservation stays intact, but adaptation flexibility is reduced.

The role of the block count $r$ is therefore not just about saving parameters. It controls the balance between flexibility and constraint. When $r=1$, you have one big block and recover a standard full orthogonal transform, which is the most flexible version within OFT. As $r$ increases, the blocks get smaller, the number of trainable parameters drops from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ toward $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and the model is forced to make more local, less entangled changes to the weight space. That can improve stability and reduce overfitting or collapse, but at the cost of limiting how much the representation can move. This is consistent with the paper’s broader theme that finetuning needs a balance between flexibility and regularity.

A good intuition is to think of a full orthogonal matrix as allowing a global rotation of the neuron space, while a block-diagonal one allows several independent smaller rotations on subspaces. Both preserve geometry within each transformed subspace, and since the whole matrix is still orthogonal, the global hyperspherical energy is preserved as well. What changes is not the invariant, but the set of rotations you are allowed to use.

So the short answer is: the “no sacrifice in preserving hyperspherical energy” statement is about exact geometric invariance, not about model capacity. The block parameter $r$ is the knob that trades off capacity against efficiency and regularization. The paper’s claim is that this trade-off is useful in practice, and in fact one of the reasons OFT is stable.
