\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 10: Chapter 10: Subject-Driven Generation Results

# Problem setup for subject-driven generation

Subject-driven generation asks a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to produce images of a specific subject from only a few reference images, while still following a text prompt that places that subject into new contexts. In the paper, this task is treated as one of the two main downstream applications for finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models. The central challenge is not only to learn the subject identity from a tiny set of images, but also to preserve the model’s pretrained ability to generate high-fidelity and semantically rich images.

The paper emphasizes that this is a difficult balancing act. A finetuning method must adapt the model enough to bind the subject identity to a learned token, yet it must avoid destroying the pretrained generative structure. For subject-driven generation, this means that a good method should both preserve identity-specific details and respond correctly to the surrounding text prompt. The paper frames this as a test of finetuning stability, sample efficiency, and preservation of pretrained semantic knowledge.

# Using the DreamBooth loss for all compared methods

For the subject-driven generation experiments, all compared methods use the same loss function as DreamBooth. This is important because it isolates the effect of the finetuning strategy itself rather than differences in the objective. In other words, the comparison is not about whether one method has access to a better training signal, but whether OFT and COFT can make better use of the same subject-driven generation setup.

The paper follows the DreamBooth framework and uses a customized token together with a few subject images. DreamBooth itself uses a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior preservation loss, and OFT adopts this same framework. The main difference is that DreamBooth performs standard finetuning with a small learning rate, whereas OFT replaces naive weight updates with orthogonal transformations of the pretrained weights. COFT is the constrained version of this idea, adding an explicit deviation constraint to improve stability.

# Baselines: DreamBooth and LoRA

The subject-driven generation experiments compare OFT and COFT against DreamBooth and LoRA. DreamBooth is the natural baseline because the paper’s subject-driven setup follows that framework directly. LoRA is included because it is a widely used parameter-efficient finetuning method and is especially relevant for comparison with OFT in terms of training efficiency.

The paper positions DreamBooth and LoRA as two different ways of adapting a pretrained diffusion model without full retraining. DreamBooth updates the model in the standard way using a small learning rate, while LoRA adds a low-rank update to the weights. Both are meant to preserve the pretrained model’s generative behavior, but the paper argues that neither method can guarantee semantic preservation. OFT is introduced as a more principled alternative because it preserves hyperspherical energy by construction, and this turns out to matter for keeping the pretrained model’s semantic structure intact.

# Convergence speed of OFT and COFT versus competing methods

A major result in the paper is that OFT and COFT converge faster than DreamBooth and LoRA. The experiments show that after 400 iterations, both DreamBooth and the OFT variants can achieve good control, but LoRA already begins to fail at preserving the subject identity. This indicates that LoRA, despite being parameter-efficient, is less reliable at quickly adapting the model to the subject-driven task.

The advantage becomes even clearer after longer finetuning. At 2000 iterations, DreamBooth starts producing collapsed images, and LoRA fails to preserve the intended appearance, such as generating yellow fur instead of a yellow shirt in the example discussed in the paper. By contrast, both OFT and COFT remain stable and continue to generate consistent results. The paper interprets this as evidence that OFT provides fast yet stable convergence, which is especially valuable because it reduces the need to carefully tune the exact number of training iterations for each subject.

# Training stability across short and long finetuning runs

Stability is one of the paper’s central claims, and the subject-driven experiments strongly support it. The results show that OFT and COFT remain reliable over both short and long finetuning schedules. They can produce good subject preservation early in training and continue to do so without degrading into collapsed or inconsistent generations.

This behavior matters because finetuning large diffusion models can be highly sensitive to optimization settings. The paper stresses that stable training dynamics make a method easier to use in practice, since users do not need to constantly search for the exact stopping point. In this regard, OFT and especially COFT are presented as practical methods rather than merely mathematically elegant ones. Their training curves are smoother and less fragile than those of the baselines.

# Model collapse behavior in DreamBooth under longer finetuning

The paper specifically notes that DreamBooth begins to generate collapsed images after longer training. This is an important failure mode because it shows that naive finetuning can gradually harm the pretrained generative ability even if it appears to work earlier in training. In the subject-driven setting, collapse means that the model loses the ability to produce coherent or faithful outputs as training continues.

This result supports the paper’s broader argument that preserving the pretrained structure matters. DreamBooth may initially capture the subject identity, but continued training can damage the model’s generative quality. The paper uses this to motivate OFT’s structural constraint: rather than merely making the parameter update small, OFT preserves the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, which the authors argue are crucial for retaining semantic knowledge.

# Identity preservation failures observed in LoRA

LoRA is also shown to struggle with identity preservation in the subject-driven generation experiments. The paper reports that after 400 iterations, LoRA fails to preserve the subject identity, and in the qualitative examples it can completely lose the identity of the subject in certain cases, such as the bowl example. This is a key weakness because subject-driven generation is fundamentally about keeping the same subject recognizable across new contexts.

The paper uses this failure to highlight a limitation of low-rank additive updates. Although LoRA is efficient, the paper argues that its update mechanism does not directly preserve the structural relationships in the pretrained weights that encode semantic information. OFT, in contrast, changes the neuron directions through orthogonal transformation while preserving hyperspherical energy, which the authors find to be more compatible with subject preservation.

# Why OFT is less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations

One of the most practically useful findings in the paper is that OFT is relatively insensitive to the number of finetuning iterations. The authors explicitly note that this reduces the trouble of tuning the iteration number for different subjects. In practice, this means that one can use a relatively large number of iterations without carefully searching for the best stopping point, because OFT remains stable across a wide range of training durations.

The paper connects this behavior to the fact that OFT updates neuron directions in a controlled way. Instead of making unconstrained changes to the model, OFT preserves the pairwise angles among neurons and therefore retains the pretrained semantic structure. COFT goes one step further by adding an explicit deviation constraint, which makes its behavior even more predictable. As a result, both methods are described as easier to tune than standard finetuning or LoRA.

# Quantitative results on DINO and CLIP-I subject fidelity

To evaluate subject fidelity quantitatively, the paper uses DINO and CLIP-I. DINO measures similarity using ViT S/16 DINO embeddings, while CLIP-I computes the average pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between generated and real images. These metrics are intended to capture how well the generated images preserve the identity-related details of the subject.

The paper reports that both COFT and OFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA by a considerable margin on DINO and CLIP-I. This means that the subject appearance in the generated images is more faithfully preserved when using orthogonal finetuning. The improvements are not marginal; the paper describes them as substantial, indicating that the structural constraint used by OFT translates into better identity retention in practice.

# Quantitative results on CLIP-T prompt fidelity

The paper also evaluates prompt fidelity using CLIP-T, which measures the cosine similarity between the CLIP embeddings of the text prompt and the generated image. This metric asks whether the finetuned model still follows the user’s prompt after learning the subject identity. In subject-driven generation, this is especially important because the subject must be placed into different contexts rather than merely copied.

According to the paper, OFT and COFT achieve slightly better or comparable prompt fidelity relative to the baselines. This is notable because a method could in principle improve identity preservation while harming prompt compliance. The results show that OFT does not force this trade-off; it can strengthen subject retention without losing the ability to track the text prompt. The qualitative examples later reinforce this point by showing that OFT better respects the requested scene or context.

# Quantitative results on LPIPS diversity

The paper evaluates diversity using LPIPS, computed as an average cosine similarity between generated images of the same subject under the same text prompt. This helps determine whether the model can generate varied outputs rather than reproducing nearly identical images each time. In a subject-driven setting, diversity matters because a useful model should produce multiple plausible images of the subject in different configurations, not just a single memorized pose.

The paper reports that OFT and COFT achieve slightly better or comparable diversity compared with DreamBooth and LoRA. This suggests that improving fidelity does not come at the cost of collapsing variety. Instead, OFT appears to preserve enough of the pretrained distribution to support multiple valid generations while still holding onto the subject identity.

# Effect of repeated trials with many random seeds

To reduce randomness, the paper repeats finetuning on the same pretrained model with 30 different random seeds. This is an important experimental detail because finetuning results can vary significantly depending on initialization, optimization noise, and data sampling. By averaging over many trials, the authors aim to show that the observed improvements are not accidental.

The repeated trials support the claim that OFT is consistently better, not just occasionally lucky. The paper reports that the methods are compared under multiple random seeds and that OFT’s superior convergence and final performance persist across these runs. This strengthens the argument that orthogonal finetuning is genuinely more stable and reliable than standard finetuning or LoRA.

# Qualitative examples showing improved identity retention

The paper provides qualitative examples to complement the quantitative metrics. In these examples, OFT and COFT are shown to preserve the semantic identity of the subject very well. The generated images remain recognizably tied to the reference subject while also adapting to the new prompt. This is the type of outcome that subject-driven generation is supposed to deliver, and the paper uses these examples to show that the quantitative improvements correspond to visibly better outputs.

In contrast, LoRA often fails to preserve the subject identity. The paper highlights cases where LoRA loses the subject completely, demonstrating that a low-rank update alone is not enough to maintain identity across generated scenes. DreamBooth can preserve identity better than LoRA in some cases, but as the paper notes, it may fail to follow the prompt as accurately. OFT and COFT are presented as better balancing these two goals.

# Qualitative examples showing stronger prompt controllability

The qualitative comparisons also show that OFT and COFT provide stronger prompt controllability. That is, they better reflect the specific scene or context described by the text prompt while still keeping the subject recognizable. This dual success is central to the paper’s claim that orthogonal finetuning improves controllability without sacrificing semantic fidelity.

The paper points out that DreamBooth may preserve the subject but still fail to generate the image according to the prompt, whereas OFT and COFT are more accurate in both respects. The examples suggest that the model can insert the subject into the desired context more reliably when finetuned with orthogonal transformations. This is consistent with the paper’s broader intuition that changing neuron directions, while preserving relational structure, is an effective way to modify semantics without destroying the pretrained model.

# Practical implication: easier hyperparameter tuning for OFT and especially COFT

A final practical takeaway from the subject-driven generation experiments is that OFT makes hyperparameter tuning easier, and COFT makes it even easier. Because OFT is less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations, users do not need to finely calibrate the training length for each subject. This is a major usability advantage over methods that require careful stopping criteria to avoid collapse or overfitting.

COFT improves this further by adding an explicit deviation constraint. The paper notes that with a proper $\epsilon$, both the learning rate and the iteration number become easier to set. In other words, COFT gives the user a more controlled optimization process by limiting how far the finetuned model can move away from the pretrained one. The practical result is a more stable and predictable finetuning pipeline for personalized subject generation.
