\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why does multiplying a weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix preserve the paper's hyperspherical energy?
Question:
I'm reading the OFT paper and I want to make sure I understand the main claim correctly. For a layer with weights $\mathbf{W}^0 = \{\mathbf{w}_1^0, \dots, \mathbf{w}_n^0\}$, they define hyperspherical energy using normalized neurons, for example

$$
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i \ne j} \left\| \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j \right\|^{-1}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}.
$$

Then they say that if $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ with an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$, the hyperspherical energy is preserved exactly.

I can see informally that orthogonal transforms preserve lengths and angles, but I'm still not fully seeing the exact argument for the normalized neurons in this definition. Is the reason simply that

$$
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{R}\mathbf{b}\| = \|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\|
$$

for all vectors $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}$, so every pairwise term stays the same after normalization? Or is there some more subtle point because the normalization happens before measuring distances?

A step-by-step explanation of why $\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0) = \mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)$ would help.
Answer:
Yes, the reason is essentially the one you wrote, but it helps to unpack why the normalization does not introduce any extra complication.

The key fact is that an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ preserves Euclidean norms and distances. So for any vector $\mathbf{w}$, we have
$$
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}\|=\|\mathbf{w}\|.
$$
This immediately implies that normalization commutes with the orthogonal transform:
$$
\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}
=\frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}\|}
=\frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}
=\mathbf{R}\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}
=\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}.
$$
So although the hyperspherical energy is defined using normalized neurons, after applying $\mathbf{R}$ the normalized version of each neuron is just the old normalized neuron rotated by the same orthogonal transform.

Now look at one pairwise term in the energy:
$$
\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}_i-\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}_j\right\|
=
\left\|\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\right\|.
$$
Because $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal, it preserves distances, so
$$
\left\|\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\right\|
=
\left\|\mathbf{R}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j)\right\|
=
\left\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\right\|.
$$
Therefore each reciprocal distance term is unchanged:
$$
\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}_i-\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}_j\right\|^{-1}
=
\left\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\right\|^{-1}.
$$
Summing over all $i\neq j$ gives
$$
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)=\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0).
$$

So there is no subtle extra issue from the normalization step. In fact, the normalization is exactly what makes the quantity “hyperspherical”: it throws away magnitudes and keeps only directions. Since orthogonal maps preserve both lengths and angles, they preserve the geometry on the unit sphere, and thus preserve the hyperspherical energy exactly.

A slightly more conceptual way to say it is that OFT is not trying to keep the weights numerically close in Euclidean coordinates; it is trying to preserve the relative angular configuration of the neurons. Orthogonal transformations are precisely the transformations that change coordinates without changing that internal geometry. That is why the paper can claim exact preservation of hyperspherical energy when $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ with $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$.

### What exactly is meant by a 'neuron' in OFT when the layer is written as $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$?
Question:
The paper considers a fully connected layer written as $\mathbf{W} = \{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ and says $\mathbf{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the $i$-th neuron, with output

$$
\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}.
$$

I'm confused about the terminology here. In standard linear algebra for neural nets, I would usually think of each column of $\mathbf{W}$ as the weight vector for one output unit, but calling that entire vector a "neuron" feels a bit unusual.

So in this paper, when they talk about preserving pairwise neuron angles, are they really just talking about preserving the angles between the column vectors of the weight matrix? If so, does OFT preserve relationships among output channels rather than individual scalar activations?

I want to be sure I'm interpreting the geometry correctly before trying to understand the rest of the method.
Answer:
Yes — in this paper a “neuron” means the whole column vector $\mathbf{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ of a fully connected layer, not a single scalar activation.

The paper is using the common matrix convention for a linear layer written as
$$
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}, \qquad \mathbf{W}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
$$
so each column $\mathbf{w}_i$ is the weight vector for the $i$-th output coordinate $z_i$. In other words, one column corresponds to one output unit / output channel in that layer. The paper then treats those column vectors as the “neurons” whose geometry it wants to preserve.

That is exactly why hyperspherical energy is defined from the normalized columns
$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i=\frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|},
$$
and why preserving “pairwise neuron angles” means preserving the angles between these column vectors, equivalently preserving their cosine similarities. Since the pairwise angle between $\mathbf{w}_i$ and $\mathbf{w}_j$ is
$$
\cos \theta_{ij}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_j}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\,\|\mathbf{w}_j\|},
$$
OFT is really preserving the angular relationships among the output units’ weight vectors in the same layer.

So your interpretation is correct: OFT is not talking about individual scalar activations directly, but about the geometry of the layer’s weight vectors, which correspond to output channels. The reason this matters is that, in the authors’ view, these directions encode semantic structure, and an orthogonal transform preserves all pairwise angles among the columns while still allowing the layer to be changed. That is the key invariant OFT exploits.

One small caveat is that this discussion is framed for the fully connected layer as an illustrative example, but the same idea is applied more broadly to the layers they finetune, especially attention weights in diffusion models. The “neuron” terminology is a bit loose, but mathematically it always refers to the layer’s weight vectors, not scalar neuron outputs.

### Why is preserving pairwise angles between weight vectors supposed to preserve semantic generation ability?
Question:
The key hypothesis of the paper seems to be that preserving hyperspherical energy, or equivalently preserving pairwise angular relationships between neurons in a layer, helps preserve the pretrained model's semantic generation ability.

I understand that angular information can matter, and the paper mentions prior work like SphereNet and a toy autoencoder experiment. But I'm struggling with the jump from "angles can encode semantics" to "preserving pairwise angles among weights should preserve the semantics learned by a diffusion model."

Why should the set of pairwise angles between weight vectors be the right structural quantity here? For example, couldn't two weight matrices have the same pairwise angles among columns but still behave quite differently once they interact with nonlinearities, residual connections, layer norms, and the rest of the diffusion architecture?

Is this claim meant as a formal guarantee about function preservation, or more as an empirical inductive bias that seems to work well?
Answer:
The short answer is that the paper is **not claiming a formal theorem that pairwise angles alone guarantee semantic preservation of the whole diffusion model**. What it does claim is narrower: if, during finetuning, you preserve the **pairwise angular geometry of the neurons within a layer**, then you preserve a structural property that the authors argue is strongly correlated with the model’s pretrained semantic behavior. In other words, this is an **inductive bias**, not a full functional equivalence guarantee.

The reasoning in the paper is roughly this. They treat the columns of a weight matrix as neuron vectors and define hyperspherical energy from pairwise cosine-like relationships among the normalized neurons. If you apply the same orthogonal matrix $R$ to all neurons in a layer, then inner products and angles between those neurons are preserved, because orthogonal maps preserve dot products. So the layer’s neurons are not being “reshaped” arbitrarily; they are being rotated or reflected together as a rigid configuration. The paper’s intuition is that this keeps the pretrained layer’s internal relational structure intact while still allowing the layer to move to a different coordinate system that can better fit the downstream task.

That is why the authors emphasize “adjusting the canonical coordinate system” rather than changing the geometry itself. Their hypothesis is that in a pretrained diffusion model, much of the useful semantic information is carried by these angular relationships, so keeping them fixed should help retain the model’s generative knowledge. This is also why they cite prior work on angular features and their toy autoencoder experiment: those are meant as evidence that direction/angle can encode semantic information better than raw magnitude.

But your concern is exactly the right one: preserving pairwise angles among weights in one layer does **not** by itself imply the whole network function is preserved, because the layer is embedded in a much more complicated architecture with nonlinearities, residual paths, normalization, and cross-layer interactions. The paper does not prove otherwise. In fact, the paper explicitly frames OFT as preserving hyperspherical energy, and says this property is **crucial for preserving semantic generation ability** based on empirical observation. That is an empirical claim about a useful regularization principle, not a mathematical guarantee that two networks with the same layerwise angular structure will generate the same outputs.

So the correct interpretation is: orthogonal finetuning is designed to preserve a **structured similarity** to the pretrained model that is more expressive than small Euclidean weight drift, but less destructive than unrestricted finetuning. The orthogonality constraint gives a provable invariance at the level of pairwise neuron angles, and the authors then show experimentally that this tends to preserve generation quality. That makes it a principled heuristic with strong empirical support, not a strict function-preservation theorem.

### If OFT uses $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$, isn't that just a change of basis that could be canceled by the previous layer?
Question:
I'm confused about the functional effect of OFT. The paper says that OFT learns a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ and replaces a pretrained weight matrix by

$$
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0.
$$

At first glance, this looks like a rotation or reflection of the input-side coordinates for that layer. But if we only rotate the weights of one layer, is this really adding expressive power, or is it just a basis change that another layer could absorb or cancel?

More concretely, for a single linear layer we have

$$
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{x}.
$$

So it seems like OFT is equivalent to rotating the input activation $\mathbf{x}$ before applying the original weights. Why does that help fine-tune the model for a new task rather than just reparameterize the same computation?

I suspect the answer has to do with the surrounding fixed layers and nonlinearities, but I'd like a clearer explanation.
Answer:
At the level of a single isolated linear map, your intuition is right: if you replace $\mathbf{W}^0$ by $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ with $\mathbf{R}$ orthogonal, then

$$
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{x},
$$

so this is equivalent to applying a rotation/reflection to the input coordinates before the original weight matrix. If the *entire network* were just a stack of linear layers and you rotated every layer consistently, then yes, this would mostly amount to a change of basis and could be canceled out.

But that is not the setting of OFT. The paper is explicit that OFT is a finetuning method for a pretrained diffusion model, where only selected layers are adapted, and the surrounding layers are left fixed. In that situation, the orthogonal transform is not “free” to cancel globally, because the next layer does not automatically receive the inverse transform. The network as a whole is not invariant to rotating one layer’s hidden representation unless the adjacent layers are transformed in a coordinated way, which OFT does not do.

This is exactly why the paper emphasizes that OFT “adjusts the canonical coordinate system for the neurons in the same layer” while preserving pairwise angles. The key point is not that the function of one linear layer changes in an arbitrary way; rather, OFT changes the internal geometry of the representation in a controlled, structure-preserving way. That gives the model a way to move into a better task-specific configuration without badly disrupting the pretrained semantic structure.

The surrounding nonlinearities matter too. In a transformer or diffusion U-Net, the rotated activations pass through nonlinearities, normalization, residual connections, attention, and other fixed components. Once you have those, a basis change at one point in the network is no longer something that can be algebraically “pulled through” and canceled. So even though the local formula looks like a coordinate change, the *global* effect on the network is a genuine functional adaptation.

The paper’s intuition is that this is a good trade-off: an orthogonal transform is flexible enough to change neuron directions, which they argue carries semantic information, but restrictive enough to preserve hyperspherical energy and therefore keep the pretrained generative behavior stable. That is why OFT is not just a reparameterization in practice, even though it has a basis-change interpretation at the level of a single layer.

### Why does the paper define hyperspherical energy as $\sum_{i \ne j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ instead of directly using cosine similarity?
Question:
The paper motivates OFT using pairwise hyperspherical similarity and says hyperspherical energy characterizes neuron relationships on the unit sphere. But then the actual definition they use is

$$
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i \ne j} \left\| \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j \right\|^{-1}.
$$

I would have expected something more directly based on cosine similarity, like a sum over $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j$ or angular distances.

Is there a specific reason for using inverse Euclidean distance on the unit sphere? Since for unit vectors this is related to the angle anyway, is this just inherited from prior hyperspherical energy literature, or does it have some special optimization or theoretical property here?

I'm trying to understand whether the exact form of the energy matters for OFT, or whether any angle-preserving similarity measure would have led to the same method.
Answer:
The short answer is that the paper is following the standard definition of hyperspherical energy from the cited hypersphere-regularization literature, and on the unit sphere inverse Euclidean distance is just a convenient way to write a function of pairwise angle. It is not meant to be a fundamentally different notion from cosine similarity; it is a particular “repulsive” energy whose preservation is guaranteed by orthogonal transformations.

For two unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j$, the Euclidean distance is determined entirely by their cosine similarity:
\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^2
= 2 - 2\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j
= 2(1-\cos\theta_{ij}).
\]
So
\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}
= \bigl(2-2\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\bigr)^{-1/2}.
\]
That means the energy is still entirely angle-based. Using $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j$ directly would also be angle-based, but the inverse-distance form has a useful interpretation inherited from the hyperspherical energy literature: it acts like a repulsive potential on the sphere, penalizing neurons that become too close much more strongly than neurons that are already far apart. This “spread the points out on the sphere” viewpoint is exactly what hyperspherical energy is designed to capture.

In the context of OFT, the exact functional form is not what drives the method. The key property is that if all neurons in a layer are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$, then pairwise angles, cosine similarities, and Euclidean distances on the unit sphere are all preserved. So the energy
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\ne j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}
\]
is unchanged under $\mathbf{W}\mapsto \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}$. That invariance is what OFT exploits: it can change the coordinate system of the layer without changing the pairwise geometric structure that the paper argues encodes semantic information.

So yes, this is largely inherited from prior hyperspherical energy work, but not arbitrarily so. The inverse-distance form gives a standard, geometry-sensitive energy whose preservation is exactly equivalent to preserving the pairwise spherical structure that OFT cares about. A sum of raw cosine similarities would be related, but it would not be the canonical hyperspherical energy used in that line of work, and the paper’s theoretical and empirical discussion is built around that established notion.

### How is the COFT constraint $\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon$ actually enforced when using Cayley parameterization?
Question:
In the constrained version, the paper introduces

$$
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}, \quad \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}, \quad \|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon.
$$

Then it says that with the Cayley transform,

$$
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}, \quad \mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top,
$$

we can use the approximation

$$
\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2)
$$

and therefore move the constraint to something like $\|\mathbf{Q}\| \le \epsilon'$.

This is the part where I get lost. Is this really equivalent, or only approximately equivalent for small $\mathbf{Q}$? If it is only approximate, then what norm are they projecting under in practice, and how much does the actual bound on $\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\|$ depend on higher-order terms?

I'm not sure whether COFT is enforcing a true geometric constraint on $\mathbf{R}$ or just a heuristic proxy through $\mathbf{Q}$.
Answer:
It is only an approximation, not an exact equivalence.

What the paper is doing is the following. In COFT the desired constraint is on the orthogonal matrix itself, namely $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\leq \epsilon$. But because $\mathbf{R}$ is not optimized directly, it is parameterized by a skew-symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ through the Cayley transform
$$
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}, \qquad \mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^\top.
$$
For small $\mathbf{Q}$, the Neumann-series expansion gives
$$
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} \approx \mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}^2+\cdots,
$$
so
$$
\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2).
$$
This is why they say the deviation of $\mathbf{R}$ from identity can be controlled by constraining $\mathbf{Q}$ near $0$. But that is only a first-order approximation: the actual difference is
$$
\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}=2\mathbf{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2),
$$
so the higher-order terms are ignored in the argument that motivates the constraint transfer. There is no exact identity of the form “$\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ if and only if $\|\mathbf{Q}\|\leq \epsilon'$”.

The paper is quite explicit about this being a practical move: it says the constraint on $\mathbf{Q}$ “can be easily enforced by projected gradient descent,” and that $\epsilon'$ is “another error hyperparameter.” That means they are not deriving a mathematically exact bound on $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|$ from a chosen $\epsilon'$, but using $\|\mathbf{Q}\|\leq \epsilon'$ as a proxy constraint that is easier to project onto. In practice, you would project $\mathbf{Q}$ back into a norm ball after each update, while maintaining skew-symmetry. The paper does not specify a more exotic projection; the natural reading is projection in the same matrix norm used in the approximation argument, namely the operator norm they mention when discussing convergence of the Neumann series.

So the right way to think about COFT is: it enforces orthogonality exactly via Cayley parametrization, and it enforces proximity to the pretrained model only approximately by keeping $\mathbf{Q}$ small. The true bound on $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|$ does depend on the neglected higher-order terms, so the guarantee is local and approximate rather than a globally exact geometric constraint. In other words, COFT is a principled heuristic proxy, but a very natural one: small skew-symmetric $\mathbf{Q}$ means a small orthogonal perturbation away from identity.

### What norm is used in the COFT constraint $\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon$, and how should I interpret it geometrically?
Question:
The paper says COFT constrains the finetuned model to stay within a neighborhood of the pretrained model by requiring

$$
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon.
$$

But I couldn't tell which norm this is supposed to be. Is it the Frobenius norm, spectral norm, or something else?

This seems important, because different norms correspond to very different notions of how far an orthogonal matrix can move from the identity. For example, a small Frobenius norm controls the average amount of rotation across dimensions, while a small spectral norm controls the worst-case direction.

Do the authors specify this somewhere implicitly, and is there an intuitive geometric meaning of $\epsilon$ in terms of rotation angle?
Answer:
In the paper, the norm is not explicitly redefined in the COFT equation, but the surrounding derivation makes it pretty clear that they are thinking in terms of the operator norm, i.e. the spectral norm induced by the Euclidean vector norm.

The key clue is this sentence:

> “under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm”

They then use the approximation
$$
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} \approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2),
$$
and say the constraint $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le \epsilon$ can be moved to an equivalent constraint on $\|\mathbf{Q}\|$. That argument is naturally stated in operator norm, because the Neumann-series convergence condition is $\|\mathbf{Q}\|<1$ in an induced matrix norm, and the spectral norm is the standard choice when one says “operator norm” without further qualification.

So the safest reading is: $\|\cdot\|$ here means the spectral/operator norm, not the Frobenius norm.

Geometrically, this constraint means that the orthogonal transform $\mathbf{R}$ is close to the identity as a linear map, so it cannot rotate any unit vector by too much in the worst case. If $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal, all of its eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, and being close to $\mathbf{I}$ in spectral norm means those eigenvalues stay near $1$, i.e. the principal rotation angles are small. In that sense, $\epsilon$ controls the maximum angular deviation allowed by the finetuning. The paper itself phrases this intuition informally: larger $\epsilon$ makes COFT behave more like unconstrained OFT, while smaller $\epsilon$ keeps the finetuned model closer to the pretrained one.

If you want a more concrete picture, for a pure rotation in a 2D plane by angle $\theta$, the spectral distance to the identity is
$$
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_2 = 2\left|\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\right|,
$$
so small $\epsilon$ corresponds to small rotation angles. In higher dimensions, the same idea applies to the largest principal rotation angle. By contrast, a Frobenius norm bound would measure the total amount of rotation spread across all dimensions, which is a different and less worst-case notion than what the paper’s operator-norm argument suggests.

So, in short: the paper does not spell it out in a standalone definition, but the derivation indicates the spectral/operator norm, and geometrically $\epsilon$ is best understood as a bound on the maximum allowed deviation of the orthogonal transform from the identity, i.e. a bound on how much the layer can rotate its neuron directions.

### Why does the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix still preserve hyperspherical energy if it cannot mix all dimensions?
Question:
To make OFT parameter-efficient, the paper replaces a full orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ with a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix,

$$
\mathbf{R} = \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1, \dots, \mathbf{R}_r).
$$

They also say that despite this restriction, there is "no sacrifice in preserving hyperspherical energy" because the resulting matrix is still orthogonal.

I think I understand the literal statement that any orthogonal matrix preserves pairwise distances and angles, including block-diagonal ones. But I'm confused about the trade-off here. If block-diagonal $\mathbf{R}$ cannot rotate across blocks, isn't the set of reachable finetuned weights much smaller than with a full orthogonal matrix?

So is the claim only that energy preservation is exact, while adaptation flexibility is reduced? If so, how should I think about the role of the block count $r$ beyond just parameter efficiency?
Answer:
Yes — the paper is making two different claims, and it helps to separate them.

The claim about preserving hyperspherical energy is purely an orthogonality claim. Hyperspherical energy is built from pairwise cosine-like relations between normalized neurons, and any orthogonal map $ \mathbf{R} $ preserves inner products and therefore preserves angles and pairwise distances on the unit sphere. So if you replace the full orthogonal matrix by a block-diagonal one like
$$
\mathbf{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r),
$$
it still remains orthogonal, so the energy preservation statement is still exact. The fact that the matrix cannot mix coordinates across different blocks does not matter for that invariant: orthogonality alone is enough.

What you are noticing, however, is the separate issue of expressivity. A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix is a strict subset of all orthogonal matrices, so yes, the set of reachable finetuned weights is smaller. The paper does not deny that; in fact it explicitly frames the block-diagonal form as a parameter-efficiency and regularization device. In their words, the block structure is “a stronger regularization of the orthogonal matrix,” and later they note that this “inevitably introduces additional biases and limits the flexibility.” So the trade-off is exactly what you suspect: energy preservation stays intact, but adaptation flexibility is reduced.

The role of the block count $r$ is therefore not just about saving parameters. It controls the balance between flexibility and constraint. When $r=1$, you have one big block and recover a standard full orthogonal transform, which is the most flexible version within OFT. As $r$ increases, the blocks get smaller, the number of trainable parameters drops from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ toward $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and the model is forced to make more local, less entangled changes to the weight space. That can improve stability and reduce overfitting or collapse, but at the cost of limiting how much the representation can move. This is consistent with the paper’s broader theme that finetuning needs a balance between flexibility and regularity.

A good intuition is to think of a full orthogonal matrix as allowing a global rotation of the neuron space, while a block-diagonal one allows several independent smaller rotations on subspaces. Both preserve geometry within each transformed subspace, and since the whole matrix is still orthogonal, the global hyperspherical energy is preserved as well. What changes is not the invariant, but the set of rotations you are allowed to use.

So the short answer is: the “no sacrifice in preserving hyperspherical energy” statement is about exact geometric invariance, not about model capacity. The block parameter $r$ is the knob that trades off capacity against efficiency and regularization. The paper’s claim is that this trade-off is useful in practice, and in fact one of the reasons OFT is stable.

### In what sense is OFT more parameter-efficient than LoRA when a full orthogonal matrix seems quadratic in $d$?
Question:
The parameter efficiency discussion confused me a bit. A full orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ has about $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom, which is still quadratic in $d$. LoRA with rank $r'$ has about $r'(d+n)$ parameters.

The paper then argues that with block-diagonal structure, OFT can become very efficient, even giving an example where a $128 \times 128$ layer uses fewer parameters for OFT than LoRA.

What I'm unsure about is whether this is a fair apples-to-apples comparison. OFT's efficiency seems to depend heavily on choosing many blocks, which also imposes a strong structural bias. LoRA's efficiency depends on low rank, which is a different kind of restriction.

Is there a principled way to compare the expressive capacity of block-diagonal OFT with rank-constrained LoRA, or is the comparison purely empirical?
Answer:
The short answer is that the paper is comparing two different kinds of restrictions, so there is no clean theoretical notion of “same expressive capacity” that would make OFT and LoRA directly equivalent. The comparison in the paper is mainly about parameter count and empirical finetuning behavior, not about a proven capacity ordering.

What OFT is counting is the number of free parameters needed to specify an orthogonal transform. A full orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ indeed has about $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom, so by itself it is quadratic. The paper’s efficiency claim comes from not using an unconstrained full matrix in practice, but a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix. If the matrix is split into $r$ blocks, each block lives in dimension about $d/r$, so the total number of degrees of freedom drops to about
\[
r \cdot \frac{(d/r)(d/r-1)}{2} \approx \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{d^2}{r}\right),
\]
and with shared blocks even further. So yes, the efficiency depends heavily on the block structure.

But that is not merely a trick to “cheat” the comparison; it is part of the method’s inductive bias. LoRA and OFT are constraining the update in fundamentally different ways. LoRA adds a low-rank correction to the weight matrix, so the updated weight is of the form
\[
W^0 + \Delta W,\qquad \mathrm{rank}(\Delta W)\le r',
\]
which says the change lies in a low-dimensional additive subspace. OFT instead uses
\[
W = R W^0,\qquad R^\top R = I,
\]
so it is multiplicative and constrained to preserve pairwise angles between neurons. The block-diagonal version just makes that orthogonal transform cheaper to parameterize.

So the fair way to read the paper is: OFT is not claiming to be “more expressive per parameter” in some universal theoretical sense. It is claiming that, for these diffusion finetuning tasks, a structured orthogonal update can preserve pretrained semantics better while using a small number of trainable parameters, and that block-diagonal orthogonal matrices can be made cheaper than the particular LoRA setting they benchmark against. The paper itself is quite explicit that the block structure introduces extra bias and that improving parameter efficiency “in a more effective and less biased way” is an open problem.

So the comparison is partly empirical and partly architectural. There is no principled theorem in the paper that says block-diagonal OFT strictly dominates rank-constrained LoRA in expressive capacity. Instead, the paper argues that the two methods encode different priors, and that OFT’s prior is better matched to preserving the pretrained diffusion model’s structure. That is why the authors emphasize stability, convergence, and preservation of hyperspherical energy rather than a general capacity equivalence.

### Why does re-scaling neurons with a diagonal matrix on the right preserve hyperspherical energy?
Question:
The paper proposes a re-scaled OFT variant with forward pass

$$
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D})^\top \mathbf{x},
$$

where $\mathbf{D} = \mathrm{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_n)$ and each $s_i > 0$.

I think the idea is that right-multiplying by $\mathbf{D}$ rescales the columns of $\mathbf{W}^0$, so after normalization each column direction stays the same and hyperspherical energy is unchanged. But I want to check that I am interpreting the matrix multiplication correctly.

Since $\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, right-multiplying by $\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ scales each column independently, while left-multiplying would scale rows. Is that the whole reason the erratum says $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D}$ is correct and $\mathbf{D}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ was wrong?

Also, why do they require $s_i > 0$ instead of allowing negative values?
Answer:
Yes — your interpretation of the matrix multiplication is essentially correct, and that is exactly why the erratum matters.

In the paper, the weight matrix for a fully connected layer is written as $\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where each column $\mathbf{w}_i^0 \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is one neuron. Because the neurons are stored as columns, multiplying on the right by a diagonal matrix $\mathbf{D} = \mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ scales each column independently:
$$
\mathbf{W}^0 \mathbf{D} = [\, s_1 \mathbf{w}_1^0,\; s_2 \mathbf{w}_2^0,\; \dots,\; s_n \mathbf{w}_n^0 \,].
$$
So right-multiplication is the correct way to attach a separate learned scale to each neuron. Left-multiplication would instead scale the rows, which is a completely different operation: it changes the coordinates inside every neuron rather than scaling neurons as units.

That is why the corrected forward pass
$$
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D})^\top \mathbf{x}
$$
is the right one. The erratum says the version $\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{D}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}$ was a typo, and this is not just cosmetic: $\mathbf{D}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ would generally not mean “rescale each neuron after the orthogonal transform.” The intended construction is “rotate/reflect the neurons with $\mathbf{R}$, then scale each resulting neuron with $\mathbf{D}$.”

As for hyperspherical energy, the key point is that it is computed after normalizing each neuron:
$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}.
$$
If you replace $\mathbf{w}_i$ by $s_i \mathbf{w}_i$ with $s_i > 0$, then
$$
\frac{s_i \mathbf{w}_i}{\|s_i \mathbf{w}_i\|} = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}.
$$
So positive rescaling does not change any normalized neuron direction at all. Since hyperspherical energy depends only on pairwise relations between these normalized directions, it is unchanged. In the OFT setting, the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ preserves angles, and the diagonal scaling $\mathbf{D}$ is “washed out” by normalization, so the hyperspherical energy remains the same.

The restriction $s_i > 0$ is there because negative values would flip a neuron to the opposite point on the sphere:
$$
\frac{-\mathbf{w}_i}{\|-\mathbf{w}_i\|} = -\frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}.
$$
That is not just a harmless rescaling; it changes the direction of the neuron and can alter pairwise spherical relationships. In other words, a negative scale does not preserve the ray spanned by the neuron, only its line through the origin. Since the paper’s invariance argument is about preserving normalized neuron directions up to positive scaling, they explicitly require $s_i > 0$.

So the short answer is: yes, right-multiplication by $\mathbf{D}$ is used because the columns are the neurons, and positive diagonal scaling preserves hyperspherical energy because normalization removes the magnitudes without introducing sign flips. The erratum corrects the matrix order to match exactly that interpretation.

### Does preserving pairwise angles within each layer imply anything about preserving the overall diffusion model function?
Question:
A deeper confusion I have is about what OFT actually guarantees at the network level. The method preserves pairwise angular relationships among the columns of each modified weight matrix, but the diffusion model is a very deep composition of linear maps, nonlinearities, cross-attention, residual paths, and normalization.

So even if every modified layer satisfies some geometric invariance internally, does that imply anything meaningful about the end-to-end denoising function or the generated image distribution? Or is the preservation of hyperspherical energy only a layerwise structural prior with no direct functional guarantee?

I'm asking because the paper sometimes sounds almost causal, as if keeping hyperspherical energy unchanged is the reason semantic generation ability is preserved. I can believe it as an empirical bias, but I'm not sure what should be taken as theorem versus intuition.
Answer:
Short answer: no, not in a strict end-to-end sense. In OFT, the provable guarantee is layerwise: if a weight matrix is updated as $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I}$, then the pairwise angles among the columns of that matrix are preserved, so the hyperspherical energy of that layer is unchanged. The paper does not prove that this implies the entire diffusion model computes the same denoising function, nor that the generated image distribution is preserved.

What OFT is really doing is imposing a strong structural prior on each modified layer. The intuition is that the semantic information in a layer is partly encoded in the angular geometry of its neurons, so preserving that geometry should reduce the risk of destroying pretrained knowledge while still allowing the model to move into a new task. But because the full model is a deep composition of linear maps, nonlinearities, residual connections, attention, and normalization, a layerwise invariance does not compose into a global functional invariance. Changing one layer by an orthogonal transform generally changes activations downstream, and once you pass through nonlinearities and attention, there is no theorem in the paper saying the same end-to-end function is preserved.

So the right way to read the paper is: OFT gives a provable geometric constraint on the weights, not a provable guarantee about the final function. The claim that semantic generation ability is preserved is therefore empirical and hypothesis-driven, not a formal consequence of the orthogonality itself. The paper explicitly says that hyperspherical energy is used as a measure of semantic preservation, that preserving it is “crucial” for keeping generative ability, and that OFT “empirically” finds this to work well. That wording is important: it is an observed inductive bias, not a theorem about diffusion models.

COFT makes this interpretation even clearer. Its extra constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ is there precisely because orthogonality alone may be too flexible; the paper says this improves stability by keeping the finetuned model in a neighborhood of the pretrained one. That is another sign that the method is controlling how much the network is allowed to move, rather than guaranteeing any exact preservation of the global mapping.

So if you want a mathematically precise summary, it is this: OFT guarantees preservation of pairwise angular structure within each finetuned layer, and the authors hypothesize that this preserves the pretrained model’s semantic “geometry” well enough to maintain good generation quality. But the preservation of the overall diffusion model function or output distribution is not guaranteed by the paper, only supported by experiments and intuition.

### Why would minimizing hyperspherical energy be good for classification but bad for finetuning a pretrained diffusion model?
Question:
The paper contrasts OFT with prior orthogonal over-parameterized training work, saying that in classification one may want to minimize hyperspherical energy because uniformly spread neurons can improve generalization, but for finetuning diffusion models this could destroy pretrained semantic structure.

I follow the high-level claim, but I don't quite see the mechanism. If low hyperspherical energy means less redundancy and more uniform neuron directions, why is that beneficial in classification but potentially harmful in generation? Is the idea that a pretrained generative model already contains a highly task-specific geometry that should be preserved rather than "regularized away"?

A more concrete explanation of this difference would help me understand why OFT preserves the original energy instead of trying to optimize it toward some universal optimum.
Answer:
The key difference is that in the two settings, hyperspherical energy is playing two very different roles.

In orthogonal over-parameterized training for classification, the model is being learned from scratch, so there is no pretrained semantic geometry to protect. In that context, the paper you cite from \cite{liu2021orthogonal} uses orthogonal transformations to keep the neurons well spread out on the hypersphere, which corresponds to low hyperspherical energy. Intuitively, that encourages the network to use its representational capacity efficiently: fewer redundant or highly correlated neurons, better-conditioned features, and often better generalization. Since the model is still free to organize itself around the classification task, “making the neurons more uniform” is a useful regularizer rather than a destructive one.

For finetuning a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model, however, the situation is inverted. The pretrained weights already encode a highly structured semantic representation learned from large-scale image-text data. The paper explicitly argues that pairwise neuron angles carry semantic information, and that preserving the hyperspherical energy preserves that relational structure. So here, the goal is not to find a universally “better” geometry, but to stay close to the geometry that already makes the model capable of generating meaningful images. If you push the layer toward a new minimum-energy configuration, you are no longer just removing redundancy; you are actively rearranging the neuron directions that the pretrained model uses to represent concepts, textures, composition, and prompt-image alignment. That can erase or distort the semantic organization the model already has.

So the phrase “minimizing hyperspherical energy” is beneficial in classification because the representation is being built, whereas it can be harmful in diffusion finetuning because the representation is already built and should be preserved. The paper’s intuition is essentially that the pretrained model is not an arbitrary starting point; it is a delicate semantic manifold. OFT therefore does not try to optimize hyperspherical energy toward some new optimum. Instead, it preserves the original hyperspherical energy by restricting updates to orthogonal transformations, since orthogonal maps preserve pairwise angles exactly. This lets the model adapt to the downstream task while keeping the pretrained neuron geometry intact.

That is also why the paper emphasizes preserving energy rather than minimizing it. In finetuning, the objective is a trade-off between flexibility and stability: enough freedom to learn the new control signal or subject, but not so much freedom that the model “forgets” how to generate coherent images. Orthogonal finetuning is a way of changing coordinates without changing the underlying geometry, which is why it fits diffusion finetuning much better than the “spread everything out as much as possible” principle used in classification.
