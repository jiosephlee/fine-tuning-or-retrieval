\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Why a 7B Open Model Is Suddenly So Good at Solving Math Problems

Large language models are often judged by how fluently they write, but math is a much tougher test. It requires structured reasoning, careful step-by-step work, and consistency across many tokens. In that setting, DeepSeekMath is notable for a simple reason: a 7B-parameter open model reaches competition-level math performance that used to be associated with much larger or closed systems.

The paper’s headline result is that DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reaches 51.7% on the MATH benchmark without external tools or voting tricks. With self-consistency over 64 samples, it reaches 60.9%. That puts it in the range of the best proprietary systems reported in the paper, while still being a publicly available open model.

## What DeepSeekMath Is

DeepSeekMath starts from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, not from a general-purpose language model. The authors continue pre-training it on a large math-focused corpus built from Common Crawl, plus some natural language and code data. That corpus contains 120B math-related tokens, which is large by the standards of math training datasets.

This matters because the model is not relying only on post-training instruction tuning. The authors argue that the base model itself becomes much stronger at math reasoning through high-quality pre-training data, and then improves further with supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning.

## What the Benchmarks Actually Measure

Two of the most important benchmarks in the paper are GSM8K and MATH.

GSM8K is a grade-school math benchmark. The problems are short, but they still require multi-step reasoning, arithmetic, and staying on track through a solution.

MATH is much harder. It contains competition-level problems spanning areas like algebra, geometry, number theory, calculus, and probability. The questions are designed to test more advanced reasoning, not just computation.

So when a model scores well on GSM8K, that tells you it can handle relatively structured word problems. When it does well on MATH, that is a much stronger signal: it can often work through olympiad-style or contest-style problems that are significantly more demanding.

The paper also evaluates on Chinese math benchmarks such as CMATH, MGSM-zh, and Gaokao-style tasks, which is important because the training corpus is multilingual rather than English-only.

## How It Compares to GPT-4, Gemini, and Open Models

The comparison in the paper is what makes the result stand out.

On MATH, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reaches 51.7% without tool use. The authors say this approaches the performance level of Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4 on that benchmark. They also note that self-consistency with 64 samples pushes performance to 60.9%.

Just as important, the model outperforms open-source competitors by a wide margin. The paper reports that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B beats existing open-source base models such as Mistral 7B and Llemma-34B on the math benchmarks they test. It also surpasses Minerva 540B on some evaluations, which is striking because Minerva is 77 times larger.

DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B is also strong after supervised fine-tuning. Under settings where tool use is not allowed, it outperforms all open-source models and many proprietary ones on MATH. When tool use is allowed, it approaches 60% on MATH and surpasses existing open-source models.

The larger message is not just that the model is good for its size. It is that a carefully trained 7B open model can compete with systems that are much larger, and in some cases get surprisingly close to closed models.

## Why This Is a Big Deal for Open AI

There are two reasons this result matters.

First, it shows that parameter count is not the whole story. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is much smaller than models like Minerva 540B, yet it performs competitively because of the quality and scale of the math-focused data. The paper’s takeaway is clear: a smaller model trained on the right data can be very strong at mathematical reasoning.

Second, it strengthens the case for open models. Closed models like GPT-4 and Gemini are powerful, but they are not publicly available. DeepSeekMath shows that the open ecosystem can close a meaningful part of the gap, at least on some reasoning tasks. That makes it more practical for researchers and developers to study, reproduce, and build on these capabilities.

## Where the Performance Comes From

The paper attributes the gains to two main ingredients: a carefully engineered data pipeline and a reinforcement learning method called Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO.

The data pipeline is a big part of the story. Instead of relying mainly on textbooks or arXiv papers, the authors mine Common Crawl for math-related pages. They use an iterative classifier-based process to find more and more useful math content, then manually refine the data selection. In the end, they build a 120B-token math corpus that is multilingual and, in their experiments, clearly better than several existing math corpora.

The other major ingredient is GRPO. This is a variant of PPO that avoids training a separate critic model. Instead, it uses the relative quality of multiple sampled answers to estimate the learning signal. In practice, that reduces memory use and still improves performance.

The paper shows that RL with GRPO boosts the instruction-tuned model further, even though the RL stage uses only a subset of the math instruction data. On GSM8K and MATH, performance improves substantially.

## A Few Practical Takeaways

The paper also reports some interesting lessons that are easy to miss if you only look at the headline score.

Code training helps. Starting from a code model appears to improve math reasoning, including program-aided reasoning.

ArXiv is less useful than many people might expect. The authors tested arXiv-heavy corpora and did not see clear gains on their benchmarks.

RL seems to improve the model’s ranking of correct answers more than it improves raw pass rate. In other words, it may make the model more reliable at putting the right solution near the top of its output distribution.

That last point is important because it suggests the gains are not just about making the model “smarter” in a vague sense. They are also about making its answers more robust and more likely to be correct when sampled.

## The Bottom Line

DeepSeekMath is a strong demonstration that open models can make serious progress on mathematical reasoning without relying on massive scale alone.

A 7B model reaching 51.7% on MATH is already impressive. Doing it with an open model, after training on web-mined math data and a lightweight RL method, is what makes the result noteworthy. It suggests that better data, better training recipes, and better optimization methods can move open AI systems much closer to the state of the art.

For anyone following reasoning models, the key lesson is simple: math capability is no longer reserved for the largest closed models. A smaller open model, trained carefully, can now get much closer than many would have expected.
