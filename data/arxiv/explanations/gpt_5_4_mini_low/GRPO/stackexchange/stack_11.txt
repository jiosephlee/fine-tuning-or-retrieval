\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### In the unified training paradigm, in what sense are DPO and RFT 'simplified RL' methods?
Question:
The paper proposes a unified paradigm where the gradient is written as

$\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q,o,t,\pi_{rf}) \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(o_t \mid q,o_{<t}) \right],$

and then says methods such as RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO can all be understood in a common framework, with some of them being direct or simplified RL.

This sounds interesting, but I'm confused about what is being unified and what is being abstracted away. In particular, DPO is usually presented as a preference-optimization objective without explicit rollout-based RL. So in what precise sense is DPO being treated as an RL-like method here? Is it because its gradient can also be written as log-probability weighting by some implicit reward-derived coefficient?

I'm looking for a conceptual explanation of this unification, not just the claim that the gradients can be written in a similar algebraic form.
Answer:
In the paper’s usage, “simplified RL” does not mean that DPO or RFT literally run a full actor-critic loop with an environment, value function, and explicit return estimation. It means something narrower: they fit into the same *policy-gradient-shaped* template as RL methods, where training updates are always of the form “increase the log-probability of some sampled output tokens, weighted by a coefficient derived from a reward or preference signal.”

That is exactly what the unified equation is abstracting. The paper writes the gradient as

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q,o,t,\pi_{rf})\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})\right],
$$

and then says the differences between SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO are mainly in three things: what data $\mathcal{D}$ they train on, what reward signal or reference signal $\pi_{rf}$ they use, and how they turn that signal into the coefficient $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$.

So the unification is not about claiming that all these methods are identical as algorithms. It is about observing that, at the level of the gradient update, they all push token probabilities up or down according to some scalar signal.

For RFT, this is the easiest to see. The “reward” is effectively just a correctness filter on sampled outputs. If an output is judged good, it gets reinforced; if not, it is discarded. In the paper’s framework, that means $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ is essentially a simple binary or rule-based coefficient. So RFT is “simplified RL” because it uses an RL-like reinforce-good-samples / suppress-bad-samples update, but without learning a critic or doing fine-grained credit assignment.

DPO is a bit more subtle, but the paper treats it the same way for the same reason: its loss still induces a gradient that increases the log-probability of preferred responses and decreases that of dispreferred ones. Although DPO is usually presented as preference optimization rather than rollout-based RL, the paper’s viewpoint is that preference pairs are just another kind of reward signal, and the resulting update can be interpreted as a policy-gradient-style update with an implicit reward-derived coefficient. In other words, DPO does not need explicit sampled trajectories, but it still behaves like RL in that it is optimizing a policy against a preference/reward signal while staying anchored to a reference model. That is why the paper groups it with RL-like methods.

The key distinction is that PPO and GRPO are “fuller” RL in this framing because they actually sample outputs from the current policy and use reward models to compute advantages, whereas DPO and RFT are more static or offline. But the paper’s point is that all of them share the same core mechanism: they are different ways of constructing the coefficient multiplying $\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}$. That is what makes DPO and RFT “simplified RL” rather than ordinary supervised learning.

So your intuition is right: the unification is grounded in the fact that even DPO can be seen as implicitly reward-weighted log-likelihood optimization. The paper is abstracting away the procedural differences — online vs. offline, actor-critic vs. no critic, sampled returns vs. preference pairs — and keeping only the common gradient form.
