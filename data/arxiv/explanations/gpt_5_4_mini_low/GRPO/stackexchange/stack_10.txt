\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How should I interpret the claim that RL improves Maj@$K$ but not Pass@$K$?
Question:
In the discussion section, the paper says reinforcement learning improves Maj@$K$ but not Pass@$K$, and they interpret this as making the output distribution more robust rather than improving the model's fundamental capability.

I am not fully comfortable with that interpretation. My understanding is:

- Pass@$K$ asks whether at least one of $K$ samples is correct.
- Maj@$K$ asks whether majority voting over $K$ samples gives the correct answer.

If Pass@$K$ stays about the same but Maj@$K$ improves, does that really mean the set of reachable correct solutions is unchanged, while the probability mass shifts toward correct reasoning traces? If so, is that evidence that RL is mainly doing distribution reshaping rather than discovering new capabilities?

I'd like a more precise explanation of what these two metrics diagnose.
Answer:
A good way to read the paper’s claim is that $Pass@K$ and $Maj@K$ are probing two different parts of the model’s output distribution.

$Pass@K$ is a “coverage” metric: if you draw $K$ samples, did you get at least one correct answer anywhere in that sample set? So it is sensitive to whether the model ever puts nontrivial probability mass on a correct solution at all. If $Pass@K$ does not change much after RL, that suggests RL is not dramatically expanding the set of prompts for which a correct solution becomes reachable under the same decoding scheme.

$Maj@K$, by contrast, is a “dominance” metric: among the $K$ sampled answers, does the majority vote land on the correct answer? This depends not just on whether correct solutions appear, but on whether they appear often enough to outvote incorrect ones. So $Maj@K$ improves when RL shifts probability mass toward the correct reasoning trace and away from competing wrong traces, even if the chance of seeing at least one correct sample stays about the same.

That distinction is exactly why the authors interpret their result as distribution reshaping rather than a fundamental capability gain. In the paper’s own wording, RL “enhances the model’s overall performance by rendering the output distribution more robust,” and “it seems that the improvement is attributed to boosting the correct response from $TopK$ rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities.” In other words, the model already knows how to solve many of these problems in some parts of its support, but RL makes those correct trajectories more likely to be sampled and more likely to win the vote.

So your intuition is basically right, with one caveat. If $Pass@K$ stays flat while $Maj@K$ improves, the strongest conclusion is not “RL found entirely new reasoning abilities.” It is “RL reweighted existing behaviors so that correct reasoning traces are sampled more reliably and are less often drowned out by plausible but wrong traces.” That is a form of robustness, calibration, or distribution sharpening. It is not evidence that the hypothesis space of solutions changed in a deep sense; rather, it suggests the model’s pre-existing correct solutions became more accessible under sampling.

Of course, this does not prove there was absolutely zero capability gain. $Pass@K$ is only measured for a particular $K$ and a particular decoding setup, so some finer-grained gains could be hidden. But within the paper’s own experimental framework, the clean interpretation is that RL mostly improves the frequency and stability of correct outputs, not the underlying breadth of solvable problems.
