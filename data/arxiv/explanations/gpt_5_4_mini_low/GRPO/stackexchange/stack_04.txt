\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why would arXiv pretraining fail to improve mathematical reasoning in this paper?
Question:
One of the most counterintuitive claims in the paper is that arXiv papers 'seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning'. They report little or no gain, and sometimes deterioration, when training on arXiv-heavy corpora.

This confuses me because arXiv contains a huge amount of advanced mathematics and formal-looking reasoning. Shouldn't that be ideal data for downstream math tasks?

Is the likely explanation that benchmark tasks such as GSM8K and MATH are much closer to worked examples, educational exposition, and problem-solution style web pages than to research papers? Or is the issue that arXiv text has too much notation, domain specificity, and incomplete derivations to teach the kind of reasoning these benchmarks test?

I'm looking for an intuitive explanation of why 'more advanced mathematical text' might not translate into better 'mathematical reasoning' scores.
Answer:
The paper’s claim is less surprising if you separate “mathematical text” from “mathematical reasoning data.”

What the benchmarks in this paper reward, such as GSM8K, MATH, and CMATH, is not just exposure to advanced symbols or definitions, but the ability to produce a complete solution path from a short problem statement to a final answer. That is very close to the style of worked examples, instructional pages, and problem-solution web pages. The DeepSeekMath authors explicitly argue that their Common Crawl-derived math corpus is high quality precisely because it contains this kind of web text, and they report that it outperforms corpora dominated by arXiv papers. In contrast, arXiv papers “seem ineffective” in their experiments, even when the arXiv corpus is large and cleaned.

Intuitively, arXiv papers are often the wrong supervision signal for these benchmarks. A research paper usually assumes a lot of context, compresses arguments aggressively, and leaves many steps implicit. It is optimized for communicating new results to experts, not for teaching step-by-step problem solving. So although arXiv contains advanced mathematics, most of that content is not in the form “here is a problem, here is a full derivation, now predict the next step,” which is exactly the kind of pattern language models can most easily turn into benchmark performance. The paper’s own discussion points in this direction: it contrasts arXiv-heavy corpora with web corpora mined for mathematical content and finds the latter much more useful.

There is also a representation issue. The paper suggests that arXiv-only training can even hurt performance on some tasks. That is plausible because arXiv text has a very different distribution from benchmark questions: it is denser, more domain-specific, and often more notation-heavy. If the model mostly sees theorem statements, proofs, and technical exposition, it may learn to model that genre well without learning the “problem solving” format tested by these benchmarks. In other words, it may become better at reading papers, but not necessarily better at solving grade-school or competition-style questions.

So the short answer is: yes, your intuition is basically right, but with an important nuance. The issue is not that arXiv is “too advanced” in some absolute sense; it is that the benchmarked skill is closer to structured solution generation than to consuming research prose. Advanced mathematics in arXiv is not automatically good training data for reasoning unless it is presented in a form that exposes intermediate steps and solution structure.

The paper also hedges appropriately: the authors say this conclusion should be taken “with a grain of salt.” They did not test every possible math task, they did not study arXiv combined with other data, and they note that larger models might benefit more. So the result is really about this experimental setting: for the kinds of mathematical reasoning benchmarks they evaluate, arXiv-heavy pretraining is not as helpful as one might expect.
