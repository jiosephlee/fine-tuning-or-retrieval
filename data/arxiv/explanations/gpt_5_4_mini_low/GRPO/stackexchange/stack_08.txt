\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### What does the 'unbiased KL estimator' in the GRPO section actually estimate?
Question:
In the GRPO section, the paper gives the following expression for estimating the KL divergence:

$\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\theta} \| \pi_{ref}] = \frac{\pi_{ref}(o_{i,t} \mid q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t} \mid q,o_{i,<t})} - \log \frac{\pi_{ref}(o_{i,t} \mid q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t} \mid q,o_{i,<t})} - 1.$

This confuses me for two reasons:

1. A KL divergence is an expectation over a distribution, but the formula shown looks like a sample-wise function of a single token.
2. The ratio is written as $\pi_{ref} / \pi_{\theta}$, which is not the usual log-ratio form I expect from KL.

How should I interpret this equation? Is it an unbiased estimator whose expectation under samples from $\pi_{\theta}$ equals $\mathbb{D}_{KL}(\pi_{\theta} \| \pi_{ref})$? If so, what derivation justifies that specific form?
Answer:
Yes — the expression is meant as an unbiased single-sample estimator of the token-level KL divergence, where the sample token is drawn from the current policy $\pi_\theta$.

The key identity is the standard variational trick behind KL estimation. If $x \sim p$, then

$$
\mathbb{D}_{KL}(p \| q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right].
$$

But instead of estimating this with just the raw log-ratio, the paper uses the function

$$
\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} - \log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)} - 1.
$$

If you take expectation under $x \sim p$, this is indeed exactly $\mathbb{D}_{KL}(p\|q)$, because

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right]
= \sum_x p(x)\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} = \sum_x q(x) = 1,
$$

so

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} - \log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)} - 1\right]
= 1 - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right] - 1
= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right]
= \mathbb{D}_{KL}(p\|q).
$$

In the paper’s notation, set $p = \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid q,o_{<t})$ and $q = \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid q,o_{<t})$. Then for a sampled token $o_{i,t} \sim \pi_\theta$, the quantity

$$
\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
-\log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
-1
$$

is a one-sample estimator whose expectation is the forward KL

$$
\mathbb{D}_{KL}\!\left[\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}}\right].
$$

So your intuition is correct: the displayed formula is not the KL itself, but a sample-wise estimator of it.

Why this particular form instead of just the log-ratio? Because the raw log-ratio $\log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ is also an unbiased estimator under samples from $\pi_\theta$, but the paper is using the estimator from \cite{kl_approx}, which has the useful property of being nonnegative pointwise since for $u>0$,

$$
u - \log u - 1 \ge 0.
$$

This makes it a convenient penalty term for optimization. Intuitively, it measures how much the current policy deviates from the reference policy, while staying well behaved numerically.

One small nuance: the paper writes the estimator at the token level, but the true sequence-level KL is a sum/expectation over tokens conditioned on prefixes. So in practice they are applying this estimator per token and averaging, which is the usual way KL regularization is handled in language-model RL.
