\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why normalize rewards within each question group instead of across the whole batch in GRPO?
Question:
GRPO samples $G$ outputs for the same question, scores them, and then normalizes the rewards using the mean and standard deviation of that group only.

I think I understand that this creates a question-specific baseline, but I don't see why this is preferable to global normalization across the batch. If one question is inherently harder than another, group-wise normalization seems to erase that information completely.

Is the idea that raw reward magnitudes are not comparable across different prompts, so only relative ranking among outputs for the same prompt is trustworthy? Or is there another variance-reduction argument here?

I'm looking for intuition about why 'within-question relative quality' is the right signal for mathematical RL in this setup.
Answer:
In GRPO, the group-wise normalization is not just a convenience; it matches the structure of the supervision signal the paper is using.

The key point is that the reward model is trained on comparisons between multiple candidate answers to the same question, not on an absolute, globally calibrated score across unrelated questions. So a reward of, say, $0.8$ for one question and $0.8$ for another does not necessarily mean the same thing in any meaningful sense. The model is really telling you, “among these $G$ attempts at this one prompt, which ones are better?” That is why the paper says the group-relative formulation “aligns well with the comparative nature of reward models.”

If you normalized across the whole batch, you would mix together outputs from different questions whose reward scales may differ for reasons unrelated to policy quality. Some questions are genuinely easier, some are harder, and some reward-model scores may be systematically higher or lower depending on the prompt. A global mean and standard deviation would therefore introduce a noisy reference point: a response could look “good” relative to the batch simply because it came from an easy question, or “bad” because it came from a hard question, even if it was one of the best answers for its own prompt. That would blur the learning signal.

Within-question normalization solves this by turning the reward into a relative advantage inside each prompt’s candidate set:
$$
\widetilde{r}_i=\frac{r_i-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf r)}.
$$
So the policy is reinforced for responses that are better than the other samples for the same question, and penalized for worse ones. Intuitively, this gives you a “which of these completions should I prefer?” signal, rather than “how good was this completion in some absolute sense?” For mathematical reasoning, where the same problem can have multiple plausible but not equally correct derivations, that relative signal is especially useful.

There is also a variance-reduction angle, which is exactly why baselines are used in policy-gradient methods. Subtracting the group mean removes the average difficulty level of that question, so the gradient focuses on differences among candidate outputs rather than on question-specific reward offsets. In that sense, the group baseline is acting like a per-question control variate. The additional division by the group standard deviation makes the scale of the advantage more comparable across questions, so very high-variance reward groups do not dominate training just because their raw scores are spread out more.

So the short answer is: yes, the paper is assuming that raw reward magnitudes are not meaningfully comparable across different prompts, and GRPO intentionally learns from within-question relative quality. That is both statistically cleaner and better matched to how the reward model is constructed.
