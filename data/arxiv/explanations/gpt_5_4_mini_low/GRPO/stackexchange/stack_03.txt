\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How strong is the paper's decontamination method based on exact 10-gram matching?
Question:
DeepSeekMath says they avoid benchmark contamination by removing any text segment containing a 10-gram that exactly matches benchmark text, and for shorter benchmark text they use exact matching when the text has at least 3 grams.

I understand the basic idea, but I don't know how reliable this is in practice. For math problems, many solutions and statements can be paraphrased while keeping the same numbers, formulas, or structure. So I am wondering:

1. Does exact 10-gram matching mostly remove verbatim copies but still miss lightly reworded benchmark leakage?
2. Could it also over-remove benign pages that happen to share common mathematical phrasing?
3. When they say they remove 'any text segment' with a match, does that mean the whole page is removed or only the matching span?

I'm trying to judge how convincing the contamination control is for math benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH.
Answer:
The short answer is that this is a fairly standard and reasonably conservative *verbatim* decontamination strategy, but it is not a guarantee against all leakage. In the paper, DeepSeekMath says they “filter out web pages containing questions or answers” from the benchmarks, and the criterion is that “any text segment containing a $10$-gram string that matches exactly with any sub-string from the evaluation benchmarks is removed.” That means they are trying to eliminate pages with direct textual overlap, not to detect semantic similarity.

So to your first point: yes, exact $10$-gram matching will mainly catch near-verbatim copies, and it will miss lightly paraphrased benchmark leakage. If a benchmark problem is rewritten with the same numbers and structure but different wording, it can easily evade an exact $10$-token match. This is a limitation of all exact-string decontamination methods: they are good at detecting copied text, but not at detecting “same problem, rephrased.” For math datasets like GSM8K and MATH, that matters because many problems can indeed be paraphrased while preserving the underlying reasoning task.

On the second point, over-removal is possible in principle, but the $10$-gram threshold is chosen precisely to reduce accidental matches from common mathematical phrasing. Short exact overlaps like “find the value of” would be too noisy, which is why they only use exact matching for benchmark texts shorter than $10$ grams if they are at least $3$ grams long. A full $10$-gram exact match is relatively specific, so false positives should be much rarer than with shorter n-grams. Still, some benign pages could be removed if they happen to contain a long formulaic segment that appears in a benchmark, especially in math where wording is repetitive. So the method is conservative in the sense of preferring false negatives over false positives, but it is not immune to either.

For your third question, the wording in the paper suggests that the removal unit is the surrounding text segment or page, not just the matched span. They say “any text segment containing” the matching $10$-gram is removed, and earlier they describe filtering out “web pages containing questions or answers” from the benchmarks. So the intention is to exclude the whole web page or extracted segment that contains the match, rather than surgically deleting only the overlapping substring. That is consistent with typical contamination filtering pipelines, because keeping the rest of the page would still leave nearby context that could leak the benchmark content.

So how convincing is this for GSM8K and MATH? I would call it a useful and defensible baseline, but not airtight. It strongly reduces the risk of memorizing exact benchmark text, which is the most direct kind of contamination. But it does not rule out paraphrased benchmark problems, equivalent algebraic variants, or pages that indirectly reveal solutions. In other words, it controls for *textual overlap*, not for *task-level overlap*. For a math benchmark, that is enough to make “the model saw the benchmark verbatim” much less plausible, but not enough to prove there was no benchmark-related leakage at all.
