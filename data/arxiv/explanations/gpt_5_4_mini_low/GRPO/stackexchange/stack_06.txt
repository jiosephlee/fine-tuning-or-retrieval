\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How is GRPO different from PPO if both still use importance ratios and clipping?
Question:
At first glance, GRPO looks very similar to PPO because the objective still contains the same kind of clipped importance ratio term

$\frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t} \mid q, o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_{i,t} \mid q, o_{i,<t})}$

with a $\min(\cdot, \mathrm{clip}(\cdot))$ structure.

The paper says the main difference is that GRPO removes the critic and uses group-relative rewards as a baseline. But I'm not sure how deep this difference really is. Is GRPO best understood as 'PPO with a handcrafted baseline instead of a learned value function', or does the group normalization fundamentally change the optimization target?

I'd appreciate a careful explanation of what is preserved from PPO and what is genuinely new in GRPO.
Answer:
GRPO is best understood as keeping the *PPO-style policy update* but replacing PPO’s *advantage estimation machinery* with a group-relative one, and then moving the KL regularization from the reward into the loss. So the clipped importance ratio is still there because GRPO is still doing a proximal, on-policy policy-gradient update; what changes is how the scalar signal $A_{i,t}$ is obtained and how much extra machinery is needed to compute it.

In PPO, the clipped term

$$
\min\left[r_t(\theta) A_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)A_t\right]
$$

is only half the story. The important part is that $A_t$ comes from a learned value function $V_\psi$ via GAE, so PPO is really an actor-critic method: the critic estimates “how much better than expected” a token/action was, and that estimate is used as the policy-gradient weight. In the paper’s formulation, PPO also typically includes a KL penalty inside the reward

$$
r_t = r_\varphi(q,o_{\le t}) - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}.
$$

GRPO removes the critic entirely. Instead, for each prompt $q$, it samples a *group* of outputs $\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G$ from the old policy, scores them, and computes advantages from the *relative* reward within that group. In the simplest outcome-supervision case, the advantage is just the normalized reward

$$
\hat A_{i,t}=\frac{r_i-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf r)},
$$

and this same value is used for all tokens in the sampled completion. So the baseline is not learned per token; it is the empirical mean of the group, which acts as a local, question-conditioned baseline. That is the key idea: “better than the other answers to the same question” replaces “better than the critic’s prediction.”

This is more than just a cosmetic baseline swap, because it changes the variance-reduction mechanism and the supervision signal. PPO’s critic tries to predict absolute expected return token by token, which is hard in language generation, especially when the reward is often only available at the end of the completion. GRPO instead uses comparisons among multiple samples for the same prompt, which fits the comparative nature of reward models much better. In other words, the optimization is still policy-gradient-like, but the *target signal* is now relative rather than absolute.

So the right mental model is: GRPO preserves PPO’s proximal/clipped policy update, but simplifies the estimator by dropping the critic and replacing it with group-relative advantages. That is why the paper describes it as a “variant of PPO” rather than a completely different algorithm. It is not changing the fundamental objective into something unrelated; it is changing how the advantage is constructed and how the KL constraint is applied. The result is lower memory use and, in this paper, better suitability for mathematical reasoning where relative correctness across multiple sampled solutions is especially informative.
