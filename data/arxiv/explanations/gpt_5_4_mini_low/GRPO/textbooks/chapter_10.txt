\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 10: 10. Outcome Supervision, Process Supervision, and Iterative RL

# Outcome supervision in RL for mathematical reasoning

In DeepSeekMath, reinforcement learning is used after supervised fine-tuning to further improve mathematical reasoning. The paper presents a practical variant of this idea through Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO, and then shows how GRPO can be instantiated in different supervision styles. One of the simplest is outcome supervision. In this setting, the model is given a question, generates several candidate solutions, and each complete solution is scored by a reward model. The reward is attached only to the final outcome of the solution, meaning the training signal says, in effect, whether the whole answer is good or bad rather than which individual reasoning step helped or hurt.

This design matches the structure of many mathematical tasks in the paper, especially benchmark-style problems where the final answer is what matters. For each question $q$, the policy samples a group of outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$ from the old policy. A reward model then assigns a scalar reward $r_i$ to each full output $o_i$. These rewards are not used directly as raw scores. Instead, they are normalized within the group, so the model learns from relative quality among multiple candidate solutions to the same question. This is important because reward modeling in the paper is based on comparisons over outputs for the same prompt, so the group structure is not incidental; it is part of the training logic.

Outcome supervision is especially attractive because it is straightforward. It requires only a reward on the final response, and it fits naturally with benchmarks where correctness can be judged from the answer. At the same time, the paper makes clear that this simplicity comes with a limitation: the signal is sparse. If a mathematical solution is long and multi-step, a single end-of-sequence reward may not be enough to tell the model which parts of the reasoning were helpful and which parts were not.

# Normalized final-sequence rewards as token-level advantages

GRPO converts the group of final rewards into training signals for every token in each sampled output. The paper’s outcome supervision rule is simple: after scoring the $G$ outputs, the rewards are normalized by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation. The normalized score for output $o_i$ is

$$
\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})},
$$

where $\mathbf{r} = \{r_1, r_2, \cdots, r_G\}$. This normalized value is then assigned to all tokens in the output. In other words, every token in the same generated solution receives the same advantage value:

$$
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i.
$$

This is a crucial design choice. Even though the reward is only computed at the end of the sequence, the policy update is still token-level, because the objective sums over token log-probabilities. By giving every token in a sampled output the same advantage, the method pushes the model to increase the likelihood of entire high-scoring solutions and decrease the likelihood of low-scoring ones.

The normalization step is also important for stability and comparability. Because the reward model may produce scores on different scales across questions, the paper uses group-relative normalization so that learning depends on relative quality within each set of candidates rather than on an absolute score. This reinforces the comparative nature of the reward model used in the paper, which is trained on comparisons between outputs for the same question.

# Process supervision and step-level reward signals

The paper also studies process supervision, which differs from outcome supervision in one major way: instead of waiting until the end of the solution, the reward model evaluates intermediate reasoning steps. This is especially relevant for mathematical reasoning, where the final answer may be correct or incorrect for many reasons, and where the value of a solution often depends on the correctness of the chain of reasoning itself.

In the process supervision setup, each sampled output is broken into reasoning steps. For output $o_i$, the process reward model produces a reward at the end of each step. If the output contains $K_i$ steps, the rewards are written as

$$
\{r_i^{\mathrm{index}(1)}, \cdots, r_i^{\mathrm{index}(K_i)}\},
$$

where $\mathrm{index}(j)$ denotes the end token index of the $j$-th step. The paper then normalizes these step rewards across the full set of step-level rewards from the group, again using the mean and standard deviation. The result is a set of normalized step rewards that can be used to supervise token updates more finely than a single final score.

This approach gives the learner more detailed feedback. Instead of simply rewarding the whole final answer, the model can learn which intermediate steps contribute positively to the solution and which steps should be discouraged. The paper emphasizes that this can be more informative and efficient for complex mathematical tasks, where a correct final answer may depend on many linked steps.

# Computing advantages from future step rewards

The key technical step in process supervision is how the step rewards are turned into token-level advantages. The paper defines the advantage for a token as the sum of normalized rewards from the current step onward. If $\widetilde{r}_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}$ is the normalized reward at the end of the $j$-th step, then the advantage for token $t$ in output $o_i$ is

$$
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{\mathrm{index}(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}.
$$

This means that a token inherits credit from all future step rewards that occur after it, including the reward at the step where its contribution becomes visible. The intuition is that a token should be reinforced if it helps produce a sequence of later steps that are judged well by the process reward model.

Compared with outcome supervision, this creates a much denser learning signal. In outcome supervision, all tokens in a trajectory get the same final score. In process supervision, earlier tokens can receive credit or blame based on the downstream steps they enable. The paper presents this as a way to better support complex reasoning, since long mathematical solutions often need more guidance than a single end result can provide.

# When process supervision is more informative than outcome supervision

The paper’s motivation for process supervision is that outcome supervision may not be sufficient for difficult mathematical reasoning. If only the final answer is scored, then many different internal reasoning paths may receive the same training signal, even though some are much better than others. Process supervision gives the model finer-grained instruction by scoring the reasoning process itself.

This matters most when the problem is complex and the correctness of the reasoning path is more important than just the final answer. The paper treats process supervision as especially suitable for mathematical tasks where intermediate steps can be assessed. In such cases, the model receives feedback not only on whether the answer is right, but on how it arrived there. That makes the signal more informative, because the policy can learn to improve specific parts of its reasoning rather than only the final result.

At the same time, the paper does not present process supervision as a universal replacement for outcome supervision. Instead, it is one option within the GRPO framework. The key takeaway is that process supervision can provide more detailed guidance, but it also depends on having a reward model that can score intermediate steps reliably. So the benefit comes from richer signal, while the cost is greater complexity in reward modeling.

# Iterative RL with alternating policy and reward model updates

The paper also goes beyond one-shot reinforcement learning and considers iterative RL. The reason is that as the policy improves, an old reward model may no longer be adequate for supervising the new policy. If the policy starts producing better or more diverse outputs than those seen during reward-model training, then the reward model can become stale.

To address this, the paper proposes iterative GRPO. In this setup, the policy is trained, then its sampled outputs are used to refresh the reward model, and then the updated reward model is used to train the policy again. The training alternates between the two components rather than keeping the reward model fixed throughout. This creates a feedback loop in which the reward model is continually adapted to the current policy distribution.

The paper describes this as generating new training sets for the reward model from the policy’s sampling results and then continually training the old reward model using a replay mechanism that includes $10\%$ of historical data. After that, the reference model is reset to the current policy model, and policy training continues with the refreshed reward model. This is a practical response to the fact that reinforcement learning changes the policy distribution over time.

# Replay mechanisms for reward model stability

A central detail in iterative RL is the replay mechanism. The paper does not retrain the reward model only on newly generated data. Instead, it includes $10\%$ of historical data when updating the reward model. This helps preserve stability while still letting the reward model adapt to the current policy.

The need for replay comes from the changing nature of the data distribution. If the reward model were trained only on the newest policy samples, it could forget earlier patterns or become too narrowly specialized to recent outputs. By mixing in historical data, the model remains grounded in prior training while still tracking the evolving policy. In the paper’s framing, this is a practical way to keep the reward model useful as the RL loop progresses.

This also fits the broader iterative design. Since the policy is changing, the reward model must stay aligned with the kinds of outputs the policy now produces. Replay is the mechanism that prevents this update process from becoming unstable or overly myopic.

# Algorithmic workflow of iterative GRPO

The paper gives a concrete iterative workflow for GRPO. First, an initial policy model $\pi_{\theta_{\text{init}}}$ is set as the starting point. In each outer iteration, the reference model is updated to the current policy. Then, for each training step, a batch of questions is sampled, the current policy generates $G$ outputs for each question, and the reward model scores them. Using those scores, advantages are computed by either outcome supervision or process supervision. The policy is then updated for a small number of GRPO iterations, and after the training steps, the reward model itself is refreshed using data from the policy’s recent outputs.

This alternation between policy updates and reward-model updates is the defining feature of iterative GRPO. It is not just training the policy harder; it is changing the supervision signal as the policy evolves. The workflow reflects the paper’s view that a fixed reward model may be insufficient once the policy has moved beyond the region where the reward model was originally trained.

The paper reports that iterative RL can significantly improve performance, especially in the first iteration. That suggests the extra adaptation of the reward model is not merely a technical detail but can materially affect learning outcomes.

# Tradeoffs among simplicity, signal quality, and compute cost

The paper’s discussion of outcome supervision, process supervision, and iterative RL can be understood as a set of tradeoffs. Outcome supervision is the simplest: it requires only a final reward for each completed solution, and it is easy to apply. But the signal is sparse, because all the model learns is how good the final answer was. Process supervision is richer: it provides step-level feedback and can better support complex reasoning. However, it requires a more elaborate reward model and more structured supervision. Iterative RL goes one step further by updating the reward model as the policy changes, which makes the system more adaptive but also more costly and operationally complex.

The paper’s GRPO framework tries to make these variants practical by avoiding the critic model used in PPO and by using group-relative rewards instead. That reduces memory usage while preserving a useful learning signal. Within this framework, outcome supervision, process supervision, and iterative updates are different ways to balance the same underlying goals: giving the policy a meaningful training signal, keeping the computation manageable, and ensuring the reward model remains relevant as the policy improves.

From the paper’s perspective, the best choice depends on the task and the available supervision. Outcome supervision is attractive when final-answer correctness is enough. Process supervision becomes more valuable when the reasoning path itself matters. Iterative RL is useful when the policy evolves enough that the reward model needs to be refreshed. Together, these variants show how GRPO can be adapted to different levels of supervision while staying grounded in the comparative, group-based training logic that underlies the method.
