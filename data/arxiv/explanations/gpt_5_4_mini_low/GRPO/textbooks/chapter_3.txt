\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 3: 3. Building the DeepSeekMath Corpus from the Web

# Why web-scale mathematical data is valuable

The central idea behind DeepSeekMath is that the public web already contains a large amount of mathematical content, but this content is scattered, noisy, and difficult to collect directly. The paper argues that Common Crawl can be mined for useful mathematical text at scale if the retrieval process is carefully designed. This matters because mathematical reasoning is not only about having more parameters; it also depends heavily on the quality and diversity of the training data. The authors show that a large, high-quality web-derived corpus can substantially improve mathematical reasoning, even for a relatively small $7$B-parameter model.

This motivation is supported by their downstream results. After building a $120$B-token corpus, they train a base model that performs strongly on mathematical benchmarks and even surpasses much larger open and closed models in some settings. The broader message is that web-scale data, if filtered well, can contain enough signal to meaningfully train a math-focused language model. In other words, the challenge is not whether the web contains math, but how to find it reliably.

# Using OpenWebMath as the initial seed corpus

The construction process begins with a seed corpus: OpenWebMath. The paper describes OpenWebMath as a collection of high-quality mathematical web texts, and it is used as the starting point for discovering more pages in Common Crawl that resemble mathematical content. The intuition is simple: if one has a small set of trustworthy examples, those examples can be used to train a classifier that recognizes similar pages elsewhere on the web.

This seed corpus serves two roles. First, it provides positive examples for training a page-retrieval model. Second, it defines the style of web page the authors want to expand from: mathematical, web-based, and broadly similar to the examples already curated in OpenWebMath. The paper emphasizes that this seed is only the beginning; the key challenge is to expand beyond it without drifting into irrelevant or low-quality content.

# Training a fastText classifier for math page retrieval

To mine Common Crawl, the authors train a fastText classifier. The classifier is used to estimate whether a page looks like the positive examples in the seed corpus. This is a retrieval-style setup: rather than generating math text, the model identifies candidate pages that are likely to be math-related. The paper chooses fastText because it is simple, efficient, and suitable for large-scale web filtering.

The training procedure is iterative. In the first round, the classifier is trained on positive examples from OpenWebMath and negative examples drawn from Common Crawl. The resulting model is then used to search the deduplicated crawl for additional mathematical pages. Those newly found pages are later refined and used to improve the classifier itself. This creates a feedback loop in which the retrieval model gets better as the corpus becomes more diverse.

# Positive and negative examples for classifier training

The classifier is initially trained on $500{,}000$ positive examples sampled from the seed corpus and $500{,}000$ negative examples sampled from Common Crawl. The positive set contains pages that are already known to be mathematical. The negative set is designed to represent broad web content that is not specifically mathematical. This balanced setup helps the classifier learn what distinguishes a math page from a general webpage.

The paper also describes the concrete training configuration for fastText. The authors set the vector dimension to $256$, the learning rate to $0.1$, the maximum word n-gram length to $3$, the minimum word occurrence count to $3$, and train for $3$ epochs. These details matter because the classifier is not a large neural retriever; it is a lightweight filter whose purpose is to efficiently score enormous numbers of web pages. The quality of the final corpus depends on how well this classifier can separate math-like pages from the rest of the crawl.

# Deduplication and near-deduplication of Common Crawl

Before retrieval, the authors reduce redundancy in Common Crawl using URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication. This step shrinks the original crawl into a set of $40$B HTML web pages. The purpose is to avoid wasting effort on repeated or near-repeated content and to make the retrieval process more efficient and less biased by duplicate pages.

This preprocessing step is important because web corpora often contain many copies of the same material, or pages that differ only slightly. If these were left untreated, the classifier could overcount repetitive content and the final corpus could be dominated by duplicates. By removing duplicated pages first, the authors ensure that the mining process better reflects the diversity of the web.

# Ranking and selecting high-scoring math-related web pages

After deduplication, the fastText classifier is applied to Common Crawl to recall mathematical pages. The pages are then ranked according to the classifier’s scores, and only the top-ranking ones are kept. This ranking stage acts as a quality filter: even if a page is somewhat math-like, it is not necessarily worth including unless it scores highly enough to pass the selection threshold.

The paper evaluates how much data to preserve by running pre-training experiments on different cutoff sizes, including the top $40$B, $80$B, $120$B, and $160$B tokens. In the first iteration, the authors choose to keep the top $40$B tokens. This is an important design choice because it uses downstream learning behavior, not just classifier score, to decide how much content to retain. The approach reflects a practical principle: retrieval quality should be judged by how useful the resulting data is for training a model.

# Iterative corpus expansion through domain-level analysis

The initial retrieval pass does not collect all available mathematical pages. The authors explain that many mathematical pages remain uncollected because the first classifier was trained on a seed corpus that did not cover enough kinds of math content. To address this, they use the pages discovered in one iteration to improve the next. The process becomes an iterative expansion pipeline rather than a one-shot mining step.

A key idea in this expansion strategy is domain-level analysis. Instead of looking only at isolated pages, the authors organize Common Crawl into disjoint domains. A domain is defined as all web pages sharing the same base URL. This allows them to measure how concentrated mathematical content is within each website. By looking at domain-level collection patterns, they can identify websites that likely contain many additional math pages beyond those found in the first pass.

# Identifying math-related domains by collection density

The authors define a domain as math-related if more than $10\%$ of its pages were collected in the first iteration. This threshold turns domain discovery into a practical heuristic: if a website yields a relatively high number of math pages early on, then the website likely hosts more math content worth mining. The paper gives $\url{mathoverflow.net}$ as an example of such a domain.

This step matters because mathematical pages are often clustered. A single math forum, Q\&A site, or educational domain may contain many related pages, and finding one page can provide a clue about the rest. By measuring the density of collected pages within each domain, the authors use the structure of the web itself to guide further retrieval. This is more effective than treating every page as an independent sample.

# Manual annotation of URL patterns within domains

Once math-related domains are identified, the authors manually annotate URL patterns associated with mathematical content inside those domains. For example, in the case of $\url{mathoverflow.net}$, they identify relevant URL paths such as $\url{mathoverflow.net/questions}$. This manual step is used to locate additional pages within domains that the classifier may have missed.

The paper’s strategy here is not to annotate every page, but to annotate URL structures that are likely to point to useful pages. This is a lightweight form of human supervision that expands coverage without requiring full manual labeling of the entire crawl. The annotated URL patterns become a way to harvest new positive examples more efficiently than relying on the classifier alone.

# Bootstrapping new positive examples for improved recall

The pages linked to the annotated URLs, if not already collected, are added to the seed corpus. This is the bootstrapping mechanism that strengthens the next round of classifier training. The idea is that once the seed corpus contains more diverse mathematical pages, the classifier can learn a broader notion of what mathematical web content looks like.

This is crucial for recall. If the initial seed is too narrow, the classifier may only find pages similar to the original examples and miss whole subgenres of mathematical content. By adding human-identified pages from newly discovered domains, the authors enrich the positive set and retrain the classifier to recognize a wider variety of math pages. The result is a more capable retrieval model for the subsequent iteration.

# Stopping criteria after multiple collection iterations

The corpus construction proceeds through four iterations. By the end, the authors collect $35.5$M mathematical web pages totaling $120$B tokens. They report that in the fourth iteration, nearly $98\%$ of the data had already been collected in the third iteration, so they stop data collection.

This stopping criterion is based on diminishing returns. Once nearly all the newly retrievable data has already been found, further iterations are unlikely to add enough useful material to justify the extra effort. The paper’s stopping decision reflects an empirical balance between scale and efficiency: the pipeline is iterated until additional rounds contribute very little new content.

# Benchmark decontamination using n-gram overlap filtering

To avoid benchmark contamination, the authors filter out web pages containing questions or answers from evaluation benchmarks. They apply this procedure to English benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH, as well as Chinese benchmarks such as CMATH and AGIEval. The filtering is performed using exact n-gram matching.

The rule is that any text segment containing a $10$-gram string that exactly matches a substring from the evaluation benchmarks is removed from the math training corpus. If a benchmark text is shorter than $10$ grams but has at least $3$ grams, exact matching is still used to filter out contaminated pages. This is a direct safeguard against training on evaluation items or near copies of them, which would inflate benchmark scores and make the results unreliable.

# Strengths and limitations of the data selection pipeline

The paper presents the data selection pipeline as evidence that public web data can be mined effectively for mathematical pre-training. Its strengths are scale, iterative improvement, and the use of human-guided domain discovery to extend beyond the initial seed. The resulting corpus is not only large but also high quality, multilingual, and useful for downstream mathematical reasoning.

At the same time, the authors are careful not to overclaim. They explicitly frame their approach as a starting point for the research community, noting that there is significant room for improvement. The pipeline relies on an initial seed corpus, a lightweight classifier, and manual inspection of domain structures, so it is effective but not exhaustive. The paper also shows that the corpus is multilingual, which helps Chinese benchmarks, but it does not claim to solve every aspect of mathematical data selection. The broader lesson is that high-quality mathematical pre-training data can be built from the web, but doing so requires careful filtering, iterative expansion, and contamination control.
