\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 1: # Mathematical Reasoning in the Era of Large Language Models

Mathematical reasoning has become one of the clearest and most demanding tests for large language models. Unlike tasks where a model can often succeed by pattern matching or producing fluent general knowledge, mathematics requires the model to follow structured multi-step logic, keep track of intermediate quantities, and arrive at an answer that is often either exactly right or clearly wrong. The paper behind DeepSeekMath treats this as a central challenge for language models and argues that progress in mathematical reasoning reveals something important about reasoning more broadly.

This chapter introduces the problem the paper addresses, explains why open mathematical language models matter, and shows how DeepSeekMath is positioned relative to both closed and open systems. The key message is that mathematical ability is not just a narrow specialty: it is also a revealing stress test for the design of training data, model initialization, instruction tuning, and reinforcement learning.

# Why Mathematical Reasoning Is Difficult for Language Models

The paper emphasizes that mathematical reasoning is hard because math is both complex and structured. A model must often interpret a word problem, translate it into a formal or semi-formal representation, carry out several linked reasoning steps, and maintain consistency across the entire solution. In many cases, the correct answer depends not just on the final computation but on the quality of the intermediate reasoning process.

This difficulty shows up across the benchmarks the paper studies. Some benchmarks, such as GSM8K, test grade-school quantitative reasoning. Others, such as MATH, are competition-level and require significantly more sophisticated reasoning. The paper also considers formal mathematics, where the challenge is not only to reason correctly but to generate a proof in a formal proof assistant like Isabelle. The fact that these tasks vary from ordinary word problems to formal theorem proving illustrates why mathematical reasoning is such a rich testbed: it spans informal language understanding, symbolic manipulation, and rigorous logic.

The paper also notes that models can be evaluated in different ways. Some settings disallow tool use and require the model to generate self-contained text solutions. Other settings allow the model to use Python programs or tool-integrated reasoning. This distinction matters because it separates the model’s raw reasoning ability from its ability to call on external computation. DeepSeekMath is designed to improve both.

# The Gap Between Closed-Source and Open-Source Mathematical LLMs

A major motivation for the paper is the gap between the best closed-source models and the currently available open-source models. The authors note that models such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra are not publicly available, while open models have generally lagged behind on mathematical benchmarks. This creates a practical and scientific limitation: researchers and users can see that top closed models are strong, but they cannot inspect or directly build on them.

DeepSeekMath is presented as an attempt to close that gap in the open-source setting. The paper reports that the model achieves $51.7\%$ on the competition-level MATH benchmark without using external toolkits or voting techniques, and that self-consistency over $64$ samples raises performance to $60.9\%$. On GSM8K, the reinforcement-learning version reaches $88.2\%$. These results are notable not just because they are strong, but because they come from an open model rather than a closed proprietary one.

The paper also stresses that the open-source gap is not simply about scale. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, a model with only $7$ billion parameters, is reported to be competitive with Minerva 540B on mathematical benchmarks. This suggests that data quality, training strategy, and reinforcement learning can matter as much as, or even more than, raw parameter count.

# What DeepSeekMath Aims to Achieve

DeepSeekMath is designed to do two things at once. First, it aims to build a strong open mathematical language model through large-scale math pre-training. Second, it aims to improve that model further through reinforcement learning. The paper argues that these are complementary: pre-training gives the model a strong foundation, and reinforcement learning sharpens its reasoning behavior.

The authors frame DeepSeekMath as a domain-specific language model that significantly outperforms open-source models and approaches the performance of GPT-4 on academic benchmarks. The central ambition is not merely to make a model that can answer math questions, but to demonstrate that publicly available web data and carefully designed training methods can produce a strong mathematical reasoner.

The paper also emphasizes a broader scientific goal. By building and analyzing DeepSeekMath, the authors want to better understand what ingredients actually improve mathematical reasoning. Their findings about data selection, code training, arXiv, online versus offline reinforcement learning, and group-relative optimization are presented as lessons that could guide future research beyond this specific model.

# Overview of DeepSeekMath-Base, DeepSeekMath-Instruct, and DeepSeekMath-RL

The paper introduces DeepSeekMath in three stages, each representing a different point in the training pipeline.

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is the pre-trained base model. It is initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B and then continually trained on $500$ billion tokens. The training mix includes the DeepSeekMath Corpus, code, arXiv, and natural language data in English and Chinese. This model is meant to capture the effect of large-scale math-oriented pre-training before any supervised instruction tuning.

DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B is obtained by supervised fine-tuning DeepSeekMath-Base on $776$K mathematical instruction examples. These examples include chain-of-thought solutions, program-of-thought solutions, and tool-integrated reasoning. The goal is to teach the model how to express mathematical reasoning in formats that are useful for solving problems step by step.

DeepSeekMath-RL 7B is the result of reinforcement learning applied to DeepSeekMath-Instruct. The paper’s reinforcement learning stage uses only a subset of the instruction-tuning data, specifically chain-of-thought-format questions from GSM8K and MATH, and then applies the Group Relative Policy Optimization method. This stage is intended to further improve reasoning performance, especially on benchmarks where the model already has some ability but can still benefit from better policy optimization.

Together, these three versions let the paper study the full progression from pre-training to instruction tuning to reinforcement learning. That progression is important because the paper’s claims are not only about final performance, but about where the improvements come from.

# Two Central Ideas of the Paper: Large-Scale Math Pre-Training and Reinforcement Learning

The paper argues that DeepSeekMath’s reasoning ability comes from two main ideas.

The first is large-scale math pre-training from public web data. The authors build the DeepSeekMath Corpus by mining Common Crawl with a fastText-based classifier, iteratively improving the classifier using newly found math-related sources and human annotation. The corpus grows to $120$ billion math tokens, which the paper describes as much larger than previous math web corpora. The key point is that useful mathematical text exists in public web data if it is carefully selected.

The second idea is reinforcement learning, specifically Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO. GRPO is a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization that removes the need for a critic model and instead uses the relative scores of multiple sampled outputs as a baseline. The paper argues that this reduces memory usage while still improving mathematical reasoning. In the reported experiments, GRPO boosts performance beyond the already strong instruction-tuned model.

These two ideas are tightly connected. The first builds a strong foundation; the second exploits that foundation to push reasoning performance further. The paper’s broader message is that progress in mathematical language modeling comes not only from more data or more parameters, but from carefully designed training pipelines.

# Main Benchmark Headline Results, Including MATH and GSM8K

The headline results are central to the paper’s argument. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B achieves $64.2\%$ on GSM8K and $36.2\%$ on MATH, outperforming Minerva 540B in the base-model setting. This is an important claim because it shows that a much smaller open model can compete with a very large closed model when the training data and initialization are chosen well.

After supervised fine-tuning, DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B becomes competitive with much larger open-source models and performs strongly both with and without tool use. When tool use is disallowed, it surpasses all open-source models and many proprietary models on MATH by a significant margin. When tool use is allowed, it approaches $60\%$ on MATH.

The reinforcement-learning stage produces the strongest open-source results in the paper. DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reaches $88.2\%$ on GSM8K and $51.7\%$ on MATH using chain-of-thought reasoning. The paper notes that these results exceed all open-source models in the $7$B to $70$B range and surpass most closed-source models as well. For the first time in the open-source community, the model exceeds $50\%$ on the competition-level MATH benchmark.

These numbers are important not only as performance claims, but as evidence for the paper’s core thesis: strong mathematical reasoning is possible in open language models when large-scale math pre-training is combined with effective reinforcement learning.

# Why Mathematical Reasoning Is a Useful Testbed for Studying General Reasoning

The paper uses mathematical reasoning as a window into broader reasoning ability. One reason is that math tasks are structured enough to reveal whether a model can handle multi-step inference, but varied enough to include many forms of reasoning. The evaluation suite spans arithmetic word problems, competition mathematics, multiple-choice STEM questions, formal theorem proving, and even general language understanding and coding.

This variety lets the paper ask whether improvements in math are isolated or whether they transfer more broadly. The answer, according to the paper, is that math training does more than improve math. DeepSeekMath-Base improves on MMLU and BBH relative to its predecessor, DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5, suggesting that math pre-training also benefits general reasoning and understanding. The paper also reports that code-related abilities are preserved when code tokens are included in continual training.

At the same time, the paper uses mathematical reasoning to study reinforcement learning itself. Its analysis of Pass@K and Maj@K suggests that reinforcement learning improves performance mainly by making the output distribution more robust, rather than by fundamentally changing the model’s capabilities. This makes math a useful experimental domain: because the tasks are sharply measurable, one can study whether a training method improves top-level correctness, output robustness, or both.

In that sense, DeepSeekMath is not only a mathematical model. It is also a case study in how to understand reasoning-focused training for language models. The paper’s combination of large-scale data curation, supervised fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning offers a concrete example of how open-source models can be pushed toward the performance frontier in a highly demanding domain.
