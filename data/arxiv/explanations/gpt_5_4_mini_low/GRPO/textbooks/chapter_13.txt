\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 13: 13. A Unified View of SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO

#
Motivation for a Unified Paradigm

DeepSeekMath does not treat supervised fine-tuning, rejection sampling, preference optimization, and reinforcement learning as isolated techniques. Instead, it argues that these methods can be understood through a shared lens. This is useful because the methods may look very different on the surface: some train on human-written solutions, some train on sampled outputs filtered for correctness, some optimize preference losses, and some use a reward model to guide policy updates. But beneath these differences, they all update a language model by changing the probabilities of generated tokens in response to some training signal.

The paper’s motivation is practical as well as conceptual. Practically, the authors want to compare methods that are often discussed separately in the literature, even though they are used for similar goals in alignment and reasoning improvement. Conceptually, they want to explain why certain design choices matter, such as whether the training data is sampled offline from a fixed model or online from the current policy, whether rewards come from a rule or a learned model, and whether the gradient signal is shaped token by token or only at the end of a response. This unified perspective is then used to analyze why their reinforcement learning method, GRPO, is effective for mathematical reasoning.

#
The Generic Gradient Form Across Post-Training Methods

The paper proposes a common gradient form for post-training methods. In this view, the update to model parameters $\theta$ can be written as an expectation over training examples $(q,o)$ drawn from a data source $\mathcal{D}$, where $q$ is a question and $o$ is an output. The gradient takes the form

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[(q,o)\sim \mathcal{D}] \left( \frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q,o,t,\pi_{rf}) \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t}) \right).
$$

This equation isolates the core structure shared by the methods in the paper. The model always learns by pushing up or down the log-probability of generated tokens. What changes from method to method is the gradient coefficient $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$, which determines how strongly each token is reinforced or penalized. In other words, the methods differ not in whether they do token-level learning, but in how they construct the learning signal that multiplies the token-level log-probability gradient.

This formulation is especially helpful because it separates the mechanics of learning from the source of supervision. Once the training data, reward signal, and algorithm are identified, the method can be understood as a particular way of assigning gradient coefficients. That makes it possible to compare SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO within one framework rather than as disconnected techniques.

#
Key Components: Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm

The paper states that the unified framework has three key components. The first is the data source $\mathcal{D}$, which determines what questions and outputs are used during training. The second is the reward function $\pi_{rf}$, which provides the signal used to judge outputs. The third is the algorithm $\mathcal{A}$, which transforms the data and reward into the gradient coefficient that drives learning.

This decomposition matters because many apparent differences between methods are really differences in one of these three components. For example, an offline method and an online method may use the same reward function, but they differ in where the sampled outputs come from. Likewise, two methods may both use a reward model, but one may use it to directly filter examples while another uses it to generate a continuous gradient signal. The algorithm then determines how these signals are translated into model updates.

The paper also emphasizes that the reward function can be of two broad kinds. It can be a rule-based reward, where correctness is judged directly, or a learned reward model, where another model scores the output. This distinction becomes important later when comparing methods like online rejection sampling and GRPO, because the way rewards are used changes the type of reinforcement the model receives.

#
Supervised Fine-Tuning as a Special Case

In the unified picture, supervised fine-tuning is the simplest case. The model is trained on human-selected SFT data, and the target is to imitate the provided outputs. There is no need for an external reward model in the same sense as in reinforcement learning, because the training signal is directly encoded in the examples themselves. The gradient coefficient is effectively determined by the supervised target: tokens that appear in the reference solution are reinforced through standard language modeling training.

From the perspective of the paper’s framework, SFT can be seen as the starting point for later alignment methods. DeepSeekMath first builds a mathematical instruction-tuned model using CoT, PoT, and tool-integrated reasoning data. This SFT model is then used as the base for RL. The paper’s later analysis suggests that SFT is not the final step, but a foundation on which methods like RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO build.

#
Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT)

RFT refines the SFT model by sampling outputs and keeping only those judged to be correct. In the paper’s framework, RFT is an offline method because the outputs are sampled from a fixed model, specifically the SFT model. The main idea is simple: generate candidate answers, filter them by correctness, and then train on the accepted outputs.

This makes RFT a kind of selective imitation. It does not require a learned reward model in the way PPO or GRPO do. Instead, correctness itself provides the filtering rule. Because the method only keeps good samples, the resulting training signal is positive reinforcement from selected outputs rather than a full feedback signal over the whole output distribution. In the paper’s unified view, this means RFT uses a simpler gradient coefficient than methods that continuously reward and penalize outputs with varying magnitudes.

The paper later compares RFT with online methods and finds that online sampling can outperform it, especially as training progresses. That result helps explain one limitation of RFT: because it draws from a static policy, it may not keep pace with the changing behavior of the model being trained.

#
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

DPO is also placed in the same framework, but it differs from RFT in how the training signal is expressed. Rather than simply filtering correct outputs, DPO fine-tunes the model on augmented outputs sampled from the SFT model using a pair-wise preference loss. In the paper’s summary, DPO is still an offline method, because the sampled outputs come from the initial SFT model rather than the current policy.

What matters for the unified perspective is that DPO, like RFT, transforms supervision into a gradient coefficient without running a full RL loop in the PPO sense. The paper groups DPO with other methods that can be viewed as direct or simplified RL techniques. This does not mean the methods are identical, but it does mean they share the same basic goal: change token probabilities based on feedback derived from output quality.

The paper does not focus on DPO’s internal mechanics in the way it does for PPO and GRPO. Instead, DPO serves as one of the representative methods that helps motivate the broader claim that many alignment techniques can be interpreted through the same gradient-based lens.

#
Online RFT

Online RFT is the online counterpart of RFT. The paper defines online sampling as using outputs sampled from the real-time policy model, rather than from the initial SFT model. In online RFT, the policy begins as the SFT model, but training examples are generated from the evolving policy itself.

This difference turns out to matter. The paper reports that online RFT significantly outperforms offline RFT on two benchmarks. Early in training, the gap is small because the current policy still resembles the SFT model. Later, however, the current policy diverges, and the outputs it produces reflect its updated capabilities and errors more accurately. At that stage, sampling from the real-time policy gives the method more relevant training data.

The paper interprets this as evidence that online data collection is better suited to later-stage training. The model is learning from its own current behavior, rather than from a snapshot of an earlier version of itself. That makes the learning signal more aligned with the policy the system is actually using.

#
PPO and GRPO Within the Same Framework

PPO and GRPO are the main reinforcement learning methods discussed in the paper, and they fit naturally into the unified paradigm. Both start from an SFT model and reinforce outputs sampled from the current policy. Both also use a reward signal, but they differ in how the training objective is constructed and how the baseline is handled.

PPO is an actor-critic method. It optimizes a clipped surrogate objective and uses a learned value function to estimate the advantage. Because this value function is another model of comparable size to the policy, PPO has a substantial memory and computational cost. The paper also notes that the reward model usually assigns reward only to the final token or final output, which can make token-level value estimation difficult.

GRPO keeps the general RL structure but removes the critic. Instead of learning a separate value function, it estimates the baseline from group scores. For each question, multiple outputs are sampled, scored, and compared within the group. The resulting relative rewards determine the advantage. This reduces memory usage and fits the comparative structure of reward models, which are often trained on output comparisons for the same question.

The paper’s key point is that GRPO is not just a lighter version of PPO in implementation. It also changes how the learning signal is formed, replacing the critic-based baseline with a group-relative one. That makes it both more efficient and, in the authors’ experiments, more effective for mathematical reasoning.

#
Offline Sampling vs Online Sampling

A central distinction in the paper’s framework is whether training data is offline or online. Offline sampling means outputs are generated from a fixed model, usually the SFT model. RFT and DPO are placed in this category. Online sampling means outputs are generated from the current policy during training. Online RFT, PPO, and GRPO belong here.

The paper finds that online training has a clear advantage. The intuition is that the current policy’s outputs become more informative as the model improves. If training continues to rely on the original SFT model, the sampled data may fail to reflect the model’s updated behavior. Online sampling avoids that mismatch by keeping the training distribution closer to the current policy.

The paper’s experiments support this view. Online RFT outperforms offline RFT, especially in later stages of training, which the authors interpret as evidence that real-time exploration becomes more valuable once the model has moved beyond its starting point. This is one of the key reasons the paper emphasizes online reinforcement learning as a strong direction for post-training.

#
Rule-Based Rewards vs Learned Reward Models

The paper also distinguishes between two reward sources. A rule-based reward judges outputs by correctness directly, such as whether an answer matches the expected result. A learned reward model instead predicts a score for each response. The learned reward model itself is trained using rule-based judgments.

This distinction matters because the reward source shapes the gradient signal. Rule-based rewards are straightforward when answers can be checked automatically, but they may not be enough for more complex or open-ended reasoning. Learned reward models can offer richer feedback, especially when they score outputs along a spectrum rather than only as right or wrong. In the paper, GRPO relies on reward models and uses their scores to produce differential reinforcement across outputs.

The authors also note that reward models are not perfect. In the discussion, they point out that even carefully annotated process reward datasets can contain incorrect labels. This motivates their interest in more robust learning algorithms and in future work that can better handle noisy reward signals.

#
Why Online Training Can Outperform Offline Methods

The paper’s explanation for the benefit of online training is grounded in how the sampled data changes over time. At the beginning of training, a policy model and the SFT model may generate very similar outputs, so offline samples are not too different from online ones. But later, after some learning has occurred, the current policy may produce different answers, and those answers reveal its current strengths and weaknesses more accurately.

Because of this, online training can adapt to the model’s evolving behavior. The training signal stays connected to what the model is actually doing now, rather than what it did before. The paper shows this effect in the comparison between online RFT and offline RFT, where the online version gains an advantage in the later stages.

This helps explain why GRPO, which is also an online method, can outperform methods based on static samples. Online sampling makes the feedback loop more responsive, which is especially useful when the goal is to improve reasoning rather than simply imitate a fixed dataset.

#
Why Graded Positive and Negative Reinforcement Matters

One of the paper’s main claims is that GRPO is better than methods that only keep correct responses because it uses graded reinforcement. In online RFT, correct responses are reinforced, but incorrect responses are not penalized in a differentiated way. By contrast, GRPO uses reward values to assign positive or negative gradient coefficients. Better responses are reinforced more strongly, and worse responses are penalized or reinforced less.

This difference is important because mathematical reasoning is not just about distinguishing right from wrong. There can be varying degrees of quality among outputs, even when some are correct and others are incorrect. GRPO captures this structure by letting the reward magnitude influence the update. The paper reports that this graded mechanism helps GRPO outperform online RFT.

The same idea also explains why the authors regard GRPO as a better fit for reward models. Since reward models naturally produce scores, those scores can be turned into relative advantages within a group. That makes the learning signal more informative than a simple accept-or-reject rule.

#
How Process Supervision Changes the Gradient Signal

The paper compares outcome supervision and process supervision inside GRPO. Under outcome supervision, the reward arrives only at the end of each output. The advantage for all tokens in that output is then the same normalized final reward. In effect, every token receives the same credit assignment signal, because the model only knows whether the complete answer was good or bad.

Process supervision gives a more detailed signal. Instead of scoring only the final answer, it scores each reasoning step. The advantages are then computed from the sum of the normalized rewards of the later steps. This means tokens earlier in the reasoning chain receive credit or blame according to how the subsequent steps turn out. The signal is therefore more fine-grained and more step-aware.

The paper finds that GRPO with process supervision performs better than GRPO with outcome supervision. This supports the idea that detailed feedback along the reasoning path is helpful in mathematical problems, where intermediate steps matter. In the paper’s framework, process supervision modifies the gradient coefficient by distributing reward information across the reasoning trajectory rather than collapsing it to a single terminal score.

Overall, the unified view shows that SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO are not separate worlds. They are different ways of building the same basic update: a token-level log-probability gradient weighted by a signal derived from data, rewards, and an algorithm for turning the two into learning. The main lesson of the paper is that the choice of sampling regime, reward form, and gradient construction can have a major impact on mathematical reasoning performance.
