\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: 2. Foundations: Pre-Training, Instruction Tuning, and Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

# Continual pre-training and domain adaptation in language models

DeepSeekMath is built on the idea that a language model does not have to be trained from scratch to become stronger at mathematics. Instead, a model can start from a strong existing checkpoint and then continue training on a carefully chosen domain-specific corpus. In this paper, that starting point is DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, and the continuation stage uses a large math-focused corpus drawn from Common Crawl together with some natural language and code data. This is an example of continual pre-training: the model keeps learning from more text after the initial pre-training stage, but the new data is selected to shift its abilities toward a target domain.

The intuition behind domain adaptation is that a general language model has already learned broad linguistic patterns, but it may not yet be specialized in the structures and styles that matter in a particular field. For mathematics, that means the model needs exposure not only to problem statements but also to the kinds of reasoning, notation, and explanatory patterns that appear in mathematical texts. The paper argues that this specialization can come from web data rather than only from curated textbooks or papers. Their DeepSeekMath Corpus is built by iteratively mining Common Crawl for math-related pages, showing that large-scale public web data can be turned into a useful pre-training resource when filtered carefully.

A key point in the paper is that continual pre-training is not simply about adding more tokens. The quality and composition of the data matter greatly. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is trained on a mixture in which the DeepSeekMath Corpus provides the majority of tokens, but code, arXiv, and natural language are also included. The resulting model improves not only on math benchmarks but also on broader reasoning and understanding benchmarks such as MMLU and BBH. This suggests that domain adaptation for math can reshape general reasoning behavior, not just narrow arithmetic skill.

# Instruction tuning and alignment

After pre-training, DeepSeekMath is further improved through supervised fine-tuning, or instruction tuning. This stage trains the model on examples where a question is paired with a target solution, so the model learns how to respond in the style expected by users or evaluators. In this paper, the instruction-tuning data includes English and Chinese mathematical problems, and the solutions are written in chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, or tool-integrated reasoning formats. The model trained with this data is called DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B.

Instruction tuning serves an alignment function: it teaches the model not just what information to know, but how to present it in a useful response format. In the mathematical setting studied here, that means learning to produce self-contained solutions that directly answer the problem. The paper emphasizes that instruction tuning materially improves performance on both benchmarks with and without tool use. DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B becomes competitive with much larger open-source instruction-tuned models, showing that carefully curated instruction data can substantially shape behavior.

The role of alignment is especially important because mathematical problem solving is not only about producing a correct final answer. It is also about following the conventions of a solution process. The training data in this paper reflects that by exposing the model to different reasoning styles. In practice, this means the model can learn to produce a natural language derivation, a Python program, or a combination of both. The instruction-tuned model becomes the foundation for later reinforcement learning, where the goal is to refine the policy further using reward signals.

# Chain-of-thought reasoning

Chain-of-thought, or CoT, refers to solving a problem by writing out intermediate reasoning steps before reaching the final answer. In DeepSeekMath, CoT is used both during supervised fine-tuning and during evaluation. This is important because many mathematical tasks are difficult not simply because they require knowledge, but because they require multi-step inference. The paper shows that a model trained to generate step-by-step reasoning can perform strongly on benchmarks such as GSM8K, MATH, and CMATH.

The intuition of CoT is that a language model is not restricted to jumping directly from question to answer. Instead, it can externalize intermediate computations, deductions, and subgoals in text. This makes the reasoning process more transparent and can improve correctness, especially when the task has several intermediate steps. In the paper, CoT is one of the solution formats used to build the instruction-tuning dataset, and it is also the evaluation setting for many benchmark results.

The paper uses CoT in a very practical way. For the base model, few-shot CoT prompting is used to test whether the model can solve problems through explicit reasoning. For the instruction-tuned and RL-tuned models, CoT becomes a standard way to measure in-domain mathematical performance, especially on GSM8K and MATH. This shows that CoT is both a training format and an evaluation protocol in the paper’s framework.

# Program-of-thought and tool-integrated reasoning

Not all mathematical problems are best solved by verbal reasoning alone. Some require arithmetic, symbolic manipulation, or other exact computations. To handle this, the paper uses program-of-thought, or PoT, and tool-integrated reasoning. In PoT, the model writes a Python program instead of only natural language steps, and the program execution result is treated as the answer. Tool-integrated reasoning extends this idea by allowing the model to combine natural language reasoning with explicit tool use.

This approach is valuable because it shifts some of the burden of exact calculation away from the model’s internal text generation and onto external computation. In the paper, the model is evaluated on GSM8K and MATH with libraries such as $math$ and $sympy$ available for intricate computation. The output of the program is then checked as the solution. This setup reflects a practical view of mathematical reasoning: a strong system need not perform every arithmetic step in its own hidden state if it can reliably write correct code to execute.

The paper’s instruction-tuning dataset includes examples in PoT and tool-integrated formats, so the model learns not only to explain but also to compute. This matters because the paper reports that tool use significantly boosts performance. DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B approaches about $60\%$ accuracy on MATH when tool use is allowed, surpassing all existing open-source models in that setting. The paper therefore treats reasoning with tools as a central capability rather than an optional add-on.

# Few-shot vs zero-shot evaluation

Many of the paper’s evaluations are few-shot, meaning the model is shown a small number of example question-answer pairs before it is asked to solve a new problem. Few-shot evaluation tests whether the model can adapt to the task format from context alone. The paper also discusses zero-shot behavior in its conclusions, noting that DeepSeekMath shows similar performance in zero-shot and few-shot evaluation on some settings, unlike GPT-4, which can improve further with few-shot inputs.

The distinction matters because it reveals different kinds of competence. A model that performs well only in few-shot settings may depend heavily on in-context demonstrations, while a model that performs well in zero-shot settings may have more robust internalized knowledge or reasoning ability. In the paper, few-shot CoT prompting is the standard evaluation method for many benchmarks, especially for base models. This lets the authors compare training corpora and training methods under a controlled prompting setup.

Few-shot evaluation is also used in formal mathematics and program-aided reasoning. For instance, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is evaluated on miniF2F using few-shot prompting and Isabelle. Across these settings, the paper uses few-shot evaluation to probe whether the model can generalize from a small amount of context. The authors later note that their model is still weaker than GPT-4 on few-shot capability, which helps define the limits of their approach.

# Reward models and preference-based learning

Reinforcement learning in this paper depends on reward models, which score model outputs and provide training signals for policy improvement. The reward model is central because it tells the policy which answers or reasoning traces are better. The paper builds reward models using comparisons derived from correctness judgments, and then uses those reward models during GRPO training. This makes the reward model a learned proxy for task quality.

The intuition is that in complex tasks like math, it may be difficult to specify a hand-written loss that directly captures good reasoning. Instead, one can train a reward model on examples that reflect relative quality, and then use that reward to guide the policy. The paper notes that reward models are often trained on comparisons between outputs for the same question, which matches the comparative structure of many preference-learning methods. This is one reason the paper’s GRPO method is designed around group-relative rewards.

Preference-based learning appears in the paper not as a separate side topic but as part of a broader taxonomy that includes RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO. The authors present these methods as different ways of converting data and reward signals into training updates. In this view, reward models are one of the key ingredients that determine how the policy is reinforced or penalized. The paper also emphasizes that reward models are not perfect, which motivates future work on robustness to noisy reward signals.

# Policy optimization basics: PPO and actor-critic methods

Proximal Policy Optimization, or PPO, is the main reinforcement learning baseline discussed in the paper. PPO is an actor-critic method, meaning it trains both a policy, which chooses actions, and a value function, which estimates expected future reward and acts as a baseline for advantage estimation. In the LLM setting, the “actions” are the output tokens generated by the model. The paper gives the PPO objective as a clipped surrogate loss and explains that the advantage $A_t$ is computed with generalized advantage estimation from rewards and a learned value function.

The core idea of PPO is to improve the policy without changing it too aggressively. The clipping term limits how much the new policy can deviate from the old policy on any token, which stabilizes training. PPO also typically includes a KL penalty that keeps the policy close to a reference model, often the supervised fine-tuned model. This helps prevent the policy from drifting too far toward reward-model exploitation. In the paper, the reward at each token includes this penalty term.

GRPO is introduced as a variant of PPO that avoids training a separate critic model. This is important because the value function in PPO can be expensive in memory and computation, especially when it must be as large as the policy. Instead of estimating a value function token by token, GRPO uses the average reward from a group of sampled outputs as the baseline. This makes the method more efficient while still preserving the basic policy-optimization structure.

# Why math is a special domain for training and evaluation

The paper treats mathematics as a special domain because it combines structure, exactness, and multi-step reasoning in a way that is unusually demanding for language models. Unlike many natural language tasks, math problems often have a uniquely correct answer and require intermediate reasoning steps that are easy to get subtly wrong. This makes math both a difficult benchmark and a useful testbed for reasoning methods.

Mathematics is also special because it can be evaluated in multiple ways. In this paper, the authors test self-contained text solutions without tools, program-aided solutions with Python, and formal theorem proving with Isabelle on miniF2F. They also evaluate English and Chinese benchmarks, from grade-school arithmetic to college-level problems. This broad evaluation suite shows that mathematical reasoning is not one skill but a family of related capabilities.

Another reason math is special is that improvements in math can reflect more general reasoning gains. The paper finds that math training improves performance on MMLU and BBH, and that code training before math training helps both tool-based and non-tool-based reasoning. At the same time, some data sources that seem mathematically relevant, such as arXiv papers, do not help as much as expected in their experiments. This makes the paper especially useful as a foundation: it shows that mathematical reasoning in language models depends not only on model size, but on the interplay between pre-training data, instruction tuning, reasoning format, and reinforcement learning.
