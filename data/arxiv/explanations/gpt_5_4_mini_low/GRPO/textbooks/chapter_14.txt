\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 14: 14. Why Reinforcement Learning Works Here—and What It Does Not Yet Solve

# Pass@K vs Maj@K as diagnostic tools

A central question in this paper is not just whether reinforcement learning (RL) improves the model, but what kind of improvement it produces. To examine this, the authors compare two diagnostic measures: Pass@K and Maj@K. These are used to study the behavior of the instruction-tuned model versus the RL-trained model on mathematical benchmarks.

The intuition behind these two measures is different. Pass@K asks whether the correct answer appears at least once among $K$ sampled outputs. Maj@K asks whether the answer chosen by majority vote among $K$ samples is correct. In other words, Pass@K measures whether the model can produce the right solution somewhere in its output distribution, while Maj@K measures whether the correct solution becomes the dominant choice when multiple samples are considered.

This distinction matters because it lets us separate two possible effects of RL. RL might either enlarge the set of problems for which the correct answer is reachable at all, or it might simply reorganize the model’s output probabilities so that a correct answer that was already present becomes more likely to be selected. The paper uses Pass@K and Maj@K precisely to test which of these effects is happening.

# Evidence that RL improves Maj@K more than Pass@K

The paper’s key empirical observation is that RL improves Maj@K more clearly than Pass@K. This is shown by evaluating the instruction-tuned and RL models on two benchmarks and comparing how their accuracy changes under these two metrics. The result is that RL boosts Maj@K performance, but not Pass@K.

This pattern is important because it suggests that the RL stage is not mainly discovering entirely new solution abilities. If Pass@K does not increase much, then the model is not necessarily generating a greater variety of correct answers. Instead, the correct answer may already exist in the model’s sampled outputs, and RL is making that answer more likely to be the one that dominates among several candidates.

The authors interpret this as evidence that RL helps make the output distribution more robust. The model becomes better at placing correct responses near the top of its ranked or sampled outputs, which makes majority voting more effective. In that sense, RL can yield substantial benchmark gains even without fundamentally expanding the model’s underlying reasoning competence.

# Distribution shaping vs true capability expansion

The paper argues that the main effect of RL, at least in their setting, is distribution shaping rather than true capability expansion. This is a subtle but crucial distinction. Capability expansion would mean that the model learns new reasoning skills or becomes broadly more powerful at solving problems. Distribution shaping means that the model’s probability mass is redistributed so that answers it already had some access to become more prominent and more reliably selected.

This interpretation fits the observed Maj@K result. If the model already contains some correct responses in its output space, RL can increase the likelihood of those responses while suppressing less useful ones. The model then appears much stronger on evaluation, especially when the benchmark uses sampling and selection across multiple outputs. But the improvement comes from making the output distribution more favorable, not necessarily from changing the model’s fundamental reasoning machinery in a dramatic way.

The paper’s discussion is careful about this point. It does not claim that RL is useless or superficial. On the contrary, the gains are large and practically meaningful. But the authors suggest that these gains should be understood as a refinement of how the model expresses its existing knowledge, rather than as proof that RL has solved deeper general reasoning.

# The notion of misalignment in reasoning models after SFT

To explain why RL still helps after supervised fine-tuning, the paper connects its findings to the idea of misalignment in reasoning models. The basic concern is that an SFT model may not be well aligned with the evaluation objective, even if it already has some latent ability to produce correct reasoning. In such a case, the model’s outputs are not distributed in the most useful way for selecting correct answers.

The paper points to prior work showing that reasoning performance in SFT models can be improved through preference-alignment strategies. This supports the idea that a model can be “misaligned” with respect to reasoning tasks even if it has been trained on relevant examples. The problem is not just whether the model has seen the right kind of data, but whether its output distribution is shaped so that correct reasoning is consistently favored.

In that sense, RL serves as a corrective mechanism. It uses reward signals to push the model toward output patterns that are more likely to produce correct answers under the evaluation setting. The authors frame this as one reason RL is effective in their mathematical reasoning pipeline.

# How RL may elevate correct outputs already present in the model

One of the paper’s clearest conclusions is that RL may be elevating correct outputs that already exist in the model’s distribution. This idea follows directly from the Pass@K versus Maj@K analysis. If a model can already occasionally produce correct answers, then RL does not need to invent those answers from scratch. Instead, it can increase their likelihood relative to weaker responses.

This is especially plausible in a setting where the model is trained with sampled outputs and reward signals. The model explores different candidate responses, receives feedback, and then updates its policy so that better responses receive more probability mass. The result is not necessarily a brand-new reasoning algorithm, but a more favorable allocation of probability across responses the model is already capable of generating.

The paper’s discussion of GRPO reinforces this view. GRPO is designed to work with sampled groups of outputs and compare them relative to one another. That setup naturally encourages the model to distinguish better responses from worse ones within the same question context. So when RL improves performance, it may be because the model learns to consistently place the correct answer above the alternatives, rather than because it has learned a completely new way to solve the problem.

# Implications for interpreting benchmark gains

This interpretation has important consequences for how benchmark improvements should be read. A higher score after RL is certainly meaningful, but it should not automatically be taken as evidence that the model has become fundamentally smarter in every sense. In the paper’s analysis, the gain may reflect improved robustness of the output distribution and better ranking of already available correct responses.

That means benchmark scores can sometimes be inflated by changes in sampling behavior and response selection, even when underlying capability changes are more modest. The model may appear much better because it is more likely to surface the right answer at test time. For practical evaluation, this is still valuable. But for scientific interpretation, it is important to ask whether the model has gained new competence or whether it has become better at expressing existing competence.

The paper also ties this issue to the broader idea that RL does not necessarily solve all weaknesses in the instruction-tuned model. In their experiments, RL gives strong gains on quantitative reasoning benchmarks, but the authors explicitly note that some areas, such as geometry and theorem-proof tasks, remain relatively weaker. This further supports the view that RL is powerful, but targeted: it can significantly improve performance on the benchmarks it is trained around, without universally fixing every reasoning limitation.

# What this means for the design of future RL pipelines

The paper concludes with several directions for making RL more effective, and these directions follow naturally from the interpretation above. If RL mainly reshapes distributions, then future work should focus on improving the quality of the data source, the algorithm, and the reward function so that the reshaping is more reliable and more capable of producing genuine gains.

For the data source, the authors suggest exploring out-of-distribution prompts and more advanced sampling strategies, including tree-search-based decoding. Their current pipeline uses questions from instruction tuning and naive nucleus sampling, which may partly explain why the gains are strongest in Maj@K rather than Pass@K. Better exploration could expose the model to a broader and more informative set of candidate solutions.

For the algorithm, the paper emphasizes the need for methods that are robust to noisy reward signals. Since RL methods currently trust the reward function heavily, any errors in the reward model can mislead training. This motivates the authors’ discussion of weak-to-strong alignment ideas, where the learning algorithm itself must be more resilient.

For the reward function, the paper highlights three open directions: improving generalization, reflecting uncertainty, and building stronger process reward models. These concerns are directly tied to the interpretation of RL as distribution shaping. If the reward model is weak or miscalibrated, the distribution may be shaped in unhelpful ways. If the reward signal is finer-grained and more trustworthy, the model may be pushed toward genuinely better reasoning behavior rather than merely better majority-vote outcomes.

Overall, the paper’s message is nuanced. RL works very well in this setting, but its success appears to come largely from making correct outputs more dominant in the model’s distribution. That is a powerful effect, and it explains the large benchmark gains observed here. At the same time, the results also show that RL has not yet fully solved the deeper problem of expanding fundamental reasoning capability across all mathematical domains.
