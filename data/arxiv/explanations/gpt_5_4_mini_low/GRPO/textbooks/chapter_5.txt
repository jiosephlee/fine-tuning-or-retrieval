\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 5: Training DeepSeekMath-Base 7B

# Why initialize from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is not trained from scratch. Instead, the paper initializes it from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, and this choice is presented as an important design decision rather than a neutral implementation detail. The authors explicitly state that, in their experience, starting from a code training model is a better choice than starting from a general language model. This is tied to the broader goal of building a model that can reason through mathematical problems more effectively, not only by memorizing math facts but by inheriting computational and structured reasoning tendencies that already exist in a code-oriented model.

The paper does not frame this initialization as a claim that code models are inherently mathematical, but rather as an empirical starting point that better supports subsequent continual training on mathematical data. In other words, the code model serves as a stronger foundation for math-oriented adaptation than a general LLM in their setting. This matters because the final model is intended to handle not only natural language mathematical reasoning, but also program-based reasoning and other structured forms of solution generation.

# The hypothesis that code pre-training helps math reasoning

A central theme in the paper is the hypothesis that code pre-training improves reasoning. The authors describe this as a popular but previously unverified idea, and they use their experiments to give a partial answer, at least in the mathematical domain. Their conclusion is that code training does help mathematical reasoning, both with and without tool use.

The intuition behind this result is supported by the training experiments discussed later in the paper. When a model is first trained on code tokens and then trained on math tokens, its ability to solve mathematical problems improves more than if it had instead been trained on general tokens before math. The paper further observes that code training enhances program-aided mathematical reasoning and also improves step-by-step reasoning without tools. This suggests that the benefit of code pre-training is not limited to tasks where the answer can be computed by executing a program; it also seems to shape the model’s general ability to follow structured reasoning chains.

At the same time, the paper is careful not to overstate the claim. The improvement is presented as an empirical result in the mathematical domain, not as a universal law about all reasoning tasks. Still, within the scope of DeepSeekMath, code pre-training is shown to be a valuable component of the model’s development.

# The 500B-token continual pre-training setup

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is trained through continual pre-training for 500B tokens. This means the model begins with the DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B checkpoint and is then exposed to a large, mixed training corpus that is designed to extend its abilities rather than replace them entirely. The scale of this training is a major point in the paper: the model is not merely lightly adapted for math, but trained on a very large amount of data intended to produce a substantial shift in capability.

The paper gives a specific data distribution for this 500B-token continuation. The largest portion, 56%, comes from the DeepSeekMath Corpus. Another 4% comes from AlgebraicStack, 10% from arXiv, 20% from GitHub code, and the remaining 10% from natural language data collected from Common Crawl in both English and Chinese. This mixture reflects the model’s intended role as a mathematical reasoning system that still retains code and language competence. The authors are not training a narrow math solver; they are building a general-purpose model with a strong mathematical specialization.

The continual pre-training stage is also discussed in relation to later evaluations. The authors report that this training regime yields a base model with strong reasoning capabilities, especially in mathematics, while also preserving useful general abilities. That combination is one of the paper’s main design goals.

# Training data mixture: math, AlgebraicStack, arXiv, GitHub code, and natural language

The composition of the training mixture is central to understanding what DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is meant to learn. The largest component is the DeepSeekMath Corpus, which supplies the main mathematical signal. This corpus is extracted from Common Crawl and was constructed to include high-quality math-related web pages. The model also sees AlgebraicStack, which is a source of mathematical code, and GitHub code, which supports the earlier idea that code can improve mathematical reasoning. ArXiv is included as a source of scientific and mathematical text, and Common Crawl natural language data is included in both English and Chinese to maintain linguistic breadth.

This mixture indicates that the paper treats mathematical reasoning as something supported by multiple kinds of evidence. Mathematical web pages provide direct mathematical exposition, code provides procedural and symbolic structure, arXiv provides technical prose, and natural language data helps keep the model grounded in general language understanding. The authors are therefore not isolating mathematics from the rest of the model’s knowledge base. Instead, they are combining domain-specific and general-purpose data in a way that should let the model specialize without becoming brittle.

The paper’s later discussion also clarifies why this mixture matters. For example, they observe that math training improves not only math benchmarks but also MMLU and BBH, suggesting that the training mixture helps general reasoning as well. They also note that adding code tokens helps preserve coding ability during continual training. So the mixture is not only about performance on one benchmark family; it is also a strategy for maintaining multiple competencies at once.

# Optimization settings and large-scale training choices

The paper uses a consistent training setup for the base-model experiments, with a few carefully chosen adjustments for DeepSeekMath-Base 7B. Most of the optimization settings follow the practice used in DeepSeek LLMs. The optimizer is AdamW with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, and $\mathrm{weight\_decay}=0.1$. The learning rate schedule is multi-step: it reaches its peak after 2,000 warmup steps, falls to $31.6\%$ of the peak after 80% of training, and decreases further to $10.0\%$ of the peak after 90% of training. For the corpus-comparison experiments, the paper uses a maximum learning rate of $5.3 \times 10^{-4}$, while for DeepSeekMath-Base 7B itself the maximum learning rate is set to $4.2 \times 10^{-4}$.

The training is also characterized by very large batch sizes and long context lengths. For the corpus-quality experiments, the batch size is 4M tokens with a 4K context length. For DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, the batch size is increased to 10M tokens. These choices reflect the scale of the continual pre-training regime and the paper’s focus on stable large-scale optimization rather than small experimental runs. The experiments are conducted using the HAI-LLM training framework, which the authors describe as efficient and lightweight.

Another important choice is that the model is trained for 500B tokens, which is much larger than the 150B-token training runs used when comparing corpora. This larger training budget is part of what allows the authors to build a base model that exhibits strong mathematical reasoning while still maintaining broader model behavior.

# Balancing specialization with retention of general capabilities

One of the main design problems in training DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is balancing specialization in mathematics with retention of general abilities. The paper makes clear that the model is meant to become much better at math, but not at the cost of losing too much competence in language understanding, reasoning, or coding. This is why the training data includes not only math, but also code and natural language, and why the initialization comes from a code model rather than from a generic LLM.

The evaluation results support this balancing strategy. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B improves on mathematical benchmarks, but it also shows gains in MMLU and BBH relative to its precursor, DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5. At the same time, because the training continues to include code tokens, the model largely maintains the coding performance of the original code model on HumanEval and MBPP. The authors interpret this as evidence that math training does not only sharpen mathematical behavior; it can also strengthen general reasoning while preserving other useful skills.

This balance is important because the model is intended as a base model, not a narrowly tuned solver. A base model must remain flexible enough to support later instruction tuning and reinforcement learning. The paper’s training choices are therefore motivated by downstream adaptability as well as immediate benchmark performance.

# Expected tradeoffs in domain adaptation

The paper presents DeepSeekMath-Base 7B as a case study in the tradeoffs involved in domain adaptation. On one hand, continual pre-training on a large math-heavy corpus can produce strong mathematical reasoning gains, even in a relatively small 7B-parameter model. On the other hand, such adaptation can risk narrowing the model or disrupting capabilities that are not directly targeted. The authors’ training design tries to mitigate that risk by mixing mathematics with code, arXiv, and natural language.

The paper also shows that not every seemingly relevant source helps equally. In the broader discussion, the authors report that arXiv papers did not bring notable improvements on the mathematical benchmarks they tested, which is a useful reminder that domain-adaptation data should be judged by actual downstream effects rather than by intuition alone. This finding helps explain why the training mixture was designed to include multiple sources, but with math web data as the dominant component.

The expected tradeoff, then, is not simply “more domain data equals better performance.” Instead, the paper suggests that the quality, diversity, and role of each data source matter. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is built by exploiting a large and carefully selected math corpus while still including enough code and natural language to preserve broader reasoning and programming abilities. In that sense, the model is an example of how specialization can be achieved without fully sacrificing generality, provided the training mixture is chosen with care.
