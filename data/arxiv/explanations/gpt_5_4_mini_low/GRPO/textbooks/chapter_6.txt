\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 6: 6. Evaluating the Base Model: Math, Tools, Formal Proofs, and General Reasoning

# Evaluation Philosophy for a Mathematical Base Model

The goal of evaluating DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is not simply to report a single benchmark score, but to understand what kinds of reasoning the model has learned during math-focused continual pre-training. The paper evaluates the model in several complementary settings because mathematical competence is multi-faceted. A useful model should not only solve standard word problems directly, but also benefit from tool use, support formal proof tasks, and retain broader language, reasoning, and coding abilities.

This evaluation philosophy reflects an important point in the paper: improving mathematical reasoning should not be treated as an isolated skill. The authors therefore assess the base model on direct mathematical reasoning, program-aided solving, formal theorem proving, and general-purpose benchmarks. In this way, the evaluation is designed to answer a deeper question: does math pre-training produce a model that is broadly more capable, or does it merely overfit to one narrow style of problem solving?

The results in the paper show that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is strong not only on mathematical benchmarks but also on broader reasoning tasks. This makes the evaluation especially informative, because it helps distinguish between a model that memorizes math-like patterns and a model whose internal representations support more general forms of reasoning.

# English and Chinese Mathematical Reasoning Benchmarks

The paper evaluates DeepSeekMath-Base 7B on a wide range of English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks, spanning difficulty from elementary school to college level. The English benchmarks include GSM8K, MATH, SAT, OCW Courses, and MMLU-STEM. The Chinese benchmarks include MGSM-zh, CMATH, Gaokao-MathCloze, and Gaokao-MathQA. Together, these benchmarks test both quantitative reasoning and multiple-choice mathematical reasoning across different languages and educational contexts.

This multilingual setup is important because the training corpus itself is multilingual. The paper emphasizes that previous math corpora have often been English-centric, whereas DeepSeekMath incorporates high-quality non-English data as well. The evaluation therefore checks whether multilingual pre-training translates into improved performance on Chinese mathematical tasks, not just English ones.

The benchmarks also differ in format. Some require free-form numerical answers, others require structured explanations, and others use multiple-choice answers. By testing across this variety, the paper obtains a more complete picture of the model’s capabilities. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B leads among open-source base models on all eight of the benchmarks discussed in this section, including models that are much larger or that were previously adapted for math.

# Few-Shot Chain-of-Thought Evaluation Methodology

For direct mathematical reasoning, the paper evaluates the model using few-shot chain-of-thought prompting. In this setup, the model is given a small number of solved examples before being asked to solve a new problem. The intention is to encourage step-by-step reasoning rather than one-shot guessing. The model’s output is then judged based on whether the final answer is correct.

This methodology matters because mathematical reasoning is often not just about arriving at the answer, but about organizing intermediate steps correctly. Few-shot chain-of-thought prompting allows the model to demonstrate whether it can produce coherent solutions when given a small amount of task structure. It is also a standard way to measure how well a model can generalize from examples without requiring tool use.

In the paper, this evaluation is used across multiple benchmarks in both English and Chinese. The authors report that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B performs very strongly in this setting, outperforming open-source baselines and even exceeding some much larger closed models in specific cases. The methodology therefore helps show that the model has learned more than superficial pattern matching: it can often generate organized reasoning in the same style expected by these benchmarks.

# Performance on GSM8K, MATH, SAT, OCW, MMLU-STEM, MGSM-zh, CMATH, and Gaokao Benchmarks

The paper’s direct reasoning evaluation covers a broad benchmark suite. GSM8K tests grade-school arithmetic reasoning. MATH is a competition-level benchmark with more difficult problems. SAT-style questions and OCW Courses introduce more academic and educational mathematics. MMLU-STEM includes multiple-choice science and math reasoning tasks. On the Chinese side, MGSM-zh, CMATH, Gaokao-MathCloze, and Gaokao-MathQA extend the same idea to Chinese-language mathematical reasoning.

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B performs especially well on these benchmarks. The paper states that, on the competition-level MATH benchmark, it surpasses existing open-source base models by more than $10\%$ absolute and even outperforms Minerva 540B, a much larger closed-source model. This is one of the most striking results in the paper, because it shows that parameter count alone is not the decisive factor in mathematical reasoning. A smaller model trained on high-quality mathematical data can rival or exceed a vastly larger system.

The Chinese benchmarks are also notable. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is reported to be superior on Chinese mathematical tasks, which the authors attribute to the multilingual nature of the DeepSeekMath Corpus. Since the corpus did not restrict itself to English-only mathematical webpages, the model benefits from training on high-quality non-English math content as well. This result supports the paper’s broader claim that thoughtful data selection can matter more than simply scaling model size.

# Why Beating Larger Models Like Minerva 540B Is Significant

One of the most important claims in the paper is that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, a much smaller model, can match or outperform Minerva 540B on several mathematical benchmarks. This is significant because it challenges a common assumption in large language model research: that higher parameter count automatically produces much better reasoning ability.

The result suggests that the quality and composition of pre-training data are crucial. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B starts from a code-oriented model and is then continually trained on a large, carefully mined mathematical corpus from Common Crawl, together with code and natural language data. The comparison with Minerva 540B shows that a smaller model can be highly competitive when the training data is more targeted and better curated.

This is not just a leaderboard result. It is evidence for the paper’s central thesis that publicly available web data, if selected carefully, can be turned into a large and effective mathematical pre-training corpus. Beating a much larger model therefore serves as a validation of the paper’s data-centric approach, not merely a numerical achievement.

# Program-Aided Math Evaluation with Python and Symbolic Libraries

The paper also evaluates whether DeepSeekMath-Base 7B can solve math problems with tool use. This is done through program-aided mathematical reasoning, where the model writes a Python program to compute the answer. Libraries such as `math` and `sympy` may be used for more intricate calculations. Instead of directly emitting the final answer from natural language reasoning alone, the model decomposes the problem into executable code.

This evaluation setting matters because many mathematical problems are easier to solve reliably with a program than by manual symbolic manipulation. Tool use can reduce errors in arithmetic or algebraic computation, and it can reveal whether the model knows how to translate a word problem into a computational procedure. In the paper, this is treated as an important complementary capability rather than a replacement for reasoning.

The authors evaluate program-aided reasoning on GSM8K and MATH. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B outperforms the prior state of the art, Llemma 34B, in this tool-use setting. That result is important because it shows the model is not only strong at direct reasoning, but also good at deciding how to structure a problem as code. In effect, the model has learned to combine natural language understanding, mathematical reasoning, and executable computation.

# Few-Shot Program-of-Thought Prompting

The tool-use evaluation is carried out with few-shot program-of-thought prompting. In this setup, the model is given examples where a solution is expressed as a Python program. The model then produces code for a new problem, and the execution result becomes the answer. This is different from ordinary chain-of-thought prompting because the reasoning is externalized into a program rather than a text explanation.

The intuition behind this approach is that some mathematical tasks are better represented as algorithms than as prose. If the model can write correct code, it can offload exact calculation to the interpreter while still using language understanding to select the right operations. This is especially useful for multi-step problems where intermediate computations are cumbersome or error-prone in natural language.

In the paper, this evaluation is important because it tests whether the gains from math pre-training transfer to computational reasoning. The result that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B surpasses Llemma 34B indicates that the model learned more than direct answer patterns. It learned a kind of structured problem-solving behavior that can be expressed in executable form.

# Results on GSM8K and MATH with Tool Use

When tool use is allowed, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B performs strongly on both GSM8K and MATH. The paper reports that it exceeds the prior state of the art, Llemma 34B, in this setting. This is a notable result because tool use can be a very demanding test of model competence: the model must not only understand the problem, but also generate valid code that actually computes the answer.

The improvement is especially meaningful on MATH, since MATH contains more difficult and competition-level problems than GSM8K. If a model can perform well there with program-of-thought prompting, it suggests the model has robust internal representations for translating mathematical descriptions into symbolic procedures. That ability is a key bridge between language modeling and formal problem solving.

The paper treats these results as evidence that math pre-training helps not only with pure text-based reasoning, but also with program-aided solving. This supports the broader claim that the model has gained flexible mathematical competence rather than one narrow form of benchmark-specific behavior.

# Formal Mathematics and Informal-to-Formal Proving on miniF2F

The paper also evaluates DeepSeekMath-Base 7B on formal mathematics through an informal-to-formal theorem proving task. In this task, the model receives an informal mathematical statement, a formal counterpart of the statement, and an informal proof, and must generate a formal proof. The benchmark used is miniF2F, which contains formal Olympiad-level mathematics problems.

This evaluation is important because formal proof is a much stricter setting than informal benchmark solving. Unlike natural language answers, formal proofs must satisfy the syntax and logic of a proof assistant. A correct answer cannot simply be “close”; it must be formally valid. This makes the task a strong test of exact reasoning.

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B shows strong few-shot autoformalization performance on this benchmark. That means the model can map informal mathematical reasoning into formal proof structure with relatively little supervision. The paper presents this as evidence that math pre-training supports not only computational reasoning, but also the more disciplined symbolic structure required by theorem proving systems.

# Using Isabelle and Sledgehammer in Proof Autoformalization

For the formal mathematics evaluation, the paper uses Isabelle as the proof assistant. The model generates proof sketches, and then the automated prover Sledgehammer is used to fill in missing details. This combination reflects a practical workflow in formal mathematics: the model handles high-level structure, while the prover handles lower-level proof search and filling in routine steps.

This setup is useful because it makes the evaluation more realistic and more forgiving than requiring the model to produce a complete proof from scratch without assistance. At the same time, it is still demanding: the model must generate a proof sketch that is coherent enough for Sledgehammer to complete successfully. If the sketch is badly structured, the prover cannot rescue it.

The paper reports that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B demonstrates strong performance in this autoformalization setting. The result suggests that the model has learned a meaningful correspondence between informal reasoning and formal proof structure. Even though theorem proving is weaker overall than the model’s performance on quantitative reasoning benchmarks, the formal mathematics results still show substantial capability.

# General Capability Evaluation on MMLU, BBH, HumanEval, and MBPP

Beyond mathematics, the paper evaluates DeepSeekMath-Base 7B on general understanding, reasoning, and code benchmarks. These include MMLU, which covers 57 multiple-choice tasks across diverse subjects; BBH, which includes 23 challenging tasks that typically require multi-step reasoning; HumanEval; and MBPP, both of which are standard code generation benchmarks.

These evaluations are important because the model was trained heavily on mathematical data, and one concern would be that such training could narrow the model’s abilities. The paper therefore checks whether math training hurts general-purpose performance or whether it improves broader competence as well.

The results show that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B improves on MMLU and BBH relative to its precursor, DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5. This is evidence that math training enhances general reasoning ability, not just performance on math-specific benchmarks. At the same time, because code tokens were included during continual training, the model preserves the coding performance of the precursor on HumanEval and MBPP. In the paper’s framing, this means the model gains reasoning strength without sacrificing its coding ability.

# Evidence That Math Training Improves General Reasoning While Preserving Coding Ability

One of the most interesting conclusions in the paper is that math training appears to have benefits beyond mathematics itself. The improvements on MMLU and BBH suggest that the model’s reasoning ability becomes stronger in a more general sense. In other words, mathematical pre-training does not merely teach the model to answer math questions; it seems to reinforce broader skills such as structured inference and multi-step problem solving.

At the same time, the paper is careful to note that coding performance is maintained rather than lost. This is significant because DeepSeekMath-Base 7B begins from a code model, and the continual training mixture includes code tokens along with math and natural language data. The benchmark results on HumanEval and MBPP show that the model remains competitive on coding tasks. So the training regime appears to successfully combine mathematical improvement with preservation of programming competence.

Taken together, these findings support the paper’s broader view of evaluation: a strong mathematical model should be judged not only by how well it solves math problems, but also by how its training affects related capabilities. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is presented as a model that becomes stronger at reasoning while staying useful as a general code-capable language model.
