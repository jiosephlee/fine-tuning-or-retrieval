\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 16: 16. Limitations, Broader Lessons, and the Open Math LLM Roadmap

# Summary of DeepSeekMath’s main achievements

DeepSeekMath’s central achievement is that it pushes an open $7$B language model to a level that, on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, comes surprisingly close to the strongest closed systems discussed in the paper. The paper reports that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, after continual training on a large math-focused corpus, achieves strong results on both English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks, while DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B and DeepSeekMath-RL 7B further improve performance through supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning. Most notably, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reaches $51.7\%$ on the competition-level MATH benchmark without external tools or voting, and self-consistency with 64 samples raises performance to $60.9\%$.

A key part of the achievement is not only benchmark accuracy, but the construction of the data and training pipeline that makes these results possible. The authors build a $120$B-token DeepSeekMath Corpus from Common Crawl, filtered through an iterative fastText-based selection pipeline and refined by human annotation. This corpus is large enough to support meaningful continual pre-training, and the paper emphasizes that it is both high quality and multilingual. The resulting model improves not only on mathematical tasks, but also on general reasoning benchmarks like MMLU and BBH, which suggests that the benefits of the math-focused training extend beyond narrow arithmetic or algebraic competence.

The paper also introduces Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO, as a more memory-efficient alternative to PPO. In the experiments, GRPO improves the already strong instruction-tuned model using only a subset of English instruction data, and it does so in a way that boosts both in-domain and out-of-domain mathematical performance. The overall result is a complete pipeline: large-scale math pre-training, supervised instruction tuning, and then reinforcement learning, each contributing to the final model’s capability.

# Why a 7B open model can approach far larger or proprietary systems

One of the most important lessons from the paper is that parameter count alone does not determine mathematical reasoning performance. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, despite being much smaller than systems such as Minerva 540B, achieves comparable or better performance on several benchmarks after training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus. The paper explicitly frames this as evidence that a smaller model trained on high-quality data can compete strongly with much larger models trained on less targeted data.

The authors attribute this partly to the quality and scale of the math corpus, and partly to the initialization strategy. DeepSeekMath-Base starts from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B rather than from a general-purpose language model, and the paper argues that this is a better choice. The model inherits useful structure from code pre-training, and the subsequent math training can then build on that foundation. This combination helps explain why a relatively compact open model can reach performance that would otherwise seem to require much larger scale.

The paper also shows that later-stage optimization matters. Supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning are not treated as decorative final steps, but as mechanisms that meaningfully reshape the model’s output distribution. DeepSeekMath-RL, in particular, demonstrates that even after strong instruction tuning, further gains are possible. The important point is that the model’s strength emerges from the interaction of data, initialization, and optimization, not from scale alone.

# Current weaknesses in geometry and theorem proving

Even though DeepSeekMath is strong on quantitative reasoning, the paper is clear that it remains weaker on geometry and theorem proving than the best closed models. In the conclusion, the authors note that the model is relatively weaker on geometry and theorem-proof tasks. They mention a dry run in which the model could not handle problems related to triangles and ellipses, which they present as a sign that the pre-training and fine-tuning data may not sufficiently cover certain geometric structures.

This limitation matters because geometry and theorem proving test different aspects of mathematical competence than the benchmark problems where DeepSeekMath performs best. Many of the model’s gains are shown on datasets such as GSM8K and MATH, which emphasize structured reasoning and problem solving in text form. Formal proof tasks, like miniF2F, are more demanding in a different way because they require precise symbolic control and proof construction. The paper reports strong few-shot autoformalization performance on miniF2F, but it does not claim that the model has solved formal mathematics in the broad sense. Instead, the results suggest progress, paired with clear remaining gaps.

The authors interpret these weaknesses as potentially related to data selection bias. In other words, the model may have seen enough examples of some mathematical styles to become strong there, while other subdomains remain underrepresented. This is an important limitation because it implies that model capability is not uniformly distributed across mathematics. A model can be very strong on algebraic reasoning and still be brittle on geometry or theorem proving.

# Few-shot limitations relative to GPT-4

Another limitation emphasized in the paper is that DeepSeekMath still falls behind GPT-4 in few-shot capability. The authors observe that GPT-4 improves more noticeably when given few-shot inputs, whereas DeepSeekMath shows similar performance in zero-shot and few-shot settings. This suggests that, although DeepSeekMath is strong at direct problem solving, it does not yet exploit in-context examples as effectively as GPT-4.

This difference is important because few-shot prompting is not just a benchmark trick; it is a sign of how well a model can adapt its behavior from examples at inference time. A model that benefits strongly from few-shot context is often more flexible in new situations. DeepSeekMath’s weaker few-shot performance therefore points to a remaining gap in adaptive reasoning, even when raw benchmark scores are strong.

The paper does not present this as a failure of the training pipeline, but as a constraint tied partly to model scale and partly to the model’s training history. The authors explicitly connect the gap to the model’s smaller size relative to GPT-4. So the takeaway is not that DeepSeekMath is ineffective, but that strong domain training can narrow the gap substantially without fully closing it, especially in the more flexible few-shot setting.

# Possible data selection biases in pre-training and fine-tuning

The paper repeatedly suggests that data selection bias may help explain some of the model’s remaining weaknesses. In pre-training, the authors build the DeepSeekMath Corpus by mining Common Crawl with a classifier trained on OpenWebMath-like examples. This approach is powerful, but it inevitably reflects what the seed data already contains. The authors themselves note that some mathematical web pages remain uncollected in early iterations because the positive examples lack diversity. That is precisely why they iterate on the corpus construction process by finding additional math-related domains and manually annotating URLs.

This same issue appears again in the discussion of limitations. The model’s difficulty with certain geometry problems may reflect the fact that the training data does not sufficiently cover those areas. Likewise, the paper reports that arXiv papers do not noticeably improve mathematical reasoning in their experiments, which complicates assumptions about what kinds of data are most useful. In other words, more “mathematical” text is not automatically better; the specific kinds of mathematical content matter.

Fine-tuning data can also introduce bias. The instruction-tuning set is rich, but it is still concentrated on selected sources such as GSM8K, MATH, MathInstruct, Lila-OOD, and Chinese K-12 problems. This helps the model become excellent at the kinds of reasoning represented there, but it may leave blind spots elsewhere. The paper’s broader lesson is that the performance profile of an LLM is shaped by what it is repeatedly exposed to, both in pre-training and in supervised refinement.

# The broader lesson that high-quality domain data can rival sheer parameter scale

Perhaps the most general lesson from the paper is that high-quality domain data can rival brute-force parameter scale. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B outperforming or matching much larger models on math benchmarks is the clearest evidence for this claim. The authors stress that the DeepSeekMath Corpus is not just large, but carefully selected and iteratively improved. Its quality appears to matter at least as much as its size.

The comparison with larger corpora is especially revealing. The paper finds that the DeepSeekMath Corpus outperforms other math corpora in pre-training experiments, and that improvements continue as the corpus scale grows. This supports a view of mathematical reasoning as a data-sensitive capability: the model can learn a lot from a relatively modest number of parameters if the training signal is focused, diverse enough, and sufficiently clean. The authors explicitly argue that their findings should encourage the research community to think more seriously about data engineering as a core ingredient of model performance.

The same lesson extends to multilingual training. Because the DeepSeekMath Corpus is not English-only, the model improves on Chinese mathematical benchmarks as well. That result reinforces the broader claim that domain data quality is not just about volume, but also about coverage. If the aim is robust mathematical reasoning, then a narrow English-centric pipeline may leave important capability gaps.

# The role of code pre-training in reasoning-oriented models

Another important lesson is that code pre-training appears to help mathematical reasoning. The paper treats this as a partial answer to the question of whether code training improves reasoning. In their experiments, training on code before math improves performance on mathematical tasks both with and without tool use. The effect is visible in the two-stage training setup, where code training alone already strengthens program-aided math solving, and subsequent math training improves it further.

This is significant because code and mathematics share structural properties. Both require careful sequence manipulation, explicit intermediate steps, and syntactic precision. The paper does not claim that code training magically creates reasoning ability in general, but it does show a concrete and reproducible benefit for mathematics. That makes code pre-training a useful component in the design of reasoning-oriented models.

The paper also observes a tradeoff. When code and math are mixed in one stage for a small model, the result can hurt mathematical reasoning without tool use, even though it helps preserve coding ability and program-aided reasoning. This suggests that code pre-training is beneficial, but that how it is combined with math training matters. For smaller models in particular, capacity limits may prevent the model from absorbing both sources equally well at once.

# Open problems in mathematical reasoning for LLMs

Although DeepSeekMath makes substantial progress, the paper makes it clear that mathematical reasoning in LLMs remains an open problem. One reason is that success on one class of benchmarks does not guarantee success on all forms of mathematics. The model is strong on quantitative reasoning, but weaker on geometry and formal theorem proving. It also lags behind GPT-4 in few-shot adaptability. These gaps show that the problem is not solved by scaling one training recipe.

A second open problem is robustness under different supervision styles. The paper explores outcome supervision, process supervision, online versus offline training, and iterative reinforcement learning. These experiments show that RL can help, but they also reveal that the details matter a great deal. For example, the paper argues that RL may be improving the ranking of correct answers in the model’s output distribution rather than fundamentally changing its underlying reasoning ability. That is a useful but sobering interpretation: performance can improve without the model becoming truly reliable in every sense.

A third open problem is reward quality. The paper emphasizes that reward models can be noisy, and even carefully annotated datasets may contain errors. That means reinforcement learning for reasoning depends on imperfect supervisory signals. Designing reward functions and learning algorithms that are robust to this noise remains an important unresolved issue.

# Future work in corpus construction, reward modeling, and RL design

The paper points to three broad directions for future work: better corpus construction, better reward modeling, and better reinforcement learning algorithms. On the corpus side, the authors clearly believe there is still room to improve their engineering pipeline. They suggest that the current data selection process is a promising starting point, not a final solution. More diverse positive examples, better multilingual coverage, and more careful handling of underrepresented mathematical subfields could all improve the next version of the corpus.

On the reward-model side, the paper highlights three future questions: improving generalization, modeling uncertainty, and building high-quality process reward models. Generalization matters because a reward model that only works on familiar prompts may help stabilize output distributions but not truly improve reasoning. Uncertainty matters because the reward signal itself is not always trustworthy, and future algorithms may need to account for that explicitly. Process reward models matter because they can provide more detailed feedback than end-of-answer supervision, which may be especially important for complex reasoning tasks.

On the RL-design side, the paper argues for methods that are more efficient and more robust. GRPO is one step in that direction because it removes the need for a separate critic and uses group-relative baselines instead. But the authors also suggest that future systems may need better data sampling strategies, more advanced decoding methods, improved inference efficiency, and algorithms that can handle noisy rewards. They frame this as a move toward weak-to-strong alignment methods and more reliable reasoning training.

Taken together, the roadmap is clear: open math LLMs can improve a great deal through careful data engineering and targeted optimization, but the remaining frontier lies in making those systems broader, more adaptive, and more reliable. DeepSeekMath demonstrates how far a well-designed open model can go, while also showing exactly where the next generation of work needs to focus.
