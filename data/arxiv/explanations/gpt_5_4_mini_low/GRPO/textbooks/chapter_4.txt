\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 4: 4. Validating Corpus Quality: Why Data Matters More Than Size Alone

# The role of corpus evaluation in domain-specific pre-training

A central claim of the paper is that building a successful mathematical language model is not only a matter of training longer or using a larger model. The quality of the pre-training corpus matters enormously, especially when the goal is domain-specific reasoning rather than broad next-token prediction. For this reason, the authors do not simply report that they collected a large math corpus; they explicitly validate that corpus by training models on it and measuring downstream performance. This kind of validation is essential because a corpus can look impressive by token count yet still be weak if it contains too much noise, too little diversity, or too narrow a distribution of mathematical content.

In this paper, corpus evaluation is therefore treated as an empirical question. The authors ask whether the DeepSeekMath Corpus actually produces better mathematical reasoning than other math-oriented corpora already used in prior work. Rather than assuming that more tokens automatically imply better training, they compare corpora under controlled conditions and inspect how performance changes during training. This allows them to separate the effect of the data itself from the effect of model size or training setup.

The broader lesson is that domain-specific pre-training should be judged by what it enables the model to do afterward. In this case, the relevant question is not whether the corpus is mathematically themed, but whether training on it improves benchmark performance on mathematical reasoning tasks in a measurable and sustained way.

# Comparison corpora: MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2

To evaluate the quality of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, the paper compares it against three existing math-related corpora. The first is MathPile, a multi-source corpus of $8.9$B tokens aggregated from textbooks, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, CommonCrawl, StackExchange, and arXiv, with more than $85\%$ of its data coming from arXiv. The second is OpenWebMath, a CommonCrawl-derived corpus filtered for mathematical content, totaling $13.6$B tokens. The third is Proof-Pile-2, a larger mathematical corpus consisting of OpenWebMath, AlgebraicStack mathematical code, and arXiv papers, with training conducted using an arXiv:Web:Code ratio of $2:4:1$.

These corpora are important baselines because they represent different design philosophies. MathPile relies heavily on scientific papers, OpenWebMath focuses on web-filtered mathematics, and Proof-Pile-2 combines web, code, and arXiv sources. By comparing against all three, the authors can assess not only whether DeepSeekMath is large, but whether its data selection pipeline produces a more useful training distribution.

A key point in the paper is that DeepSeekMath Corpus is substantially larger than these alternatives. It is described as almost $7$ times the size of the math web pages used by Minerva and $9$ times the size of OpenWebMath. However, the comparison is not just about quantity. The authors are interested in whether this larger corpus contains higher-quality mathematical data on average, and whether that quality remains visible when the same base model is trained on each corpus under the same protocol.

# Experimental setup with DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B

The corpus comparison is carried out using DeepSeek-LLM $1.3$B, a general pre-trained language model from the same framework as the DeepSeek LLMs. The model is chosen as a controlled testbed so that differences in downstream performance can be attributed primarily to the training corpus rather than to a large or specialized architecture. For each corpus, the model is trained separately for $150$B tokens.

This setup is important because it standardizes the experiment. If one corpus were trained for much longer, or with a different optimizer or batch size, the comparison would be hard to interpret. Instead, the paper intentionally uses the same model family and similar training conditions for all corpora. The goal is to isolate the effect of corpus content.

The authors also note that all experiments are conducted using the HAI-LLM training framework. This matters because it supports a consistent and efficient implementation across all runs. The model is trained under the same mathematical pre-training recipe, which makes the resulting differences in benchmark performance a meaningful indicator of corpus quality.

# Training protocol for controlled corpus comparison

The training protocol follows a carefully specified setup. The optimizer is AdamW with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, and weight decay $0.1$. The learning rate follows a multi-step schedule: it peaks after $2{,}000$ warmup steps, decays to $31.6\%$ of its peak after $80\%$ of training, and further decays to $10\%$ after $90\%$ of training. The maximum learning rate is set to $5.3 \times 10^{-4}$, and training uses a batch size of $4$M tokens with a context length of $4$K.

These details matter because they eliminate confounding factors. If one corpus had been trained with a different optimization schedule, then any observed performance gap might reflect training instability rather than corpus quality. By keeping the protocol fixed, the paper ensures that the comparison is as fair as possible.

The controlled setup also reveals another important feature of the study: the corpora are compared at the same token budget, namely $150$B tokens. That means the model sees each corpus for the same amount of training, so improvements cannot be explained simply by one dataset being used longer. In this sense, the experiment asks a direct question: if the model is given the same amount of training, which corpus teaches it mathematics better?

# Evaluation benchmarks for English and Chinese math reasoning

The quality of each corpus is assessed through downstream performance on eight mathematical benchmarks using few-shot chain-of-thought prompting. These benchmarks cover English and Chinese mathematical reasoning and span a range of difficulty levels. The paper emphasizes that the model is evaluated on tasks involving quantitative reasoning and multiple-choice problem solving, with the broader goal of measuring actual mathematical competence rather than corpus familiarity.

The English and Chinese coverage is especially important. English benchmarks are more common in prior work, but the DeepSeekMath Corpus is multilingual, so the evaluation must reflect that. The paper therefore examines not only standard English tasks but also Chinese mathematical benchmarks. This allows the authors to test whether multilingual training data translates into better reasoning performance beyond English-only settings.

By using the same benchmark suite to evaluate each corpus-trained model, the authors can compare how well each training distribution supports general mathematical reasoning. The benchmark results then serve as the core evidence for judging whether a corpus is high-quality, multilingual, and scalable.

# Evidence that DeepSeekMath Corpus has higher average quality

The paper reports a clear performance lead for the model trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus. Across the benchmark suite, the model consistently outperforms models trained on the comparison corpora. The authors interpret this as evidence that the average quality of the DeepSeekMath Corpus is higher, not merely that it is larger.

One especially informative comparison is with Proof-Pile-2. Figure comparisons in the paper indicate that a model trained on DeepSeekMath Corpus at $50$B tokens, which corresponds to one full epoch of Proof-Pile-2, already performs better than the Proof-Pile-2-trained model at that stage. This suggests that DeepSeekMath provides stronger training signals per token. In other words, a token from DeepSeekMath appears to be more useful, on average, than a token from the baseline corpus.

This result is central to the paper’s message. It demonstrates that scale alone does not explain performance. Instead, the composition and selection of the corpus matter. The DeepSeekMath Corpus appears to contain a larger concentration of genuinely useful mathematical content, which produces stronger downstream reasoning even when the training budget is held constant.

# Learning curve analysis and data scaling behavior

The paper goes beyond final benchmark scores and also studies learning curves over the course of training. This is important because two corpora might end at similar performance while behaving very differently during optimization. Learning curves reveal whether a corpus provides sustained gains or whether its benefits are exhausted early.

According to the paper, the DeepSeekMath Corpus produces a steeper learning curve and more lasting improvements than the smaller baseline corpora. This means that as training proceeds, the model continues to benefit from the DeepSeekMath data rather than quickly reaching a plateau. By contrast, smaller corpora are repeatedly cycled through during training, and the resulting model performance tends to flatten earlier.

This behavior is exactly what one would expect when a dataset has limited diversity. If a corpus is too small, the model sees the same patterns many times, and the training signal becomes repetitive. Once the model has absorbed the most common structures, additional passes over the same data contribute less. The DeepSeekMath Corpus avoids this problem better because it is much larger and more varied, so the model continues encountering new useful mathematical patterns during training.

# Multilingual coverage and its effect on Chinese benchmarks

A particularly distinctive feature of the DeepSeekMath Corpus is that it is multilingual, with English and Chinese as the two most represented languages. This is a notable departure from prior math corpora, which are described in the paper as primarily English-centric. The multilingual nature of the corpus has direct consequences for benchmark performance.

The paper reports that training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus improves mathematical reasoning in both English and Chinese. In contrast, the earlier corpora show limited gains on Chinese benchmarks and may even hurt performance there. This suggests that English-only math data is not automatically transferable to Chinese mathematical reasoning, especially when the evaluation tasks themselves are presented in Chinese.

The result is significant because it shows that corpus language composition matters just as much as topic composition. A math corpus can be rich in English mathematical expression yet still underperform on Chinese tasks if it lacks appropriate multilingual coverage. The DeepSeekMath Corpus appears to avoid this limitation, which helps explain its stronger performance on Chinese benchmarks such as MGSM-zh, CMATH, and the Gaokao-based tasks.

# Why small corpora plateau earlier during training

The paper emphasizes that smaller corpora tend to plateau early during training. This is not surprising when one considers how repeated exposure to the same data affects learning. Once a model has fit the most salient patterns in a small corpus, additional training epochs provide diminishing returns. The model may continue to memorize details, but it does not necessarily acquire meaningfully new reasoning ability.

In the comparison experiments, the baseline corpora are much smaller and are repeated multiple times during training. The authors observe that this leads to quick saturation in model performance. The model’s improvements slow down because it is no longer encountering enough fresh information to continue refining its mathematical representations.

The DeepSeekMath Corpus behaves differently because it is much larger. Since the model can train on a broader range of examples before repeating content, it experiences a more gradual and extended learning process. This is why the paper describes its learning curve as steeper and more durable. The larger corpus supports ongoing improvement instead of early stagnation.

# Interpreting corpus quality through downstream benchmark gains

The most important conclusion from the corpus comparison is that corpus quality can be inferred from downstream benchmark gains. The DeepSeekMath Corpus is judged not by its size alone, but by the fact that it consistently improves mathematical reasoning across benchmarks, languages, and training durations. Those gains are the evidence that the corpus is high-quality.

The paper’s broader lesson is that mathematical pre-training should be treated as a data selection problem. A large corpus is only useful if it is carefully constructed so that the model sees mathematically meaningful and diverse content. The DeepSeekMath Corpus succeeds because it combines scale, multilinguality, and targeted selection from Common Crawl. Its value becomes visible in benchmark performance, where it outperforms alternative corpora even when all models are trained under the same conditions.

For a student reading the paper, this is one of the most important takeaways. The results show that domain-specific reasoning depends strongly on what the model learns from during pre-training. Data quality, not just model size or token count, is a decisive factor in whether a model can become strong at mathematical reasoning.
