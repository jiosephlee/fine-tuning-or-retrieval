\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 7: 7. Supervised Fine-Tuning for Mathematical Instruction Following

# Why supervised fine-tuning is needed after pre-training

After pre-training, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B has already learned a strong amount of mathematical and general reasoning ability from the large-scale math corpus, code data, and natural language data. However, pre-training alone does not yet turn the model into a reliable instruction-following assistant. In the paper’s framing, the base model is strong at capability, but supervised fine-tuning is the stage that reshapes that capability into a form that is directly useful for solving posed problems in the style seen in benchmarks and user queries.

This distinction matters because the evaluation tasks in the paper are not merely about whether the model has seen mathematical content. They ask whether the model can respond in a structured way, produce complete solutions, and follow instructions in the format expected by benchmark settings. Supervised fine-tuning provides that bridge. It takes a capable base model and teaches it how to express reasoning in solution formats such as chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated reasoning. In other words, pre-training builds latent mathematical competence, while supervised fine-tuning turns that competence into an instruction-following behavior.

The paper also makes clear that this stage is not limited to improving performance in one narrow setting. The resulting DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B is evaluated both with and without tool use, and it is intended to behave as a stronger mathematical assistant overall. So supervised fine-tuning is not simply a cosmetic adaptation layer; it is the stage where raw model ability becomes a usable problem-solving system.

# Design goals for a mathematical instruction dataset

The supervised fine-tuning dataset is designed to cover mathematical problems across English and Chinese, across different branches of mathematics, and across varying difficulty levels. The paper’s dataset contains 776K training examples, which is large enough to expose the model to many forms of mathematical instruction while still being curated toward the task of reasoning.

A central design goal is that the dataset should not consist of a single rigid style of solution. Instead, the paper pairs problems with solutions in multiple reasoning formats. This choice reflects the fact that mathematical problem solving can be represented in different ways: sometimes as a natural-language derivation, sometimes as a program that performs computations, and sometimes as a hybrid of language reasoning and tool use. By training on all of these formats, the model learns not only to answer, but to choose a style of response compatible with the problem.

Another important goal is breadth. The paper explicitly aims for coverage of different mathematical fields and complexity levels. This means the data should not be concentrated only on one subset, such as arithmetic or algebra, but should include a wide span of topics and difficulty. The Chinese side of the dataset is also described as spanning 76 sub-topics, which indicates that the curation process is intended to produce broad topical coverage rather than a small set of repetitive patterns. That diversity is important because a mathematical assistant must generalize across many types of tasks, not merely memorize one benchmark style.

# English data sources: GSM8K, MATH, MathInstruct, and Lila-OOD

The English portion of the supervised fine-tuning dataset is built from several sources. First, the paper uses GSM8K and MATH problems, which are important because they represent widely used benchmarks and provide high-value mathematical instruction examples. These are annotated with tool-integrated solutions. That means the model is not only shown the final answer, but also how to use tools as part of the reasoning process.

The paper also adopts a subset of MathInstruct and the training set of Lila-OOD. These sources contribute problems solved with chain-of-thought or program-of-thought. This is significant because the goal is not to train the model on one single reasoning style. Instead, the dataset intentionally mixes styles so the model can learn to handle both textual derivations and executable reasoning. MathInstruct contributes broad math instruction data, while Lila-OOD adds examples that are useful for out-of-distribution generalization within the mathematical domain.

The paper states that the English collection covers diverse fields of mathematics, including algebra, probability, number theory, calculus, and geometry. This breadth is part of the design rather than an accidental property of the source mix. A dataset that covered only one family of tasks would likely overfit that family and fail on others. By including a range of fields, the authors aim to teach the model a general pattern of mathematical instruction following.

# Chinese data sources and K-12 topic coverage

The Chinese supervised fine-tuning data is constructed separately and is focused on Chinese K-12 mathematical problems. This choice complements the English sources and matches the paper’s broader emphasis that DeepSeekMath should be multilingual, not English-only. The paper notes that the Chinese collection spans 76 sub-topics, with examples such as linear equations.

This coverage is important for two reasons. First, it broadens the model’s mathematical competence beyond English benchmarks. Second, it ensures the fine-tuning data reflects the style and structure of Chinese educational math problems, which can differ from English benchmark problems. Since the model is intended to perform well on Chinese mathematical benchmarks such as MGSM-zh, CMATH, and Gaokao-style tasks, training on Chinese K-12 problems is a direct way to align the model with that evaluation setting.

The Chinese dataset is also annotated in both chain-of-thought and tool-integrated reasoning format. That means the model learns not only content in Chinese, but also the reasoning presentation style that the paper considers useful for mathematical instruction following. In this sense, the Chinese side of the dataset is not just translated data; it is structured supervision designed to teach mathematical problem solving in the same manner as the English side.

# Reasoning formats: chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated reasoning

A defining feature of the supervised fine-tuning stage is the use of multiple reasoning formats. The paper explicitly says that problems are paired with solutions in chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated reasoning format. These are not interchangeable labels; each one teaches the model a slightly different kind of behavior.

Chain-of-thought trains the model to express intermediate reasoning steps in natural language. This is useful when the solution can be derived by step-by-step explanation without external tools. It encourages the model to be explicit rather than jumping directly to the answer, which is important for both interpretability and multi-step reasoning.

Program-of-thought goes a step further by having the model write a Python program to solve the problem. This format is especially valuable when the task requires precise computation. The paper treats libraries such as $\textit{math}$ and $\textit{sympy}$ as examples of tools that can be used in this framework. Program-of-thought teaches the model to translate a word problem into executable logic, which is a different skill from pure natural-language explanation.

Tool-integrated reasoning combines language reasoning with tool use in a more explicit way. In the paper, this is used for annotated solutions to GSM8K and MATH, among others. The value of this format is that it models how a mathematical assistant may need to call external computation or symbolic tools while still maintaining a coherent explanation. Taken together, these formats provide the model with multiple pathways to solve the same class of problems, which makes the resulting instruction-tuned model more flexible.

# Balancing subject diversity and difficulty levels

The paper’s dataset design is not only about choosing reasoning formats; it is also about balancing subject diversity and problem difficulty. This matters because a mathematical model can fail in two different ways: it may know only a narrow topic area, or it may know a topic but only at a limited difficulty level. The dataset is therefore curated to span different fields and complexities so the model sees a representative range of instruction-following situations.

On the English side, the paper explicitly mentions algebra, probability, number theory, calculus, and geometry. This indicates that the dataset is intended to expose the model to both symbolic and applied forms of mathematics. On the Chinese side, the K-12 collection spans many sub-topics, suggesting a similarly broad range of problem types. The result is a dataset that is more like a curriculum than a single benchmark set.

This balance is especially important because the paper later evaluates the model on tasks from elementary to college-level complexity. If the fine-tuning set were too narrow or too easy, the model would not be prepared for those evaluations. If it were too concentrated on a small number of benchmark styles, it might overfit patterns rather than learn robust instruction following. The curation strategy is therefore aimed at general mathematical usability rather than benchmark memorization.

# Training setup for DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B

DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B is obtained by supervised fine-tuning DeepSeekMath-Base on the curated mathematical instruction dataset. The paper describes the training setup directly: the training examples are randomly concatenated until a maximum context length of 4K tokens is reached. The model is then trained for 500 steps, using a batch size of 256 and a constant learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-5}$.

This setup reflects the practical goal of turning the base model into an instruction-following model without changing the underlying architecture. The use of a constant learning rate suggests a straightforward supervised optimization regime, and the batch size indicates that the model is trained on many instruction examples per update. Because the data are concatenated up to the context limit, the model is exposed to sequences that may contain multiple training examples packed together, which is an efficient way to use the available context window.

The paper’s evaluation shows that this supervised fine-tuning stage significantly improves mathematical performance both with and without tool use. In the paper’s comparison, DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B is stronger than all 7B counterparts and competitive with much larger open-source instruction-tuned models. That is the practical outcome of this stage: the base model’s mathematical ability is transformed into a much more capable instruction-following assistant.

# Concatenation and context-length management during SFT

A technical detail that matters in supervised fine-tuning is how the training examples are arranged within the model’s context window. The paper states that examples are randomly concatenated until reaching a maximum context length of 4K tokens. This means the fine-tuning process does not necessarily present one isolated problem per sequence. Instead, multiple examples are packed together to form a training sequence within the allowed length.

This approach is a form of context-length management. Because the model has a fixed maximum context, the training pipeline must decide how to use that capacity efficiently. Random concatenation helps fill the context window and makes the most of the available sequence length during training. It also allows the model to repeatedly see instruction-response patterns in varied arrangements, which can be useful for learning robust formatting behavior.

At the same time, context-length management matters because mathematical solutions can be long. Chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated solutions may all consume significant tokens. By working within a 4K-token limit, the fine-tuning process must fit both the problem statement and the solution in a manageable sequence. The paper’s training choice reflects this constraint directly: the dataset and packing strategy are organized so that instruction examples can be learned effectively within the model’s context budget.

Overall, supervised fine-tuning in DeepSeekMath is the stage that converts a strong pre-trained mathematical base into a usable mathematical instruction model. It does this through a carefully curated multilingual dataset, diverse reasoning formats, and a training setup designed to fit within the model’s context while preserving breadth and complexity.
