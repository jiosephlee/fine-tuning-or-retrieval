\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does starting from a code model help math reasoning if the final task is mostly natural language?
Question:
I'm reading the DeepSeekMath paper, and one claim I find surprising is that initializing from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B works better than starting from a general language model, and they even say code training improves mathematical reasoning both with and without tool use.

I'm confused about the mechanism here. If the final evaluation includes chain-of-thought solutions written in natural language, why would code pretraining help so much? Is the idea that code teaches precise step-by-step execution, symbolic structure, and long dependency tracking, or is it mainly helping with the program-of-thought/tool-use setting and only indirectly transferring to plain reasoning?

I would appreciate an explanation of what kind of inductive bias code data might provide, and whether this paper actually demonstrates a causal benefit for reasoning itself or just a correlation with their training recipe.
Answer:
What the paper shows is not that “code is secretly math,” but that code is a very good source of structural pretraining for a model that will later be adapted to mathematical reasoning.

The key point is that the authors do compare against a control: they train one model for $400\text{B}$ tokens on code and another for $400\text{B}$ tokens on general text, and then continue both with $150\text{B}$ math tokens. In that setup, the code-first model does better on mathematical benchmarks. So within the paper’s training recipe, code is not just a random correlated feature; it appears to provide a better initialization than generic web text.

Why might that happen? The paper’s own discussion is careful here: it does not claim a deep causal theory, only a “partial response” to the hypothesis that code improves reasoning. The intuitive reason is that code has several properties that overlap with the demands of mathematical reasoning. Code is highly formal, local changes can have global effects, syntax matters, long-range dependencies matter, and execution is unforgiving. That encourages representations that are sensitive to exact symbols, variable binding, nested structure, and multi-step transformations. Those are all useful for math, even if the final answer is written in natural language. In other words, the model is not learning “Python-style answers”; it is learning a style of computation and symbol manipulation that transfers well to chain-of-thought reasoning.

The paper also gives evidence that this is not only about tool use. They explicitly say code training improves mathematical reasoning “both with and without tool use.” That matters because if the benefit were only from program-of-thought or Python execution, you would expect the gains to be limited to the tool-using setting. Instead, they observe improvements even when the model must produce self-contained natural-language solutions. So the transfer is broader than just “it got better at writing programs”; it seems to sharpen the model’s internal reasoning behavior as well.

At the same time, the evidence is still empirical rather than causal in a mechanistic sense. The paper does not isolate a single latent factor such as “step-by-step execution” or “symbolic composition” and prove that this is what matters. It shows a useful ablation: code-first pretraining beats general-text-first pretraining in their experiments, and mixing code with math can further help tool-based reasoning. But that is best read as a strong training-result claim, not a universal proof about why reasoning emerges.

One more nuance from the paper is that the benefit depends on how the data are combined. They note that a one-stage mixture of code and math helps coding and program-aided reasoning, but it can hurt math reasoning without tools, which they attribute to limited model capacity at $1.3\text{B}$. That is a good reminder that code is not magic; it is just one kind of pretraining signal, and the final effect depends on scale and data balance.

So the short answer is: in DeepSeekMath, code pretraining helps because it gives the model an inductive bias toward precise symbolic transformation and long dependency handling, which transfers to both program-aided and plain mathematical reasoning. The paper supports that as an empirical finding with a reasonable control, but it does not establish a deeper causal mechanism beyond that.

### What exactly is meant by '120B math tokens' in DeepSeekMath's corpus construction?
Question:
The paper says DeepSeekMath continues pretraining on '120B math-related tokens sourced from Common Crawl', and later says they ended up with 35.5M mathematical web pages totaling 120B tokens.

I'm not sure how to interpret this number. Are these 120B tokens all unique after deduplication, or is this just the size of the final selected text before any training-time resampling? Also, when they compare top 40B, 80B, 120B, and 160B tokens during data selection, are those nested subsets ranked by the fastText score?

In short, what does '120B tokens' mean operationally in this paper: raw corpus size, unique post-dedup size, or effective training exposure size?
Answer:
In the paper, “$120\,\text{B}$ math tokens” means the size of the **final selected corpus after mining and filtering**, measured after tokenization, not the amount of training exposure and not something like “effective tokens seen by the optimizer.”

The key sentence is that after four iterations they “end up with $35.5\,\text{M}$ mathematical web pages, totaling $120\,\text{B}$ tokens.” That is describing the **corpus content**: if you concatenate all the selected pages and tokenize them, you get about $120\times 10^9$ tokens. It is not saying the model trained on $120\,\text{B}$ *unique* tokens only once, nor that the training run was capped at exactly $120\,\text{B}$ tokens. In fact, the training run for DeepSeekMath-Base is separate and lasts $500\,\text{B}$ total tokens, of which $56\%$ come from the DeepSeekMath Corpus. So the corpus size and the training exposure are different quantities.

Operationally, the “top $40\,\text{B}$, $80\,\text{B}$, $120\,\text{B}$, and $160\,\text{B}$ tokens” are indeed nested prefixes of the pages ranked by the fastText score. The paper says they “rank the collected pages according to their scores predicted by the fastText model, and only preserve the top-ranking ones,” then vary the amount preserved by taking the top $40\,\text{B}$, $80\,\text{B}$, $120\,\text{B}$, or $160\,\text{B}$ tokens for pre-training experiments. So those are best understood as **score-ranked subsets**, with larger cutoffs containing the smaller ones.

One subtle point is that the paper’s “120B tokens” is a corpus-size number after their iterative collection and dedup/filtering pipeline, but before any downstream training schedule is applied. The model may then see those tokens once, multiple times, or as part of a mixture, depending on the training setup. In that sense, “120B tokens” is neither raw Common Crawl size nor “effective training tokens”; it is the **final post-selection corpus size**.

### How strong is the paper's decontamination method based on exact 10-gram matching?
Question:
DeepSeekMath says they avoid benchmark contamination by removing any text segment containing a 10-gram that exactly matches benchmark text, and for shorter benchmark text they use exact matching when the text has at least 3 grams.

I understand the basic idea, but I don't know how reliable this is in practice. For math problems, many solutions and statements can be paraphrased while keeping the same numbers, formulas, or structure. So I am wondering:

1. Does exact 10-gram matching mostly remove verbatim copies but still miss lightly reworded benchmark leakage?
2. Could it also over-remove benign pages that happen to share common mathematical phrasing?
3. When they say they remove 'any text segment' with a match, does that mean the whole page is removed or only the matching span?

I'm trying to judge how convincing the contamination control is for math benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH.
Answer:
The short answer is that this is a fairly standard and reasonably conservative *verbatim* decontamination strategy, but it is not a guarantee against all leakage. In the paper, DeepSeekMath says they “filter out web pages containing questions or answers” from the benchmarks, and the criterion is that “any text segment containing a $10$-gram string that matches exactly with any sub-string from the evaluation benchmarks is removed.” That means they are trying to eliminate pages with direct textual overlap, not to detect semantic similarity.

So to your first point: yes, exact $10$-gram matching will mainly catch near-verbatim copies, and it will miss lightly paraphrased benchmark leakage. If a benchmark problem is rewritten with the same numbers and structure but different wording, it can easily evade an exact $10$-token match. This is a limitation of all exact-string decontamination methods: they are good at detecting copied text, but not at detecting “same problem, rephrased.” For math datasets like GSM8K and MATH, that matters because many problems can indeed be paraphrased while preserving the underlying reasoning task.

On the second point, over-removal is possible in principle, but the $10$-gram threshold is chosen precisely to reduce accidental matches from common mathematical phrasing. Short exact overlaps like “find the value of” would be too noisy, which is why they only use exact matching for benchmark texts shorter than $10$ grams if they are at least $3$ grams long. A full $10$-gram exact match is relatively specific, so false positives should be much rarer than with shorter n-grams. Still, some benign pages could be removed if they happen to contain a long formulaic segment that appears in a benchmark, especially in math where wording is repetitive. So the method is conservative in the sense of preferring false negatives over false positives, but it is not immune to either.

For your third question, the wording in the paper suggests that the removal unit is the surrounding text segment or page, not just the matched span. They say “any text segment containing” the matching $10$-gram is removed, and earlier they describe filtering out “web pages containing questions or answers” from the benchmarks. So the intention is to exclude the whole web page or extracted segment that contains the match, rather than surgically deleting only the overlapping substring. That is consistent with typical contamination filtering pipelines, because keeping the rest of the page would still leave nearby context that could leak the benchmark content.

So how convincing is this for GSM8K and MATH? I would call it a useful and defensible baseline, but not airtight. It strongly reduces the risk of memorizing exact benchmark text, which is the most direct kind of contamination. But it does not rule out paraphrased benchmark problems, equivalent algebraic variants, or pages that indirectly reveal solutions. In other words, it controls for *textual overlap*, not for *task-level overlap*. For a math benchmark, that is enough to make “the model saw the benchmark verbatim” much less plausible, but not enough to prove there was no benchmark-related leakage at all.

### Why would arXiv pretraining fail to improve mathematical reasoning in this paper?
Question:
One of the most counterintuitive claims in the paper is that arXiv papers 'seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning'. They report little or no gain, and sometimes deterioration, when training on arXiv-heavy corpora.

This confuses me because arXiv contains a huge amount of advanced mathematics and formal-looking reasoning. Shouldn't that be ideal data for downstream math tasks?

Is the likely explanation that benchmark tasks such as GSM8K and MATH are much closer to worked examples, educational exposition, and problem-solution style web pages than to research papers? Or is the issue that arXiv text has too much notation, domain specificity, and incomplete derivations to teach the kind of reasoning these benchmarks test?

I'm looking for an intuitive explanation of why 'more advanced mathematical text' might not translate into better 'mathematical reasoning' scores.
Answer:
The paper’s claim is less surprising if you separate “mathematical text” from “mathematical reasoning data.”

What the benchmarks in this paper reward, such as GSM8K, MATH, and CMATH, is not just exposure to advanced symbols or definitions, but the ability to produce a complete solution path from a short problem statement to a final answer. That is very close to the style of worked examples, instructional pages, and problem-solution web pages. The DeepSeekMath authors explicitly argue that their Common Crawl-derived math corpus is high quality precisely because it contains this kind of web text, and they report that it outperforms corpora dominated by arXiv papers. In contrast, arXiv papers “seem ineffective” in their experiments, even when the arXiv corpus is large and cleaned.

Intuitively, arXiv papers are often the wrong supervision signal for these benchmarks. A research paper usually assumes a lot of context, compresses arguments aggressively, and leaves many steps implicit. It is optimized for communicating new results to experts, not for teaching step-by-step problem solving. So although arXiv contains advanced mathematics, most of that content is not in the form “here is a problem, here is a full derivation, now predict the next step,” which is exactly the kind of pattern language models can most easily turn into benchmark performance. The paper’s own discussion points in this direction: it contrasts arXiv-heavy corpora with web corpora mined for mathematical content and finds the latter much more useful.

There is also a representation issue. The paper suggests that arXiv-only training can even hurt performance on some tasks. That is plausible because arXiv text has a very different distribution from benchmark questions: it is denser, more domain-specific, and often more notation-heavy. If the model mostly sees theorem statements, proofs, and technical exposition, it may learn to model that genre well without learning the “problem solving” format tested by these benchmarks. In other words, it may become better at reading papers, but not necessarily better at solving grade-school or competition-style questions.

So the short answer is: yes, your intuition is basically right, but with an important nuance. The issue is not that arXiv is “too advanced” in some absolute sense; it is that the benchmarked skill is closer to structured solution generation than to consuming research prose. Advanced mathematics in arXiv is not automatically good training data for reasoning unless it is presented in a form that exposes intermediate steps and solution structure.

The paper also hedges appropriately: the authors say this conclusion should be taken “with a grain of salt.” They did not test every possible math task, they did not study arXiv combined with other data, and they note that larger models might benefit more. So the result is really about this experimental setting: for the kinds of mathematical reasoning benchmarks they evaluate, arXiv-heavy pretraining is not as helpful as one might expect.

### In GRPO, why is the same normalized reward assigned to every token in an output under outcome supervision?
Question:
The paper defines outcome-supervised GRPO by sampling a group of outputs for the same question, scoring each full output with a reward model, normalizing the rewards within the group, and then setting the advantage for every token in output $o_i$ to the same value:

$\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})}$.

I'm struggling with the credit assignment here. If only the final answer is judged, why is it reasonable to reinforce or penalize every token equally? For example, an output could have mostly good reasoning but make a final arithmetic mistake, or vice versa.

Is this just a practical approximation because token-level value estimation is hard, or is there a deeper policy-gradient justification for broadcasting the same sequence-level reward to all tokens?
Answer:
In the paper’s outcome-supervised GRPO, the same normalized reward is assigned to every token because the method is treating the whole generated solution as one trajectory whose quality is judged only at the end. In that setting, there is no token-level supervision signal, so the only thing the algorithm can legitimately say is: “this entire sampled output was better or worse than the other outputs for the same question.” The token-level gradient then comes from the policy gradient decomposition, not from token-level rewards.

Concretely, the objective contains a sum over token log-probabilities, but the advantage is a single scalar sequence-level signal, $\hat{A}_{i,t}=\widetilde{r}_i$. This is exactly the standard REINFORCE idea applied to a sequence: if the return depends only on the final outcome, then every action along the trajectory gets weighted by that same return in the gradient estimate. Intuitively, you are not claiming that each token deserves the same “credit” in a causal sense; you are saying that every token in the sampled completion is part of the action sequence that led to that outcome, so each token’s probability should be nudged in the direction of or away from sequences that got higher or lower reward.

The normalization by the group mean and standard deviation is there to make this a relative advantage, i.e. to compare outputs for the same prompt and reduce variance. That matters because reward models are often better at ranking completions than assigning absolute values. So GRPO is using the group as the baseline instead of a learned critic, which is the paper’s main efficiency trick.

Your concern about “good reasoning but a final arithmetic mistake” is exactly why the paper also studies process supervision. Under process supervision, they do not broadcast one scalar to all tokens; instead they assign rewards at step boundaries and accumulate future step rewards into token advantages. So the paper’s answer is basically: outcome supervision is a coarse but valid policy-gradient estimator, while process supervision is the finer credit-assignment version when you want more local feedback.

So yes, there is a deeper policy-gradient justification here: the broadcasted reward is not an arbitrary approximation, it is the natural estimator when only a terminal reward is available. But the paper also implicitly acknowledges the limitation, and its process-supervised variant is the remedy.

### How is GRPO different from PPO if both still use importance ratios and clipping?
Question:
At first glance, GRPO looks very similar to PPO because the objective still contains the same kind of clipped importance ratio term

$\frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t} \mid q, o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_{i,t} \mid q, o_{i,<t})}$

with a $\min(\cdot, \mathrm{clip}(\cdot))$ structure.

The paper says the main difference is that GRPO removes the critic and uses group-relative rewards as a baseline. But I'm not sure how deep this difference really is. Is GRPO best understood as 'PPO with a handcrafted baseline instead of a learned value function', or does the group normalization fundamentally change the optimization target?

I'd appreciate a careful explanation of what is preserved from PPO and what is genuinely new in GRPO.
Answer:
GRPO is best understood as keeping the *PPO-style policy update* but replacing PPO’s *advantage estimation machinery* with a group-relative one, and then moving the KL regularization from the reward into the loss. So the clipped importance ratio is still there because GRPO is still doing a proximal, on-policy policy-gradient update; what changes is how the scalar signal $A_{i,t}$ is obtained and how much extra machinery is needed to compute it.

In PPO, the clipped term

$$
\min\left[r_t(\theta) A_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)A_t\right]
$$

is only half the story. The important part is that $A_t$ comes from a learned value function $V_\psi$ via GAE, so PPO is really an actor-critic method: the critic estimates “how much better than expected” a token/action was, and that estimate is used as the policy-gradient weight. In the paper’s formulation, PPO also typically includes a KL penalty inside the reward

$$
r_t = r_\varphi(q,o_{\le t}) - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}.
$$

GRPO removes the critic entirely. Instead, for each prompt $q$, it samples a *group* of outputs $\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G$ from the old policy, scores them, and computes advantages from the *relative* reward within that group. In the simplest outcome-supervision case, the advantage is just the normalized reward

$$
\hat A_{i,t}=\frac{r_i-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf r)},
$$

and this same value is used for all tokens in the sampled completion. So the baseline is not learned per token; it is the empirical mean of the group, which acts as a local, question-conditioned baseline. That is the key idea: “better than the other answers to the same question” replaces “better than the critic’s prediction.”

This is more than just a cosmetic baseline swap, because it changes the variance-reduction mechanism and the supervision signal. PPO’s critic tries to predict absolute expected return token by token, which is hard in language generation, especially when the reward is often only available at the end of the completion. GRPO instead uses comparisons among multiple samples for the same prompt, which fits the comparative nature of reward models much better. In other words, the optimization is still policy-gradient-like, but the *target signal* is now relative rather than absolute.

So the right mental model is: GRPO preserves PPO’s proximal/clipped policy update, but simplifies the estimator by dropping the critic and replacing it with group-relative advantages. That is why the paper describes it as a “variant of PPO” rather than a completely different algorithm. It is not changing the fundamental objective into something unrelated; it is changing how the advantage is constructed and how the KL constraint is applied. The result is lower memory use and, in this paper, better suitability for mathematical reasoning where relative correctness across multiple sampled solutions is especially informative.

### Why does GRPO subtract the KL term directly in the objective instead of putting it into the reward like PPO?
Question:
The paper contrasts PPO and GRPO in how they use KL regularization. PPO adds a per-token KL penalty into the reward,

$ r_t = r_\varphi(q, o_{\le t}) - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(o_t \mid q, o_{<t})}{\pi_{ref}(o_t \mid q, o_{<t})}, $

while GRPO subtracts a KL divergence term directly in the objective.

I don't fully understand why this change matters. The paper says this avoids complicating the calculation of $\hat{A}_{i,t}$, but I don't see the exact issue. Would including KL inside the reward interfere with the group-relative normalization, or would it make the baseline depend on the current policy in an undesirable way?

Could someone explain the practical and mathematical reason for moving the KL term outside the reward in GRPO?
Answer:
In GRPO, the KL term is moved outside the reward because the role of the KL penalty is different from the role of the task reward, and GRPO’s advantage estimator is built specifically to operate on the task reward only.

The paper’s GRPO advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is computed from the rewards of the sampled outputs within a group by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation. In the outcome-supervision case, for example, they set every token in an output to the same normalized group reward, $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i$. The point is that $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ measures how much better or worse one sampled completion is than the other completions for the same question. That comparison is supposed to reflect the quality of the answer, not how far the current policy has drifted from the reference model.

If you were to fold the KL penalty into the reward, then the “reward” used to build the group-relative baseline would no longer be a pure score of the sampled solution. It would become

$$
r_i^{\text{total}} = r_i - \beta \, \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})
$$

or, in the token-level PPO form, a sum of task reward and per-token KL penalties. Then the group mean and variance used to normalize rewards would mix together two conceptually different signals: answer quality and policy regularization. That makes the relative baseline depend on the current policy’s divergence from the reference model, which is not what the group-relative normalization is meant to measure.

So the practical reason is simply separation of concerns: GRPO wants the group statistics to estimate “which sampled answers are better,” while the KL term is just a regularizer that keeps the policy close to the reference model. Putting the KL term inside the reward would contaminate the group-relative advantage with a policy-shift penalty, making $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ harder to interpret and compute cleanly.

Mathematically, the paper is saying that the KL penalty should be treated as an external regularization term in the objective rather than as part of the reward signal used for advantage estimation. This is especially natural in GRPO because there is no critic/value function; the method relies entirely on group-normalized rewards, so any extra term inserted into the reward would directly affect the baseline estimation. By keeping KL outside, GRPO can compute $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ from the sampled outputs alone, exactly as the paper states, while still regularizing the policy toward $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

So the short answer is: yes, including KL inside the reward would interfere with the group-relative normalization, and that is precisely why the paper separates it. The KL is meant to constrain the policy update, not to participate in the within-group comparison of answers.

### What does the 'unbiased KL estimator' in the GRPO section actually estimate?
Question:
In the GRPO section, the paper gives the following expression for estimating the KL divergence:

$\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\theta} \| \pi_{ref}] = \frac{\pi_{ref}(o_{i,t} \mid q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t} \mid q,o_{i,<t})} - \log \frac{\pi_{ref}(o_{i,t} \mid q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t} \mid q,o_{i,<t})} - 1.$

This confuses me for two reasons:

1. A KL divergence is an expectation over a distribution, but the formula shown looks like a sample-wise function of a single token.
2. The ratio is written as $\pi_{ref} / \pi_{\theta}$, which is not the usual log-ratio form I expect from KL.

How should I interpret this equation? Is it an unbiased estimator whose expectation under samples from $\pi_{\theta}$ equals $\mathbb{D}_{KL}(\pi_{\theta} \| \pi_{ref})$? If so, what derivation justifies that specific form?
Answer:
Yes — the expression is meant as an unbiased single-sample estimator of the token-level KL divergence, where the sample token is drawn from the current policy $\pi_\theta$.

The key identity is the standard variational trick behind KL estimation. If $x \sim p$, then

$$
\mathbb{D}_{KL}(p \| q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right].
$$

But instead of estimating this with just the raw log-ratio, the paper uses the function

$$
\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} - \log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)} - 1.
$$

If you take expectation under $x \sim p$, this is indeed exactly $\mathbb{D}_{KL}(p\|q)$, because

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right]
= \sum_x p(x)\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} = \sum_x q(x) = 1,
$$

so

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} - \log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)} - 1\right]
= 1 - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right] - 1
= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right]
= \mathbb{D}_{KL}(p\|q).
$$

In the paper’s notation, set $p = \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid q,o_{<t})$ and $q = \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid q,o_{<t})$. Then for a sampled token $o_{i,t} \sim \pi_\theta$, the quantity

$$
\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
-\log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
-1
$$

is a one-sample estimator whose expectation is the forward KL

$$
\mathbb{D}_{KL}\!\left[\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}}\right].
$$

So your intuition is correct: the displayed formula is not the KL itself, but a sample-wise estimator of it.

Why this particular form instead of just the log-ratio? Because the raw log-ratio $\log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ is also an unbiased estimator under samples from $\pi_\theta$, but the paper is using the estimator from \cite{kl_approx}, which has the useful property of being nonnegative pointwise since for $u>0$,

$$
u - \log u - 1 \ge 0.
$$

This makes it a convenient penalty term for optimization. Intuitively, it measures how much the current policy deviates from the reference policy, while staying well behaved numerically.

One small nuance: the paper writes the estimator at the token level, but the true sequence-level KL is a sum/expectation over tokens conditioned on prefixes. So in practice they are applying this estimator per token and averaging, which is the usual way KL regularization is handled in language-model RL.

### Why normalize rewards within each question group instead of across the whole batch in GRPO?
Question:
GRPO samples $G$ outputs for the same question, scores them, and then normalizes the rewards using the mean and standard deviation of that group only.

I think I understand that this creates a question-specific baseline, but I don't see why this is preferable to global normalization across the batch. If one question is inherently harder than another, group-wise normalization seems to erase that information completely.

Is the idea that raw reward magnitudes are not comparable across different prompts, so only relative ranking among outputs for the same prompt is trustworthy? Or is there another variance-reduction argument here?

I'm looking for intuition about why 'within-question relative quality' is the right signal for mathematical RL in this setup.
Answer:
In GRPO, the group-wise normalization is not just a convenience; it matches the structure of the supervision signal the paper is using.

The key point is that the reward model is trained on comparisons between multiple candidate answers to the same question, not on an absolute, globally calibrated score across unrelated questions. So a reward of, say, $0.8$ for one question and $0.8$ for another does not necessarily mean the same thing in any meaningful sense. The model is really telling you, “among these $G$ attempts at this one prompt, which ones are better?” That is why the paper says the group-relative formulation “aligns well with the comparative nature of reward models.”

If you normalized across the whole batch, you would mix together outputs from different questions whose reward scales may differ for reasons unrelated to policy quality. Some questions are genuinely easier, some are harder, and some reward-model scores may be systematically higher or lower depending on the prompt. A global mean and standard deviation would therefore introduce a noisy reference point: a response could look “good” relative to the batch simply because it came from an easy question, or “bad” because it came from a hard question, even if it was one of the best answers for its own prompt. That would blur the learning signal.

Within-question normalization solves this by turning the reward into a relative advantage inside each prompt’s candidate set:
$$
\widetilde{r}_i=\frac{r_i-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf r)}.
$$
So the policy is reinforced for responses that are better than the other samples for the same question, and penalized for worse ones. Intuitively, this gives you a “which of these completions should I prefer?” signal, rather than “how good was this completion in some absolute sense?” For mathematical reasoning, where the same problem can have multiple plausible but not equally correct derivations, that relative signal is especially useful.

There is also a variance-reduction angle, which is exactly why baselines are used in policy-gradient methods. Subtracting the group mean removes the average difficulty level of that question, so the gradient focuses on differences among candidate outputs rather than on question-specific reward offsets. In that sense, the group baseline is acting like a per-question control variate. The additional division by the group standard deviation makes the scale of the advantage more comparable across questions, so very high-variance reward groups do not dominate training just because their raw scores are spread out more.

So the short answer is: yes, the paper is assuming that raw reward magnitudes are not meaningfully comparable across different prompts, and GRPO intentionally learns from within-question relative quality. That is both statistically cleaner and better matched to how the reward model is constructed.

### How should I interpret the claim that RL improves Maj@$K$ but not Pass@$K$?
Question:
In the discussion section, the paper says reinforcement learning improves Maj@$K$ but not Pass@$K$, and they interpret this as making the output distribution more robust rather than improving the model's fundamental capability.

I am not fully comfortable with that interpretation. My understanding is:

- Pass@$K$ asks whether at least one of $K$ samples is correct.
- Maj@$K$ asks whether majority voting over $K$ samples gives the correct answer.

If Pass@$K$ stays about the same but Maj@$K$ improves, does that really mean the set of reachable correct solutions is unchanged, while the probability mass shifts toward correct reasoning traces? If so, is that evidence that RL is mainly doing distribution reshaping rather than discovering new capabilities?

I'd like a more precise explanation of what these two metrics diagnose.
Answer:
A good way to read the paper’s claim is that $Pass@K$ and $Maj@K$ are probing two different parts of the model’s output distribution.

$Pass@K$ is a “coverage” metric: if you draw $K$ samples, did you get at least one correct answer anywhere in that sample set? So it is sensitive to whether the model ever puts nontrivial probability mass on a correct solution at all. If $Pass@K$ does not change much after RL, that suggests RL is not dramatically expanding the set of prompts for which a correct solution becomes reachable under the same decoding scheme.

$Maj@K$, by contrast, is a “dominance” metric: among the $K$ sampled answers, does the majority vote land on the correct answer? This depends not just on whether correct solutions appear, but on whether they appear often enough to outvote incorrect ones. So $Maj@K$ improves when RL shifts probability mass toward the correct reasoning trace and away from competing wrong traces, even if the chance of seeing at least one correct sample stays about the same.

That distinction is exactly why the authors interpret their result as distribution reshaping rather than a fundamental capability gain. In the paper’s own wording, RL “enhances the model’s overall performance by rendering the output distribution more robust,” and “it seems that the improvement is attributed to boosting the correct response from $TopK$ rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities.” In other words, the model already knows how to solve many of these problems in some parts of its support, but RL makes those correct trajectories more likely to be sampled and more likely to win the vote.

So your intuition is basically right, with one caveat. If $Pass@K$ stays flat while $Maj@K$ improves, the strongest conclusion is not “RL found entirely new reasoning abilities.” It is “RL reweighted existing behaviors so that correct reasoning traces are sampled more reliably and are less often drowned out by plausible but wrong traces.” That is a form of robustness, calibration, or distribution sharpening. It is not evidence that the hypothesis space of solutions changed in a deep sense; rather, it suggests the model’s pre-existing correct solutions became more accessible under sampling.

Of course, this does not prove there was absolutely zero capability gain. $Pass@K$ is only measured for a particular $K$ and a particular decoding setup, so some finer-grained gains could be hidden. But within the paper’s own experimental framework, the clean interpretation is that RL mostly improves the frequency and stability of correct outputs, not the underlying breadth of solvable problems.

### In the unified training paradigm, in what sense are DPO and RFT 'simplified RL' methods?
Question:
The paper proposes a unified paradigm where the gradient is written as

$\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q,o,t,\pi_{rf}) \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(o_t \mid q,o_{<t}) \right],$

and then says methods such as RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO can all be understood in a common framework, with some of them being direct or simplified RL.

This sounds interesting, but I'm confused about what is being unified and what is being abstracted away. In particular, DPO is usually presented as a preference-optimization objective without explicit rollout-based RL. So in what precise sense is DPO being treated as an RL-like method here? Is it because its gradient can also be written as log-probability weighting by some implicit reward-derived coefficient?

I'm looking for a conceptual explanation of this unification, not just the claim that the gradients can be written in a similar algebraic form.
Answer:
In the paper’s usage, “simplified RL” does not mean that DPO or RFT literally run a full actor-critic loop with an environment, value function, and explicit return estimation. It means something narrower: they fit into the same *policy-gradient-shaped* template as RL methods, where training updates are always of the form “increase the log-probability of some sampled output tokens, weighted by a coefficient derived from a reward or preference signal.”

That is exactly what the unified equation is abstracting. The paper writes the gradient as

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q,o,t,\pi_{rf})\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})\right],
$$

and then says the differences between SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO are mainly in three things: what data $\mathcal{D}$ they train on, what reward signal or reference signal $\pi_{rf}$ they use, and how they turn that signal into the coefficient $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$.

So the unification is not about claiming that all these methods are identical as algorithms. It is about observing that, at the level of the gradient update, they all push token probabilities up or down according to some scalar signal.

For RFT, this is the easiest to see. The “reward” is effectively just a correctness filter on sampled outputs. If an output is judged good, it gets reinforced; if not, it is discarded. In the paper’s framework, that means $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ is essentially a simple binary or rule-based coefficient. So RFT is “simplified RL” because it uses an RL-like reinforce-good-samples / suppress-bad-samples update, but without learning a critic or doing fine-grained credit assignment.

DPO is a bit more subtle, but the paper treats it the same way for the same reason: its loss still induces a gradient that increases the log-probability of preferred responses and decreases that of dispreferred ones. Although DPO is usually presented as preference optimization rather than rollout-based RL, the paper’s viewpoint is that preference pairs are just another kind of reward signal, and the resulting update can be interpreted as a policy-gradient-style update with an implicit reward-derived coefficient. In other words, DPO does not need explicit sampled trajectories, but it still behaves like RL in that it is optimizing a policy against a preference/reward signal while staying anchored to a reference model. That is why the paper groups it with RL-like methods.

The key distinction is that PPO and GRPO are “fuller” RL in this framing because they actually sample outputs from the current policy and use reward models to compute advantages, whereas DPO and RFT are more static or offline. But the paper’s point is that all of them share the same core mechanism: they are different ways of constructing the coefficient multiplying $\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}$. That is what makes DPO and RFT “simplified RL” rather than ordinary supervised learning.

So your intuition is right: the unification is grounded in the fact that even DPO can be seen as implicitly reward-weighted log-likelihood optimization. The paper is abstracting away the procedural differences — online vs. offline, actor-critic vs. no critic, sampled returns vs. preference pairs — and keeping only the common gradient form.

### Why does online sampling help more than offline sampling later in training?
Question:
The paper compares offline methods like RFT and DPO, where training data is sampled from the initial SFT model, with online methods like Online RFT and GRPO, where data is sampled from the current policy during training. They say online training becomes more advantageous in later stages.

I think the basic intuition is that the current policy drifts away from the initial SFT model, so old samples become less relevant. But I am unsure how to think about this formally. Is the issue mainly distribution shift, where the optimization target depends on regions of output space that the initial SFT model rarely visits? Or is it that online sampling keeps generating 'hard negatives' and near-boundary examples that are most informative for improvement?

I'd appreciate an explanation of why online exploration should dominate offline data once the policy starts to change significantly.
Answer:
In the paper’s framing, the main reason online sampling becomes better later in training is indeed distribution shift, but it is a very specific kind of shift: the policy is changing, so the questions are no longer “where should I improve according to the initial SFT model?” but “where is the current policy still making mistakes, or where does it need finer discrimination?”. Offline data sampled once from the SFT model becomes stale because it reflects the SFT model’s output distribution, not the current policy’s.

Formally, both RFT and DPO in the paper use outputs sampled from the initial SFT policy, while Online RFT and GRPO sample from the real-time policy. As training progresses, the current policy and the initial SFT model diverge. In the early stage, they are still close, so the offline samples are not too different from what the current policy would generate anyway. That is why the paper says Online RFT is “comparable to RFT in the early stage.” But later on, the current policy occupies a different region of output space, so samples from the original SFT model stop being representative. At that point, offline training is trying to optimize on a frozen view of the model’s old behavior, which is less useful for improving the current model.

Intuitively, online sampling keeps the training distribution synchronized with the learner. If the model improves, the “interesting” examples move with it. This is especially important in RL-style training, because the useful learning signal often comes from borderline cases: outputs that are near the decision boundary between correct and incorrect reasoning, or between a better and worse solution. Online sampling naturally keeps producing these harder, more informative samples as the policy changes. So your “hard negatives / near-boundary examples” intuition is also right, but it is really a consequence of the same phenomenon: the current policy defines what is hard right now, and online sampling tracks that moving frontier.

The paper’s empirical observation is consistent with this. They report that Online RFT matches RFT early on but gains an advantage later, and they explicitly interpret that as the actor and SFT model becoming increasingly different over time. So the answer is not that offline data is useless; it is that offline data is best when the policy is still close to the source model, while online data becomes more valuable once learning has moved the policy away from that source. In RL terms, the training distribution should follow the policy distribution, otherwise the update signal becomes increasingly off-policy and less aligned with the model’s current errors.

### If RL only used GSM8K and MATH chain-of-thought data, why did out-of-domain benchmarks also improve?
Question:
DeepSeekMath-RL is trained only on a subset of instruction-tuning data: chain-of-thought-format questions from GSM8K and MATH, around 144K questions. Yet the paper reports improvements not only on those in-domain tasks but also on out-of-domain mathematical benchmarks such as CMATH.

I'm not sure how to interpret this. Does it suggest the reward model and policy updates are teaching generally useful reasoning habits, such as consistency and better allocation of probability mass over good traces? Or could it simply mean those benchmarks are still close enough in style that they benefit from the same distribution reshaping?

What is the best way to think about this kind of transfer, especially given the paper's later claim that RL may not improve fundamental capability as measured by Pass@$K$?
Answer:
The safest reading of the paper is that the RL stage is not learning “GSM8K-only tricks,” but rather reshaping the model’s answer distribution in a way that transfers to other math benchmarks that share the same underlying reasoning structure.

Two details from the paper matter here. First, the RL data are only chain-of-thought questions from GSM8K and MATH, but the model being updated is already DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, which has been trained on a much broader math instruction set and has strong pre-existing general mathematical competence. So RL is not starting from scratch; it is refining an already general math model. Second, the authors explicitly say in the discussion that RL seems to “enhance the model’s overall performance by rendering the output distribution more robust,” and that the gain is “attributed to boosting the correct response from TopK rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities.” That is their own interpretation.

So the improvement on CMATH should not be read as evidence that RL learned a new abstract reasoning algorithm from only GSM8K and MATH. A more plausible interpretation is that RL shifted probability mass toward better reasoning traces, better final answers, and more robust trajectories that are useful across similar math tasks. CMATH is out-of-domain in the narrow sense that it was not in the RL training set, but it is still very much in the same broad domain: mathematical word problems with step-by-step reasoning. If a policy gets better at producing coherent multi-step solutions, avoiding brittle mistakes, and selecting more correct completions among many sampled traces, that often transfers to nearby benchmarks even without direct exposure.

This also fits the paper’s Pass@$K$ versus Maj@$K$ analysis. Their claim is essentially that RL improves the ranking or concentration of good solutions, not necessarily the set of solutions the model is capable of generating at all. In other words, the model may not know fundamentally more, but it becomes better at putting higher probability on the right reasoning path and suppressing weaker ones. That kind of distributional sharpening naturally helps both in-domain and nearby out-of-domain tasks.

So the best way to think about the CMATH gain is: it is evidence of transfer through shared reasoning format and improved calibration/selection over solution traces, not strong evidence that RL discovered a domain-general “reasoning skill” independent of the training distribution. The paper itself leans toward the former, not the latter.
