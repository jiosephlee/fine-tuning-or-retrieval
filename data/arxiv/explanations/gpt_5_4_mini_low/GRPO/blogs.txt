\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# How DeepSeekMath Turned Web Data Into a Math Powerhouse

Mathematical reasoning is one of the hardest things to teach a language model. It requires not just recognizing patterns in text, but following structured steps, keeping track of symbols, and staying consistent across multiple transformations. In the DeepSeekMath paper, the striking result is not just that a 7B model performs well on math benchmarks. It is that much of that performance comes from a carefully built dataset mined from the web.

The central idea is simple: if you can find enough high-quality math content on the public web, and filter it well, you may not need an enormous model to get strong mathematical reasoning.

## A 120B-token math corpus from Common Crawl

The researchers built the DeepSeekMath Corpus by mining Common Crawl, a massive snapshot of web pages, and turning it into a large math-focused pre-training set. The final corpus contains 120B tokens and 35.5M mathematical web pages.

This did not happen in one pass. Instead, the paper describes an iterative selection pipeline:

1. Start with a seed corpus.
2. Train a fastText classifier to recognize math-like web pages.
3. Use that classifier to retrieve more candidates from Common Crawl.
4. Manually inspect and refine the results.
5. Add newly discovered math-heavy domains back into the seed set.
6. Retrain the classifier and repeat.

The initial seed was OpenWebMath, which served as a positive example set. The classifier was trained against randomly sampled non-math Common Crawl pages as negatives. After the first retrieval round, the team looked for math-related domains that had a high fraction of collected pages, manually annotated relevant URLs within those domains, and used those as new positive examples. After four iterations, they stopped because the process had mostly saturated.

The key point is that the dataset was not built by blindly taking more web text. It was built by repeatedly refining a math detector to pull in more relevant pages.

## Quality mattered more than sheer parameter count

One of the paper’s strongest claims is that training data quality can matter as much as, or more than, model size for mathematical reasoning.

To test the corpus, the authors trained a 1.3B model on different math corpora for 150B tokens. The DeepSeekMath Corpus outperformed other math datasets, including:

- MathPile, which is relatively small and heavily based on arXiv
- OpenWebMath, an earlier Common Crawl math corpus
- Proof-Pile-2, which mixes OpenWebMath, code, and arXiv

The result was not just better benchmark scores. The learning curves also suggested that DeepSeekMath’s corpus was both larger and higher quality than the alternatives. In other words, the model kept improving for longer instead of quickly hitting a plateau.

This matters because it challenges a common assumption: that math performance mostly comes from scaling model size. Here, a 7B model trained on better data could compete with systems that were much larger.

## Why a code model was a better starting point

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B was not trained from a general language model. It was initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B.

That choice turned out to matter. The paper reports that starting from a code-trained model worked better than starting from a general-purpose LLM. This is consistent with the idea that code and math share useful structure: both involve precise syntax, stepwise transformation, and careful execution of rules.

The model was then trained on a mixture of:

- 56% DeepSeekMath Corpus
- 4% AlgebraicStack
- 10% arXiv
- 20% GitHub code
- 10% general natural language from Common Crawl in English and Chinese

This mixed setup helped preserve coding ability while improving math reasoning.

The resulting DeepSeekMath-Base 7B was strong not only on math benchmarks, but also on general reasoning benchmarks like MMLU and BBH. So math training here was not a narrow specialization; it improved broader reasoning as well.

## Multilingual math data helped Chinese benchmarks

A notable design choice in the corpus was that it was not English-only. The dataset included multilingual mathematical content, especially English and Chinese.

That choice paid off. The paper reports that DeepSeekMath performed particularly well on Chinese math benchmarks, likely because the training data included high-quality non-English pages rather than relying only on English web text. This is an important reminder that “math web data” is not a single-language resource. There is useful mathematical content across languages, and models can benefit from it.

## Why arXiv was less useful than expected

One of the more surprising findings in the paper is that arXiv papers did not noticeably improve mathematical reasoning in their experiments.

This goes against a common practice in math model training, where arXiv is often treated as a valuable source of mathematical text. The authors tested arXiv-heavy corpora such as MathPile and ArXiv-RedPajama, and found that training on arXiv alone produced little improvement, and in some cases even hurt performance on math benchmarks.

That does not mean arXiv is useless in general. The paper is careful to note limitations: it does not test every possible math task, and it does not study all ways arXiv might be combined with other data. But within the benchmarks they used, carefully filtered web math content was more effective than a more traditional arXiv-centered approach.

## From pretraining to instruction tuning

After pretraining, the researchers instruction-tuned DeepSeekMath-Base on 776K math examples in both English and Chinese. These examples included chain-of-thought solutions, program-of-thought solutions, and tool-integrated reasoning traces.

This produced DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, which was already very strong for its size. On the competition-level MATH benchmark, it surpassed all open-source models and was competitive with much larger proprietary systems, though it still lagged behind the strongest closed models like GPT-4 and Gemini Ultra.

The model could also solve math problems with Python-based tool use, which pushed performance even higher.

## GRPO: reinforcement learning without a critic model

The final step was reinforcement learning, using a new method called Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO.

GRPO is designed to improve mathematical reasoning while reducing the memory burden of standard PPO. PPO usually relies on a separate critic model to estimate advantages. GRPO removes that critic and instead uses the relative scores of multiple sampled answers to the same question as its baseline.

In practice, the model samples a group of outputs for each question, scores them, and then updates the policy based on how each output compares to the group average. This makes training more efficient and better aligned with how reward models are usually trained, since they already compare answers to the same prompt.

Using only a subset of the instruction-tuning data, GRPO improved DeepSeekMath-Instruct from 82.9% to 88.2% on GSM8K and from 46.8% to 51.7% on MATH. That is a substantial gain from a relatively focused RL setup.

## What the paper shows

DeepSeekMath makes a strong case for a few ideas that matter beyond this one model:

- Web data can contain high-quality mathematical content if you know how to filter it.
- Iterative data mining can uncover much more useful training data than a one-shot crawl.
- Code pretraining can help math reasoning.
- Multilingual data matters.
- Better data can let a 7B model compete with much larger systems.
- Traditional sources like arXiv are not automatically better than carefully selected web pages.

The broader lesson is that mathematical reasoning is not just a scaling problem. It is also a data problem. DeepSeekMath shows that if you build the right corpus and train on it carefully, a relatively small open model can get much closer to frontier-level math performance than many people might expect.



# Why a 7B Open Model Is Suddenly So Good at Solving Math Problems

Large language models are often judged by how fluently they write, but math is a much tougher test. It requires structured reasoning, careful step-by-step work, and consistency across many tokens. In that setting, DeepSeekMath is notable for a simple reason: a 7B-parameter open model reaches competition-level math performance that used to be associated with much larger or closed systems.

The paper’s headline result is that DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reaches 51.7% on the MATH benchmark without external tools or voting tricks. With self-consistency over 64 samples, it reaches 60.9%. That puts it in the range of the best proprietary systems reported in the paper, while still being a publicly available open model.

## What DeepSeekMath Is

DeepSeekMath starts from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, not from a general-purpose language model. The authors continue pre-training it on a large math-focused corpus built from Common Crawl, plus some natural language and code data. That corpus contains 120B math-related tokens, which is large by the standards of math training datasets.

This matters because the model is not relying only on post-training instruction tuning. The authors argue that the base model itself becomes much stronger at math reasoning through high-quality pre-training data, and then improves further with supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning.

## What the Benchmarks Actually Measure

Two of the most important benchmarks in the paper are GSM8K and MATH.

GSM8K is a grade-school math benchmark. The problems are short, but they still require multi-step reasoning, arithmetic, and staying on track through a solution.

MATH is much harder. It contains competition-level problems spanning areas like algebra, geometry, number theory, calculus, and probability. The questions are designed to test more advanced reasoning, not just computation.

So when a model scores well on GSM8K, that tells you it can handle relatively structured word problems. When it does well on MATH, that is a much stronger signal: it can often work through olympiad-style or contest-style problems that are significantly more demanding.

The paper also evaluates on Chinese math benchmarks such as CMATH, MGSM-zh, and Gaokao-style tasks, which is important because the training corpus is multilingual rather than English-only.

## How It Compares to GPT-4, Gemini, and Open Models

The comparison in the paper is what makes the result stand out.

On MATH, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reaches 51.7% without tool use. The authors say this approaches the performance level of Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4 on that benchmark. They also note that self-consistency with 64 samples pushes performance to 60.9%.

Just as important, the model outperforms open-source competitors by a wide margin. The paper reports that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B beats existing open-source base models such as Mistral 7B and Llemma-34B on the math benchmarks they test. It also surpasses Minerva 540B on some evaluations, which is striking because Minerva is 77 times larger.

DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B is also strong after supervised fine-tuning. Under settings where tool use is not allowed, it outperforms all open-source models and many proprietary ones on MATH. When tool use is allowed, it approaches 60% on MATH and surpasses existing open-source models.

The larger message is not just that the model is good for its size. It is that a carefully trained 7B open model can compete with systems that are much larger, and in some cases get surprisingly close to closed models.

## Why This Is a Big Deal for Open AI

There are two reasons this result matters.

First, it shows that parameter count is not the whole story. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is much smaller than models like Minerva 540B, yet it performs competitively because of the quality and scale of the math-focused data. The paper’s takeaway is clear: a smaller model trained on the right data can be very strong at mathematical reasoning.

Second, it strengthens the case for open models. Closed models like GPT-4 and Gemini are powerful, but they are not publicly available. DeepSeekMath shows that the open ecosystem can close a meaningful part of the gap, at least on some reasoning tasks. That makes it more practical for researchers and developers to study, reproduce, and build on these capabilities.

## Where the Performance Comes From

The paper attributes the gains to two main ingredients: a carefully engineered data pipeline and a reinforcement learning method called Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO.

The data pipeline is a big part of the story. Instead of relying mainly on textbooks or arXiv papers, the authors mine Common Crawl for math-related pages. They use an iterative classifier-based process to find more and more useful math content, then manually refine the data selection. In the end, they build a 120B-token math corpus that is multilingual and, in their experiments, clearly better than several existing math corpora.

The other major ingredient is GRPO. This is a variant of PPO that avoids training a separate critic model. Instead, it uses the relative quality of multiple sampled answers to estimate the learning signal. In practice, that reduces memory use and still improves performance.

The paper shows that RL with GRPO boosts the instruction-tuned model further, even though the RL stage uses only a subset of the math instruction data. On GSM8K and MATH, performance improves substantially.

## A Few Practical Takeaways

The paper also reports some interesting lessons that are easy to miss if you only look at the headline score.

Code training helps. Starting from a code model appears to improve math reasoning, including program-aided reasoning.

ArXiv is less useful than many people might expect. The authors tested arXiv-heavy corpora and did not see clear gains on their benchmarks.

RL seems to improve the model’s ranking of correct answers more than it improves raw pass rate. In other words, it may make the model more reliable at putting the right solution near the top of its output distribution.

That last point is important because it suggests the gains are not just about making the model “smarter” in a vague sense. They are also about making its answers more robust and more likely to be correct when sampled.

## The Bottom Line

DeepSeekMath is a strong demonstration that open models can make serious progress on mathematical reasoning without relying on massive scale alone.

A 7B model reaching 51.7% on MATH is already impressive. Doing it with an open model, after training on web-mined math data and a lightweight RL method, is what makes the result noteworthy. It suggests that better data, better training recipes, and better optimization methods can move open AI systems much closer to the state of the art.

For anyone following reasoning models, the key lesson is simple: math capability is no longer reserved for the largest closed models. A smaller open model, trained carefully, can now get much closer than many would have expected.



# The Secret Sauce Behind DeepSeekMath: A New Reinforcement Learning Trick Called GRPO

DeepSeekMath is not just a stronger math model because it saw more math text. A big part of its jump in reasoning comes from a reinforcement learning method the authors call **Group Relative Policy Optimization**, or **GRPO**.

At a high level, GRPO is a simpler and more memory-efficient alternative to PPO, the reinforcement learning method often used to fine-tune language models. The key idea is surprisingly intuitive: instead of judging one answer in isolation, the model generates a **group of answers to the same question** and learns by **comparing them against each other**. That relative comparison turns out to be very effective for mathematical reasoning.

## Why Reinforcement Learning Matters for Math

Supervised fine-tuning can teach a model how math solutions are usually written, but it does not always make the model better at choosing the right reasoning path. Reinforcement learning adds another layer: the model generates answers, gets feedback, and adjusts its behavior to produce better outputs over time.

In DeepSeekMath, the authors first build a strong instruction-tuned model, then apply reinforcement learning on top. The result is DeepSeekMath-RL 7B, which reaches **88.2\% on GSM8K** and **51.7\% on MATH** using chain-of-thought reasoning, improving over the already strong instruction-tuned model.

The notable part is that this reinforcement learning stage uses only a subset of the instruction-tuning data, specifically GSM8K and MATH-style chain-of-thought questions, yet still produces gains on both in-domain and out-of-domain math benchmarks.

## The Problem With PPO

GRPO is introduced as a variant of **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**. PPO is widely used in RL fine-tuning, but it has a major downside for large language models: it usually needs a separate **value function model** to estimate how good a token or response is compared to what was expected.

That extra model is expensive. It adds both memory and compute overhead, which becomes painful when the policy model is already large.

PPO also typically relies on token-level reward shaping with a KL penalty against a reference model. In practice, this means the training setup gets more complicated, especially when the reward signal is sparse, as it often is in math problems where only the final answer may be clearly scored.

DeepSeekMath’s GRPO is designed to avoid that extra burden.

## What GRPO Changes

GRPO removes the critic model entirely. Instead of training a separate value network, it estimates the baseline from the **group of sampled outputs** for the same question.

Here is the intuition:

1. Take one math problem.
2. Sample several answers from the current model.
3. Score each answer.
4. Compare the scores within that group.
5. Push the model toward the better answers and away from the worse ones.

So rather than asking, “Was this answer good in an absolute sense?” GRPO asks, “Was this answer better or worse than the other answers I sampled for the same problem?”

This relative scoring matters because reward models are often trained on comparisons anyway. In other words, GRPO matches the way reward supervision is naturally constructed.

## Why Comparing Answers Helps

Math reasoning is often less about producing one perfect solution immediately and more about searching through candidate steps. If the model can generate several possible solutions to the same problem, then ranking them against each other gives a strong learning signal.

This is especially useful because mathematical outputs are structured. A solution can be:

- clearly wrong,
- partially correct,
- or correct but inefficient.

By comparing multiple outputs from the same prompt, GRPO can tell the model which reasoning style is more promising. That makes it easier to reinforce useful patterns and suppress bad ones.

The paper’s results suggest that this kind of **group-based relative feedback** is effective not just for final-answer accuracy, but also for improving the model’s overall reasoning behavior.

## How GRPO Uses Rewards

The paper describes two GRPO variants:

- **Outcome supervision**, where each full response gets one reward at the end.
- **Process supervision**, where intermediate reasoning steps also get rewards.

In outcome supervision, the rewards for all sampled answers are normalized within the group, and each token in an output gets the same advantage signal.

In process supervision, the model gets step-level feedback, and the advantages are built from the rewards of later reasoning steps. This gives more fine-grained guidance for complex reasoning chains.

The important point is that GRPO does not need a separate value model to calculate these advantages. That is where the memory savings come from.

## Why GRPO Uses Less Memory Than PPO

PPO usually trains:

- a policy model, which generates answers,
- and a value model, which estimates expected reward.

GRPO trains only the policy model and uses the group of sampled outputs to form the baseline. That means one less large model to keep in memory and update during training.

For large language models, this is a meaningful simplification. The paper emphasizes that GRPO **significantly reduces training resources** compared to PPO, while still improving performance.

So the tradeoff is favorable:

- less memory overhead,
- simpler training,
- and better math reasoning.

## What the Results Show

GRPO is not just a theoretical cleanup of PPO. It actually improves performance.

Starting from DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, GRPO increases:

- **GSM8K** from **82.9\% to 88.2\%**
- **MATH** from **46.8\% to 51.7\%**

It also improves several out-of-domain mathematical tasks, including **CMATH**, where the score rises from **84.6\% to 88.8\%**.

That is an important result: the gains are not limited to the exact data used during reinforcement learning. The model becomes more robust more broadly across math benchmarks.

## Why This Might Work So Well

The paper offers a useful interpretation of why reinforcement learning helps here. Their analysis suggests that RL does not necessarily improve the model’s basic underlying abilities in the same way pretraining does. Instead, it seems to make the model’s output distribution more robust, so the correct answer appears more often among the top candidates.

They show this by comparing **Pass@K** and **Maj@K** behavior. The RL model improves Maj@K, meaning the correct answer becomes more likely to show up among multiple samples, even if Pass@K does not change as much.

That fits the picture of GRPO as a method that helps the model **rank and prefer better reasoning paths**, rather than learning math from scratch.

## The Bigger Idea: Relative Judgement Beats Absolute Guessing

GRPO’s core insight is simple but powerful. For a math problem, it can be easier to learn from **relative comparisons** than from a single absolute label.

If a model samples several candidate solutions, some will be better than others. By learning which ones win within the group, the model gets a stronger training signal than it would from just one sampled answer.

This is a good fit for math because math reasoning often has many plausible-looking paths, but only a few are truly correct. Group-based comparison gives the model a way to tell them apart.

## Takeaway

GRPO is one of the paper’s most practical contributions. It keeps the spirit of PPO, but removes the value model and replaces it with a group-relative baseline. That makes training lighter, simpler, and better aligned with how reward comparison works in reasoning tasks.

In DeepSeekMath, that design choice pays off. The model gets a substantial boost in mathematical reasoning, while using less memory than standard PPO.

If you want the one-sentence summary: **GRPO helps a model learn math by making it compete against its own alternate answers.**



# Does Coding Make AI Better at Reasoning? DeepSeekMath Offers a Surprising Clue

One of the most interesting side findings in *DeepSeekMath* is not about math data at all. It is about code.

The authors found that starting from a code-trained model, specifically DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, worked better for math reasoning than starting from a general language model. And this was not just a small effect. After continued pre-training on math data, the resulting model improved on mathematical benchmarks, and the paper reports that code training helps both math solving with tools and math solving without tools.

That is a useful clue for understanding why programming data may help language models reason better.

## Why code might help with math

At a high level, code is structured thinking. It forces a model to learn:

- precise syntax,
- ordered steps,
- explicit variable relationships,
- constraints that must be respected,
- and the habit of breaking a problem into smaller parts.

Those properties look a lot like what mathematical reasoning needs. In math, the model cannot just produce something that sounds plausible. It has to keep track of quantities, rules, and intermediate steps. Code creates a training environment where that kind of discipline matters.

DeepSeekMath’s results suggest that this transfer is real. The paper reports that code training before math training improves the model’s ability to solve math problems both with and without tool use. In their experiments, code training alone already improved performance on program-aided math tasks, and subsequent math training improved it further.

## The tool-use connection

The tool-use result is especially interesting.

DeepSeekMath evaluates models in a few different ways. In one setup, the model solves problems using step-by-step reasoning in natural language. In another, it uses program-of-thought prompting, where the model writes Python code and the execution result becomes the answer.

The paper finds that code training is particularly helpful here. That makes sense: if a model has already learned to generate code, it may be better prepared to use Python as a reasoning tool during math problem solving. It is not just writing software. It is learning to externalize computation.

For example, a model trained on code may be more comfortable with the idea that a difficult problem can be turned into an executable procedure. That is exactly the mindset behind tool-augmented reasoning: convert part of the task into a form a calculator or interpreter can handle.

## More than just programming skill

What is notable is that the benefits were not limited to tool-based math.

The authors also report that code training improves mathematical reasoning without tool use. In other words, learning to write code seems to help the model become better at internal step-by-step reasoning as well.

This matters because it suggests that code training is not merely teaching the model how to produce programs. It may be shaping the model’s internal representation of structured problems. A programming corpus repeatedly exposes the model to patterns like:

- input-output transformations,
- conditional logic,
- loops and decomposition,
- and exact symbolic manipulation.

Those patterns overlap with the cognitive style needed for algebra, word problems, and multi-step reasoning.

## Why one-stage mixing is not always enough

The paper also adds an important nuance. When code tokens and math tokens were mixed together in one-stage training, the results were not as strong for math reasoning without tools. The authors suggest that a model of this size may not have enough capacity to absorb both types of data equally well at the same time.

That means the order of training matters. The strongest results came from a staged approach, where code training first built a structured reasoning base, and math training later specialized that ability toward mathematical tasks.

So the lesson is not simply “more code is always better.” It is that code can act as a useful foundation for reasoning, especially when followed by domain-specific math training.

## What this tells us about reasoning in language models

DeepSeekMath does not prove that code magically creates reasoning ability. But it does provide strong evidence that programming data is useful for more than software generation.

Code seems to help because it trains models on a domain where correctness, structure, and sequential execution are essential. Those same qualities matter in mathematical reasoning. If a model learns to handle code well, it may also become better at representing and manipulating abstract structure in general.

That makes programming data a kind of reasoning substrate. It may not teach math directly, but it teaches the model how to think in a more disciplined, stepwise way.

## The bigger picture

This finding fits a broader pattern in language model research: data quality and data type matter as much as model size. DeepSeekMath shows that a 7B model can become highly competitive on math benchmarks when it is trained on the right mixture of data, including a strong code component.

The paper’s main story is about math pre-training and reinforcement learning, but this code result is one of its most practical takeaways. If you want a model to reason better, training it on programming data may be a surprisingly effective place to start.

That does not mean code is a substitute for math data. DeepSeekMath still relies heavily on math-specific pre-training and instruction tuning. But code appears to give the model an important structural advantage before that specialization begins.

And that is the real clue: programming may not just make AI better at coding. It may make AI better at thinking.



# What DeepSeekMath Gets Right—and Where It Still Struggles

DeepSeekMath is a strong reminder that mathematical reasoning in language models is not just about scale. In this paper, the authors show that a 7B model can get surprisingly far when it is trained on carefully selected math-heavy web data and then further improved with reinforcement learning. The result is a model that reaches 51.7\% on the competition-level MATH benchmark without external tools or voting, and even higher with self-consistency. That is a major result for open models.

But the paper is just as interesting for what it does not solve. DeepSeekMath is much stronger at arithmetic, algebra, and symbolic-style reasoning than it is at geometry and theorem proving. That gap matters, because it points to the limits of today’s data pipelines and training methods, even when they are well engineered.

## A small model, trained the right way

DeepSeekMath starts from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B rather than a general-purpose LLM. The authors found that code-pretrained models are a better starting point for math than generic language models. That choice already hints at one of the paper’s core messages: mathematical reasoning seems to benefit from structured, program-like representations.

The model is then continually pretrained on 120B math-related tokens mined from Common Crawl, plus natural language and code. This is not a small filtered dataset pulled from textbooks alone. Instead, the authors build an iterative web-mining pipeline using a fastText classifier, starting from OpenWebMath as seed data and expanding the corpus over several rounds.

That pipeline matters because it shows that the open web still contains a lot of useful mathematical text if you know how to find it. The resulting corpus is multilingual, with both English and Chinese content, and the paper reports gains on Chinese math benchmarks as a result.

## Strong results on arithmetic and symbolic reasoning

The clearest success of DeepSeekMath is on benchmarks that reward step-by-step reasoning and careful computation. On GSM8K and MATH, the model performs strongly in both direct reasoning and tool-using settings. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B beats prior open-source base models, and DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B and DeepSeekMath-RL 7B push performance further.

The reinforcement learning stage is especially notable. Using Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO, the authors improve DeepSeekMath-Instruct with only a subset of English instruction-tuning data. The RL model reaches 88.2\% on GSM8K and 51.7\% on MATH with chain-of-thought reasoning, which is enough to surpass most open-source models and many proprietary ones.

These numbers matter because they show that the model is not just memorizing patterns. It can generate useful intermediate steps, adapt to tool use when allowed, and benefit from a training scheme that explicitly rewards better reasoning outputs.

## Why the training recipe works

One of the paper’s most practical contributions is the data story. The authors do not treat “math data” as a vague category. They construct a large corpus from Common Crawl using a multi-round selection process:

- start with a high-quality math seed corpus,
- train a classifier to retrieve similar web pages,
- manually expand the seed with more math-heavy domains,
- repeat until additional collection yields little new data.

This approach produces a 120B-token corpus, much larger than earlier math corpora the paper compares against. The authors also decontaminate benchmark text to reduce the risk of test leakage.

They then show that the corpus is not only large, but useful. A 1.3B model trained on it performs well on math benchmarks, and the authors argue that the quality of the data is a key reason the smaller model can compete with much larger systems.

Another important finding is that code training helps math. In their experiments, code-first training improves mathematical reasoning both with and without tools. That does not fully answer whether code training improves general reasoning, but it does provide strong evidence in the context of math.

## Where DeepSeekMath still falls short

The paper is careful not to overclaim. DeepSeekMath is excellent on many quantitative reasoning tasks, but it is not uniformly strong across all areas of mathematics.

The most obvious weakness is geometry. The authors say that the model is relatively weaker on geometry problems, and in their dry run it struggles with topics involving triangles and ellipses. That suggests the training data may not cover some geometric reasoning patterns well enough, or that the model has not learned the spatial and diagrammatic structure geometry often requires.

Theorem proving is another weaker area. DeepSeekMath-Base does show strong few-shot autoformalization performance on miniF2F with Isabelle, but this is not the same as being a reliable theorem prover. Formal mathematics is a much stricter setting than natural-language problem solving, and the paper does not claim to have solved that problem.

This is an important distinction. A model can perform well on benchmark-style math questions while still being far from dependable on formal proof tasks, where every step must be correct and machine-checkable.

## What the gaps tell us

The weaknesses in DeepSeekMath are not just missing points on a leaderboard. They reveal something about the current state of AI math systems.

First, data selection still matters enormously. The paper shows that public web data can be useful for math, but the retrieval process is biased toward the kinds of math that are common and easy to surface. That may explain why arithmetic and symbolic reasoning improve more than geometry or proof-heavy tasks.

Second, reinforcement learning improves output quality, but it may mostly sharpen the model’s ability to rank correct answers higher rather than build entirely new reasoning skills. The authors support this interpretation by showing that RL improves Maj@K more than Pass@K. In other words, the model becomes better at putting a correct answer near the top of its sampled outputs.

Third, scale alone is not the answer. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B compares favorably with Minerva 540B on several benchmarks, which is impressive. But the remaining weaknesses suggest that simply making the model bigger will not automatically solve the harder parts of math.

## The bigger picture

DeepSeekMath is a genuine step forward for open mathematical reasoning. It shows that a carefully built data pipeline, a code-friendly base model, and efficient reinforcement learning can produce a model that is far stronger than typical open-source systems of similar size.

At the same time, the paper is refreshingly clear about what is still missing. Geometry and theorem proving remain hard. Few-shot behavior is still weaker than GPT-4. And the model’s success on benchmark math does not mean it can handle every kind of mathematical thinking.

That balance is what makes the paper useful. It is not just a story about progress; it is also a map of the remaining gaps. For future AI math systems, the challenge is no longer only to answer more questions correctly. It is to expand the kinds of reasoning they can do: symbolic manipulation, multi-step derivations, geometric structure, and formal proof. DeepSeekMath gets us closer, but it also makes the next set of problems very clear.
