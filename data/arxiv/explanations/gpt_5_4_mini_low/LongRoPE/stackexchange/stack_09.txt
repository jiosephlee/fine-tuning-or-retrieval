\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What is the relationship between non-uniform interpolation across dimensions and the relative-position property of RoPE?
Question:
One thing I thought I understood about RoPE is that it naturally encodes relative positions through rotations, which is part of why it works well in attention. LongRoPE then applies different scale factors $\lambda_i$ to different RoPE dimensions, and even uses a token threshold $\hat{n}$ so early positions are treated differently from later ones.

I am confused about what this does to RoPE's original structure.

- Does using per-dimension $\lambda_i$ preserve the relative-position behavior, just at altered frequencies?
- Does introducing the threshold $\hat{n}$ break translation equivariance, since the encoding rule now depends on absolute position being before or after $\hat{n}$?
- If some of RoPE's nice algebraic properties are broken, why does the model still benefit overall?

I would like a structural explanation rather than just "the experiments say it works."
Answer:
RoPE’s “relative position” behavior comes from the fact that the encoding at position $n$ is a rotation by angle $n\theta_i$ in each two-dimensional subspace. That means that when attention compares two tokens at positions $m$ and $n$, the dot product depends on their angle difference, i.e. on $m-n$, not on the absolute positions separately. In that sense, vanilla RoPE is translation-equivariant.

LongRoPE keeps the same basic mechanism, but it changes the angle schedule from a single global rule to a searched, non-uniform one. For dimensions with rescale factor $\lambda_i$, the angle becomes roughly $\frac{n}{\lambda_i \beta^i}$ instead of $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$. If the same rule is used for both tokens, then the relative-angle structure is still there: the difference between positions $m$ and $n$ in that dimension is just scaled by the same factor, so you still get a relative-position encoding, but at altered effective frequencies. So your first intuition is right: per-dimension $\lambda_i$ does not destroy the basic RoPE idea; it changes the “wavelength” of each dimension, and the paper explicitly motivates this as preserving the original RoPE information in a non-uniform way.

The threshold $\hat{n}$ is different. Once the encoding rule depends on whether $n<\hat{n}$ or $n\geq \hat{n}$, the scheme is no longer globally translation-equivariant, because shifting the whole sequence can move tokens across the threshold and change their encoding rule. So yes, the threshold breaks the exact symmetry RoPE had before. In the paper’s notation, positions below $\hat{n}$ use the original angle $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$, while later positions use $\frac{n}{\lambda_i \beta^i}$. That means the encoding is piecewise rather than uniform across the whole sequence.

The important point is that LongRoPE is not trying to preserve RoPE’s algebraic symmetries for their own sake; it is trying to preserve the parts of the positional signal that matter most for language modeling. The paper argues empirically that the earliest tokens receive especially large attention and are therefore disproportionately important, so leaving them un-interpolated helps retain short-range behavior. Likewise, different RoPE dimensions carry information at different frequencies, so interpolating them equally is wasteful. The search procedure is then used to find a compromise that minimizes perplexity rather than maintaining exact invariances.

So structurally, LongRoPE is best thought of as a controlled relaxation of RoPE’s clean symmetry: it preserves the relative-position mechanism within each region where the same rule applies, but it introduces non-uniformity across dimensions and across token positions to reduce information loss when extrapolating far beyond the pretrained context. That tradeoff is exactly why it can extend context so much while still keeping short-context performance reasonably intact.
