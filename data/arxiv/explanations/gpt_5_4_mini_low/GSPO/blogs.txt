# Why Today’s AI Training Breaks: The Big Idea Behind GSPO

Reinforcement learning has become one of the key tools for pushing large language models beyond basic next-token prediction. It is what helps models get better at hard tasks like competition math, programming, and long-form reasoning. But as models get larger and responses get longer, training can become unstable. In the worst cases, the model does not just stop improving — it collapses.

The paper on Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO, is about fixing that failure mode.

## The problem: token-level training can become noisy and unstable

Many modern RL methods for language models update the model using samples generated by an older version of the policy. That means the training data is already slightly “off-policy” by the time the optimizer sees it. To handle this, methods like PPO and GRPO use clipping to prevent updates from drifting too far.

The issue, according to the paper, is that GRPO applies importance weighting at the token level. In other words, it tries to correct the mismatch between old and new policies one token at a time.

That sounds reasonable, but the paper argues this is the wrong unit of correction for language generation. A single token probability is only one small piece of a full response, and using token-level importance weights does not properly play the role that importance sampling is supposed to play. Instead of correcting distribution mismatch, it injects high-variance noise into the gradients. As responses get longer, that noise accumulates. With clipping on top, the instability can become severe enough to cause model collapse.

The core claim is simple: when the reward is assigned to the whole response, the optimization should also happen at the level of the whole response.

## GSPO’s main idea: optimize whole responses, not individual tokens

GSPO changes the unit of optimization from tokens to complete sequences.

Instead of computing an importance ratio for each token, GSPO computes a single sequence-level importance ratio based on the likelihood of the entire response under the new policy versus the old policy. That ratio is length-normalized, so responses of different lengths can be compared more consistently.

Then, rather than clipping each token independently, GSPO clips the whole response as one unit.

This matters because the reward in these RL setups is already sequence-level. A model gets scored on the quality of the full answer, not on whether each token was individually good. GSPO aligns the optimization with that reality.

In the paper’s framing, this is the key design principle: the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward.

## Why this helps training stability

The sequence-level view changes the gradient behavior in an important way.

Under GRPO, different tokens in the same response can receive different effective weights, which can create unpredictable training dynamics over time. Under GSPO, all tokens in a response are treated equally, since the response-level importance ratio applies uniformly.

That has two practical benefits:

First, it reduces instability caused by token-level noise. Instead of making many fine-grained corrections that may not be reliable, GSPO makes one correction per response.

Second, it makes clipping more meaningful. If a sampled response is too far from the current policy, GSPO can exclude it at the response level rather than trying to patch up individual tokens that may have very different behavior.

The paper reports that this leads to more stable training, better efficiency, and improved performance compared with GRPO.

## A particularly important benefit for Mixture-of-Experts models

The paper places a lot of emphasis on Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, models. These models are powerful, but their sparse activation patterns can make RL training even more fragile. After each update, the experts activated for the same response can change significantly, which makes token-level importance ratios even less reliable.

To work around that problem, previous training setups used a strategy called Routing Replay, which forced the old and new policies to reuse the same routing decisions. That helped GRPO converge, but it added extra complexity and overhead.

GSPO avoids that workaround. Because it operates at the sequence level, it is much less sensitive to expert-activation volatility. The paper argues that this lets MoE RL training proceed stably without Routing Replay.

## Why this is interesting beyond the algorithm itself

GSPO is not just a new loss function. It also points to a cleaner way to think about RL infrastructure for language models.

Because GSPO relies on sequence-level likelihood rather than token-level likelihood, it is more tolerant of small precision differences between training and inference systems. The paper suggests this could make it easier to use likelihoods produced by inference engines directly, instead of recomputing them with the training engine.

So the benefit is not only better optimization. It is also a simpler system design.

## The takeaway

The big idea behind GSPO is straightforward: if the reward is for the whole response, then the correction and optimization should also happen for the whole response.

That one shift from token-level to sequence-level optimization is what the paper argues makes GSPO more stable than GRPO. In the authors’ experiments, it improved training stability, efficiency, and benchmark performance, and it was important enough to help power the latest Qwen3 models.

For large language models, especially MoE models and long-response tasks, that is a practical lesson with broad implications: sometimes the way to make RL work better is not to add more machinery, but to align the math with the thing you actually care about.



# From Tokens to Full Answers: A Beginner-Friendly Guide to Group Sequence Policy Optimization

Large language models are often trained and improved one response at a time. In reinforcement learning for language models, the model generates an answer, a verifier scores that answer, and the model is updated to make better answers more likely. That sounds simple, but the details matter a lot.

The new paper on **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** argues that one of the biggest mistakes in earlier methods is *where* they apply the learning signal. Instead of optimizing each token separately, GSPO optimizes the **entire response as a sequence**. That turns out to match the way rewards are actually assigned in tasks like math and coding, where the whole answer is judged, not each token in isolation.

## Why token-level optimization is a mismatch

In standard language modeling, a response is produced token by token. So it is natural to think about training token by token too. Methods like GRPO do exactly that: they compute an importance ratio for each token and apply clipping at the token level.

The problem is that the reward usually does **not** come token by token. For many RL training setups, a verifier gives a score to the complete response \(y\), not to each intermediate token \(y_t\). The paper argues that this creates a mismatch:

- the **reward** is sequence-level,
- but the **optimization** is token-level.

GSPO is built around a simple principle: the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward.

## The sequence-level idea

GSPO changes the importance ratio from token likelihood to **sequence likelihood**. For a response \(y\) to a query \(x\), the model likelihood is

\[
\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
\]

Instead of comparing the current and old policies token by token, GSPO compares them at the response level:

\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.
\]

That exponent of \(1/|y_i|\) is a length normalization term. It helps keep the ratio in a stable numeric range and reduces variance across responses of different lengths.

So the model is no longer asking, “How much should I trust this token?” It is asking, “How much should I trust this entire answer?”

## How GSPO uses rewards

GSPO is still group-based. For each query \(x\), it samples a group of responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\). Each response gets a reward \(r(x, y_i)\), and the rewards are normalized within the group to form advantages:

\[
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}.
\]

Then GSPO optimizes responses using the sequence-level ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) and clips that ratio just like PPO-style methods do:

\[
\min\left(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\right).
\]

The important difference is that clipping is applied to the **whole response**, not to each token.

## Why this is a better fit for language tasks

In tasks like coding and math, a response is usually judged as a whole. A code solution either passes tests or it does not. A mathematical derivation either reaches the right answer or it does not. In these settings, token-level correction is a somewhat awkward proxy.

GSPO better matches this evaluation style because it treats a response as a single unit of decision-making. If a sampled answer is good, the full response is reinforced. If it is bad, the full response is downweighted. That aligns optimization with the actual training signal.

This also helps explain one of the paper’s key observations: sequence-level optimization is more stable than token-level optimization when response lengths get large. Token-level importance ratios can become noisy and compound over long generations. GSPO avoids that by using one ratio for the whole sequence.

## What changes in the gradient

The paper shows that GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally when computing the gradient. That is a subtle but important point. In GRPO, each token can receive its own importance weight, which can vary unpredictably. In GSPO, every token in the sampled response shares the same sequence-level weight.

So although the model still updates token log-likelihoods internally, the update signal is controlled at the response level. That makes the learning dynamics more consistent with the reward structure.

## A practical consequence: simpler and more stable training

The paper reports that GSPO improves training stability and efficiency compared to GRPO, especially for long responses and large Mixture-of-Experts models. It also removes the need for a workaround called Routing Replay in MoE RL training.

But the central innovation is conceptually simple: **stop pretending that every token is independently judged when the reward is actually assigned to the whole answer**.

GSPO makes that idea explicit by using sequence likelihood as the importance ratio and applying sequence-level clipping and optimization. That small shift in perspective turns out to matter a lot for scaling RL in language models.

## Takeaway

GSPO’s main contribution is not a new reward model or a new verifier. It is a better match between the structure of the reward and the structure of the optimization.

If the model is being judged on complete answers, then the policy update should also be based on complete answers. GSPO does exactly that, and the paper shows that this sequence-level approach is more stable, more efficient, and better suited to the kinds of reasoning tasks where large language models are now being trained.



# GSPO vs. GRPO: What Changed, and Why It Matters for Smarter LLMs

Reinforcement learning has become one of the main ways to push large language models beyond simple next-token prediction. It is what helps models learn longer reasoning chains, better math, stronger code generation, and more robust problem solving. But as model sizes grow and responses get longer, training stability becomes a major bottleneck.

That is the context for **Group Sequence Policy Optimization**, or **GSPO**. It is a new RL algorithm introduced for training large language models, and it is designed as a direct response to the instability seen in **Group Relative Policy Optimization** or **GRPO**. The key difference is simple to state but important in practice: **GRPO works at the token level, while GSPO moves the optimization to the sequence level**.

## Why GRPO Became a Problem

GRPO was attractive because it removes the need for a value model, which PPO depends on. Instead of estimating token-by-token advantages with a separate model, GRPO compares multiple responses to the same query and uses the relative reward of each response as the advantage signal.

That sounds efficient, but the paper argues that GRPO has a deeper issue: its importance sampling weights are applied at the token level in a way that does not match the fact that reward is assigned to the whole response.

In GRPO, each token gets its own importance ratio:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

The paper’s critique is that this token-level ratio is not doing the job importance sampling is supposed to do. Importance sampling is meant to correct a mismatch between a behavior distribution and a target distribution using enough samples to estimate an expectation reliably. But in GRPO, each token is only a single sample from a next-token distribution, so the correction is noisy instead of stabilizing.

Over long responses, that noise accumulates. And because clipping is then applied to those token-level ratios, the instability can get worse. The paper reports that this can lead to catastrophic model collapse, especially in large models and long-generation settings.

## GSPO Changes the Unit of Optimization

GSPO starts from a basic observation: if the reward is given to an entire sequence, then the importance ratio should also be defined at the sequence level.

Instead of comparing individual tokens, GSPO compares the likelihood of the whole response:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.
\]

The exponent \(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\) is a length normalization term. The paper says this helps reduce variance and keeps the ratio on a more stable numerical scale across different response lengths.

This is the main conceptual shift in GSPO: **clip, reward, and optimize entire responses rather than individual tokens**. That makes the optimization line up with the structure of the reward signal, which is sequence-level.

## Clipping Looks Similar, But It Behaves Very Differently

Both GRPO and GSPO use clipping to keep training within a safe range. But the clipping target is different.

In GRPO, clipping is applied to each token’s importance ratio. In GSPO, clipping is applied to the sequence-level ratio. That difference matters because token-level ratios can fluctuate a lot, especially in longer responses. The paper argues that this creates noisy gradient estimates and unpredictable training behavior.

GSPO avoids that by treating each response as the basic unit. If a response is too far off-policy, the whole response is clipped out of the update, rather than allowing token-by-token noise to leak into the gradient.

One interesting empirical result in the paper is that GSPO clips far more tokens than GRPO, by about two orders of magnitude in the reported comparison. At first glance, that sounds worse: more clipping should mean less data used for training. But the opposite happens.

Despite clipping more aggressively, GSPO is more sample efficient and achieves better performance. The paper’s interpretation is that this is because GSPO is not throwing away useful signal; it is removing noisy signal that GRPO was misusing in the first place.

## Why GSPO Can Be More Efficient Even When It Clips More

This is one of the most important practical lessons from the paper.

If you only look at the fraction of clipped tokens, GSPO seems harsher than GRPO. But the key point is that token-level clipping in GRPO does not necessarily preserve a clean learning signal. In fact, the paper argues that those token-level gradients are inherently noisy.

GSPO’s stronger clipping is more like better filtering. It focuses learning on responses that are still close enough to the old policy to provide a reliable update. That leads to better training efficiency, not worse.

The empirical results in the paper support this claim. On the evaluated setup, GSPO shows:

- stable training throughout optimization,
- better performance than GRPO under the same compute,
- better benchmark results on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces,
- and the ability to keep improving with more compute, updated queries, and longer generation lengths.

## The MoE Advantage: GSPO Fixes a Real Stability Pain Point

The paper also highlights a particularly important benefit for Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, models.

MoE training introduces a new source of instability: expert routing can change between the old and current policy, even for the same response. The paper reports that in the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, after each RL update, about 10\% of the experts activated under the new policy can differ from those under the old policy for the same rollout sample.

That makes token-level importance ratios even less reliable, because the underlying computation graph is shifting.

To work around this with GRPO, the authors previously used a strategy called **Routing Replay**, which reuses the old routing patterns during ratio computation. This helps stabilize training, but it adds overhead and constrains the model.

GSPO removes the need for that workaround. Because it works with sequence likelihood rather than token-level likelihood, it is less sensitive to routing volatility. The paper reports that GSPO can train MoE models normally without Routing Replay, which simplifies the training stack and avoids extra memory and communication costs.

## A Practical Infrastructure Benefit

GSPO may also make RL infrastructure easier to build.

In many training setups, likelihoods from the inference engine and the training engine do not match exactly because of precision differences. With token-level methods, those differences can matter. But GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods, which are more tolerant of these discrepancies.

That means in some cases, the likelihoods produced by the inference engine may be usable directly for optimization, avoiding recomputation in the training engine. The paper points out that this could be useful in partial rollout, multi-turn RL, and training-inference disaggregated systems.

## GSPO-Token: A More Flexible Variant

The paper also introduces **GSPO-token**, a variant for cases where token-level advantage customization is useful, such as multi-turn RL.

Even here, the sequence-level ratio is still central. GSPO-token allows token-wise advantages, but the sequence-level likelihood still anchors the update. When all token advantages are set equal, GSPO-token and GSPO become numerically identical in objective, clipping, and theoretical gradient.

So GSPO-token adds flexibility without giving up the core stability benefit of sequence-level weighting.

## The Bottom Line

The main message of the paper is that the training unit should match the reward unit.

GRPO kept optimization at the token level even though the reward lives at the sequence level. GSPO fixes that mismatch by using sequence-likelihood importance ratios and sequence-level clipping. According to the paper, that change makes training more stable, more efficient, and more scalable, especially for large MoE models and long-response RL.

The surprising part is that GSPO can outperform GRPO even while clipping more aggressively. But once you view GSPO as a way to remove noisy token-level corrections rather than preserve every possible update, the result makes sense.

For the latest Qwen3 models, the paper says these benefits were not just theoretical. GSPO helped enable the reported performance gains in practice.

In short: **GSPO does not just tweak GRPO. It changes the level at which RL is done, and that seems to be the difference between unstable optimization and scalable training.**



# Why GSPO Is a Big Deal for Mixture-of-Experts Models

Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, models are attractive because they let a large language model scale capacity without activating every parameter on every token. In principle, that makes them a strong fit for reinforcement learning (RL): you get more modeling power, but only pay for part of it at inference and training time.

In practice, though, RL training for MoE models has been fragile. The GSPO paper identifies a very specific failure mode: the router inside an MoE model can change which experts are activated from one update to the next, even for the same response. That instability interacts badly with token-level RL objectives like GRPO, and it can make training unreliable or even collapse entirely.

GSPO matters because it sidesteps that problem. Instead of trying to stabilize a token-by-token learning signal, it moves the optimization to the sequence level. For MoE models, that turns out to be much more robust.

## The core MoE problem: routing volatility

An MoE model does not use all of its parameters for every token. Instead, a routing mechanism selects a subset of experts to handle each token. That makes the model efficient, but it also introduces a new kind of instability during RL training.

The paper observes that after one or more gradient updates, the experts chosen for the same response can change noticeably under the updated policy. In their Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base MoE model, they report that after each RL update, roughly $10\%$ of the experts activated for the same rollout sample under the new policy are different from those under the old policy.

That may sound like a small change, but for RL it is a serious issue. GRPO relies on token-level importance ratios, meaning it compares the probability of each token under the new policy and the old policy. If the routing pattern changes, those token-level probabilities can fluctuate a lot, and the importance ratios become noisy and unreliable.

The paper argues that this is not just a nuisance. It is one reason why GRPO can struggle to converge normally on MoE models.

## The old workaround: Routing Replay

Before GSPO, the paper’s team used a workaround called Routing Replay.

The idea is straightforward: cache the expert routing decisions from the old policy and “replay” them when computing the new policy’s likelihoods. That way, both the old and new policy evaluate each token through the same activated experts. This makes the token-level importance ratios more stable and allows training to proceed more reliably.

In other words, Routing Replay forces consistency in the very part of the model that is otherwise changing underneath you.

The paper shows that this technique is important for getting GRPO to converge normally on MoE models. But it also comes with tradeoffs:

- It adds memory overhead.
- It adds communication overhead.
- It can limit the effective capacity of the MoE model, because the model is no longer free to use its routing mechanism naturally.

So while Routing Replay solves part of the problem, it does so by adding complexity to the training system.

## Why GSPO is different

GSPO takes a different route. Instead of defining importance ratios at the token level, it defines them at the sequence level:

\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
\]

That sounds like a small mathematical change, but it has a big practical effect.

With GSPO, the optimization is based on the likelihood of the entire response, not each individual token. That means the algorithm is less sensitive to the exact routing decisions made at intermediate token positions. As the paper puts it, GSPO focuses on the sequence likelihood and is not sensitive to the individual token likelihood.

This matters for MoE models because the sequence-level probability is much more stable than token-level probabilities when routing changes. Even if the model routes tokens slightly differently after an update, the full response likelihood does not fluctuate as violently as the per-token likelihoods can.

As a result, GSPO can train MoE models stably without needing Routing Replay.

## Why this is a big deal in practice

The practical implication is simple: GSPO removes a workaround that MoE RL training previously depended on.

That is important for two reasons. First, it simplifies the training stack. If you do not need Routing Replay, you avoid extra engineering complexity and extra resource costs. Second, it lets the model use its routing mechanism more naturally, without artificially constraining which experts can be used during optimization.

The paper emphasizes that GSPO “fundamentally resolves” the expert-activation volatility issue in MoE models. In their experiments, GSPO converged normally and stably, while also avoiding the special routing strategy needed by GRPO.

This is one of those cases where a small change in the objective has an outsized systems impact. By moving from token-level to sequence-level optimization, GSPO does not just improve learning dynamics. It also makes the RL infrastructure easier to run.

## The bigger picture

MoE models are appealing because they promise scale and efficiency at the same time. But RL training has exposed a weakness in that promise: routing instability can undermine optimization if the learning algorithm is too sensitive to token-level probability shifts.

GSPO addresses that by matching the unit of optimization to the unit of reward. Since the reward is for the whole response, it makes sense to optimize at the whole-response level as well.

For MoE models, that turns out to be more than a theoretical cleanup. It is a practical stability fix.

The paper reports that GSPO eliminated the need for Routing Replay and supported stable RL training for large MoE systems, including the latest Qwen3 models. That makes GSPO not just an algorithmic refinement, but a cleaner foundation for scaling RL on sparse models.

## Takeaway

If you care about MoE models, GSPO is a big deal because it removes one of the main sources of RL instability: expert-routing volatility.

Instead of fighting that instability with a workaround like Routing Replay, GSPO changes the optimization target so that routing changes matter less. The result is a simpler and more stable training process, better use of model capacity, and less infrastructure complexity.

For large MoE language models, that is exactly the kind of change that can unlock more reliable RL scaling.



# The Hidden Infrastructure Win of GSPO: Simpler, Cheaper RL for Large AI Systems

Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO, is presented in the paper as a reinforcement learning algorithm for training large language models more stably than GRPO. But beyond the model-quality gains, there is a quieter engineering story here: GSPO can make RL systems simpler to operate.

That matters because once RL is scaled up to large models, long responses, and Mixture-of-Experts architectures, the hard part is often no longer just the math. It is the pipeline. How do you store rollouts, recompute likelihoods, keep training and inference engines aligned, and avoid brittle workarounds that consume memory and communication bandwidth? GSPO’s sequence-level design addresses several of those pain points directly.

## Why RL Pipelines Get Complicated

In large-scale RL for language models, responses are typically generated by an inference system, then reused for optimization in a training system. Because the policy changes over time, these rollouts are off-policy by the time gradients are computed. That is why methods like PPO and GRPO use clipping: to keep updates from drifting too far away from the sampled data.

But there is a systems cost to that setup.

In GRPO, the optimization is done at the token level, using token likelihood ratios. In practice, the paper points out that this can be fragile, especially for long responses and sparse MoE models. For MoE systems, the problem gets worse because the activated experts can change after each update. The paper describes this as expert-activation volatility: even for the same response, the network path can shift enough that token-level likelihood ratios become highly unstable.

To work around that, the authors previously used a strategy called Routing Replay. The idea is to cache the expert routing from the old policy and replay it when computing likelihoods under the updated policy, so that the token-level ratio is measured against a consistent activated network. This restores stability, but it also introduces extra memory and communication overhead, and it limits the model’s effective capacity.

## GSPO Moves the Unit of Optimization to the Sequence

GSPO changes the unit of optimization from token to sequence. Instead of using token-level importance ratios, it uses a sequence-level ratio based on the full response likelihood:

$$
s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.
$$

The paper normalizes by response length to keep this ratio in a stable numerical range. The key point for systems design is that GSPO now works with sequence likelihoods, not per-token likelihoods.

That sounds like a mathematical change, but it has practical consequences:

- It is less sensitive to token-by-token fluctuations.
- It aligns better with sequence-level rewards, which are what the verifier actually provides.
- It reduces the need for extra machinery to make token likelihoods comparable across training steps.

In other words, GSPO does not just stabilize learning. It also reduces how much the infrastructure has to “fake” stability.

## Less Need for Token-Level Recomputation

One of the most interesting engineering implications in the paper is that GSPO may reduce the need to recompute token-level likelihoods with the training engine.

This is a big deal because large RL systems often split responsibilities across different engines. An inference engine like SGLang or vLLM may generate responses, while a training engine like Megatron is used to recompute likelihoods under the old policy. That recomputation exists partly because training and inference systems can differ in precision and execution details, so token-level values from inference are not always trusted for optimization.

GSPO changes the calculus. Since it only needs sequence-level likelihoods, the paper argues that it is more tolerant of precision discrepancies between inference and training engines. That opens the door to a simpler workflow: use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine directly for optimization, instead of recomputing token-level likelihoods in the training stack.

For engineering teams, this could mean:

- fewer cross-system dependencies,
- less duplicated computation,
- easier integration between rollout and training infrastructure,
- and a cleaner path for disaggregated training-inference setups.

This is especially relevant for partial rollout and multi-turn RL, where the boundary between generation and training is already more complex.

## Why This Helps MoE Systems in Particular

The paper’s MoE results are important because they show where GSPO can remove infrastructure complexity rather than just shift it around.

With GRPO, MoE training needed Routing Replay to remain stable. GSPO removes that dependency. The paper’s explanation is straightforward: because GSPO uses sequence likelihood, it is not as sensitive to the individual token likelihood changes caused by routing volatility. As long as the model preserves its overall language modeling behavior, the sequence likelihood remains usable for optimization.

That means GSPO can support MoE RL without a special routing replay mechanism. From an engineering perspective, that is attractive because it avoids:

- caching and replaying routing decisions,
- extra memory usage,
- extra communication overhead,
- and hidden constraints on model capacity.

So GSPO is not just a better objective. It is also a better fit for sparse, large-scale training systems.

## More Stable Training, Fewer Operational Surprises

The paper emphasizes that GRPO can suffer catastrophic and irreversible model collapse, especially on long-response tasks. GSPO is reported to train stably across increasing compute, changing query sets, and longer generations.

That stability matters operationally because unstable RL is expensive in more ways than one. If a run collapses, you lose not only compute but also the data pipeline state, training schedule, and tuning effort attached to that run. The paper notes that once GRPO collapse happens, simply going back to a previous checkpoint and adjusting hyperparameters is often not enough.

GSPO’s sequence-level formulation reduces that risk by removing the token-level variance source the authors identify as fundamental to GRPO’s instability. In practice, that means fewer brittle edge cases for the system to absorb.

## The Bigger Picture

The main appeal of GSPO is not just that it performs well. It is that it makes the RL stack more coherent.

The paper’s core design principle is simple: the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. Since the reward is sequence-level, the optimization should be sequence-level too. Once you adopt that principle, several system-level simplifications follow naturally. You no longer need token-level off-policy correction as the primary training signal, you become less dependent on recomputing token likelihoods across engines, and you can avoid specialized tricks like Routing Replay for MoE stability.

That is the hidden infrastructure win of GSPO. It reduces the amount of machinery needed to make large-scale RL behave.

For teams building RL pipelines around large language models, that could be as valuable as the benchmark gains themselves.



# How GSPO Helped Power Qwen3: What This Means for the Future of RL-Trained AI

Reinforcement learning has become one of the most important tools for pushing large language models beyond simple next-token prediction. It is especially useful for reasoning-heavy tasks like math and coding, where the model benefits from learning not just to imitate examples, but to optimize toward externally measured success.

The challenge is that RL at scale is hard to keep stable. According to the GSPO paper, this is exactly where many existing methods run into trouble, especially when training very large models for long responses. The new algorithm, Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO, was designed to fix those stability problems. The result is not just a cleaner training objective, but a practical improvement that helped power the latest Qwen3 models.

## Why RL training for LLMs is difficult

In RL training for language models, the model generates multiple responses to the same query, and a verifier scores those responses. The model then updates itself to make high-scoring responses more likely.

That sounds straightforward, but the details matter. If training uses samples generated by an earlier version of the model, then the optimization becomes off-policy: the data comes from one policy, while the update is applied to another. This is where importance ratios and clipping come in. They are meant to keep updates controlled and prevent the model from drifting too far.

The problem, as the paper argues, is that previous methods like GRPO apply this correction at the token level, even though the reward is assigned to the whole sequence. For short outputs, that mismatch may be manageable. For long reasoning traces, it can become unstable. The paper reports that this can accumulate noise during training and, in severe cases, lead to catastrophic model collapse.

## The key idea behind GSPO

GSPO changes the unit of optimization from tokens to sequences.

Instead of computing importance ratios for each token, GSPO computes a sequence-level importance ratio based on the likelihood of the full response:

$$
s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
$$

In plain language, GSPO asks a simpler question: how much more or less likely is the entire sampled response under the current model compared to the old one?

That matters because the reward is also sequence-level. If the model gets rewarded for the quality of a whole answer, then it makes sense to optimize the whole answer as a unit. The paper argues that this alignment is the main reason GSPO is more stable than token-level alternatives.

GSPO also uses length normalization in the importance ratio. This helps keep the scale of the ratio manageable across responses of different lengths, which is especially important when the model is generating long answers.

## What changed compared to GRPO

GRPO was attractive because it removed the need for a separate value model, which is a major source of memory and compute cost in PPO-style training. GSPO keeps that advantage, but changes how the importance ratio is defined and where clipping is applied.

The practical difference is important. In GRPO, each token gets its own importance ratio, which can vary significantly within a response. In GSPO, all tokens in a response are weighted equally through the sequence-level objective. That removes a source of instability that the paper identifies as a major problem in long-response training.

The paper also introduces a token-level variant, GSPO-token, for scenarios where more fine-grained advantage adjustment is useful. But the central message is the same: the sequence should be treated as the unit of optimization when the reward is sequence-level.

## Why this mattered for Qwen3

The paper reports that GSPO delivered better training stability, better efficiency, and better performance than GRPO in their experiments. It also notes that these benefits contributed to the improvements in the latest Qwen3 models.

That is a big deal for anyone watching the progress of reasoning-oriented models. It suggests that some of the gains in model capability may not come only from larger models or more data, but from better RL infrastructure and better optimization design.

In particular, the paper highlights three outcomes:

- GSPO supported continuous performance improvement as training compute increased.
- It worked better than GRPO under the same training compute and consumed queries.
- It helped stabilize RL training for large Mixture-of-Experts models, where GRPO needed additional machinery such as Routing Replay.

That last point is especially important. MoE models are attractive because they can scale capacity efficiently, but they are also harder to train stably. The paper reports that GSPO resolves the expert-activation volatility issues that made GRPO fragile in this setting, eliminating the need for Routing Replay.

## Why MoE stability matters

Mixture-of-Experts models route different tokens through different experts, which makes them powerful but also sensitive to routing behavior during training. The paper reports that under GRPO, the activated experts for the same response can change significantly after a gradient update. That makes token-level importance ratios even less reliable.

GSPO avoids this problem by focusing on the sequence likelihood rather than the token-level likelihood. Since the model’s overall ability to generate a valid response remains more stable than the exact routing path of individual tokens, the sequence-level ratio is less sensitive to these fluctuations.

This is more than a technical detail. Stable MoE RL training opens the door to scaling reasoning models more aggressively without relying on increasingly complex training hacks.

## What this suggests for future reasoning models

The bigger picture is that RL for language models is moving from a research curiosity to a core capability-building method. For tasks like competition math, code generation, and other long-horizon reasoning problems, the model has to learn from feedback on complete solutions, not just isolated tokens.

GSPO is important because it makes that process more reliable.

If the paper’s results hold up broadly, the implications are significant:

- Reasoning models may be able to train longer without collapsing.
- Larger and more sparse architectures may become easier to optimize with RL.
- Training pipelines could become simpler, especially if sequence-level likelihoods reduce the need for engine-specific recomputation and routing workarounds.
- More compute spent on RL could translate more directly into better performance, rather than being lost to instability.

This is the kind of change that can shift the field in a quiet but meaningful way. It does not change the architecture of a language model, but it changes how effectively we can improve it.

## A step toward cleaner RL infrastructure

One of the most practical claims in the paper is that GSPO may simplify RL infrastructure. Because sequence-level likelihood is more tolerant of precision differences between training and inference engines, the authors suggest that GSPO could make it easier to use likelihoods returned by inference systems directly, rather than recomputing them with the training engine.

That matters for modern large-scale systems, where rollout generation and optimization are often split across different stacks. If GSPO really reduces this friction, it could make RL training pipelines easier to build and maintain.

## The bottom line

GSPO is not just another incremental RL variant. The paper presents it as a response to a fundamental mismatch in earlier methods: token-level optimization for sequence-level rewards. By switching the importance ratio and clipping mechanism to the sequence level, GSPO improves stability, efficiency, and performance.

Most importantly, those gains were not just theoretical. They were strong enough to contribute to the latest Qwen3 models, including their reasoning-focused capabilities.

For the future of RL-trained AI, that points to a clear lesson: better reasoning models may depend as much on better optimization algorithms as on larger datasets or bigger parameter counts. GSPO is a reminder that when the training objective matches the structure of the task, scaling becomes much more practical.
