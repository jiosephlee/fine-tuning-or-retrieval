\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models: Why a New Algorithm Is Needed

# How RL Helps Language Models Improve Reasoning and Problem-Solving

Reinforcement learning has become an important way to scale language models beyond what is typically achieved through pretraining alone. In the setting discussed in this paper, the goal is not just to make a model produce fluent text, but to improve its ability to solve difficult tasks by learning from feedback. The paper emphasizes that large-scale reinforcement learning can help language models develop stronger capabilities on competition-level mathematics and programming problems, where success often requires deeper and longer chains of reasoning.

The key intuition is that a language model can be viewed as a policy that generates a response to a query. During reinforcement learning, the model is not simply imitating examples; instead, it is adjusted so that responses receiving higher reward become more likely. In the paper’s setting, a verifier scores a query-response pair, and the model is trained using that signal. This makes reinforcement learning especially useful for tasks where there is a clear notion of success or failure, such as producing a correct mathematical derivation or a working program.

The paper frames this as part of the broader push to scale language models through reinforcement learning. As more computation is invested in training, models can be pushed toward more sophisticated behavior, especially when the training process is stable enough to support that scale.

# The Importance of Stability When Scaling RL to Larger Models and Longer Responses

The paper makes stability the central prerequisite for successful large-scale reinforcement learning. This is not presented as a secondary concern, but as the main condition that must be satisfied before stronger capabilities can be obtained reliably. When models become larger, when they use sparse architectures such as Mixture-of-Experts models, and when the responses become longer, the training dynamics become harder to control. The paper argues that these conditions make stable optimization more difficult, not easier.

Longer responses are especially important because the rewards in the paper are assigned to entire sequences, while the learning signal must flow through many generated tokens. As response length grows, small instabilities in training can accumulate over many token decisions. The paper suggests that this accumulation can eventually disrupt training in a serious way. Thus, scaling reinforcement learning is not just a matter of using more compute; it requires an algorithm that remains well-behaved under larger models, longer rollouts, and repeated parameter updates.

The paper also explains that practical reinforcement learning training often uses a large rollout batch to maximize hardware utilization. Those rollout samples are then divided into mini-batches for gradient updates. This design is useful for efficiency, but it also creates a mismatch between the policy used to generate the samples and the policy being updated. That mismatch makes stability even more important, because the learning algorithm must remain robust while operating in an off-policy regime.

# The Failure Modes of Current State-of-the-Art RL Training for LLMs

The paper identifies GRPO as the main current method that suffers from severe instability when applied to very large language models. Although GRPO was developed to bypass the value model used in PPO, it introduces its own problems. According to the paper, the instability is not just a minor tuning issue, but stems from a deeper flaw in how importance sampling weights are used.

In the paper’s analysis, GRPO applies token-level importance ratios, but these ratios are not well matched to the way the reward is defined. The reward is assigned to the full response, yet the optimization is performed at the token level. The paper argues that this creates a fundamental mismatch. Instead of reliably correcting for the difference between the rollout policy and the updated policy, the token-level importance weights inject high-variance noise into the gradients.

This noisy behavior becomes especially problematic as response length increases. The paper states that the noise accumulates progressively over long sequences and is further amplified by clipping. In practice, this can lead to catastrophic and irreversible collapse of the model. Once collapse happens, the paper reports that training cannot be recovered simply by returning to an earlier checkpoint, adjusting clipping ranges, increasing generation length, or changing the RL queries. This is presented as a serious failure mode of current large-scale RL training.

# Catastrophic Model Collapse in Large-Scale RL

A major theme of the paper is that unstable RL training can lead to catastrophic model collapse. This is described as a severe breakdown in training where the model stops behaving normally and the collapse becomes irreversible. The paper emphasizes that this is not merely a temporary dip in reward or a noisy optimization phase. Rather, it is a failure from which training cannot be meaningfully restored.

The paper attributes this collapse to the interaction between long responses, token-level importance weights, and clipping. Because the importance ratio is applied at each token, the errors introduced by this design can build up over the course of a long sequence. The clipping mechanism, which is meant to protect training from overly off-policy samples, does not solve the underlying problem. Instead, according to the paper, it can amplify the harmful effect of the noisy token-level estimates.

This is why stability is treated as more than a desirable property. Without it, scaling reinforcement learning can become self-defeating: additional compute, longer training, and larger models can push the system closer to collapse rather than closer to improved performance.

# Why Long Responses, Large Batch Sizes, and Mini-Batch Updates Create Off-Policy Challenges

The paper explains that large rollout batches are needed in modern language model RL to make good use of hardware. At the same time, it is standard practice to split those batches into smaller mini-batches for multiple gradient updates. This means the model parameters can change while the same sampled responses are reused. As a result, the samples used for optimization come from an older policy than the one currently being updated.

This creates an off-policy learning setting. The model is updated using responses generated by $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, while the current model being optimized is $\pi_\theta$. The longer the response and the more mini-batch updates are performed, the more the updated model can drift away from the behavior policy that generated the data. The paper notes that clipping is used in PPO and GRPO precisely because of this issue: it prevents overly off-policy samples from dominating the gradient.

However, the paper argues that clipping only treats the symptom. The deeper problem is that GRPO uses an importance ratio in a way that does not properly serve as a distribution correction. When this is combined with long sequences, the mismatch becomes increasingly harmful. The paper’s motivation is built around the idea that if the learning objective itself is misaligned with the structure of the data and the reward, stability problems are likely to follow.

# Sequence-Level Rewards Need Sequence-Level Optimization

The paper’s main conceptual claim is that the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. Since the verifier assigns a reward to the entire sequence, the algorithm should reason at the sequence level rather than at the token level. This is the central idea behind GSPO.

The paper argues that the sequence-level importance ratio,
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)},
$$
has a clear theoretical meaning: it measures how far a full response sampled from the old policy deviates from the current policy. That interpretation naturally matches a reward defined over the whole response. In contrast, the token-level ratio used by GRPO is based on single token samples from next-token distributions, which the paper argues is not an effective way to perform importance correction.

This leads to the paper’s broader conclusion that reward and optimization should be aligned at the same granularity. Since the reward is sequence-level, the optimization should be sequence-level too. GSPO is introduced as the algorithmic embodiment of this principle.

# High-Level Summary of GSPO’s Contributions

GSPO, or Group Sequence Policy Optimization, is the paper’s proposed solution to the stability and efficiency problems in current RL training for large language models. Its main innovation is to define the importance ratio using sequence likelihood rather than token likelihood. It then performs clipping, rewarding, and optimization at the sequence level, which the paper argues is more consistent with the structure of the reward.

The paper claims several benefits for GSPO. First, it is more stable than GRPO, especially during large-scale training. Second, it is more efficient, achieving better performance with the same training compute and consumed queries. Third, it is robust for Mixture-of-Experts models, where GRPO can be destabilized by expert-activation volatility. GSPO avoids the need for the Routing Replay strategy that was previously required to make GRPO work in MoE settings. Finally, GSPO may simplify RL infrastructure because it can be more tolerant of precision differences between training and inference engines, making it possible in some cases to use likelihoods directly from inference systems.

The paper presents these contributions as more than incremental improvements. GSPO is positioned as a stable and scalable foundation for future large-scale RL training of language models, and the reported gains are described as part of the progress behind the latest Qwen3 models.

2. Mathematical and Algorithmic Foundations

# Autoregressive language models as policies

The paper treats an autoregressive language model as a policy, written as $\pi_\theta$, where $\theta$ denotes the model parameters. This framing is the standard bridge between language modeling and reinforcement learning: instead of thinking of the model only as a next-token predictor, we regard it as an agent that chooses a sequence of tokens in response to a query. Under this view, generating a response is a sequential decision process, and the model’s probability distribution over outputs becomes the central object of optimization.

In an autoregressive model, each token is generated conditioned on the query and all previously generated tokens. That means the probability of a full response is factorized into a product of token-level probabilities. This factorization is what makes the model “autoregressive”: the model does not choose the entire response at once, but instead builds it token by token, with each token depending on the history already produced. This sequential structure is essential for understanding both the policy-gradient objectives used in the paper and the distinction between token-level and sequence-level importance ratios.

# Query-response notation and sequence likelihood

The paper uses $x$ to denote a query and $\mathcal{D}$ to denote the query set. A response to a query is written as $y$, and the pair $(x,y)$ is the basic unit on which rewards are computed and policy updates are based. The length of the response is denoted by $|y|$, meaning the number of tokens in the response.

For an autoregressive policy $\pi_\theta$, the likelihood of a response $y$ given a query $x$ is

$$
\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),
$$

where $y_t$ is the token at position $t$ and $y_{<t}$ is the prefix of tokens generated before position $t$. This expression is central because it captures the probability assigned by the model to the entire response, not just to one token at a time. In the paper, this full-response likelihood is later used to define sequence-level importance ratios in GSPO.

The notation also makes clear how the same response can be evaluated under different policies. When the model parameters change from $\theta_\text{old}$ to $\theta$, the probability of the same response can change as well. Comparing these probabilities is what allows RL algorithms to estimate how much the policy has moved during training.

# Reward functions and verifier-based scoring

The paper assumes that each query-response pair $(x,y)$ can be scored by a verifier $r$, producing a reward $r(x,y) \in [0,1]$. This means the reward is not generated by the language model itself, but by an external scoring process that evaluates the response with respect to the query. The reward is bounded between 0 and 1, which gives the training signal a standardized scale.

This verifier-based reward is important because it allows the training procedure to optimize for task success rather than simply likelihood under pretraining data. In the paper’s setting, the reward is attached to the entire response, not to individual tokens. That choice becomes significant later, because the paper argues that the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. Since the reward is assigned to a full sequence, methods that optimize token by token can become misaligned with the structure of the supervision signal.

# The policy optimization objective in RL for LLMs

The paper presents reinforcement learning for language models as policy optimization under sampled responses. In practice, the model generates responses from an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and the parameters are then updated to improve performance based on the observed rewards. Because the samples used for training are generated by an earlier policy, the optimization is off-policy in a limited sense: the data comes from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, while the gradient is used to update the current policy $\pi_\theta$.

This setup motivates importance sampling and clipping. Since the sampled data was not drawn from the exact current policy, the training objective needs a way to account for the mismatch between the behavior policy and the target policy. At the same time, if the new policy moves too far from the old one, the update can become unstable. The paper frames PPO and GRPO as two prior methods that handle this tradeoff in different ways, with PPO relying on a value model and GRPO removing that dependence through group-based relative advantages.

# Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO): objective, clipping, and token-level importance ratios

PPO is introduced in the paper as a standard RL method for policy optimization with a clipping mechanism. The objective is written as

$$
\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min \left(
w_t(\theta)\widehat{A}_t,\,
\mathrm{clip}(w_t(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_t
\right)
\right],
$$

where the token-level importance ratio is

$$
w_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}.
$$

The purpose of the clipping term is to restrict how far the policy update can move relative to the old policy. If the ratio grows too large or too small, the clipped term prevents the update from becoming overly aggressive. In the paper’s description, this is the mechanism that keeps policy optimization in a proximal region around the old policy.

PPO applies the objective at the token level: each token contributes its own importance ratio and its own advantage estimate. The average over tokens produces the final training signal for a response. The intuition is that the model is nudged at each position based on whether that token, under the current policy, appears more or less favorable than under the old policy, weighted by the estimated advantage.

# The role of value models in PPO

A defining feature of PPO in the paper is the use of a value model to estimate advantages. The advantage $\widehat{A}_t$ for a token is not computed directly from the reward alone; instead, it is estimated by another model. This value model is intended to estimate how much better or worse an action is relative to an expected baseline, which helps stabilize policy updates.

The paper emphasizes that this dependence on the value model is a practical bottleneck. The value model is usually similar in size to the policy model, so maintaining and training it requires substantial memory and computation. In large language model RL, where model scale is already very large, that extra cost is significant. The method’s effectiveness also depends heavily on the accuracy of the value estimate, so the optimization quality is tied to the quality of another learned model that must itself scale with the difficulty of the task.

# Why value models are expensive and difficult to scale for LLM RL

The paper argues that value models are not just an engineering inconvenience; they are a major obstacle to scaling RL for large language models. Because a value model is typically comparable in size to the policy model, it introduces a large additional memory footprint. It also adds computational overhead during both training and inference.

More importantly, the paper stresses that estimating values reliably becomes harder as responses become longer and tasks become more complex. In the setting of competition-level mathematics or programming, the model may need to reason through long sequences before producing a correct answer. Under such conditions, it is increasingly difficult for a separate value model to provide accurate and scalable token-level estimates. This is one of the motivations for looking for algorithms that avoid value models altogether.

# Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): removing the value model via group-based relative advantages

GRPO is introduced as a way to bypass the value model entirely. Instead of estimating token-level advantages with a separate value network, GRPO compares multiple responses sampled for the same query and uses their relative rewards to define advantages. The paper presents this as a more lightweight alternative to PPO, one that avoids the cost and complexity of maintaining a value model.

In GRPO, a query $x$ is paired with a group of $G$ generated responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$. The algorithm then computes the relative advantage of each response within that group. This relative comparison is the key idea: a response is considered better or worse not in absolute terms, but relative to other sampled responses to the same query. This allows the method to generate an advantage signal directly from reward comparisons.

# Group sampling and normalized reward advantages in GRPO

The GRPO objective in the paper is

$$
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min \left(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t},\,
\mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_{i,t}
\right)
\right],
$$

where the token-level ratio is

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})},
$$

and the advantage is defined as

$$
\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}.
$$

This means every token in a given response shares the same advantage value. The reward of the whole response is normalized within the group by subtracting the mean reward of the group and dividing by the standard deviation. The result is a standardized advantage score that indicates whether a response performed above or below the group average.

The group-based design is practical because it removes the need for a learned value function while still preserving a notion of advantage. Instead of asking a value model to predict expected return, GRPO uses the spread of rewards among sampled responses as a relative baseline. In the paper’s presentation, this is a major reason GRPO became attractive for large-scale language model RL.

# Token-level optimization in GRPO and its practical appeal

GRPO applies the same response-level advantage to every token in the response, but the optimization itself is still token-based. Each token is weighted by its own importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, and the clipped PPO-style objective is applied at each position. This design is appealing because it preserves the familiar structure of policy gradient optimization while eliminating the overhead of a value model.

The practical attraction of this token-level formulation is that it lets the algorithm operate directly on token probabilities, which are already natural outputs of an autoregressive language model. It also keeps the connection to standard PPO-style clipping, so the update remains constrained and can be implemented with familiar machinery. In the paper, however, this same token-level structure is later identified as the source of instability when the importance ratios are misapplied, especially for long responses and large models. Even so, understanding GRPO’s token-level objective is essential, because GSPO is introduced specifically as a sequence-level correction to this earlier design.

3. Importance Sampling and the Core Flaw in GRPO

# The purpose of importance sampling in off-policy learning

The paper’s critique of GRPO begins with a basic issue in reinforcement learning for language models: the data used for optimization is often generated by a policy that is not exactly the one currently being updated. In the paper’s setting, responses are sampled from an old policy, denoted $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, while learning seeks to improve a current policy, $\pi_\theta$. This is an off-policy situation because the samples come from one distribution and the optimization target is another.

Importance sampling is the standard mathematical tool for handling this mismatch. Its role is to let us estimate an expectation under a target distribution even when our samples were drawn from a different behavior distribution. The paper emphasizes that this is the conceptual reason clipping exists in PPO- and GRPO-style methods: if the sampled responses are too far from the current policy, then the off-policy discrepancy becomes too large to trust during gradient estimation. Importance sampling is therefore meant to correct for distribution mismatch, not merely to reweight arbitrary pieces of text.

The key point is that this correction must be used in a way that actually reflects how the samples were drawn. If the samples are sequence-level responses, then the correction should also be defined at the sequence level. The paper argues that GRPO breaks this basic logic by applying importance ratios token by token, even though the rewards and optimization targets live at the level of full responses.

# Estimating expectations under one distribution using samples from another

The paper presents importance sampling in its standard form. If we want the expectation of a function $f$ under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$, but we only have samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$, then we can rewrite the expectation as

$$
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right]
=
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z) \right].
$$

This identity is the mathematical heart of importance sampling. The ratio $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ compensates for the fact that the samples were not drawn from the target distribution. In the paper’s discussion, this is the principle that policy optimization methods are supposed to respect when learning from rollouts produced by an older policy.

The intuition is simple: if a certain sample is more likely under the target distribution than under the behavior distribution, then it should count more heavily in the estimate. If it is less likely, then it should count less. The ratio corrects the sample so that, in aggregate, the weighted behavior samples behave like target samples. But the important caveat is that this works as an estimator because it is aggregating over multiple samples from the behavior distribution.

# Why importance weights require averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution

A central claim in the paper is that importance weights only serve their intended correction role when they are used with enough samples from the behavior distribution. The paper explicitly notes that the ratio $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ relies on averaging over multiple samples, with $N \gg 1$, to effectively correct the distributional mismatch.

This matters because importance sampling is not meant to make a single sample magically representative of the target distribution. Rather, it rebalances a collection of samples. If one sample happens to be unusually likely under the target policy, that does not by itself guarantee a good estimate. The correction becomes meaningful only when many behavior samples are reweighted and averaged together, so that random fluctuations cancel out and the reweighting can approximate the target expectation.

The paper uses this principle to argue that applying importance weights at the token level is suspect. In language generation, each next-token choice is a single sample from a next-token distribution. If the algorithm tries to use a token-level importance ratio based on just that one token sample, then it is not really performing the kind of distribution correction that importance sampling is supposed to provide.

# The difference between token-level samples and sequence-level samples in language generation

Language generation is autoregressive, so a full response $y$ is built token by token. The paper defines the likelihood of a response under a policy as

$$
\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
$$

This means that a response is naturally a sequence-level object, even though it is composed of token-level decisions. In the paper’s framework, the reward $r(x,y)$ is assigned to the whole query-response pair and lies in $[0,1]$. So while the model internally emits tokens one at a time, the supervision signal is given only after the full response is produced.

That distinction is essential. A token is not the same thing as a rewarded sample. The paper argues that the training signal should respect the granularity at which rewards are defined. Since the reward belongs to the entire response, the unit of optimization should also be the entire response. This becomes the paper’s guiding principle for why GSPO uses sequence-level importance ratios rather than token-level ones.

GRPO, by contrast, breaks the sequence into token-level contributions and then applies an importance ratio to each token. The paper’s argument is that this is not aligned with how the data and reward are structured. The response is the sample that was generated off-policy, not the individual token considered in isolation.

# Why GRPO’s token-level importance ratios do not correctly perform distribution correction

In GRPO, the importance ratio is defined at each token position as

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

The paper’s critique is that this token-level ratio is not a valid way to correct the distributional mismatch created by off-policy sampling. The reason is that each token is only one sample from the next-token distribution. A single sampled token does not provide enough information for importance sampling to do the job it is designed to do.

The paper emphasizes that this token-level weighting therefore fails to perform true distribution correction. Instead of compensating for the mismatch between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, it introduces randomness into the gradient estimate. The weight is calculated from a single next-token sample, so it is highly sensitive to the particular token that happened to be sampled. This makes the weight noisy rather than corrective.

The problem becomes more severe because language generation is sequential. A noisy weighting at one step is not isolated. It affects the gradient update for the current token and contributes to the changing policy that will generate the next token, where another noisy weighting will be applied. The paper argues that this turns a local misuse of importance sampling into a sequence-wide instability.

# Single-sample next-token weighting as a source of noise

The paper’s key technical objection is that GRPO applies the importance weight to each token based on only one observed next-token sample. Because each token comes from a single draw from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t})$, the ratio is computed from a sparse piece of evidence. It is not averaging over a set of token samples from the same conditional distribution.

As a result, the token-level ratio does not act like a stable estimator of the target-to-behavior mismatch. Instead, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient. The paper describes this as progressively accumulating training noise. Since the model is updated over many tokens and many responses, even modest noise at each step can compound into much larger instability over the course of training.

The paper also notes that clipping does not eliminate this problem. The ratio is still built from a single token sample, so even if its value is restricted to a range, the gradient is still driven by noisy per-token fluctuations. In the paper’s view, the core issue is not merely that the ratio can become too large or too small; it is that the object being clipped is the wrong object in the first place.

# How variance accumulates over long sequences

The paper specifically highlights long-response tasks as especially problematic for GRPO. When responses are long, the model produces many tokens, and each token contributes its own token-level importance ratio and gradient term. If each of these terms is noisy, then the total variance of the update can become very large.

This accumulation matters because the instability is not isolated to a single long sample. The paper argues that high-variance training noise builds up across token positions and across optimization steps. Over time, this can lead to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. Once collapse occurs, the paper reports that recovery is difficult even if one reverts to an earlier checkpoint, adjusts the clipping ranges, changes generation length, or switches RL queries.

The intuition is that long sequences create many opportunities for the flawed token-level weighting to introduce distortion. Since every token is treated as if it were separately important in the importance-sampling sense, the update can drift away from what the actual sequence-level reward supports. The longer the response, the more these local distortions can accumulate.

# Why clipping can amplify rather than solve the instability

Clipping is usually introduced to keep policy updates within a proximal region and to reduce the influence of overly off-policy samples. In GRPO, the token-level importance ratio is clipped to a range such as $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$. In principle, this should prevent extreme updates.

The paper’s argument is that clipping does not fix the fundamental misuse of importance weights. Instead, because the token-level ratios are already noisy, clipping can interact with that noise in undesirable ways. The paper states that the clipping mechanism can exacerbate the instability and help precipitate model collapse. The issue is not simply that the ratios are too large; it is that the per-token weights are not the right objects for sequence-level reward optimization in the first place.

This is a subtle but important point. Clipping is designed to prevent excessive deviation from the old policy, but if the underlying gradient estimate is ill-posed, then constraining its magnitude does not make the estimate well-founded. The paper’s position is that clipping a noisy token-level ratio merely bounds the noise; it does not turn the noise into a meaningful correction.

# The mismatch between reward granularity and optimization granularity

The paper’s most important conceptual claim is that the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. In the RL setup considered here, the verifier assigns a reward to the full response $y$, producing $r(x,y) \in [0,1]$. The reward is not issued token by token. It evaluates the entire response as a whole.

Given that structure, the paper argues that optimization should also happen at the response level. If the reward is sequence-level, then applying importance correction and clipping at the token level introduces a mismatch between what is being optimized and what is being judged. This mismatch is the paper’s explanation for why GRPO becomes unstable, especially in large models and long-response tasks.

The paper frames this as more than an implementation detail. It is a basic alignment principle: the granularity of the training objective should reflect the granularity of the learning signal. If the sequence as a whole receives the reward, then the sequence should be the unit on which off-policy correction is defined. This is why GSPO moves from token-level importance ratios to sequence-level importance ratios.

# The principle that the optimization unit should match the reward unit

The paper distills its critique into a simple principle: the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward. This principle is the conceptual bridge from the failure of GRPO to the design of GSPO.

In GRPO, the reward is sequence-level, but the importance ratio and gradient weighting are token-level. The paper argues that this mismatch is the source of the algorithm’s ill-posed nature. Since the reward is assigned to the entire response, it is the full response that should be considered the sample for importance sampling and optimization. Token-level weighting breaks that correspondence and turns the update into a noisy approximation with no proper distribution-correction meaning.

This principle also explains why the paper later defines GSPO’s importance ratio using sequence likelihood rather than token likelihood. By making the ratio reflect the response as a whole, GSPO restores the alignment between sampling, weighting, and reward. In the paper’s view, this alignment is what GRPO lacks, and that lack is what ultimately leads to high-variance gradients and model collapse.

4. From Token-Level to Sequence-Level RL: The Design Logic of GSPO

# Why sequence likelihood has a clear theoretical meaning in language generation

The central design move in GSPO is to stop treating individual tokens as the basic unit of off-policy correction and instead treat the entire response sequence as the unit of optimization. This shift is not just a technical convenience; it follows directly from how language generation works. In an autoregressive language model, the probability of a response $y$ given a query $x$ is defined as the product of the conditional probabilities of its tokens:

$$
\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
$$

This sequence likelihood is therefore the natural object that summarizes how the model assigns probability to the whole response. A generated answer is not a collection of independent token decisions in isolation; it is one coherent trajectory produced by the policy. GSPO takes this view seriously and uses the likelihood of the full response as the basis for importance weighting.

The paper argues that this sequence-level quantity has a clear theoretical meaning in language generation. Specifically, the sequence-level importance ratio compares how likely a response is under the current policy relative to the old policy. Because the response is sampled from the old policy, this ratio tells us how much the current policy has shifted in its view of that entire response. In this sense, the ratio is not merely a number attached to a token; it is a measure of how the policy as a whole has changed with respect to a complete generated output.

This is the key conceptual difference from GRPO. In GRPO, importance ratios are computed token by token, but the paper argues that this is a misapplication of the importance sampling principle. The token-level quantity does not represent a full sample from the policy distribution over sequences, so it does not have the same clean interpretation as a correction factor for distribution mismatch. GSPO restores this interpretation by moving to the sequence level, where the importance ratio is defined on the exact object that was sampled: the complete response.

# The sequence-level importance ratio as a measure of policy deviation

GSPO defines the importance ratio for a response $y_i$ as

$$
s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})} \right).
$$

The paper emphasizes that this quantity measures how far the response sampled from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ deviates from $\pi_\theta$. That is, it is not just a reweighting trick; it is an explicit indicator of policy deviation at the response level. If the current policy assigns much higher likelihood to the response than the old policy, the ratio increases. If it assigns lower likelihood, the ratio decreases. This makes the ratio a meaningful signal for deciding how much to trust a rollout sample during optimization.

The length normalization by $|y_i|$ is important in the paper’s design. Without it, responses of different lengths would produce sequence ratios on very different numerical scales, and even a few token-level likelihood changes could cause large swings in the overall ratio. By taking the $|y_i|$-th root, GSPO controls the ratio within a more unified numerical range and reduces variance. This is part of what makes the sequence-level definition practical rather than merely conceptual.

The paper also notes that the clipping ranges used in GSPO differ in magnitude from those used in token-level methods such as GRPO, precisely because the importance ratio itself is defined differently. This reinforces the idea that GSPO is not a minor modification of GRPO, but a redefinition of the correction unit.

# Alignment between sequence-level reward and sequence-level correction

A key argument in the paper is that the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. In the RL setup described here, the verifier returns a reward for the entire query-response pair, $r(x,y) \in [0,1]$. That means the reward is assigned to the complete sequence, not to individual tokens. GSPO therefore computes the advantage at the response level as

$$
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}.
$$

This group-based normalization compares each sampled response to the other responses generated for the same query. The resulting advantage is a sequence-level signal that says whether a response is better or worse than its peers. Since the reward itself is sequence-level, the correction should also be sequence-level. GSPO’s objective follows this logic exactly.

This alignment matters because it keeps the learning signal coherent. When reward is attached to a whole response, it is natural to ask how much the policy should update toward or away from that whole response as a unit. GSPO does exactly that. It does not attempt to break the reward into token-specific pieces that were never directly observed. Instead, it uses the overall response likelihood and the overall response advantage to guide optimization.

The paper makes clear that this is not merely a matter of elegance. It is the mechanism by which GSPO avoids the ill-posedness identified in GRPO. The response-level reward and response-level importance ratio speak the same conceptual language: both operate on complete generated sequences. This consistency is what the authors mean when they say GSPO aligns sequence-level rewarding and optimization.

# Why sequence-level clipping is more meaningful than token-level clipping

Clipping in proximal policy optimization methods is intended to prevent overly off-policy samples from dominating the gradient. In GRPO, clipping is applied to token-level importance ratios, but the paper argues that this does not correctly address the mismatch between sampling and reward. The core issue is that the sample being evaluated is not a token in isolation but an entire response. Consequently, if clipping is meant to decide whether a sample is too far from the old policy to be useful, the natural object to clip is the response-level importance ratio.

GSPO therefore applies clipping to $s_i(\theta)$ rather than to each token ratio separately. This means the entire response is either accepted as sufficiently on-policy or downweighted as too far from the current policy. In the paper’s interpretation, this is a more meaningful exclusion rule because the reward and the optimization target are both defined at the sequence level. Sequence-level clipping is thus not just a different implementation; it is a correction that matches the structure of the problem.

The authors also observe that token-level clipping in GRPO can amplify training noise. Because each token is clipped independently based on an importance ratio derived from a single token sample, the resulting gradient can vary unpredictably across long sequences. GSPO avoids this by treating the full response as a single unit of clipping. That reduces the number of places where noisy token-level discrepancies can distort the update.

Another practical implication emphasized in the paper is that clipping fractions behave very differently in GSPO and GRPO. GSPO can clip a much larger fraction of tokens while still training more efficiently, because it is not trying to exploit every token-level signal. Instead, it uses a sequence-level estimate that the paper argues is more reliable. This is presented as evidence that sequence-level clipping is not wasteful but structurally better matched to the learning problem.

# The intuition behind treating a response as the basic optimization unit

The paper’s intuition is straightforward: a response is the natural outcome of the policy, so it should be the natural unit of optimization. When a model generates an answer, it produces a complete sequence of tokens that collectively form a single candidate solution. The reward verifier evaluates that candidate solution as a whole. Therefore, the policy update should also treat that response as a whole object.

This perspective becomes especially compelling in settings where the response is long and the task requires deeper reasoning. In such cases, the quality of the final answer depends on the global structure of the sequence, not on isolated token decisions. If the reward only comes after the full response is generated, then it makes sense that the algorithm should use the full response likelihood to decide how strongly that trajectory should influence learning.

The paper argues that this viewpoint resolves an important mismatch in token-level methods. Token-level weighting suggests that each token independently carries a meaningful correction signal, but the reward does not support that interpretation. A token in the middle of a response is not individually rewarded or penalized by the verifier. It only contributes as part of the final sequence. GSPO embraces this reality by using the response as the primitive object for weighting, clipping, and optimization.

This is also why GSPO can be seen as simplifying the logic of RL infrastructure. Once the response is the basic unit, the algorithm no longer needs to manage token-by-token correction in a setting where the meaningful signal is sequence-wide. The paper presents this as a cleaner foundation for RL training, especially when working with long responses and large models.

# How the proposed approach addresses the root cause of GRPO instability

The paper identifies the root cause of GRPO instability as the invalid use of token-level importance weights. In GRPO, the importance ratio is computed at each token position using only a single sample from each next-token distribution. The paper argues that this does not function as proper importance sampling correction. Instead of reliably adjusting for the mismatch between the behavior policy and the target policy, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient.

This noise becomes worse as response length increases, because many noisy token-level terms accumulate over a sequence. The clipping mechanism does not fix the problem; according to the paper, it can even exacerbate instability by interacting with these token-level fluctuations. In large models, this accumulation can lead to catastrophic model collapse, and once collapse occurs, recovery is often impossible even if one returns to an earlier checkpoint and retunes hyperparameters.

GSPO addresses this at the source by changing the unit of correction. By moving the importance ratio from tokens to sequences, it removes the invalid assumption that each token-level sample can independently serve as a reliable importance weight. The gradient now depends on the whole response likelihood, which is much more stable and consistent with the reward structure. In the paper’s view, this is what restores a valid learning signal.

The gradient expressions make the distinction especially clear. In GSPO, all tokens in a response are weighted equally through the sequence-level ratio. In GRPO, each token receives its own importance weight, and these weights can vary in unstable ways. The paper argues that these unequal token weights are a major source of unpredictability. GSPO avoids that by giving the response a single correction factor and then distributing it uniformly across the tokens in the sequence. This reduces the instability that comes from token-wise off-policy correction and helps explain why GSPO trains more stably than GRPO.

In this sense, GSPO does not merely improve GRPO; it changes the logic of the optimization problem so that the sampling correction, clipping, reward assignment, and gradient estimation all operate at the same level of abstraction. That alignment is what the paper presents as the fundamental solution to GRPO’s instability.

5. The GSPO Objective

# Formal definition of the GSPO objective

Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO, is defined as a reinforcement learning objective for training a language model policy $\pi_\theta$ using groups of responses sampled from an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The core GSPO objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min \left( s_{i}(\theta)  \widehat{A}_{i},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i} \right) 
\right].
\]

This expression captures the main design of GSPO in a compact form. A query $x$ is drawn from the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and for that query, $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ are generated from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Each response is then scored by a verifier reward function $r(x,y_i)$, and that reward is converted into an advantage estimate $\widehat{A}_i$. The model update is determined by the product of this advantage with a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, while the clipping operator constrains the ratio to stay within a proximal range around $1$.

The key conceptual change relative to token-based methods is that GSPO performs its optimization at the level of the entire response sequence rather than at the level of individual tokens. This means the policy update is aligned with how the reward is assigned: the reward belongs to the whole sequence, so the corrective weighting and clipping also happen at the whole-sequence level.

# Sampling groups of responses from the old policy

GSPO is built around group-based rollouts. For each query $x$, the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ generates a group of $G$ responses. This group structure is essential because the advantage estimate is computed by comparing the rewards of responses within the same group, rather than relying on a learned value model.

The use of the old policy is also important from the standpoint of optimization. During training, rollout data are usually collected in large batches and then split into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates. This creates an off-policy setting because the sampled responses come from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ rather than from the current policy $\pi_\theta$ being updated. GSPO explicitly accounts for this mismatch through its importance ratio and clipping mechanism.

In this framework, the group of responses gives the algorithm a local comparison set for the same query. Instead of asking whether a response is absolutely good or bad, GSPO asks how a response compares to its peers for the same prompt. That comparison is what produces the normalized reward signal used as the advantage.

# Group-based normalized reward as the advantage estimate

GSPO uses a group-relative advantage estimate defined by normalizing rewards within the response group:

\[
\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) }{ \mathrm{std} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) }.
\]

This quantity measures how much better or worse one sampled response is relative to the other responses generated for the same query. A response with above-average reward receives a positive advantage, while a below-average response receives a negative advantage. Dividing by the standard deviation scales the signal so that the relative differences are normalized within the group.

The paper emphasizes that this group-based advantage estimate is part of what makes GSPO self-contained: it avoids the need for an external value model. In PPO, the advantage is estimated by a separate value function, which adds memory and computation overhead and introduces the challenge of obtaining reliable value estimates. In GSPO, the group of responses itself serves as the reference structure for advantage estimation.

This normalization is especially natural in the setting described in the paper because rewards are given to full query-response pairs. Since the reward is assigned to the entire sequence, it is appropriate for the advantage estimate to also be associated with the entire sequence.

# Sequence-level clipping with proximal updates

Like PPO and GRPO, GSPO uses clipping to keep the policy update proximal to the old policy. The objective contains the term

\[
\min \left( s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i, \, \mathrm{clip}\left(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon\right)\widehat{A}_i \right).
\]

The role of this minimum is to prevent the update from exploiting overly large importance ratios. If the ratio moves too far from $1$, the clipped version becomes active and limits the contribution of that sample to the gradient. In this way, clipping excludes overly off-policy samples from dominating optimization.

The crucial difference in GSPO is that clipping is applied to the entire response sequence rather than to individual tokens. This is consistent with the sequence-level reward and the sequence-level importance ratio. The paper argues that this alignment matters because the token-level mechanism used in GRPO misapplies importance weights in a way that becomes unstable, especially for long responses and large models. GSPO avoids that issue by treating the full response as the unit of optimization.

In practical terms, sequence-level clipping means that the response is accepted or down-weighted as one unit. Rather than allowing each token to contribute its own separate clipped ratio, GSPO uses a single scalar measure of deviation from the old policy for the entire response. That design is a central reason the algorithm is described as more stable.

# Defining the GSPO importance ratio from full-sequence likelihood

The GSPO importance ratio is defined using the likelihood of the full response sequence:

\[
s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right).
\]

This definition is one of the main innovations of GSPO. Instead of constructing an importance ratio from individual token probabilities, GSPO uses the ratio of full-sequence likelihoods. The paper interprets this as a theoretically grounded measure of how far a sampled response from the old policy has drifted relative to the current policy.

The ratio has a clear meaning at the sequence level: it compares the probability assigned to the entire response under the new policy and the old policy. Because the reward is also sequence-level, this ratio naturally matches the structure of the learning signal. The paper explicitly frames this as aligning with the basic principle of importance sampling.

The second equality shows that the sequence ratio can also be written as the exponential of the average token-level log-likelihood ratio. This form reveals that the sequence ratio is a geometric-mean style aggregate over the tokens, rather than a plain product of potentially unstable per-token terms. That distinction is central to why the method behaves differently from token-level objectives.

# Geometric-mean style length normalization of sequence ratios

GSPO does not use the raw sequence likelihood ratio directly. Instead, it raises the ratio to the power of $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$, which the paper describes as length normalization. This produces a normalized importance ratio on a comparable scale across responses of different lengths.

The intuition is that response length matters because longer sequences naturally accumulate more token-level likelihood changes. If one simply multiplies token ratios together, even moderate changes across many tokens can lead to very large or very small values. By taking the $|y_i|$-th root of the full-sequence ratio, GSPO compresses that accumulation into an average per-token scale.

The paper notes that this choice reduces variance and keeps $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range. That is important because responses of different lengths should not require different clipping ranges merely because they contain different numbers of tokens. Length normalization ensures that the importance ratio remains interpretable and numerically manageable across examples.

# Why raw sequence likelihood ratios would be numerically unstable

The paper explicitly motivates length normalization by pointing out that raw sequence likelihood ratios can fluctuate dramatically. Since a sequence likelihood is a product over token probabilities, even small changes in a few token probabilities can produce large changes in the overall product. This makes the unnormalized ratio highly sensitive to local changes in the response.

Such sensitivity would be especially problematic when responses are long. The longer the response, the more factors contribute to the likelihood ratio, and the more likely it is that the ratio becomes extreme. In that case, clipping would need to operate over a very wide and length-dependent numerical range, which would make optimization harder to control.

The paper also emphasizes that the goal is not merely numerical convenience. A raw sequence ratio that becomes too extreme would make the policy update unstable. Length normalization prevents that by turning the product-like growth of sequence likelihood into an average-scale quantity. In this sense, normalization is part of the algorithmic stability of GSPO, not just an implementation detail.

# How length normalization reduces variance

Length normalization reduces variance by preventing the importance ratio from being dominated by the sheer number of tokens in a response. If every token contributes multiplicatively to the sequence likelihood ratio, then the final ratio can vary sharply across samples even when the underlying policy change is modest. Averaging the log-ratios and then exponentiating tempers this effect.

The paper’s derivation makes this explicit by rewriting the normalized ratio as

\[
\exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right).
\]

This is a per-token average in log space, which is much less volatile than the product of token-wise ratios. As a result, a small number of token-level likelihood changes does not cause dramatic swings in the overall importance ratio.

The paper treats this as one reason GSPO can train more stably than GRPO. Because the ratio is less noisy, the gradient signal is also less noisy. That stability matters especially when training large models on long-response tasks.

# Why clipping ranges in GSPO differ in scale from clipping ranges in GRPO

The paper notes that the clipping ranges in GSPO and GRPO differ in order of magnitude because the importance ratios themselves are defined differently. In GRPO, the ratio is token-level and can vary substantially from token to token. In GSPO, the ratio is a length-normalized sequence-level quantity, so its natural scale is different.

This means that clipping thresholds cannot be compared directly between the two algorithms. A clipping value that is reasonable for token-level ratios in GRPO does not correspond to the same numerical regime as a clipping value for sequence-level ratios in GSPO. The paper therefore stresses that the difference in clipping ranges is a consequence of the different definitions of importance ratio.

The broader point is that clipping is not an isolated hyperparameter; it depends on the scale of the ratio being clipped. Since GSPO changes the ratio from a token-level quantity to a normalized sequence-level quantity, the appropriate clipping range changes as well.

# Interpreting GSPO as sequence-level reward, clipping, and optimization

The GSPO objective can be understood as a direct match between three components: sequence-level reward, sequence-level clipping, and sequence-level optimization. The reward is assigned to the entire response, the importance ratio is computed for the entire response, and the clipping is applied to the entire response. This structural alignment is one of the paper’s main claims about why GSPO is well posed.

The paper contrasts this with GRPO, where the reward is sequence-level but the importance ratio is token-level. That mismatch is presented as the source of instability: the token-level ratio is not a faithful correction for a sequence-level objective, and it injects high-variance noise into the gradient. GSPO resolves that mismatch by using the same unit, the sequence, throughout the objective.

Viewed this way, GSPO is not just a modified clipping scheme. It is a redefinition of the optimization problem so that the importance-sampling correction matches the level at which the reward is defined. That alignment is what makes the algorithm coherent, stable, and efficient within the paper’s framework.

6. Gradient Analysis: Why GSPO Is More Stable

# Gradient derivation for the unclipped GSPO objective

To understand why GSPO is more stable than GRPO, it helps to begin with the unclipped version of the GSPO objective. GSPO defines optimization at the response level, not the token level. For a query $x$, and a sampled response $y_i$ from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the sequence-level importance ratio is

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.
\]

If we ignore clipping for the moment, the GSPO objective reduces to maximizing the expected product of this sequence ratio and the response advantage. In the paper’s notation, the unclipped form is

\[
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i\right].
\]

The gradient is then obtained by differentiating through $s_i(\theta)$. Because $s_i(\theta)$ is an exponential of an average of token log-likelihood ratios, its gradient is especially clean. The paper shows that

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i \cdot \nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)
\right].
\]

This already reveals the central structure: the entire response contributes a single multiplicative weight, $s_i(\theta)$, and this weight is shared by all tokens in the response.

# Expressing the GSPO gradient in terms of token log-likelihoods

The sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is defined using the full response likelihood, which itself factorizes autoregressively across tokens. Expanding the log of the ratio gives

\[
\log s_i(\theta)
=
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

Taking the gradient of this expression yields a simple average of token-level log-likelihood gradients. Substituting into the GSPO gradient gives

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\widehat{A}_i
\cdot
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

This form makes the mechanism of GSPO easy to interpret. The gradient is an average over token log-likelihood gradients, but every token in the response is multiplied by exactly the same response-level factor. In other words, the response’s sequence likelihood determines how strongly the whole trajectory is updated, while the tokens inside that response are not individually reweighted against one another.

That uniformity is important. GSPO does not try to decide that one token within a response is “more important” than another based on token-wise importance ratios. Instead, the response as a whole is treated as the optimization unit, which matches the fact that the reward is also assigned to the whole response.

# How the sequence-level ratio modulates the whole response uniformly

The paper emphasizes that the sequence-level ratio has a clear interpretation: it measures how far the response $y_i$ sampled from the old policy deviates from the current policy. Because the ratio is computed on the entire response likelihood, it naturally aligns with sequence-level reward and sequence-level optimization.

This alignment matters not just conceptually but mathematically. Under GSPO, if a response receives a positive advantage, then the full response is encouraged proportionally to the same sequence-level weight. If it receives a negative advantage, the full response is discouraged, again under the same response-level weight. The update is therefore coherent across the entire sequence.

The paper also notes that length normalization is built into the definition of $s_i(\theta)$ through the exponent $1/|y_i|$. This is done to reduce variance and keep the ratio in a unified numerical range. Without this normalization, even a few token likelihood changes could create large fluctuations in the response-level importance ratio, and responses of different lengths would require different clipping ranges. The normalized sequence ratio therefore serves two purposes at once: it makes the optimization more stable, and it makes the importance ratio comparable across responses of varying length.

# Gradient derivation for the unclipped GRPO objective

GRPO starts from a similar group-based advantage construction, but it applies the importance ratio at the token level. For each response $y_i$ and token position $t$, the importance ratio is

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

With clipping ignored, the GRPO objective has token-wise terms of the form $w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_i$, averaged over tokens and responses. Differentiating that objective gives the gradient shown in the paper:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\widehat{A}_i
\cdot
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

This formula looks superficially similar to GSPO because it also contains token log-likelihood gradients. But there is a crucial difference: each token gradient is multiplied by its own token-specific importance ratio, rather than by a single response-level ratio shared across the entire sequence.

That difference is the core of the stability argument in the paper. In GRPO, the response is no longer updated uniformly. Instead, each token is scaled by a potentially different factor, and those factors are themselves derived from single token samples. The result is a much noisier gradient signal.

# Comparison of token weighting in GRPO and GSPO

The paper’s key observation is that GSPO and GRPO differ in how they weight the gradients of token log-likelihoods. In GSPO, all tokens in a response are weighted equally by the same response-level factor. In GRPO, each token receives its own importance weight.

This distinction is not just cosmetic. Because the importance ratios in GRPO are token-specific, the gradient contributions of different tokens within the same response can be unevenly amplified or suppressed. The paper points out that these weights can vary in ranges that depend on the sign of the advantage. For positive advantages, the weights can lie in $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$, while for negative advantages they can lie in $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$. As a result, the gradient signal within a response is not uniform.

GSPO avoids this internal imbalance. Since its ratio is computed at the sequence level, every token in a response is modulated equally. This means the response behaves as a single unit during optimization, which is much closer to how the reward is assigned. The paper argues that this alignment between reward unit and optimization unit is a central reason GSPO is more stable.

# Unequal token weighting in GRPO as an instability source

The paper identifies GRPO’s token-level importance weighting as a fundamental source of instability. The problem is not merely that the weights are different; it is that they are different for reasons that do not faithfully correct the sampling mismatch. Importance sampling is meant to reweight samples drawn from a behavior distribution so that they approximate expectations under a target distribution. But that logic requires averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution. In GRPO, the token-level ratio is computed from a single token sample at each position, which is too small a unit to reliably perform the distribution correction it is supposed to represent.

Because the token-level weights are misapplied in this way, they introduce high-variance noise into the gradients. The paper argues that this noise accumulates over long responses. Each token contributes a slightly distorted weighted gradient, and as the response grows longer, those distortions pile up. The clipping mechanism does not remove the underlying issue; instead, it can amplify the instability because it interacts with already noisy token-wise ratios.

This is why the paper describes GRPO’s objective as ill-posed in practice. The instability is not simply an optimization inconvenience. It is presented as a structural flaw that can eventually cause catastrophic model collapse.

# How clipping intervals interact with positive and negative advantages

Clipping is used in both PPO-style methods and GRPO-style methods to restrict overly off-policy updates. In the paper’s analysis, clipping matters because the effect of the importance ratio depends on the sign of the advantage.

When the advantage is positive, a larger ratio increases the update, but clipping prevents the ratio from becoming too large. When the advantage is negative, the ratio pushes the update in the opposite direction, and clipping again bounds the update. However, because GRPO applies this mechanism token by token, the clipping threshold is enforced separately at each token position rather than on the response as a whole.

That tokenwise clipping creates another layer of irregularity. The paper notes that the actual ranges of the importance ratios can be very different between GRPO and GSPO because their ratio definitions are different. In GSPO, the response-level ratio is already length-normalized and therefore numerically more controlled. In GRPO, the token-level ratios can fluctuate independently across positions, causing some token updates to be clipped while others are not. This uneven clipping contributes to the unpredictability of the total response gradient.

Thus, clipping is not inherently the problem; the problem is how it interacts with the unit of optimization. Sequence-level clipping in GSPO is coherent with sequence-level reward. Token-level clipping in GRPO is not, because it acts on a finer granularity than the signal being optimized.

# Accumulation of token-level weighting errors across long responses

The paper repeatedly emphasizes that the instability in GRPO becomes worse as response length grows. This is a natural consequence of token-wise weighting. Each token contributes its own weighted gradient term, and each of those terms may deviate from the ideal distribution-corrected signal. Over long sequences, the discrepancies accumulate.

This accumulation is especially problematic in the settings the paper cares about, such as large language models trained on long-response tasks. In such settings, the sampling mismatch between the old policy and the updated policy becomes more pronounced, and the token-level importance ratios become increasingly unreliable. Since the response has many tokens, even small distortions at each position can compound into a large gradient error.

GSPO avoids this by collapsing the response into a single importance ratio. The response-level ratio still reflects the degree of mismatch between the old and new policies, but it does so in a way that is tied to the whole trajectory rather than to individual token samples. That makes the gradient less sensitive to local fluctuations and therefore more robust over long outputs.

# Why equal per-response weighting improves robustness

The stability advantage of GSPO comes from a simple principle stated explicitly in the paper: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward. Since the reward is assigned to the whole response, the response should be the unit of clipping, weighting, and optimization.

Equal weighting within a response improves robustness because it avoids making the update depend on arbitrary token-by-token fluctuations. The sequence-level ratio captures the overall deviation of a response from the old policy, while the shared response-level factor ensures that all tokens move together. This reduces variance and prevents one part of the response from being disproportionately emphasized simply because its token-wise ratio happened to be extreme.

The paper’s gradient derivation shows that GSPO weights all the token log-likelihood gradients in a response equally. That is the key structural difference. Rather than producing a patchwork of token-specific updates, GSPO produces a coherent response-level update signal. The authors argue that this makes training more stable, more efficient, and more resistant to collapse.

# Practical interpretation of the theoretical gradient difference

The gradient formulas are not just mathematical details; they explain the empirical behavior reported in the paper. GSPO’s response-level gradient is simpler, more consistent, and more aligned with the reward structure. GRPO’s token-level gradient is noisier, because each token carries its own importance ratio and the resulting weights can fluctuate significantly as training progresses.

This difference helps explain why GSPO can continue improving with more compute, longer generations, and regularly updated query sets, while GRPO is more vulnerable to instability. It also helps explain why GSPO is especially effective in MoE training, where token-level likelihoods can fluctuate more sharply because the activated experts can change after each update. In such settings, token-wise importance ratios become even less reliable, which makes GSPO’s response-level formulation particularly valuable.

In practical terms, the gradient analysis says that GSPO trains on a cleaner learning signal. It does not try to over-interpret each token as its own importance-sampled event. Instead, it treats the whole generated response as the unit of optimization, which is exactly where the reward lives. That alignment is the theoretical reason the paper gives for GSPO’s improved robustness over GRPO.

7. GSPO-token: A Flexible Token-Level Variant

# Motivation for token-wise advantage customization

GSPO was introduced as a sequence-level policy optimization method because the paper argues that, for standard single-response reinforcement learning with a sequence-level reward, the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. In that setting, a whole response receives a single score, so it is natural for the importance ratio and clipping mechanism to operate on the whole sequence rather than on individual tokens.

At the same time, the paper notes that there are scenarios in which a purely sequence-level advantage assignment may be too coarse. In particular, in multi-turn reinforcement learning, different parts of a response may play different roles, and one may want finer-grained control over how credit is assigned during optimization. That is the motivation for GSPO-token: it keeps the same sequence-level importance perspective introduced by GSPO, but allows advantages to be customized at the token level when a more detailed reward assignment is useful.

# Scenarios such as multi-turn RL and structured reward assignment

The paper presents multi-turn RL as the main motivating example for GSPO-token. In multi-turn settings, a response may contain multiple pieces of behavior that should not necessarily be treated as equally responsible for the final reward. A single response-level advantage may be too blunt if one wants to emphasize or de-emphasize specific tokens according to the structure of the task.

GSPO-token is therefore introduced as a flexible variant that supports token-wise advantage customization. The paper does not change the basic sequence-level correction principle of GSPO; instead, it creates a way to attach token-level advantage signals while still using the same sequence-level likelihood ratio as the central weighting mechanism. This makes GSPO-token suitable when one wants more detailed reward allocation but does not want to return to the problematic token-level importance weighting used in GRPO.

# Definition of the GSPO-token objective

The GSPO-token objective is written as

$$
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\min \left( s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) 
\right],
$$

where $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ is the token-specific advantage for token $y_{i,t}$, and $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is defined through a construction that is numerically equal to the sequence-level importance ratio. The objective is structurally similar to GSPO, but the advantage can now vary by token rather than being fixed for the entire response.

This formulation preserves the same outer logic as GSPO: samples are drawn from the old policy, the update uses clipped importance-based optimization, and the goal is still to use a stable importance ratio that reflects the response as a whole. The difference is that the reward signal inside the sum may now change from token to token.

# The stop-gradient or detach operation and why it is used

A key technical device in GSPO-token is the stop-gradient operator, denoted $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$. The paper explains that this operator means taking the numerical value of an expression while preventing gradients from flowing through it, corresponding to the `detach` operation in PyTorch.

This is used so that GSPO-token can build a token-level term that has the same numerical value as the sequence-level importance ratio without changing the gradient path in an unintended way. The construction uses

$$
s_{i,t}(\theta) 
= \mathrm{sg} \left[ s_{i}(\theta) \right] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }.
$$

Because the denominator is detached, the fraction has numerical value $1$. The purpose is not to introduce a new probability correction factor, but to embed the token-specific optimization term inside a construction that still behaves like the sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$ in value. In other words, the detach operation is what makes the token-level expression usable without turning it into a different importance weight.

# Constructing token-level terms that are numerically equal to the sequence ratio

The paper emphasizes that $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$. This is because the ratio

$$
\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }
$$

has numerical value $1$. Therefore, multiplying by $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ yields a token-level expression whose value matches the sequence-level importance ratio. This lets the token-level objective remain aligned with GSPO’s sequence-based scaling, even though the reward weights $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ may vary across positions.

This construction is important because it avoids replacing the sequence-level importance ratio with a genuinely token-level ratio of the GRPO type. The paper is explicit that GSPO-token is not intended to reintroduce the unstable token-wise off-policy correction that GSPO was designed to avoid. Instead, the token-level term is a mathematical device for distributing advantages across tokens while preserving the same sequence-level ratio numerically.

# Gradient derivation for GSPO-token

The gradient derivation in the paper shows that GSPO-token remains tied to the sequence-level ratio in its optimization behavior. Starting from the objective and ignoring clipping for brevity, the gradient is derived as

$$
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}  s_i (\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\widehat{A}_{i,t} \frac{ \nabla_{\theta} \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }
\right],
$$

which is then rewritten as

$$
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}  \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})  
\right].
$$

This derivation makes clear what GSPO-token is doing. The gradient is still scaled by the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$, and the per-token terms only appear inside the inner sum through their advantages and log-likelihood gradients. Thus, the optimization is sequence-weighted, not token-weighted in the GRPO sense.

# How GSPO-token preserves sequence-level weighting in the gradient

The paper’s central claim about GSPO-token is that it preserves the same sequence-level weighting behavior as GSPO. This is visible in the gradient, where the factor

$$
\left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
$$

multiplies the whole token-wise gradient contribution for a response. So although the advantages may differ across tokens, the response itself is still weighted as a sequence. The sequence ratio acts as a shared multiplier for all tokens in the response.

This is exactly the distinction the paper wants to preserve. GSPO-token does not assign independent importance ratios to individual tokens. Instead, it uses a single sequence-level ratio to control how much the full response contributes to the update, while letting the advantage inside the response vary by token. In that sense, GSPO-token keeps GSPO’s stable sequence-level optimization structure intact.

# Conditions under which GSPO-token is equivalent to GSPO

The paper states that GSPO-token and GSPO are numerically identical in the optimization objective, clipping condition, and theoretical gradient when all tokens in a response share the same advantage, that is, when $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$ for every token $t$ in response $y_i$. Under this condition, the token-level sum collapses to the same behavior as the original GSPO objective.

This equivalence is important because it shows that GSPO-token is not a fundamentally different algorithm in the common case where the reward is response-level. Instead, it is a generalization. When token-specific advantages are unnecessary, GSPO-token reduces to GSPO. When token-specific advantages are useful, it offers more flexibility without changing the underlying sequence-level ratio machinery.

# The tradeoff between flexibility and conceptual simplicity

GSPO-token introduces a useful degree of flexibility, but the paper also makes clear that this flexibility comes with a tradeoff. GSPO is conceptually simpler because everything is defined at the sequence level: the ratio, the clipping, and the advantage all align directly with the response as a whole. GSPO-token adds extra freedom by permitting token-wise advantages, but that also makes the reward assignment more elaborate.

The important point is that this added flexibility does not come at the cost of reverting to the problematic token-level importance ratio design of GRPO. GSPO-token keeps the sequence-level weighting principle intact while allowing finer control over how advantages are distributed across tokens. In the paper’s framing, this makes GSPO-token a practical extension for cases like multi-turn RL, while GSPO remains the cleaner default when a single response-level advantage is sufficient.

8. Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol

# The cold-start model based on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base

The experiments in the paper are carried out with a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base. This matters because the evaluation is not about training a model from scratch, but about starting from a strong pretrained base and then applying reinforcement learning to improve reasoning and coding performance. In the paper’s framing, this kind of setup is the relevant testbed for studying whether an RL algorithm can stably and efficiently scale up large-language-model capability.

Using a cold-start model also makes the comparison between algorithms more informative. The model already has substantial language competence, so the RL procedure is not trying to discover basic linguistic ability. Instead, it is pushing the model toward stronger performance on tasks that require deeper reasoning, longer deliberation, and better answer selection. In that setting, training stability becomes central: if the optimization procedure is unstable, the model can collapse even after it has already acquired useful capabilities. The paper uses this experimental context to show that GSPO can continue improving such a model reliably.

# Training with rollout batches partitioned into multiple mini-batches

A key part of the training setup is that each batch of rollout data is partitioned into four mini-batches for gradient updates. This detail is important because it reflects the practical regime in which reinforcement learning for large language models is actually run. Rollout generation is expensive, so the collected samples are reused across multiple optimization steps rather than discarded after a single update.

This reuse means the policy being updated is no longer exactly the same as the policy that generated the samples. In other words, the training procedure naturally creates an off-policy learning setting. The paper emphasizes that this is not a side issue but a fundamental consequence of using large rollout batches and then subdividing them to improve sample efficiency and hardware utilization. The comparison between GSPO and GRPO is therefore made under a realistic multi-mini-batch training regime rather than an idealized on-policy one.

# Why this setup creates a meaningful off-policy optimization regime

The paper’s discussion makes clear that splitting rollout data into multiple mini-batches is what turns the optimization into an off-policy problem. Samples are generated by an older policy, $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and then used to update a newer policy, $\pi_{\theta}$. As gradient updates proceed over the mini-batches, the current policy drifts farther from the behavior policy that produced the samples. This discrepancy is exactly why importance ratios and clipping are needed in PPO- and GRPO-style methods.

In this evaluation setting, the off-policy nature is not accidental; it is the mechanism that tests whether the algorithm can handle practical large-scale RL training. The paper argues that this is also where GRPO becomes fragile, because its token-level importance ratios are not well suited to correcting the mismatch. By contrast, GSPO is designed specifically for this regime by using sequence-level likelihood ratios, which the paper argues are more aligned with the actual optimization target and more stable under repeated mini-batch updates.

# Benchmarks used in evaluation: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces

The paper evaluates training progress and final performance on three benchmarks: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces. These benchmarks are used to track how RL training affects the model’s ability to solve difficult reasoning and coding problems. The choice of these tasks is consistent with the paper’s broader theme that large-scale RL is meant to improve performance on competition-level mathematics and programming.

The evaluation is reported as training reward curves together with benchmark performance curves, so the benchmarks function not just as end-of-training tests but as indicators of learning dynamics over time. The paper reports that GSPO yields stable progress throughout training and continues improving as compute increases, the query set is updated, and generation length is extended. This makes the benchmark suite part of the evidence that GSPO supports sustained capability gains rather than short-lived improvements.

# Metrics such as Pass@1 and Elo Rating

The paper uses different metrics depending on the benchmark. For AIME'24, the reported metric is average Pass@1 over 32 samplings. For LiveCodeBench, the metric is average Pass@1 over 8 samplings, using the 202410-202502 split. For CodeForces, the paper uses Elo Rating.

These metrics capture different aspects of model quality. Pass@1 measures how often the model’s first sampled answer is correct under repeated sampling, which is a useful way to evaluate the model’s effective problem-solving ability under stochastic generation. Elo Rating, on the other hand, is used to characterize CodeForces performance in a ranking-style setting. The paper does not present these metrics as abstract quantities; rather, they are used to show whether the RL algorithm improves the model in a way that transfers to demanding external evaluations.

# Comparison protocol between GSPO and GRPO

The paper compares GSPO against GRPO as the baseline. The comparison is intended to be fair, and for that reason the clipping ranges in each method are carefully tuned. The authors explicitly note that the GRPO settings are chosen to ensure a fair comparison, rather than simply using default values. This is important because clipping strongly affects the stability and behavior of policy-gradient RL methods.

The comparison also reflects a broader methodological point: GSPO is not evaluated in isolation, but in the same RL training pipeline where GRPO would normally be used. The paper highlights that GSPO achieves better training efficiency and stronger benchmark performance under the same training compute and consumed queries. In other words, the protocol is designed to measure not only end performance but also how much useful learning each method extracts from the same training budget.

# Clipping hyperparameters for GSPO and GRPO

The paper reports different clipping ranges for the two methods. For GSPO, the left and right clipping ranges in the sequence-level objective are set to $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$, respectively. For GRPO, the corresponding clipping ranges in its token-level objective are set to $0.2$ and $0.27$, respectively.

These values are not interchangeable because GSPO and GRPO define importance ratios differently. The paper stresses that the clipping ranges in GSPO and prior algorithms typically differ by orders of magnitude precisely because the underlying ratios are defined at different levels. This is why the authors do not compare the methods using identical numerical clipping values. Instead, each method is tuned in the scale appropriate to its own ratio definition.

# Why careful clipping-range tuning matters for fair evaluation

The paper gives special attention to clipping because it is one of the main mechanisms that controls stability in off-policy RL. If clipping is too loose, unstable samples can dominate the gradient; if it is too tight, learning may be overly constrained. Since GRPO’s token-level importance ratios and GSPO’s sequence-level ratios behave very differently, the paper argues that fair evaluation requires tuning each method in a way that suits its own objective.

This is also tied to the paper’s broader claim about instability. A poor choice of clipping range could make one algorithm appear worse simply because its ratio scale is mismatched, not because the algorithmic principle is inferior. By carefully selecting clipping ranges for both methods, the authors aim to isolate the effect of the optimization design itself. That is why the comparison is framed as a test of the algorithms’ intrinsic stability and efficiency rather than a test of arbitrary hyperparameter defaults.

# The role of query updates and generation length extension during training

The paper notes that GSPO supports continuous performance improvement through increasing training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending the generation length. These are not treated as incidental details; they are part of the training dynamics that demonstrate whether the method can scale. In the paper’s discussion, these changes are presented as ways to keep pushing the model’s capability boundaries during RL.

Regular query updates matter because they prevent training from overfitting to a fixed set of prompts and allow the model to keep encountering new or refreshed tasks. Extending generation length matters because the paper’s motivation centers on long-response tasks, where instability in prior methods becomes especially severe. The fact that GSPO remains stable under these changes is part of the evidence that it can serve as a practical foundation for continued RL scaling, rather than being limited to a narrow training regime.

10. Clipping Behavior and the Surprising Efficiency of GSPO

# What clipping fraction means in PPO-style algorithms

In PPO-style reinforcement learning, clipping is a mechanism for limiting how far a new policy update can move away from the policy that generated the training samples. In the paper’s notation, this is done by comparing the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$ against the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ through an importance ratio, and then constraining that ratio to lie within a fixed interval such as $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$. The purpose is to prevent overly off-policy samples from dominating the gradient update.

The “clipping fraction” is the fraction of sampled units whose importance ratios fall outside the permitted range and are therefore clipped. In a token-level method like GRPO, this fraction is measured over individual tokens. In GSPO, clipping is applied at the sequence level, so the relevant unit is the whole response rather than each token. This difference turns out to matter a great deal, because it changes what counts as an over-updated sample and how much of the generated data is excluded from direct gradient contribution.

The paper’s framing makes clipping a practical safeguard, not the main learning signal. The learning signal still comes from the reward and advantage, but clipping determines which sampled units are considered too far from the old policy to be trusted. In that sense, the clipping fraction gives a direct view into how much of the rollout batch is being treated as unstable or unreliable under the current optimization rule.

# The distinction between clipping responses and clipping individual tokens

The central design difference between GSPO and GRPO in this chapter is where clipping is applied. GRPO applies the PPO-style clipping rule to each token importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, while GSPO applies it to the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$. This means that in GRPO, every token in a response is separately judged against the clipping boundary, whereas in GSPO the entire response is judged as a single unit.

This distinction is not merely cosmetic. In the paper, the authors argue that the reward is sequence-level, so the optimization should also be sequence-level. When clipping is applied token by token, a response may be partially accepted and partially clipped, even though the reward is attached to the whole response. GSPO avoids this mismatch by making the unit of optimization align with the unit of reward.

The result is a different learning geometry. Under GRPO, the gradient estimate depends on many token-level weights that can differ within the same response. Under GSPO, all tokens in a response share the same sequence-level weight. That uniformity is important for the paper’s stability argument, because it removes a source of uneven, tokenwise perturbation that can accumulate over long generations.

# Observed two-orders-of-magnitude difference in clipped-token fractions

One of the most striking empirical observations in the paper is that GSPO and GRPO differ by about two orders of magnitude in the fraction of clipped tokens. The paper emphasizes that GSPO clips entire responses, and as a consequence, many more tokens are effectively excluded from gradient estimation than under GRPO’s token-level rule. This is surprising because one might expect that clipping more data would reduce learning efficiency.

Yet the paper reports the opposite: despite clipping significantly more tokens, GSPO still achieves higher training efficiency. This means that the raw number of clipped tokens does not directly predict how much useful learning occurs. What matters is not simply how many tokens are retained, but how informative and stable the resulting gradient estimates are.

The authors present this as a strong clue that the token-level gradients in GRPO are not exploiting samples efficiently. If GRPO keeps more tokens but uses them in a noisier way, then retaining those extra tokens may not provide a real benefit. GSPO’s apparently harsher clipping behavior therefore becomes a strength rather than a weakness, because it removes unreliable contributions and preserves a cleaner optimization signal.

# Why changing clipping ranges does not erase the qualitative difference

The paper notes that adjusting the clipping ranges does not remove the large gap in clipped-token fractions between GSPO and GRPO. This matters because it shows that the difference is not just a numerical artifact of selecting one range versus another. Even when the clipping thresholds are tuned, the qualitative behavior remains the same.

This reinforces the interpretation that the difference comes from the structure of the importance ratio itself. GRPO uses token-level ratios, so clipping is sensitive to the behavior of each token probability. GSPO uses a sequence-level ratio formed from the whole response likelihood and normalized by length. Because these are fundamentally different quantities, they respond differently to the same clipping logic.

The paper’s point is that the clipping rule is not independent of the importance-ratio definition. Once the ratio is defined at a different level of granularity, the meaning of “too far off-policy” changes as well. So even if the clipping range is modified, the gap between token-level and sequence-level clipping behavior remains because the underlying optimization unit has changed.

# Why more clipping can still yield better learning

The seemingly counterintuitive result in the paper is that GSPO clips more but learns better. The explanation given is that clipping more can be beneficial when what gets removed is noisy or unreliable. In GSPO, entire responses that are too far from the old policy are excluded from gradient estimation at the sequence level, which makes the remaining learning signal more trustworthy.

This is tied to the paper’s broader argument that GRPO’s token-level importance weights do not properly correct distribution mismatch. Because each token ratio is based on a single sampled token from the next-token distribution, the weights introduce high-variance noise instead of a reliable correction. Clipping then interacts with this noise in a way that can destabilize training further. GSPO avoids this by moving the correction to the response level, where the likelihood ratio has a clearer theoretical meaning and is less volatile.

So the paper’s lesson is not that clipping is inherently good or bad. Rather, clipping is helpful when it filters out genuinely problematic samples, and harmful when it is trying to manage an already noisy and ill-posed weighting scheme. GSPO makes clipping serve the right purpose by applying it at the same level as the reward.

# The idea that fewer but cleaner gradients can outperform noisier gradients

The paper’s clipping observation supports a broader optimization principle: fewer but cleaner gradients can outperform noisier gradients. GSPO uses more aggressive exclusion of unreliable samples, but the gradients that remain are more stable and more aligned with the actual reward structure. As a result, the method can make better use of the compute it spends on training.

This is why the paper emphasizes training efficiency rather than simply the amount of data used. A method that reuses more tokens is not necessarily more sample efficient if those tokens produce gradients with high variance or unstable direction. In contrast, GSPO’s sequence-level filtering can discard more data while still extracting a stronger learning signal from what remains.

In the paper’s empirical discussion, this is reflected in the claim that GSPO achieves better training accuracy and benchmark performance under the same training compute and consumed queries. The message is that efficiency is not about maximizing token usage at all costs. It is about converting rollout data into a reliable policy update.

# What this tells us about GRPO’s token-level gradient estimates

The clipping results are used in the paper to support a deeper diagnosis of GRPO: its token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient. The authors argue that because each token importance ratio is based on a single sampled token, it does not act like a proper importance-sampling correction. Instead, it injects variance into the gradient estimate, and that variance accumulates over the length of the response.

The clipping fraction observation strengthens this argument. If GRPO can retain many tokens yet still underperform GSPO, then the issue is not simply how much data is used. The issue is that the token-level weights themselves are not producing a stable learning signal. The paper connects this directly to the instability and even collapse observed in large-scale RL training.

In other words, the clipping experiment is not just a side note. It is evidence for the claim that the token-level objective is fundamentally misaligned with sequence-level reward. The fact that GSPO clips more but learns better suggests that the token-level route is keeping too much low-quality signal, while the sequence-level route is filtering that signal into something more usable.

# Implications for sample efficiency and optimization design

The main design lesson from this chapter is that sample efficiency depends on the quality of the optimization signal, not just the quantity of retained tokens. GSPO shows that a method can exclude a larger fraction of tokens and still make better use of the available compute. This is because the response-level formulation better matches the structure of the reward and avoids the unstable behavior of token-wise importance ratios.

The paper also suggests a more general principle for RL algorithm design: the unit of correction, clipping, and optimization should match the unit at which reward is assigned. When this alignment is broken, as in GRPO, the update can become noisy, especially for long responses and large models. When it is restored, as in GSPO, clipping becomes a mechanism for improving robustness rather than a compromise that merely limits damage.

This has practical consequences. It means that the apparent cost of heavier clipping in GSPO does not translate into worse learning. Instead, it can be part of why GSPO scales better. The paper’s empirical results show that this sequence-level approach yields better training stability, stronger efficiency, and improved performance, which together justify the claim that more selective but cleaner optimization can be superior to broader but noisier token-level learning.

11. Mixture-of-Experts Models and the Routing Replay Problem

# What Mixture-of-Experts models are and why they matter

The paper discusses Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, models as a special class of large language models whose training behavior differs in important ways from dense models. The key feature of an MoE model is its sparse activation nature: for a given input, only a subset of the model’s experts are activated rather than the entire network. This design matters because it allows the model to scale up capacity while keeping the per-token computation manageable. In the context of the paper, this is one reason MoE models are attractive for large-scale reinforcement learning, especially when the goal is to improve reasoning performance on hard tasks through continued training.

However, the same sparse structure that makes MoE models attractive also creates a new source of instability during reinforcement learning. Because different experts may be selected for different responses, and even for the same response after a policy update, the behavior of the model can change in ways that are not well captured by token-level optimization methods. The paper argues that this is not a minor implementation detail but a structural issue that becomes especially important when training large models with long responses.

# Sparse activation and its consequences for RL training

The paper emphasizes that sparse activation introduces unique stability challenges compared with dense models. In RL training, one repeatedly samples responses from an old policy and then performs gradient updates to improve the current policy. For dense models, the mapping from a token to its likelihood under the model is relatively stable across small updates. For MoE models, this is complicated by the fact that the experts active for a response may change after an update.

This matters because GRPO and related token-level RL methods rely on token-level importance ratios. If the activated computation path changes, then the probability assigned to a token is no longer based on the same underlying activated network before and after the update. In other words, the token likelihoods being compared are not fully comparable. The paper presents this as one reason why sparse activation can break the assumptions behind token-level policy optimization and make training unstable.

The practical consequence is that RL training on MoE models can fail to converge properly. The paper treats this as a serious obstacle, because one of the major goals of RL scaling is to make large models better by training them longer and on harder tasks. If the training dynamics become unstable, then the benefits of scaling are lost.

# Expert-activation volatility across policy updates

The paper introduces the term expert-activation volatility to describe what happens when the set of experts activated for the same response changes significantly after one or more gradient updates. This volatility is especially important because RL training is iterative: the policy is updated repeatedly, and each update can alter which experts are selected for a given token sequence.

The authors provide a concrete observation from a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model. After each RL gradient update, for the same rollout sample, roughly $10\%$ of the experts activated under the new policy $\pi_\theta$ differ from those under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The paper notes that this phenomenon becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models. That is an important point: as the model becomes more complex, the routing behavior can become even less stable.

This volatility is not just a curiosity. It directly undermines the validity of token-level importance ratios such as
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
If the activated experts change, then the likelihood ratio at the token level can fluctuate drastically from update to update. The paper argues that this makes the token-level ratio increasingly unreliable as a basis for optimization.

# How routing changes affect token-level likelihood ratios

The paper’s core criticism of GRPO in the MoE setting is that routing changes make token-level likelihood ratios unstable and effectively invalid. Since the token probability depends on the particular expert path used to process that token, any change in routing can alter the likelihood in a way that is unrelated to the intended policy improvement signal.

This becomes especially problematic because GRPO uses those token-level ratios inside its optimization objective. If the ratio varies sharply because the routing path changed, then the gradient estimate inherits this instability. The paper connects this directly to the broader issue discussed earlier in the chapter on GSPO: the token-level importance weight is being used in a situation where it does not serve its intended distribution-correction role. In the MoE case, routing volatility makes that failure even worse.

The result is that the gradient signal becomes noisy and difficult to interpret. The paper states that this can hinder the normal convergence of RL training. In other words, even if the reward signal is well defined, the optimization machinery that tries to use it is disrupted by the instability of the token-level probabilities.

# Why the GRPO problem becomes worse in deeper MoE models

The paper explicitly notes that expert-activation volatility becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models. This is important because deeper models are precisely where one might expect to push RL scaling further. If the routing instability increases with depth, then the mismatch between token-level ratios and actual model behavior becomes more severe as the model scales.

The paper’s example of the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model illustrates this point. Even after a single RL update, a substantial fraction of experts for the same response can change. The deeper the model, the more opportunities there are for routing differences to accumulate. As a result, the token-level importance ratios fluctuate more dramatically, and the instability of GRPO is amplified.

This is why the paper treats MoE models not as a minor edge case, but as a revealing stress test for RL algorithms. If an algorithm cannot remain stable in this setting, it is unlikely to be a robust foundation for large-scale RL training.

# The Routing Replay strategy: caching and replaying expert routes

Before introducing GSPO, the paper describes a workaround that was previously used to make GRPO training converge on MoE models: Routing Replay. The basic idea is to cache the activated experts used under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and then replay those same routing modes when computing likelihoods under the current policy $\pi_\theta$.

This means that, for each token $y_{i,t}$, the model computes both $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$ using the same activated network. By forcing the current policy to follow the same routing pattern as the old policy, Routing Replay makes the token-level importance ratio more stable.

The purpose of this strategy is straightforward: if the ratio is unstable because the computation path changes, then reuse the old computation path so that the ratio compares like with like. In the paper’s discussion, this is presented as a way to restore a consistent activated network across gradient updates and thereby make the token-level optimization of GRPO workable.

# How Routing Replay stabilizes GRPO

According to the paper, Routing Replay is essential for the normal convergence of GRPO when training MoE models. By eliminating routing differences between the old and current policies during likelihood computation, it reduces the volatility that would otherwise make the token-level importance ratios fluctuate unpredictably.

This stabilization is valuable because it allows the optimization procedure to proceed using a consistent network path. In effect, Routing Replay patches over the mismatch between the token-level objective and the routing behavior of the model. With the same experts replayed across policies, the gradient update is less likely to be distorted by changes in expert assignment.

The paper presents this as a practical workaround rather than a true solution. It helps GRPO avoid immediate failure, but it does so by constraining the model’s routing behavior during optimization. That means the training process remains tied to a special mechanism whose purpose is purely compensatory.

# Memory, communication, and capacity costs of Routing Replay

Although Routing Replay helps with stability, the paper argues that it comes at a cost. First, caching and replaying expert routes adds extra memory overhead. Second, it introduces communication overhead. Third, it can limit the actual capacity of the MoE model.

These costs matter because one of the main reasons to use MoE models is to gain capacity efficiently. If the training procedure requires holding onto routing information and reusing it in a way that constrains the model, then the model may not fully realize its sparse-activation advantage. The paper frames this as an artificial limitation imposed by the workaround.

In this sense, Routing Replay is not an elegant or scalable answer. It keeps GRPO from breaking, but it does so by adding complexity to the training pipeline and by interfering with the natural behavior of the model. The paper presents GSPO as a way to eliminate the need for this workaround altogether.

# Why GSPO does not depend on token-level routing consistency

The central reason GSPO avoids the Routing Replay problem is that it does not rely on token-level likelihood ratios. Instead, it uses the sequence-level importance ratio
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.
\]
This ratio is based on the likelihood of the whole response rather than the likelihood of each token independently.

That difference is crucial for MoE models. The paper argues that GSPO focuses on the sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)$ and is therefore not sensitive to the individual token likelihoods $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$ in the same way GRPO is. Since the model’s language modeling capability as a whole remains intact, the sequence likelihood does not fluctuate as drastically as the token-level likelihoods caused by routing volatility.

As a result, GSPO can compute importance ratios conventionally, without needing to preserve the exact token-level routing path from the old policy. This makes the algorithm much less vulnerable to the expert-activation instability that harms GRPO.

# Sequence likelihood stability despite token-level expert changes

The paper’s intuition is that even if some experts change between $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$, the overall sequence likelihood remains a more stable object than individual token probabilities. This is because GSPO evaluates the response as a whole, and its optimization signal is aligned with the sequence-level reward.

That alignment matters. The reward in the paper is assigned to the full query-response pair $(x,y)$, not to individual tokens. Since the scoring function operates at the sequence level, GSPO’s use of sequence likelihood is a better match for the structure of the problem. The paper presents this as one reason why GSPO can remain stable even when the internal routing choices of an MoE model change.

This is the key conceptual shift: rather than trying to force token-level consistency in a setting where the routing itself is volatile, GSPO changes the unit of optimization to the sequence. That lets it bypass the specific failure mode that makes GRPO difficult to use on MoE models.

# GSPO as a fundamental solution to MoE RL instability

The paper’s main conclusion in this setting is that GSPO fundamentally resolves the expert-activation volatility issue in MoE models. Unlike Routing Replay, which compensates for the instability by preserving old routes, GSPO removes the dependence on token-level routing consistency in the first place.

This has several consequences in the paper’s account. It simplifies training, since there is no need for cached routes or replayed expert paths. It stabilizes optimization, since the importance ratio is computed at the sequence level. It also allows the model to use its full capacity without being artificially constrained by replayed routing modes.

In the broader narrative of the paper, this is a central advantage of GSPO. The algorithm does not merely work around instability; it matches the optimization unit to the reward unit and therefore avoids the mismatch that makes token-level methods fragile. For MoE RL training in particular, this is presented as a clean and scalable solution.

12. Infrastructure Implications: Toward Simpler RL Systems

# Training-engine and inference-engine precision mismatches

A central systems issue discussed in the paper is that the engines used for training and for inference are often not identical in practice. The paper specifically notes precision discrepancies between training engines such as Megatron and inference engines such as SGLang and vLLM. This matters because reinforcement learning for language models relies on likelihoods computed for sampled responses under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and those likelihoods are used inside the optimization objective.

The practical consequence is that the same sampled response may not receive exactly the same numerical likelihood when evaluated by different engines. The paper treats this as a precision mismatch problem rather than an algorithmic one. In a system that depends heavily on token-level likelihoods, even small numerical differences can become problematic because the optimization is sensitive to per-token values. GSPO is introduced partly as a response to this systems friction, because its sequence-level formulation is described as more tolerant to such discrepancies.

# Why old-policy likelihoods are often recomputed in practice

The paper explains that, in practice, likelihoods for sampled responses under the old policy are typically recomputed using the training engine. This is done because the training procedure needs those likelihoods for optimization, and if the rollout was produced elsewhere, the training system wants a consistent computation path. In other words, even though samples may come from an inference system, the training stack often recomputes the relevant quantities so that optimization can proceed using the training engine’s own numerical outputs.

This recomputation is a practical burden because it adds extra work to the RL pipeline. The paper presents it as something that becomes less necessary when the optimization objective is sequence-level rather than token-level. Since GSPO uses sequence-level likelihoods, it reduces the need to insist on exact token-by-token recomputation under the training engine.

# The burden of token-level likelihood dependence in existing methods

The paper’s critique of existing methods is not only statistical but also infrastructural. Methods like GRPO depend on token-level importance ratios, meaning that the training objective is tied directly to individual next-token likelihoods. This creates a strong dependency on fine-grained likelihood computations, which must be accurate at every token position.

That token-level dependence makes the system brittle. If the likelihoods differ slightly across engines, or if the routing behavior of a model changes between the old and new policies, the token-level ratios can fluctuate substantially. The paper argues that this is especially problematic for GRPO and even more so in Mixture-of-Experts settings, where token-level likelihoods can become unstable due to expert-activation volatility. From an infrastructure standpoint, token-level dependence forces the system to do more work and to maintain tighter numerical consistency.

# Why sequence-level optimization is more tolerant to precision discrepancies

GSPO replaces token-level importance ratios with a sequence-level importance ratio based on the full response likelihood, defined as $\frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)}$, with length normalization. The paper’s systems argument is that this sequence-level quantity is much more tolerant of precision discrepancies than token-level likelihoods.

The intuition given is straightforward: a single response-level likelihood aggregates over the entire sequence, so it is less sensitive to small numerical inconsistencies at individual token positions. In contrast, token-level objectives expose every local mismatch directly to the gradient. By operating at the sequence level, GSPO therefore makes the optimization signal more robust to the kinds of precision differences that arise when training and inference use different engines.

# Potential to use inference-engine likelihoods directly

One of the clearest infrastructure implications in the paper is that GSPO may allow practitioners to use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine directly for optimization. The paper emphasizes that this is possible because GSPO uses sequence-level likelihoods rather than token-level ones. Since the sequence-level quantity is more tolerant of precision discrepancies, the system does not necessarily need to recompute the old-policy likelihoods with the training engine.

This is presented as a meaningful simplification. If the inference engine can already provide the likelihoods needed by GSPO, then the training pipeline can avoid an extra recomputation step. The paper frames this as a direct benefit of the algorithmic design, not as an auxiliary trick. In this sense, GSPO does more than improve stability and efficiency; it also opens the door to a leaner implementation strategy.

# Benefits for partial rollout settings

The paper specifically mentions that this direct use of inference-engine likelihoods can be especially beneficial in partial rollout settings. Although the paper does not elaborate extensively on the mechanics of partial rollout, it highlights this setting as one where the infrastructure simplification becomes valuable. In such cases, the system may be assembling or processing only part of the rollout data at a time, which makes a clean separation between rollout generation and optimization particularly useful.

Because GSPO works at the sequence level, it aligns more naturally with these settings. The system can rely on the response-level likelihoods associated with sampled sequences rather than having to reconstruct or re-evaluate every token-level contribution in the training engine. This reduces friction in the rollout-to-training pipeline.

# Benefits for multi-turn RL

The paper also identifies multi-turn RL as a setting that can benefit from GSPO’s infrastructure properties. Multi-turn interaction naturally creates more complex rollout structures, because a response may be embedded in a longer interaction history and may require finer handling of how data is passed through the system. In such settings, the simplicity of sequence-level likelihood use becomes especially attractive.

The paper already introduces a token-level variant, GSPO-token, for cases where finer-grained advantage adjustment is desired. Even so, the broader infrastructure claim remains: GSPO’s sequence-level formulation makes the optimization side less dependent on precise token-level recomputation. That means multi-turn RL systems may be easier to organize if they can work directly with sequence likelihoods rather than maintaining a strict token-level training-engine computation path.

# Benefits for training-inference disaggregated architectures

The paper points out that sequence-level likelihoods are useful in training-inference disaggregated frameworks. The central reason is that these architectures separate the system that generates rollouts from the system that performs optimization. Once the rollout and training components are decoupled, the question of which engine computes the likelihoods becomes important.

GSPO reduces the pressure to recompute likelihoods in the training engine, because its optimization signal is built from sequence-level likelihoods that are less sensitive to precision mismatches. This makes it more compatible with a pipeline in which inference-time generation and training-time optimization are handled by different systems. In effect, GSPO helps bridge the gap between the rollout side and the optimization side without requiring exact low-level parity in every token probability computation.

# How GSPO may simplify RL infrastructure design

The paper’s broader infrastructure claim is that GSPO has the potential to simplify RL system design. This follows from several properties taken together. First, GSPO removes dependence on token-level importance ratios. Second, it reduces sensitivity to precision discrepancies between inference and training engines. Third, it may allow direct use of inference-engine likelihoods without recomputation. Fourth, it avoids some of the specialized workarounds that were needed for stability in previous methods, especially in MoE training.

In the paper’s view, these properties mean that GSPO is not only a better optimization algorithm but also a better systems primitive. A system built around sequence-level likelihoods can be simpler because it has fewer moving parts tied to exact token-level consistency. The paper closes this line of argument by suggesting that GSPO could serve as a robust and scalable foundation for RL infrastructure, helping future systems train large language models more cleanly and with less complexity.

13. Practical Guidance for Implementing GSPO

# Choosing group size and constructing grouped rollouts

GSPO is built around the idea that each query should be paired with a group of multiple sampled responses, and that the rewards of those responses should be compared relative to one another. In the paper, this group structure is not just a convenience; it is central to how GSPO defines advantage. For each query $x$, a set of responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ is sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)$, and the group size $G$ determines how many responses are available for relative normalization. The paper uses this same group construction both in the GRPO baseline and in GSPO, so implementing GSPO begins with building rollout batches that preserve the query-level grouping.

The purpose of grouping is to enable normalized relative reward computation within each query. Each response receives a reward $r(x,y_i)$ from a verifier, and then the group statistics, namely the mean and standard deviation of the rewards in the group, are used to define the advantage. This means that the group should be constructed so that the responses genuinely correspond to the same query, because the normalization is meaningful only within that shared context. The paper’s formulation assumes that the group members are comparable samples from the same prompt, not arbitrary responses mixed across different queries.

In practice, this also means that the rollout batch is not treated as a flat set of samples. The implementation must preserve the indexing of which responses belong to which query, because the advantage calculation and the sequence-level optimization both operate at that grouped level. The paper emphasizes that the objective is aligned with sequence-level rewarding, so the grouping structure should be maintained throughout the data pipeline rather than reconstructed later in an ad hoc way.

# Computing normalized group advantages safely

GSPO uses a group-based advantage estimate:
\[
\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}.
\]
This is one of the most important implementation pieces because it replaces the need for a value model. Instead of learning a separate critic, the method normalizes the rewards across the group so that each response is judged relative to its peers. The paper’s framing makes clear that this is not an optional detail: it is the mechanism by which GSPO keeps the advantage signal consistent with the sequence-level reward setting.

When implementing this step, the most important requirement is to compute the mean and standard deviation over the reward set associated with the same query. The normalized advantage should then be assigned to the full response in GSPO. In the paper’s baseline comparison, every token in a response shares the same $\widehat{A}_i$, and GSPO keeps that same group-level advantage structure. The key practical idea is that the reward is for the whole sequence, so the advantage should also be sequence-level unless one intentionally uses the GSPO-token variant.

Because the paper describes reward normalization rather than any learned estimate, the implementation should preserve the exact group statistics used to form the advantage. This is especially important in long-response or large-scale settings, where the paper notes that the sequence-level approach avoids the high variance issues that arise from token-level weighting. The safe implementation is therefore the one that keeps the reward normalization tightly tied to the sampled group and does not mix statistics across unrelated samples.

# Implementing sequence likelihood ratios from token log probabilities

The central change from GRPO to GSPO is how the importance ratio is defined. GSPO uses the sequence-level ratio
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}.
\]
The paper also gives the equivalent log form:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
This means that implementation can proceed directly from token log probabilities, but the final ratio is sequence-level and length-normalized.

The practical point is that the code should accumulate token log-probability differences over the whole response, then exponentiate the average difference. The paper emphasizes that this sequence-level ratio has a clear theoretical meaning: it measures how far the sampled response under the old policy deviates from the current policy, and it aligns naturally with the sequence-level reward. This is precisely what makes GSPO different from the tokenwise ratios used in GRPO.

A useful mental model is that GSPO does not ask, token by token, whether each token is “important” in isolation. Instead, it asks whether the whole response has become more or less likely under the current policy compared to the old one. That entire-response viewpoint is what the clipping mechanism is applied to, and it is what the gradient ultimately weights.

# Applying length normalization correctly

The paper explicitly introduces length normalization in the sequence importance ratio:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}.
\]
This normalization is not presented as a cosmetic choice. The paper says it is used to reduce variance and to keep $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range. Without this normalization, a few token-level likelihood changes could produce dramatic fluctuations in the sequence-level ratio, and responses of different lengths would require different clipping ranges.

This is important for implementation because GSPO is intended to compare responses of potentially different lengths inside the same group and across mini-batches. If the ratio were left unnormalized by length, longer responses could produce ratios on a much wider scale simply because more token factors are multiplied together. By taking the $1/|y_i|$ power, GSPO stabilizes the scale of the ratio so that clipping behaves more consistently.

The paper also notes that the clipping ranges in GSPO and previous algorithms are typically different in order of magnitude because the ratio definitions differ. That means length normalization is part of the reason GSPO can use much smaller numerical clipping thresholds than token-level methods. When implementing GSPO, the length-normalized ratio should therefore be treated as the actual ratio that drives clipping, not as an approximation or a postprocessing detail.

# Selecting clipping ranges appropriate for GSPO’s ratio scale

Clipping is used in GSPO exactly as in PPO-style objectives, but now it acts on sequence-level ratios rather than token-level ones. The objective uses
\[
\min\left(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\right).
\]
The paper’s experiments use clipping ranges of $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$ for GSPO, whereas the GRPO baseline uses $0.2$ and $0.27$. The paper explicitly says that these ranges differ in order of magnitude due to the distinct definitions of importance ratios, so one should not transfer GRPO clipping values directly to GSPO.

The implementation lesson is that the scale of the ratio determines the scale of the clipping range. Since GSPO’s ratio is sequence-level and length-normalized, it lives on a different numerical scale from tokenwise ratios. As a result, the clipping hyperparameters must be selected in a way that matches this ratio definition. The paper does not present a universal rule beyond this scale alignment, but it does make clear that the GSPO clipping range is intentionally much smaller than the GRPO one in their experiments.

The clipping operation itself should be applied to the sequence ratio for the whole response. This is conceptually important because GSPO’s main claim is that clipping should match the unit of reward and the unit of optimization. The response is the unit of reward, so the response is also the unit of clipping. That alignment is one of the core practical differences between GSPO and GRPO.

# Handling mini-batch updates in off-policy training

The paper explains that large rollout batches are typically partitioned into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates. This makes RL training off-policy, because samples are generated from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and then used to update $\theta$ over several optimization steps. GSPO is designed for this setting, and the clipping mechanism is part of what prevents overly off-policy samples from dominating the gradient.

In implementation terms, the important point is that the old-policy samples must remain associated with their generating policy when the update is computed. The paper repeatedly emphasizes that the importance ratio is defined relative to $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, not the current policy at every intermediate step. As mini-batch updates proceed, the current policy changes, so the ratio should continue to be computed against the fixed old policy for that rollout batch. This is what makes the objective a stable off-policy approximation rather than a moving target.

The paper also argues that GSPO is more tolerant of this off-policy setup because its sequence-level ratio is more meaningful and less noisy than the token-level ratio. That is especially relevant when multiple gradient steps are taken over the same rollout data. In such cases, the implementation should preserve the rollout boundary clearly, because the old-policy probabilities need to remain available for the whole batch until the mini-batch optimization is complete.

# Monitoring reward curves and clipping statistics

The paper’s empirical discussion highlights reward curves and performance curves as primary indicators of training progress. GSPO is reported to produce stable training throughout, with continuous performance improvement as compute increases, the query set is refreshed, and generation length is extended. In that sense, a practical implementation should monitor not only training loss but also the reward trajectory and downstream benchmark behavior if available.

Clipping statistics are also important in the paper’s discussion. The authors note a striking difference between GSPO and GRPO in the fraction of clipped tokens, with GSPO clipping a much larger fraction of tokens even though it operates at the response level. This observation is used to argue that clipping more aggressively does not necessarily harm efficiency when the learning signal is better aligned. Therefore, tracking how often clipping is activated can help diagnose whether the training dynamics are operating in the intended regime.

The key interpretive point is that clipping fractions alone do not tell the whole story. The paper argues that GRPO’s token-level gradients are inherently noisy and inefficient for sample exploitation, while GSPO’s sequence-level approach gives a more reliable learning signal even when more tokens are clipped. So the practical reading of clipping statistics should always be tied back to training stability and reward improvement rather than interpreted as a standalone measure of quality.

# Recognizing signs of instability or collapse

A major motivation for GSPO is the catastrophic and sometimes irreversible collapse observed with GRPO in large models, especially on long-response tasks. The paper says that this instability stems from the misapplication of importance sampling at the token level, which introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences and is amplified by clipping. In practice, then, a key part of implementing GSPO is knowing what failure looked like in the baseline the method was designed to replace.

The paper describes collapse as a state from which training is difficult or impossible to recover, even if one returns to a previous checkpoint, tunes clipping hyperparameters, extends generation length, or changes the RL queries. This means that a healthy GSPO run should be monitored for the absence of such abrupt degradations in reward and performance. The expected behavior is stable progression, not a fragile rise followed by a sudden breakdown.

In the context of the paper, instability is also visible in the mismatch between token-level importance weights and actual learning behavior. If the implementation is incorrectly using tokenwise ratios instead of sequence-level ratios, it would be reproducing precisely the kind of noisy training signal GSPO was introduced to avoid. So the first diagnostic step for instability is to verify that the ratio, clipping, and reward all live at the sequence level.

# When to use GSPO versus GSPO-token

The paper presents GSPO-token as a variant for situations where finer-grained advantage adjustment is desired, especially in scenarios like multi-turn RL. GSPO-token keeps the same sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$, but allows token-wise advantage customization through $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. This gives more flexibility when different tokens in a response should receive different learning signals.

The key distinction is that GSPO-token is not changing the ratio scale or the fundamental sequence-level likelihood structure. The paper states that GSPO-token is numerically identical to GSPO in the objective, clipping condition, and theoretical gradient when all tokens in the response share the same advantage value, that is, when $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$. So the choice between GSPO and GSPO-token is mainly about how much per-token control is needed over the advantage signal.

For implementation, the default should be GSPO when the reward is naturally sequence-level, which is the setting emphasized throughout the paper. GSPO-token should be reserved for cases where the task structure justifies token-level advantage customization. Even then, the paper’s definition keeps the sequence-level ratio, so the main GSPO design principle remains intact.

# Considerations for dense versus MoE models

The paper places special emphasis on Mixture-of-Experts models because sparse activation creates a unique stability problem under GRPO. The issue is called expert-activation volatility: after an update, the experts activated for the same response can change significantly between the old and new policies. The paper gives an example in which about $10\%$ of the experts activated under the new policy differ from those under the old policy for the same rollout sample in a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model.

This volatility makes token-level importance ratios fluctuate drastically, which in turn invalidates them and destabilizes training. The paper explains that a previous workaround, Routing Replay, caches and reuses the activated experts from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ when computing the GRPO ratio. That restores convergence but adds memory and communication overhead and can limit the effective capacity of the MoE model.

GSPO avoids this problem because it operates at the sequence-likelihood level rather than the token-likelihood level. The paper argues that the sequence likelihood is much less sensitive to the exact routing changes inside the model, since the model’s overall language modeling capability remains intact. As a result, GSPO is presented as a direct remedy for the MoE stability issue and as a way to eliminate the need for Routing Replay. For dense models, the same sequence-level logic still applies, but the paper’s strongest practical benefit is clearly in MoE training.

# Numerical and systems considerations in large-scale training

The paper makes several system-level observations that matter for implementation. First, it notes precision discrepancies between training engines such as Megatron and inference engines such as SGLang and vLLM. In existing practice, the training engine is often used to recompute old-policy likelihoods for sampled responses. GSPO changes this picture because it only uses sequence-level likelihoods for optimization, and the paper argues that these are more tolerant of precision discrepancies.

This creates an implementation advantage: the likelihoods returned by the inference engine may be usable directly for optimization, reducing the need for recomputation by the training engine. The paper highlights that this is especially helpful in partial rollout, multi-turn RL, and training-inference disaggregated frameworks. So from a systems perspective, GSPO can simplify the RL stack by reducing dependence on exact token-level likelihood recomputation.

The paper also suggests that GSPO may simplify RL infrastructure more broadly. This is tied to the fact that GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay in MoE training and may reduce the sensitivity to engine-level precision mismatches. In large-scale training, these system simplifications matter as much as algorithmic stability, because they reduce the number of special cases and workarounds required to keep training running normally.

Overall, the implementation message of the paper is that GSPO is not just a different objective; it is a more coherent alignment between the level at which reward is assigned, the level at which optimization is performed, and the level at which stability is maintained. That alignment is what makes it suitable for large-scale RL training of language models, especially when responses are long and models are sparse.

14. Broader Lessons, Limitations, and Future Directions

# The broader lesson of matching optimization granularity to reward granularity

A central lesson of the paper is that the granularity of the optimization objective should match the granularity at which reward is assigned. In the setting studied here, the verifier produces a reward for an entire query-response pair, not for individual tokens. That means the learning signal is naturally sequence-level: the model is being told whether a whole response is good or bad. The paper argues that when optimization is instead done token by token with per-token importance ratios, as in GRPO, the resulting update can become misaligned with the structure of the reward itself.

This point is not merely stylistic. The paper’s motivation section explains that token-level importance weights do not actually play the role that importance sampling requires, because they are computed from a single sampled token rather than from a sufficiently representative set of samples. As a result, the token-level correction introduces noise rather than correcting distribution mismatch. GSPO’s broader lesson is that when reward is sequence-level, the cleaner and more principled choice is to optimize at the sequence level as well.

# Why theoretically grounded importance weighting matters in practice

The paper makes a strong practical argument that theoretical correctness is not an abstract concern. The main claim is that the instability of GRPO comes from a fundamental misuse of importance sampling. Importance sampling is meant to reweight samples from a behavior distribution so they can estimate expectations under a target distribution. In the paper’s framing, this only works properly when the weight is used in a way that matches the sampling and averaging structure of the problem.

GSPO is introduced as a correction to that issue. Rather than using token-level importance ratios, it defines the importance ratio using the full sequence likelihood,
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}},
\]
and then applies clipping at the sequence level. The paper emphasizes that this is not just a mathematical reformulation: it changes training behavior substantially. Empirically, the sequence-level definition avoids the high-variance training noise that can accumulate across long responses and lead to collapse. The lesson is that a theoretically grounded ratio is valuable because it preserves the meaning of the update and helps make the optimization stable enough to scale.

# GSPO as a foundation for more scalable RL with LLMs

The paper presents GSPO as more than a fix for a specific instability. It is framed as a foundation for scaling reinforcement learning on large language models. This is because GSPO addresses several practical bottlenecks at once: it removes the dependence on a value model, stabilizes training on long responses, improves efficiency relative to GRPO, and simplifies certain infrastructure requirements.

The paper’s experiments are intended to show exactly this broader role. GSPO supports continuous performance improvement as training compute increases, the query set is refreshed, and generation length is extended. It also performs well in the large Mixture-of-Experts setting, where GRPO required additional machinery such as Routing Replay to maintain convergence. GSPO avoids that workaround. In this sense, the algorithm is positioned not only as a better objective, but as a more scalable operating principle for RL in language modeling.

# Potential extensions to richer reward structures and multi-turn settings

The paper itself suggests one extension beyond the basic sequence-level objective: GSPO-token. This variant is introduced for scenarios such as multi-turn RL, where finer-grained advantage adjustment may be desirable. In GSPO-token, the sequence-level importance ratio is retained, but token-wise advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ can vary across a response. The paper notes that this makes the method more flexible while preserving the key property that the sequence-level correction remains the basis of the update.

This suggests a natural direction for future work within the paper’s own framework: richer reward structures may require more nuanced advantage assignment, but not necessarily a return to the unstable token-level importance weighting criticized earlier. GSPO-token is important because it shows that sequence-level correction and token-level credit shaping need not be mutually exclusive. The paper’s formulation leaves open how best to design these token-wise advantages in more complex tasks, especially when the reward signal is not simply a single scalar for the whole response.

# Open questions around token-level credit assignment under sequence-level correction

Although the paper argues strongly against token-level importance ratios, it does not claim that token-level information is useless. In fact, the GSPO-token variant exists precisely because there are settings in which more detailed credit assignment may be helpful. The open question is how to assign such credit without reintroducing the instability that sequence-level GSPO is designed to avoid.

The paper’s discussion implies a tension here. On one hand, the reward is sequence-level, so the correction should also be sequence-level. On the other hand, some tasks may benefit from token-wise advantage customization. GSPO-token resolves this by keeping the importance ratio sequence-based while allowing per-token advantages. But the paper does not fully explore the design space of such advantages, leaving open how to best derive them in multi-turn or more structured settings. This is one of the clearest areas where additional research would be needed.

# Possible interactions with KL regularization and other RL stabilizers

The paper notes that it omits the KL regularization term in the PPO formulation for brevity, and the focus remains on clipping-based objectives. It also repeatedly frames clipping as a mechanism for excluding overly off-policy samples. However, the paper’s main contribution is about the correctness of the importance ratio itself, not about replacing all other stabilizers in RL.

That means a natural open question is how GSPO interacts with other stabilization methods, including KL regularization or any additional tricks used in large-scale RL systems. The paper does not analyze these interactions in detail. What it does show is that GSPO can already stabilize MoE training without Routing Replay, and that it tolerates practical precision discrepancies between training and inference engines better because it relies on sequence likelihoods. But how GSPO combines with other stabilizers, or whether some become unnecessary while others remain useful, is not fully resolved in the paper.

# Questions about generalization across tasks, models, and verifier types

The paper’s experiments focus on a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base and evaluate on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces. The reported gains are strong, and the paper also emphasizes successful application to the latest Qwen3 models. Still, the scope of the empirical evidence is tied to these settings.

This leaves open a broader question: how broadly does GSPO generalize across model families, task types, and verifier designs? The paper assumes rewards from a verifier $r(x,y) \in [0,1]$, and the reasoning is built around whole-response scoring. That makes GSPO especially natural in settings where a verifier can assess a complete answer. But the paper does not systematically study other reward forms, other response structures, or different model architectures outside the cases reported. So while the claims are compelling, the full range of applicability remains an open empirical question.

# Implications for future RL algorithm design in language modeling

The main design implication of the paper is that future RL algorithms should pay close attention to what exactly the importance ratio is correcting and at what level the reward is defined. GSPO shows that if the reward is sequence-level, then a sequence-level objective can be both simpler and more stable than a token-level one. It also suggests that RL algorithm design should be judged not only by asymptotic correctness, but by whether the correction mechanism remains meaningful under long sequences, large models, sparsity, and off-policy mini-batch training.

The paper also points toward infrastructure-aware algorithm design. Because sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision discrepancies, GSPO may reduce the need for recomputation under the training engine and simplify training-inference disaggregated systems. In that sense, algorithm design is not separate from systems design. A better objective can make the surrounding infrastructure simpler and more robust.

# Summary of the paper’s main contributions and impact

The paper’s main contribution is the proposal of Group Sequence Policy Optimization, a reinforcement learning algorithm for large language models that replaces token-level importance ratios with a sequence-level ratio based on sequence likelihood. This change is justified as a better match to the sequence-level reward used in RL for language generation. The paper argues that this alignment removes the source of instability in GRPO, which can suffer from high-variance gradients, especially on long responses and in large models.

A second contribution is the empirical demonstration that GSPO improves stability, efficiency, and performance over GRPO, including in challenging Mixture-of-Experts training. A third contribution is the GSPO-token variant, which preserves sequence-level correction while allowing token-wise advantage customization. Finally, the paper highlights practical benefits for RL infrastructure, including the elimination of Routing Replay and the potential to rely more directly on inference-engine likelihoods. Taken together, the paper’s impact is to reframe RL optimization for language models around the principle that the correction mechanism should be as structured as the reward signal it is trying to optimize.
