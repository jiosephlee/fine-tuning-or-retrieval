\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does having only one sampled token per position make the token-level ratio useless for importance sampling?
Question:
The motivation section says that importance sampling relies on averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution, and that GRPO fails because at each token position there is only a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{i,<t})$.

I don't fully understand this argument. In Monte Carlo methods, we often estimate expectations using one sample per state/action occurrence and then average across many trajectories. Why isn't averaging across many sequences and many token positions enough here?

More concretely, suppose we want an expectation under the new policy over next-token events. Why can't the collection of all token occurrences across a batch provide the needed averaging, even if each individual context $ (x, y_{<t}) $ only contributes one sampled token?

Is the problem that each context is different, so there is no repeated sampling from the same conditional distribution? If that is the real issue, could someone explain it in a more formal way?
Answer:
Yes — the key issue is that the relevant random variable is not “a token at position $t$” in the abstract, but “a token drawn from the specific conditional distribution $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t})$ for this exact prefix.” In GRPO, the token-level ratio is

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

That looks like ordinary importance sampling, but importance sampling only works as a correction when you can average over many samples from the same behavior distribution. The paper’s point is that at each position $t$, you do not have many samples from the same conditional distribution $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x,y_{<t})$; you have exactly one sample, namely the sampled token $y_{i,t}$ from one realized prefix $y_{i,<t}$. So the “importance weight” is not correcting a Monte Carlo estimate of an expectation over that conditional distribution in the usual way. It is just a noisy multiplicative factor attached to one sample.

Your intuition about averaging across many sequences is reasonable, but it does not fix the problem, because those samples are not drawn from the same distribution. Across a batch, you have many different prefixes $y_{i,<t}$, so each token position corresponds to a different conditional distribution. Averaging one sample from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{i,<t})$ and one sample from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x, y'_{j,<s})$ is not like averaging repeated samples from one common state; it is averaging across many different states. That can be useful for estimating an overall gradient, but it does not make the token-wise ratio a good importance-sampling correction for each token-level term.

A formal way to say this is that importance sampling targets an identity of the form

$$
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
=
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}f(z)\right],
$$

where the weight is the density ratio for the *same* variable $z$. In language modeling, the natural “same variable” is the whole response $y$, whose density ratio is

$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}.
$$

That is exactly what GSPO uses. By contrast, the token-level term in GRPO is a ratio for one coordinate of a much larger sequential process, and the paper argues that applying it token by token does not give the intended distribution correction. Instead, it injects high-variance noise, and because there are many tokens per response, those noisy effects accumulate over length.

So the short answer is: yes, the issue is that each token position is a different conditional distribution, so you do not have repeated sampling from the same next-token distribution to make a token-level importance weight behave like a proper Monte Carlo correction. The paper’s remedy is to move the importance ratio to the sequence level, where the sampling unit and the reward unit match: one sampled response, one sequence-level reward, one sequence-level ratio. That is why GSPO is better aligned with importance sampling in the sense the paper is using.
