\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why should matching the unit of reward and the unit of optimization matter mathematically?
Question:
A central statement in the paper is that "the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward." In this case, the reward $r(x,y)$ is given to the whole sequence, so the authors argue for sequence-level clipping and sequence-level weighting.

I understand the intuition, but I am not sure I see the formal reason. In policy gradient methods, a sequence-level return is often decomposed into token-level log-probability gradients:

$\nabla_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta}}[r(x,y)] = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta}}\left[r(x,y) \sum_t \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\right].$

That already mixes a sequence-level reward with token-level terms, and it is normally considered valid.

So what exactly fails in GRPO? Is it not the token decomposition itself, but the use of separate token-wise importance ratios and token-wise clipping? I'd appreciate a precise explanation distinguishing the valid decomposition above from the allegedly ill-posed GRPO objective.
Answer:
The key point is that the token-level decomposition in the policy-gradient identity is mathematically valid, but GRPO is not using that decomposition in the same way. Those are two different ideas.

The identity you wrote,
$$
\nabla_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta}}[r(x,y)]
=
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta}}\!\left[r(x,y)\sum_t \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\right],
$$
is just the usual log-derivative trick. It says that if the reward is a sequence-level function $r(x,y)$, then the gradient can be written as a sum of token-level score-function terms. That is perfectly fine, because the reward is still attached to the whole sampled sequence. The tokens only appear through the gradient of the joint log-likelihood,
$$
\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\sum_t \log \pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}).
$$
So this is a decomposition of the gradient of a sequence-level objective; it is not saying that the optimization problem has become token-level.

What the paper argues is problematic in GRPO is something more specific: it applies a separate importance ratio and a separate clipping operation to each token,
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$
while the reward signal is a single sequence-level scalar $\widehat A_i$. In other words, the objective is “sequence reward, token-wise off-policy correction.” That is the mismatch the authors are complaining about.

Why does that matter? Because importance sampling is only valid when the weight matches the variable whose distribution changed. If the random object you sampled is the whole sequence $y$, then the correct density ratio is the sequence ratio
$$
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)},
$$
not a collection of per-token ratios treated as if each token were an independent sample from its own marginal. The sequence ratio factors into token ratios algebraically, but that does not mean you can use each factor separately as an importance weight in the surrogate objective. The expectation you want to correct is over complete sequences, and the one-sample-per-token ratios do not correct the sequence distribution in the importance-sampling sense described in the paper.

This is why the paper says the GRPO weights “fail to perform the intended distribution-correction role.” The issue is not the token decomposition of $\nabla \log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ itself; that decomposition is exact. The issue is the use of token-wise ratios as if they were valid importance weights for a sequence-level reward, together with token-wise clipping, which distorts the surrogate and can inject high-variance noise that accumulates over long responses.

By contrast, GSPO keeps the reward and the importance ratio at the same granularity: whole response in, whole response out. Its weight
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
$$
is sequence-level, and the gradient then distributes that sequence-level weight across the token log-probability gradients. That is consistent with the paper’s principle: the reward is sequence-level, so the off-policy correction and clipping should also be sequence-level.

So the short answer is: the valid identity is the score-function decomposition of a sequence-level objective; what fails in GRPO is not that decomposition, but the attempt to use token-wise importance ratios and token-wise clipping as the importance-sampling correction for a sequence-level reward.
