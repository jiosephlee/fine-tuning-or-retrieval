\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Is the paper's argument against GRPO empirical, theoretical, or both?
Question:
After reading the paper, I'm unsure how strong the claim against GRPO is supposed to be.

On one hand, the authors use strong language like "ill-posed" and "invalidated importance sampling weights." On the other hand, the main evidence presented in the excerpt is empirical: instability, collapse on long sequences, and MoE problems.

Is there a formal theorem or counterexample showing that GRPO's token-level objective is biased in a way that cannot correspond to any valid importance-sampling estimator of a sequence-level reward objective? Or is the claim more like: "in practice this estimator has unacceptable variance/noise and therefore behaves as if ill-posed"?

I want to understand whether the objection is mathematically definitive or mainly an empirical diagnosis backed by intuition.
Answer:
The paper’s case against GRPO is **both theoretical and empirical, but not a formal impossibility theorem**.

On the theoretical side, the authors are making a fairly strong *modeling* argument: GRPO applies an importance ratio at the **token** level,
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$
even though the reward is assigned at the **sequence** level, $r(x,y_i)$. Their claim is that this is a mismatch of the unit of correction and the unit of reward. They explicitly say the token-level ratio “fails to perform the intended distribution-correction role” because it is based on a single sample from each next-token distribution, and they argue that this introduces high-variance noise rather than a meaningful importance-sampling correction. So the theoretical objection is: the tokenwise weights do not have the same clean interpretation as sequencewise importance sampling weights,
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}.
$$

However, the paper does **not** give a theorem of the form “GRPO is mathematically invalid” or “no unbiased estimator of the sequence objective can be written in token form.” In fact, the gradient derivations they show are mostly used to argue about **how the gradients are weighted**, not to prove an impossibility result. The paper’s language like “ill-posed” and “invalidated importance sampling weights” should be read as a conceptual critique: the estimator is not aligned with the underlying sequence-level RL objective in a principled way, and this misalignment is claimed to become unstable in practice.

That is why the empirical evidence matters so much in the paper. The authors support the theoretical story with observations of instability, collapse on long responses, and particular trouble for MoE routing, where token-level likelihoods fluctuate more sharply. Their strongest evidence is therefore **diagnostic and experimental**: GRPO behaves badly in the regimes they care about, while GSPO, which uses sequence-level importance ratios, behaves stably.

So the short answer is: the paper’s objection is **partly theoretical in motivation, but mainly empirical in support**. It argues that GRPO’s token-level weighting is conceptually misaligned with sequence-level rewards and likely high-variance, but it does **not** provide a formal counterexample proving that GRPO cannot correspond to any valid objective. The claim is closer to “this estimator is a poor and unstable surrogate for the intended sequence-level optimization” than to a strict mathematical impossibility.
