\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Does the group-normalized advantage become unstable when rewards in the group have very small standard deviation?
Question:
Both GRPO and GSPO use the group-based normalized advantage

$ \widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}. $

Since the reward is bounded in $[0,1]$, I can imagine cases where all sampled responses in a group receive almost the same reward, making the denominator very small.

Does this create instability due to extremely large advantages? In practice do implementations add an epsilon to the standard deviation, or clip advantages, or skip low-variance groups?

The paper focuses on instability from importance ratios, but I wonder whether some of the training noise could also come from this normalization step.
Answer:
Yes, in principle this normalization can be numerically fragile, but the paper itself does not identify it as the source of instability, and it does not specify any extra safeguard such as an $\epsilon$ in the denominator.

The advantage used by both GRPO and GSPO is just a groupwise $z$-score,

$$
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}.
$$

So if all rewards in a group are almost the same, then the numerator is also almost zero for every sample, because each reward is close to the group mean. In that case the normalized advantages do not automatically become huge; they are only large if the reward differences remain nontrivial while the group standard deviation becomes extremely small. The truly problematic case is the degenerate one where all rewards are exactly equal, in which case the denominator is zero and the expression is undefined.

That said, the paper is mainly arguing that the major instability in GRPO comes from the token-level importance ratios, not from the advantage normalization. In fact, the authors keep the same group-normalized advantage in GSPO and still report much better stability, which suggests that whatever noise this normalization introduces is not the dominant failure mode they are studying. Their instability story is about the mismatch between token-level ratios and sequence-level rewards, plus clipping effects, not about the reward standard deviation.

So the careful answer is: mathematically, very small within-group reward variance can make the normalization ill-conditioned, and a practical implementation would usually need some numerical protection; but this paper does not discuss such a fix, and its empirical claim is that the main instability comes from importance weighting, since GSPO retains the same advantage normalization while removing the problematic token-level ratios.
