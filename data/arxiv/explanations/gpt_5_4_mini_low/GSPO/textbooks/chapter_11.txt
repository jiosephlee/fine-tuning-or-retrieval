\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 11: 11. Mixture-of-Experts Models and the Routing Replay Problem

# What Mixture-of-Experts models are and why they matter

The paper discusses Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, models as a special class of large language models whose training behavior differs in important ways from dense models. The key feature of an MoE model is its sparse activation nature: for a given input, only a subset of the model’s experts are activated rather than the entire network. This design matters because it allows the model to scale up capacity while keeping the per-token computation manageable. In the context of the paper, this is one reason MoE models are attractive for large-scale reinforcement learning, especially when the goal is to improve reasoning performance on hard tasks through continued training.

However, the same sparse structure that makes MoE models attractive also creates a new source of instability during reinforcement learning. Because different experts may be selected for different responses, and even for the same response after a policy update, the behavior of the model can change in ways that are not well captured by token-level optimization methods. The paper argues that this is not a minor implementation detail but a structural issue that becomes especially important when training large models with long responses.

# Sparse activation and its consequences for RL training

The paper emphasizes that sparse activation introduces unique stability challenges compared with dense models. In RL training, one repeatedly samples responses from an old policy and then performs gradient updates to improve the current policy. For dense models, the mapping from a token to its likelihood under the model is relatively stable across small updates. For MoE models, this is complicated by the fact that the experts active for a response may change after an update.

This matters because GRPO and related token-level RL methods rely on token-level importance ratios. If the activated computation path changes, then the probability assigned to a token is no longer based on the same underlying activated network before and after the update. In other words, the token likelihoods being compared are not fully comparable. The paper presents this as one reason why sparse activation can break the assumptions behind token-level policy optimization and make training unstable.

The practical consequence is that RL training on MoE models can fail to converge properly. The paper treats this as a serious obstacle, because one of the major goals of RL scaling is to make large models better by training them longer and on harder tasks. If the training dynamics become unstable, then the benefits of scaling are lost.

# Expert-activation volatility across policy updates

The paper introduces the term expert-activation volatility to describe what happens when the set of experts activated for the same response changes significantly after one or more gradient updates. This volatility is especially important because RL training is iterative: the policy is updated repeatedly, and each update can alter which experts are selected for a given token sequence.

The authors provide a concrete observation from a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model. After each RL gradient update, for the same rollout sample, roughly $10\%$ of the experts activated under the new policy $\pi_\theta$ differ from those under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The paper notes that this phenomenon becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models. That is an important point: as the model becomes more complex, the routing behavior can become even less stable.

This volatility is not just a curiosity. It directly undermines the validity of token-level importance ratios such as
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
If the activated experts change, then the likelihood ratio at the token level can fluctuate drastically from update to update. The paper argues that this makes the token-level ratio increasingly unreliable as a basis for optimization.

# How routing changes affect token-level likelihood ratios

The paper’s core criticism of GRPO in the MoE setting is that routing changes make token-level likelihood ratios unstable and effectively invalid. Since the token probability depends on the particular expert path used to process that token, any change in routing can alter the likelihood in a way that is unrelated to the intended policy improvement signal.

This becomes especially problematic because GRPO uses those token-level ratios inside its optimization objective. If the ratio varies sharply because the routing path changed, then the gradient estimate inherits this instability. The paper connects this directly to the broader issue discussed earlier in the chapter on GSPO: the token-level importance weight is being used in a situation where it does not serve its intended distribution-correction role. In the MoE case, routing volatility makes that failure even worse.

The result is that the gradient signal becomes noisy and difficult to interpret. The paper states that this can hinder the normal convergence of RL training. In other words, even if the reward signal is well defined, the optimization machinery that tries to use it is disrupted by the instability of the token-level probabilities.

# Why the GRPO problem becomes worse in deeper MoE models

The paper explicitly notes that expert-activation volatility becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models. This is important because deeper models are precisely where one might expect to push RL scaling further. If the routing instability increases with depth, then the mismatch between token-level ratios and actual model behavior becomes more severe as the model scales.

The paper’s example of the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model illustrates this point. Even after a single RL update, a substantial fraction of experts for the same response can change. The deeper the model, the more opportunities there are for routing differences to accumulate. As a result, the token-level importance ratios fluctuate more dramatically, and the instability of GRPO is amplified.

This is why the paper treats MoE models not as a minor edge case, but as a revealing stress test for RL algorithms. If an algorithm cannot remain stable in this setting, it is unlikely to be a robust foundation for large-scale RL training.

# The Routing Replay strategy: caching and replaying expert routes

Before introducing GSPO, the paper describes a workaround that was previously used to make GRPO training converge on MoE models: Routing Replay. The basic idea is to cache the activated experts used under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and then replay those same routing modes when computing likelihoods under the current policy $\pi_\theta$.

This means that, for each token $y_{i,t}$, the model computes both $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$ using the same activated network. By forcing the current policy to follow the same routing pattern as the old policy, Routing Replay makes the token-level importance ratio more stable.

The purpose of this strategy is straightforward: if the ratio is unstable because the computation path changes, then reuse the old computation path so that the ratio compares like with like. In the paper’s discussion, this is presented as a way to restore a consistent activated network across gradient updates and thereby make the token-level optimization of GRPO workable.

# How Routing Replay stabilizes GRPO

According to the paper, Routing Replay is essential for the normal convergence of GRPO when training MoE models. By eliminating routing differences between the old and current policies during likelihood computation, it reduces the volatility that would otherwise make the token-level importance ratios fluctuate unpredictably.

This stabilization is valuable because it allows the optimization procedure to proceed using a consistent network path. In effect, Routing Replay patches over the mismatch between the token-level objective and the routing behavior of the model. With the same experts replayed across policies, the gradient update is less likely to be distorted by changes in expert assignment.

The paper presents this as a practical workaround rather than a true solution. It helps GRPO avoid immediate failure, but it does so by constraining the model’s routing behavior during optimization. That means the training process remains tied to a special mechanism whose purpose is purely compensatory.

# Memory, communication, and capacity costs of Routing Replay

Although Routing Replay helps with stability, the paper argues that it comes at a cost. First, caching and replaying expert routes adds extra memory overhead. Second, it introduces communication overhead. Third, it can limit the actual capacity of the MoE model.

These costs matter because one of the main reasons to use MoE models is to gain capacity efficiently. If the training procedure requires holding onto routing information and reusing it in a way that constrains the model, then the model may not fully realize its sparse-activation advantage. The paper frames this as an artificial limitation imposed by the workaround.

In this sense, Routing Replay is not an elegant or scalable answer. It keeps GRPO from breaking, but it does so by adding complexity to the training pipeline and by interfering with the natural behavior of the model. The paper presents GSPO as a way to eliminate the need for this workaround altogether.

# Why GSPO does not depend on token-level routing consistency

The central reason GSPO avoids the Routing Replay problem is that it does not rely on token-level likelihood ratios. Instead, it uses the sequence-level importance ratio
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.
\]
This ratio is based on the likelihood of the whole response rather than the likelihood of each token independently.

That difference is crucial for MoE models. The paper argues that GSPO focuses on the sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)$ and is therefore not sensitive to the individual token likelihoods $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$ in the same way GRPO is. Since the model’s language modeling capability as a whole remains intact, the sequence likelihood does not fluctuate as drastically as the token-level likelihoods caused by routing volatility.

As a result, GSPO can compute importance ratios conventionally, without needing to preserve the exact token-level routing path from the old policy. This makes the algorithm much less vulnerable to the expert-activation instability that harms GRPO.

# Sequence likelihood stability despite token-level expert changes

The paper’s intuition is that even if some experts change between $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$, the overall sequence likelihood remains a more stable object than individual token probabilities. This is because GSPO evaluates the response as a whole, and its optimization signal is aligned with the sequence-level reward.

That alignment matters. The reward in the paper is assigned to the full query-response pair $(x,y)$, not to individual tokens. Since the scoring function operates at the sequence level, GSPO’s use of sequence likelihood is a better match for the structure of the problem. The paper presents this as one reason why GSPO can remain stable even when the internal routing choices of an MoE model change.

This is the key conceptual shift: rather than trying to force token-level consistency in a setting where the routing itself is volatile, GSPO changes the unit of optimization to the sequence. That lets it bypass the specific failure mode that makes GRPO difficult to use on MoE models.

# GSPO as a fundamental solution to MoE RL instability

The paper’s main conclusion in this setting is that GSPO fundamentally resolves the expert-activation volatility issue in MoE models. Unlike Routing Replay, which compensates for the instability by preserving old routes, GSPO removes the dependence on token-level routing consistency in the first place.

This has several consequences in the paper’s account. It simplifies training, since there is no need for cached routes or replayed expert paths. It stabilizes optimization, since the importance ratio is computed at the sequence level. It also allows the model to use its full capacity without being artificially constrained by replayed routing modes.

In the broader narrative of the paper, this is a central advantage of GSPO. The algorithm does not merely work around instability; it matches the optimization unit to the reward unit and therefore avoids the mismatch that makes token-level methods fragile. For MoE RL training in particular, this is presented as a clean and scalable solution.
