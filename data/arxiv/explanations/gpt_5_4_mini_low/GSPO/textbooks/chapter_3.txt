\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 3: 3. Importance Sampling and the Core Flaw in GRPO

# The purpose of importance sampling in off-policy learning

The paper’s critique of GRPO begins with a basic issue in reinforcement learning for language models: the data used for optimization is often generated by a policy that is not exactly the one currently being updated. In the paper’s setting, responses are sampled from an old policy, denoted $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, while learning seeks to improve a current policy, $\pi_\theta$. This is an off-policy situation because the samples come from one distribution and the optimization target is another.

Importance sampling is the standard mathematical tool for handling this mismatch. Its role is to let us estimate an expectation under a target distribution even when our samples were drawn from a different behavior distribution. The paper emphasizes that this is the conceptual reason clipping exists in PPO- and GRPO-style methods: if the sampled responses are too far from the current policy, then the off-policy discrepancy becomes too large to trust during gradient estimation. Importance sampling is therefore meant to correct for distribution mismatch, not merely to reweight arbitrary pieces of text.

The key point is that this correction must be used in a way that actually reflects how the samples were drawn. If the samples are sequence-level responses, then the correction should also be defined at the sequence level. The paper argues that GRPO breaks this basic logic by applying importance ratios token by token, even though the rewards and optimization targets live at the level of full responses.

# Estimating expectations under one distribution using samples from another

The paper presents importance sampling in its standard form. If we want the expectation of a function $f$ under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$, but we only have samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$, then we can rewrite the expectation as

$$
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right]
=
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z) \right].
$$

This identity is the mathematical heart of importance sampling. The ratio $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ compensates for the fact that the samples were not drawn from the target distribution. In the paper’s discussion, this is the principle that policy optimization methods are supposed to respect when learning from rollouts produced by an older policy.

The intuition is simple: if a certain sample is more likely under the target distribution than under the behavior distribution, then it should count more heavily in the estimate. If it is less likely, then it should count less. The ratio corrects the sample so that, in aggregate, the weighted behavior samples behave like target samples. But the important caveat is that this works as an estimator because it is aggregating over multiple samples from the behavior distribution.

# Why importance weights require averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution

A central claim in the paper is that importance weights only serve their intended correction role when they are used with enough samples from the behavior distribution. The paper explicitly notes that the ratio $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ relies on averaging over multiple samples, with $N \gg 1$, to effectively correct the distributional mismatch.

This matters because importance sampling is not meant to make a single sample magically representative of the target distribution. Rather, it rebalances a collection of samples. If one sample happens to be unusually likely under the target policy, that does not by itself guarantee a good estimate. The correction becomes meaningful only when many behavior samples are reweighted and averaged together, so that random fluctuations cancel out and the reweighting can approximate the target expectation.

The paper uses this principle to argue that applying importance weights at the token level is suspect. In language generation, each next-token choice is a single sample from a next-token distribution. If the algorithm tries to use a token-level importance ratio based on just that one token sample, then it is not really performing the kind of distribution correction that importance sampling is supposed to provide.

# The difference between token-level samples and sequence-level samples in language generation

Language generation is autoregressive, so a full response $y$ is built token by token. The paper defines the likelihood of a response under a policy as

$$
\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
$$

This means that a response is naturally a sequence-level object, even though it is composed of token-level decisions. In the paper’s framework, the reward $r(x,y)$ is assigned to the whole query-response pair and lies in $[0,1]$. So while the model internally emits tokens one at a time, the supervision signal is given only after the full response is produced.

That distinction is essential. A token is not the same thing as a rewarded sample. The paper argues that the training signal should respect the granularity at which rewards are defined. Since the reward belongs to the entire response, the unit of optimization should also be the entire response. This becomes the paper’s guiding principle for why GSPO uses sequence-level importance ratios rather than token-level ones.

GRPO, by contrast, breaks the sequence into token-level contributions and then applies an importance ratio to each token. The paper’s argument is that this is not aligned with how the data and reward are structured. The response is the sample that was generated off-policy, not the individual token considered in isolation.

# Why GRPO’s token-level importance ratios do not correctly perform distribution correction

In GRPO, the importance ratio is defined at each token position as

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

The paper’s critique is that this token-level ratio is not a valid way to correct the distributional mismatch created by off-policy sampling. The reason is that each token is only one sample from the next-token distribution. A single sampled token does not provide enough information for importance sampling to do the job it is designed to do.

The paper emphasizes that this token-level weighting therefore fails to perform true distribution correction. Instead of compensating for the mismatch between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, it introduces randomness into the gradient estimate. The weight is calculated from a single next-token sample, so it is highly sensitive to the particular token that happened to be sampled. This makes the weight noisy rather than corrective.

The problem becomes more severe because language generation is sequential. A noisy weighting at one step is not isolated. It affects the gradient update for the current token and contributes to the changing policy that will generate the next token, where another noisy weighting will be applied. The paper argues that this turns a local misuse of importance sampling into a sequence-wide instability.

# Single-sample next-token weighting as a source of noise

The paper’s key technical objection is that GRPO applies the importance weight to each token based on only one observed next-token sample. Because each token comes from a single draw from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t})$, the ratio is computed from a sparse piece of evidence. It is not averaging over a set of token samples from the same conditional distribution.

As a result, the token-level ratio does not act like a stable estimator of the target-to-behavior mismatch. Instead, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient. The paper describes this as progressively accumulating training noise. Since the model is updated over many tokens and many responses, even modest noise at each step can compound into much larger instability over the course of training.

The paper also notes that clipping does not eliminate this problem. The ratio is still built from a single token sample, so even if its value is restricted to a range, the gradient is still driven by noisy per-token fluctuations. In the paper’s view, the core issue is not merely that the ratio can become too large or too small; it is that the object being clipped is the wrong object in the first place.

# How variance accumulates over long sequences

The paper specifically highlights long-response tasks as especially problematic for GRPO. When responses are long, the model produces many tokens, and each token contributes its own token-level importance ratio and gradient term. If each of these terms is noisy, then the total variance of the update can become very large.

This accumulation matters because the instability is not isolated to a single long sample. The paper argues that high-variance training noise builds up across token positions and across optimization steps. Over time, this can lead to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. Once collapse occurs, the paper reports that recovery is difficult even if one reverts to an earlier checkpoint, adjusts the clipping ranges, changes generation length, or switches RL queries.

The intuition is that long sequences create many opportunities for the flawed token-level weighting to introduce distortion. Since every token is treated as if it were separately important in the importance-sampling sense, the update can drift away from what the actual sequence-level reward supports. The longer the response, the more these local distortions can accumulate.

# Why clipping can amplify rather than solve the instability

Clipping is usually introduced to keep policy updates within a proximal region and to reduce the influence of overly off-policy samples. In GRPO, the token-level importance ratio is clipped to a range such as $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$. In principle, this should prevent extreme updates.

The paper’s argument is that clipping does not fix the fundamental misuse of importance weights. Instead, because the token-level ratios are already noisy, clipping can interact with that noise in undesirable ways. The paper states that the clipping mechanism can exacerbate the instability and help precipitate model collapse. The issue is not simply that the ratios are too large; it is that the per-token weights are not the right objects for sequence-level reward optimization in the first place.

This is a subtle but important point. Clipping is designed to prevent excessive deviation from the old policy, but if the underlying gradient estimate is ill-posed, then constraining its magnitude does not make the estimate well-founded. The paper’s position is that clipping a noisy token-level ratio merely bounds the noise; it does not turn the noise into a meaningful correction.

# The mismatch between reward granularity and optimization granularity

The paper’s most important conceptual claim is that the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. In the RL setup considered here, the verifier assigns a reward to the full response $y$, producing $r(x,y) \in [0,1]$. The reward is not issued token by token. It evaluates the entire response as a whole.

Given that structure, the paper argues that optimization should also happen at the response level. If the reward is sequence-level, then applying importance correction and clipping at the token level introduces a mismatch between what is being optimized and what is being judged. This mismatch is the paper’s explanation for why GRPO becomes unstable, especially in large models and long-response tasks.

The paper frames this as more than an implementation detail. It is a basic alignment principle: the granularity of the training objective should reflect the granularity of the learning signal. If the sequence as a whole receives the reward, then the sequence should be the unit on which off-policy correction is defined. This is why GSPO moves from token-level importance ratios to sequence-level importance ratios.

# The principle that the optimization unit should match the reward unit

The paper distills its critique into a simple principle: the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward. This principle is the conceptual bridge from the failure of GRPO to the design of GSPO.

In GRPO, the reward is sequence-level, but the importance ratio and gradient weighting are token-level. The paper argues that this mismatch is the source of the algorithm’s ill-posed nature. Since the reward is assigned to the entire response, it is the full response that should be considered the sample for importance sampling and optimization. Token-level weighting breaks that correspondence and turns the update into a noisy approximation with no proper distribution-correction meaning.

This principle also explains why the paper later defines GSPO’s importance ratio using sequence likelihood rather than token likelihood. By making the ratio reflect the response as a whole, GSPO restores the alignment between sampling, weighting, and reward. In the paper’s view, this alignment is what GRPO lacks, and that lack is what ultimately leads to high-variance gradients and model collapse.
