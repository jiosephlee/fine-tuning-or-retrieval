\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 4: 4. From Token-Level to Sequence-Level RL: The Design Logic of GSPO

# Why sequence likelihood has a clear theoretical meaning in language generation

The central design move in GSPO is to stop treating individual tokens as the basic unit of off-policy correction and instead treat the entire response sequence as the unit of optimization. This shift is not just a technical convenience; it follows directly from how language generation works. In an autoregressive language model, the probability of a response $y$ given a query $x$ is defined as the product of the conditional probabilities of its tokens:

$$
\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
$$

This sequence likelihood is therefore the natural object that summarizes how the model assigns probability to the whole response. A generated answer is not a collection of independent token decisions in isolation; it is one coherent trajectory produced by the policy. GSPO takes this view seriously and uses the likelihood of the full response as the basis for importance weighting.

The paper argues that this sequence-level quantity has a clear theoretical meaning in language generation. Specifically, the sequence-level importance ratio compares how likely a response is under the current policy relative to the old policy. Because the response is sampled from the old policy, this ratio tells us how much the current policy has shifted in its view of that entire response. In this sense, the ratio is not merely a number attached to a token; it is a measure of how the policy as a whole has changed with respect to a complete generated output.

This is the key conceptual difference from GRPO. In GRPO, importance ratios are computed token by token, but the paper argues that this is a misapplication of the importance sampling principle. The token-level quantity does not represent a full sample from the policy distribution over sequences, so it does not have the same clean interpretation as a correction factor for distribution mismatch. GSPO restores this interpretation by moving to the sequence level, where the importance ratio is defined on the exact object that was sampled: the complete response.

# The sequence-level importance ratio as a measure of policy deviation

GSPO defines the importance ratio for a response $y_i$ as

$$
s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})} \right).
$$

The paper emphasizes that this quantity measures how far the response sampled from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ deviates from $\pi_\theta$. That is, it is not just a reweighting trick; it is an explicit indicator of policy deviation at the response level. If the current policy assigns much higher likelihood to the response than the old policy, the ratio increases. If it assigns lower likelihood, the ratio decreases. This makes the ratio a meaningful signal for deciding how much to trust a rollout sample during optimization.

The length normalization by $|y_i|$ is important in the paper’s design. Without it, responses of different lengths would produce sequence ratios on very different numerical scales, and even a few token-level likelihood changes could cause large swings in the overall ratio. By taking the $|y_i|$-th root, GSPO controls the ratio within a more unified numerical range and reduces variance. This is part of what makes the sequence-level definition practical rather than merely conceptual.

The paper also notes that the clipping ranges used in GSPO differ in magnitude from those used in token-level methods such as GRPO, precisely because the importance ratio itself is defined differently. This reinforces the idea that GSPO is not a minor modification of GRPO, but a redefinition of the correction unit.

# Alignment between sequence-level reward and sequence-level correction

A key argument in the paper is that the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. In the RL setup described here, the verifier returns a reward for the entire query-response pair, $r(x,y) \in [0,1]$. That means the reward is assigned to the complete sequence, not to individual tokens. GSPO therefore computes the advantage at the response level as

$$
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}.
$$

This group-based normalization compares each sampled response to the other responses generated for the same query. The resulting advantage is a sequence-level signal that says whether a response is better or worse than its peers. Since the reward itself is sequence-level, the correction should also be sequence-level. GSPO’s objective follows this logic exactly.

This alignment matters because it keeps the learning signal coherent. When reward is attached to a whole response, it is natural to ask how much the policy should update toward or away from that whole response as a unit. GSPO does exactly that. It does not attempt to break the reward into token-specific pieces that were never directly observed. Instead, it uses the overall response likelihood and the overall response advantage to guide optimization.

The paper makes clear that this is not merely a matter of elegance. It is the mechanism by which GSPO avoids the ill-posedness identified in GRPO. The response-level reward and response-level importance ratio speak the same conceptual language: both operate on complete generated sequences. This consistency is what the authors mean when they say GSPO aligns sequence-level rewarding and optimization.

# Why sequence-level clipping is more meaningful than token-level clipping

Clipping in proximal policy optimization methods is intended to prevent overly off-policy samples from dominating the gradient. In GRPO, clipping is applied to token-level importance ratios, but the paper argues that this does not correctly address the mismatch between sampling and reward. The core issue is that the sample being evaluated is not a token in isolation but an entire response. Consequently, if clipping is meant to decide whether a sample is too far from the old policy to be useful, the natural object to clip is the response-level importance ratio.

GSPO therefore applies clipping to $s_i(\theta)$ rather than to each token ratio separately. This means the entire response is either accepted as sufficiently on-policy or downweighted as too far from the current policy. In the paper’s interpretation, this is a more meaningful exclusion rule because the reward and the optimization target are both defined at the sequence level. Sequence-level clipping is thus not just a different implementation; it is a correction that matches the structure of the problem.

The authors also observe that token-level clipping in GRPO can amplify training noise. Because each token is clipped independently based on an importance ratio derived from a single token sample, the resulting gradient can vary unpredictably across long sequences. GSPO avoids this by treating the full response as a single unit of clipping. That reduces the number of places where noisy token-level discrepancies can distort the update.

Another practical implication emphasized in the paper is that clipping fractions behave very differently in GSPO and GRPO. GSPO can clip a much larger fraction of tokens while still training more efficiently, because it is not trying to exploit every token-level signal. Instead, it uses a sequence-level estimate that the paper argues is more reliable. This is presented as evidence that sequence-level clipping is not wasteful but structurally better matched to the learning problem.

# The intuition behind treating a response as the basic optimization unit

The paper’s intuition is straightforward: a response is the natural outcome of the policy, so it should be the natural unit of optimization. When a model generates an answer, it produces a complete sequence of tokens that collectively form a single candidate solution. The reward verifier evaluates that candidate solution as a whole. Therefore, the policy update should also treat that response as a whole object.

This perspective becomes especially compelling in settings where the response is long and the task requires deeper reasoning. In such cases, the quality of the final answer depends on the global structure of the sequence, not on isolated token decisions. If the reward only comes after the full response is generated, then it makes sense that the algorithm should use the full response likelihood to decide how strongly that trajectory should influence learning.

The paper argues that this viewpoint resolves an important mismatch in token-level methods. Token-level weighting suggests that each token independently carries a meaningful correction signal, but the reward does not support that interpretation. A token in the middle of a response is not individually rewarded or penalized by the verifier. It only contributes as part of the final sequence. GSPO embraces this reality by using the response as the primitive object for weighting, clipping, and optimization.

This is also why GSPO can be seen as simplifying the logic of RL infrastructure. Once the response is the basic unit, the algorithm no longer needs to manage token-by-token correction in a setting where the meaningful signal is sequence-wide. The paper presents this as a cleaner foundation for RL training, especially when working with long responses and large models.

# How the proposed approach addresses the root cause of GRPO instability

The paper identifies the root cause of GRPO instability as the invalid use of token-level importance weights. In GRPO, the importance ratio is computed at each token position using only a single sample from each next-token distribution. The paper argues that this does not function as proper importance sampling correction. Instead of reliably adjusting for the mismatch between the behavior policy and the target policy, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient.

This noise becomes worse as response length increases, because many noisy token-level terms accumulate over a sequence. The clipping mechanism does not fix the problem; according to the paper, it can even exacerbate instability by interacting with these token-level fluctuations. In large models, this accumulation can lead to catastrophic model collapse, and once collapse occurs, recovery is often impossible even if one returns to an earlier checkpoint and retunes hyperparameters.

GSPO addresses this at the source by changing the unit of correction. By moving the importance ratio from tokens to sequences, it removes the invalid assumption that each token-level sample can independently serve as a reliable importance weight. The gradient now depends on the whole response likelihood, which is much more stable and consistent with the reward structure. In the paper’s view, this is what restores a valid learning signal.

The gradient expressions make the distinction especially clear. In GSPO, all tokens in a response are weighted equally through the sequence-level ratio. In GRPO, each token receives its own importance weight, and these weights can vary in unstable ways. The paper argues that these unequal token weights are a major source of unpredictability. GSPO avoids that by giving the response a single correction factor and then distributing it uniformly across the tokens in the sequence. This reduces the instability that comes from token-wise off-policy correction and helps explain why GSPO trains more stably than GRPO.

In this sense, GSPO does not merely improve GRPO; it changes the logic of the optimization problem so that the sampling correction, clipping, reward assignment, and gradient estimation all operate at the same level of abstraction. That alignment is what the paper presents as the fundamental solution to GRPO’s instability.
