\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 8: 8. Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol

# The cold-start model based on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base

The experiments in the paper are carried out with a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base. This matters because the evaluation is not about training a model from scratch, but about starting from a strong pretrained base and then applying reinforcement learning to improve reasoning and coding performance. In the paper’s framing, this kind of setup is the relevant testbed for studying whether an RL algorithm can stably and efficiently scale up large-language-model capability.

Using a cold-start model also makes the comparison between algorithms more informative. The model already has substantial language competence, so the RL procedure is not trying to discover basic linguistic ability. Instead, it is pushing the model toward stronger performance on tasks that require deeper reasoning, longer deliberation, and better answer selection. In that setting, training stability becomes central: if the optimization procedure is unstable, the model can collapse even after it has already acquired useful capabilities. The paper uses this experimental context to show that GSPO can continue improving such a model reliably.

# Training with rollout batches partitioned into multiple mini-batches

A key part of the training setup is that each batch of rollout data is partitioned into four mini-batches for gradient updates. This detail is important because it reflects the practical regime in which reinforcement learning for large language models is actually run. Rollout generation is expensive, so the collected samples are reused across multiple optimization steps rather than discarded after a single update.

This reuse means the policy being updated is no longer exactly the same as the policy that generated the samples. In other words, the training procedure naturally creates an off-policy learning setting. The paper emphasizes that this is not a side issue but a fundamental consequence of using large rollout batches and then subdividing them to improve sample efficiency and hardware utilization. The comparison between GSPO and GRPO is therefore made under a realistic multi-mini-batch training regime rather than an idealized on-policy one.

# Why this setup creates a meaningful off-policy optimization regime

The paper’s discussion makes clear that splitting rollout data into multiple mini-batches is what turns the optimization into an off-policy problem. Samples are generated by an older policy, $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and then used to update a newer policy, $\pi_{\theta}$. As gradient updates proceed over the mini-batches, the current policy drifts farther from the behavior policy that produced the samples. This discrepancy is exactly why importance ratios and clipping are needed in PPO- and GRPO-style methods.

In this evaluation setting, the off-policy nature is not accidental; it is the mechanism that tests whether the algorithm can handle practical large-scale RL training. The paper argues that this is also where GRPO becomes fragile, because its token-level importance ratios are not well suited to correcting the mismatch. By contrast, GSPO is designed specifically for this regime by using sequence-level likelihood ratios, which the paper argues are more aligned with the actual optimization target and more stable under repeated mini-batch updates.

# Benchmarks used in evaluation: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces

The paper evaluates training progress and final performance on three benchmarks: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces. These benchmarks are used to track how RL training affects the model’s ability to solve difficult reasoning and coding problems. The choice of these tasks is consistent with the paper’s broader theme that large-scale RL is meant to improve performance on competition-level mathematics and programming.

The evaluation is reported as training reward curves together with benchmark performance curves, so the benchmarks function not just as end-of-training tests but as indicators of learning dynamics over time. The paper reports that GSPO yields stable progress throughout training and continues improving as compute increases, the query set is updated, and generation length is extended. This makes the benchmark suite part of the evidence that GSPO supports sustained capability gains rather than short-lived improvements.

# Metrics such as Pass@1 and Elo Rating

The paper uses different metrics depending on the benchmark. For AIME'24, the reported metric is average Pass@1 over 32 samplings. For LiveCodeBench, the metric is average Pass@1 over 8 samplings, using the 202410-202502 split. For CodeForces, the paper uses Elo Rating.

These metrics capture different aspects of model quality. Pass@1 measures how often the model’s first sampled answer is correct under repeated sampling, which is a useful way to evaluate the model’s effective problem-solving ability under stochastic generation. Elo Rating, on the other hand, is used to characterize CodeForces performance in a ranking-style setting. The paper does not present these metrics as abstract quantities; rather, they are used to show whether the RL algorithm improves the model in a way that transfers to demanding external evaluations.

# Comparison protocol between GSPO and GRPO

The paper compares GSPO against GRPO as the baseline. The comparison is intended to be fair, and for that reason the clipping ranges in each method are carefully tuned. The authors explicitly note that the GRPO settings are chosen to ensure a fair comparison, rather than simply using default values. This is important because clipping strongly affects the stability and behavior of policy-gradient RL methods.

The comparison also reflects a broader methodological point: GSPO is not evaluated in isolation, but in the same RL training pipeline where GRPO would normally be used. The paper highlights that GSPO achieves better training efficiency and stronger benchmark performance under the same training compute and consumed queries. In other words, the protocol is designed to measure not only end performance but also how much useful learning each method extracts from the same training budget.

# Clipping hyperparameters for GSPO and GRPO

The paper reports different clipping ranges for the two methods. For GSPO, the left and right clipping ranges in the sequence-level objective are set to $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$, respectively. For GRPO, the corresponding clipping ranges in its token-level objective are set to $0.2$ and $0.27$, respectively.

These values are not interchangeable because GSPO and GRPO define importance ratios differently. The paper stresses that the clipping ranges in GSPO and prior algorithms typically differ by orders of magnitude precisely because the underlying ratios are defined at different levels. This is why the authors do not compare the methods using identical numerical clipping values. Instead, each method is tuned in the scale appropriate to its own ratio definition.

# Why careful clipping-range tuning matters for fair evaluation

The paper gives special attention to clipping because it is one of the main mechanisms that controls stability in off-policy RL. If clipping is too loose, unstable samples can dominate the gradient; if it is too tight, learning may be overly constrained. Since GRPO’s token-level importance ratios and GSPO’s sequence-level ratios behave very differently, the paper argues that fair evaluation requires tuning each method in a way that suits its own objective.

This is also tied to the paper’s broader claim about instability. A poor choice of clipping range could make one algorithm appear worse simply because its ratio scale is mismatched, not because the algorithmic principle is inferior. By carefully selecting clipping ranges for both methods, the authors aim to isolate the effect of the optimization design itself. That is why the comparison is framed as a test of the algorithms’ intrinsic stability and efficiency rather than a test of arbitrary hyperparameter defaults.

# The role of query updates and generation length extension during training

The paper notes that GSPO supports continuous performance improvement through increasing training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending the generation length. These are not treated as incidental details; they are part of the training dynamics that demonstrate whether the method can scale. In the paper’s discussion, these changes are presented as ways to keep pushing the model’s capability boundaries during RL.

Regular query updates matter because they prevent training from overfitting to a fixed set of prompts and allow the model to keep encountering new or refreshed tasks. Extending generation length matters because the paper’s motivation centers on long-response tasks, where instability in prior methods becomes especially severe. The fact that GSPO remains stable under these changes is part of the evidence that it can serve as a practical foundation for continued RL scaling, rather than being limited to a narrow training regime.
