\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 14: 14. Broader Lessons, Limitations, and Future Directions

# The broader lesson of matching optimization granularity to reward granularity

A central lesson of the paper is that the granularity of the optimization objective should match the granularity at which reward is assigned. In the setting studied here, the verifier produces a reward for an entire query-response pair, not for individual tokens. That means the learning signal is naturally sequence-level: the model is being told whether a whole response is good or bad. The paper argues that when optimization is instead done token by token with per-token importance ratios, as in GRPO, the resulting update can become misaligned with the structure of the reward itself.

This point is not merely stylistic. The paper’s motivation section explains that token-level importance weights do not actually play the role that importance sampling requires, because they are computed from a single sampled token rather than from a sufficiently representative set of samples. As a result, the token-level correction introduces noise rather than correcting distribution mismatch. GSPO’s broader lesson is that when reward is sequence-level, the cleaner and more principled choice is to optimize at the sequence level as well.

# Why theoretically grounded importance weighting matters in practice

The paper makes a strong practical argument that theoretical correctness is not an abstract concern. The main claim is that the instability of GRPO comes from a fundamental misuse of importance sampling. Importance sampling is meant to reweight samples from a behavior distribution so they can estimate expectations under a target distribution. In the paper’s framing, this only works properly when the weight is used in a way that matches the sampling and averaging structure of the problem.

GSPO is introduced as a correction to that issue. Rather than using token-level importance ratios, it defines the importance ratio using the full sequence likelihood,
\[
s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}},
\]
and then applies clipping at the sequence level. The paper emphasizes that this is not just a mathematical reformulation: it changes training behavior substantially. Empirically, the sequence-level definition avoids the high-variance training noise that can accumulate across long responses and lead to collapse. The lesson is that a theoretically grounded ratio is valuable because it preserves the meaning of the update and helps make the optimization stable enough to scale.

# GSPO as a foundation for more scalable RL with LLMs

The paper presents GSPO as more than a fix for a specific instability. It is framed as a foundation for scaling reinforcement learning on large language models. This is because GSPO addresses several practical bottlenecks at once: it removes the dependence on a value model, stabilizes training on long responses, improves efficiency relative to GRPO, and simplifies certain infrastructure requirements.

The paper’s experiments are intended to show exactly this broader role. GSPO supports continuous performance improvement as training compute increases, the query set is refreshed, and generation length is extended. It also performs well in the large Mixture-of-Experts setting, where GRPO required additional machinery such as Routing Replay to maintain convergence. GSPO avoids that workaround. In this sense, the algorithm is positioned not only as a better objective, but as a more scalable operating principle for RL in language modeling.

# Potential extensions to richer reward structures and multi-turn settings

The paper itself suggests one extension beyond the basic sequence-level objective: GSPO-token. This variant is introduced for scenarios such as multi-turn RL, where finer-grained advantage adjustment may be desirable. In GSPO-token, the sequence-level importance ratio is retained, but token-wise advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ can vary across a response. The paper notes that this makes the method more flexible while preserving the key property that the sequence-level correction remains the basis of the update.

This suggests a natural direction for future work within the paper’s own framework: richer reward structures may require more nuanced advantage assignment, but not necessarily a return to the unstable token-level importance weighting criticized earlier. GSPO-token is important because it shows that sequence-level correction and token-level credit shaping need not be mutually exclusive. The paper’s formulation leaves open how best to design these token-wise advantages in more complex tasks, especially when the reward signal is not simply a single scalar for the whole response.

# Open questions around token-level credit assignment under sequence-level correction

Although the paper argues strongly against token-level importance ratios, it does not claim that token-level information is useless. In fact, the GSPO-token variant exists precisely because there are settings in which more detailed credit assignment may be helpful. The open question is how to assign such credit without reintroducing the instability that sequence-level GSPO is designed to avoid.

The paper’s discussion implies a tension here. On one hand, the reward is sequence-level, so the correction should also be sequence-level. On the other hand, some tasks may benefit from token-wise advantage customization. GSPO-token resolves this by keeping the importance ratio sequence-based while allowing per-token advantages. But the paper does not fully explore the design space of such advantages, leaving open how to best derive them in multi-turn or more structured settings. This is one of the clearest areas where additional research would be needed.

# Possible interactions with KL regularization and other RL stabilizers

The paper notes that it omits the KL regularization term in the PPO formulation for brevity, and the focus remains on clipping-based objectives. It also repeatedly frames clipping as a mechanism for excluding overly off-policy samples. However, the paper’s main contribution is about the correctness of the importance ratio itself, not about replacing all other stabilizers in RL.

That means a natural open question is how GSPO interacts with other stabilization methods, including KL regularization or any additional tricks used in large-scale RL systems. The paper does not analyze these interactions in detail. What it does show is that GSPO can already stabilize MoE training without Routing Replay, and that it tolerates practical precision discrepancies between training and inference engines better because it relies on sequence likelihoods. But how GSPO combines with other stabilizers, or whether some become unnecessary while others remain useful, is not fully resolved in the paper.

# Questions about generalization across tasks, models, and verifier types

The paper’s experiments focus on a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base and evaluate on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces. The reported gains are strong, and the paper also emphasizes successful application to the latest Qwen3 models. Still, the scope of the empirical evidence is tied to these settings.

This leaves open a broader question: how broadly does GSPO generalize across model families, task types, and verifier designs? The paper assumes rewards from a verifier $r(x,y) \in [0,1]$, and the reasoning is built around whole-response scoring. That makes GSPO especially natural in settings where a verifier can assess a complete answer. But the paper does not systematically study other reward forms, other response structures, or different model architectures outside the cases reported. So while the claims are compelling, the full range of applicability remains an open empirical question.

# Implications for future RL algorithm design in language modeling

The main design implication of the paper is that future RL algorithms should pay close attention to what exactly the importance ratio is correcting and at what level the reward is defined. GSPO shows that if the reward is sequence-level, then a sequence-level objective can be both simpler and more stable than a token-level one. It also suggests that RL algorithm design should be judged not only by asymptotic correctness, but by whether the correction mechanism remains meaningful under long sequences, large models, sparsity, and off-policy mini-batch training.

The paper also points toward infrastructure-aware algorithm design. Because sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision discrepancies, GSPO may reduce the need for recomputation under the training engine and simplify training-inference disaggregated systems. In that sense, algorithm design is not separate from systems design. A better objective can make the surrounding infrastructure simpler and more robust.

# Summary of the paper’s main contributions and impact

The paper’s main contribution is the proposal of Group Sequence Policy Optimization, a reinforcement learning algorithm for large language models that replaces token-level importance ratios with a sequence-level ratio based on sequence likelihood. This change is justified as a better match to the sequence-level reward used in RL for language generation. The paper argues that this alignment removes the source of instability in GRPO, which can suffer from high-variance gradients, especially on long responses and in large models.

A second contribution is the empirical demonstration that GSPO improves stability, efficiency, and performance over GRPO, including in challenging Mixture-of-Experts training. A third contribution is the GSPO-token variant, which preserves sequence-level correction while allowing token-wise advantage customization. Finally, the paper highlights practical benefits for RL infrastructure, including the elimination of Routing Replay and the potential to rely more directly on inference-engine likelihoods. Taken together, the paper’s impact is to reframe RL optimization for language models around the principle that the correction mechanism should be as structured as the reward signal it is trying to optimize.
