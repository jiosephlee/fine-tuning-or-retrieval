\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 7: 7. GSPO-token: A Flexible Token-Level Variant

# Motivation for token-wise advantage customization

GSPO was introduced as a sequence-level policy optimization method because the paper argues that, for standard single-response reinforcement learning with a sequence-level reward, the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. In that setting, a whole response receives a single score, so it is natural for the importance ratio and clipping mechanism to operate on the whole sequence rather than on individual tokens.

At the same time, the paper notes that there are scenarios in which a purely sequence-level advantage assignment may be too coarse. In particular, in multi-turn reinforcement learning, different parts of a response may play different roles, and one may want finer-grained control over how credit is assigned during optimization. That is the motivation for GSPO-token: it keeps the same sequence-level importance perspective introduced by GSPO, but allows advantages to be customized at the token level when a more detailed reward assignment is useful.

# Scenarios such as multi-turn RL and structured reward assignment

The paper presents multi-turn RL as the main motivating example for GSPO-token. In multi-turn settings, a response may contain multiple pieces of behavior that should not necessarily be treated as equally responsible for the final reward. A single response-level advantage may be too blunt if one wants to emphasize or de-emphasize specific tokens according to the structure of the task.

GSPO-token is therefore introduced as a flexible variant that supports token-wise advantage customization. The paper does not change the basic sequence-level correction principle of GSPO; instead, it creates a way to attach token-level advantage signals while still using the same sequence-level likelihood ratio as the central weighting mechanism. This makes GSPO-token suitable when one wants more detailed reward allocation but does not want to return to the problematic token-level importance weighting used in GRPO.

# Definition of the GSPO-token objective

The GSPO-token objective is written as

$$
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\min \left( s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) 
\right],
$$

where $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ is the token-specific advantage for token $y_{i,t}$, and $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is defined through a construction that is numerically equal to the sequence-level importance ratio. The objective is structurally similar to GSPO, but the advantage can now vary by token rather than being fixed for the entire response.

This formulation preserves the same outer logic as GSPO: samples are drawn from the old policy, the update uses clipped importance-based optimization, and the goal is still to use a stable importance ratio that reflects the response as a whole. The difference is that the reward signal inside the sum may now change from token to token.

# The stop-gradient or detach operation and why it is used

A key technical device in GSPO-token is the stop-gradient operator, denoted $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$. The paper explains that this operator means taking the numerical value of an expression while preventing gradients from flowing through it, corresponding to the `detach` operation in PyTorch.

This is used so that GSPO-token can build a token-level term that has the same numerical value as the sequence-level importance ratio without changing the gradient path in an unintended way. The construction uses

$$
s_{i,t}(\theta) 
= \mathrm{sg} \left[ s_{i}(\theta) \right] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }.
$$

Because the denominator is detached, the fraction has numerical value $1$. The purpose is not to introduce a new probability correction factor, but to embed the token-specific optimization term inside a construction that still behaves like the sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$ in value. In other words, the detach operation is what makes the token-level expression usable without turning it into a different importance weight.

# Constructing token-level terms that are numerically equal to the sequence ratio

The paper emphasizes that $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$. This is because the ratio

$$
\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }
$$

has numerical value $1$. Therefore, multiplying by $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ yields a token-level expression whose value matches the sequence-level importance ratio. This lets the token-level objective remain aligned with GSPO’s sequence-based scaling, even though the reward weights $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ may vary across positions.

This construction is important because it avoids replacing the sequence-level importance ratio with a genuinely token-level ratio of the GRPO type. The paper is explicit that GSPO-token is not intended to reintroduce the unstable token-wise off-policy correction that GSPO was designed to avoid. Instead, the token-level term is a mathematical device for distributing advantages across tokens while preserving the same sequence-level ratio numerically.

# Gradient derivation for GSPO-token

The gradient derivation in the paper shows that GSPO-token remains tied to the sequence-level ratio in its optimization behavior. Starting from the objective and ignoring clipping for brevity, the gradient is derived as

$$
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}  s_i (\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\widehat{A}_{i,t} \frac{ \nabla_{\theta} \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }
\right],
$$

which is then rewritten as

$$
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}  \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})  
\right].
$$

This derivation makes clear what GSPO-token is doing. The gradient is still scaled by the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$, and the per-token terms only appear inside the inner sum through their advantages and log-likelihood gradients. Thus, the optimization is sequence-weighted, not token-weighted in the GRPO sense.

# How GSPO-token preserves sequence-level weighting in the gradient

The paper’s central claim about GSPO-token is that it preserves the same sequence-level weighting behavior as GSPO. This is visible in the gradient, where the factor

$$
\left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
$$

multiplies the whole token-wise gradient contribution for a response. So although the advantages may differ across tokens, the response itself is still weighted as a sequence. The sequence ratio acts as a shared multiplier for all tokens in the response.

This is exactly the distinction the paper wants to preserve. GSPO-token does not assign independent importance ratios to individual tokens. Instead, it uses a single sequence-level ratio to control how much the full response contributes to the update, while letting the advantage inside the response vary by token. In that sense, GSPO-token keeps GSPO’s stable sequence-level optimization structure intact.

# Conditions under which GSPO-token is equivalent to GSPO

The paper states that GSPO-token and GSPO are numerically identical in the optimization objective, clipping condition, and theoretical gradient when all tokens in a response share the same advantage, that is, when $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$ for every token $t$ in response $y_i$. Under this condition, the token-level sum collapses to the same behavior as the original GSPO objective.

This equivalence is important because it shows that GSPO-token is not a fundamentally different algorithm in the common case where the reward is response-level. Instead, it is a generalization. When token-specific advantages are unnecessary, GSPO-token reduces to GSPO. When token-specific advantages are useful, it offers more flexibility without changing the underlying sequence-level ratio machinery.

# The tradeoff between flexibility and conceptual simplicity

GSPO-token introduces a useful degree of flexibility, but the paper also makes clear that this flexibility comes with a tradeoff. GSPO is conceptually simpler because everything is defined at the sequence level: the ratio, the clipping, and the advantage all align directly with the response as a whole. GSPO-token adds extra freedom by permitting token-wise advantages, but that also makes the reward assignment more elaborate.

The important point is that this added flexibility does not come at the cost of reverting to the problematic token-level importance ratio design of GRPO. GSPO-token keeps the sequence-level weighting principle intact while allowing finer control over how advantages are distributed across tokens. In the paper’s framing, this makes GSPO-token a practical extension for cases like multi-turn RL, while GSPO remains the cleaner default when a single response-level advantage is sufficient.
