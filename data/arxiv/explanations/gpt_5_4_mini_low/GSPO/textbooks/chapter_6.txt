\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 6: 6. Gradient Analysis: Why GSPO Is More Stable

# Gradient derivation for the unclipped GSPO objective

To understand why GSPO is more stable than GRPO, it helps to begin with the unclipped version of the GSPO objective. GSPO defines optimization at the response level, not the token level. For a query $x$, and a sampled response $y_i$ from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the sequence-level importance ratio is

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.
\]

If we ignore clipping for the moment, the GSPO objective reduces to maximizing the expected product of this sequence ratio and the response advantage. In the paper’s notation, the unclipped form is

\[
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i\right].
\]

The gradient is then obtained by differentiating through $s_i(\theta)$. Because $s_i(\theta)$ is an exponential of an average of token log-likelihood ratios, its gradient is especially clean. The paper shows that

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i \cdot \nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)
\right].
\]

This already reveals the central structure: the entire response contributes a single multiplicative weight, $s_i(\theta)$, and this weight is shared by all tokens in the response.

# Expressing the GSPO gradient in terms of token log-likelihoods

The sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is defined using the full response likelihood, which itself factorizes autoregressively across tokens. Expanding the log of the ratio gives

\[
\log s_i(\theta)
=
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

Taking the gradient of this expression yields a simple average of token-level log-likelihood gradients. Substituting into the GSPO gradient gives

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\widehat{A}_i
\cdot
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

This form makes the mechanism of GSPO easy to interpret. The gradient is an average over token log-likelihood gradients, but every token in the response is multiplied by exactly the same response-level factor. In other words, the response’s sequence likelihood determines how strongly the whole trajectory is updated, while the tokens inside that response are not individually reweighted against one another.

That uniformity is important. GSPO does not try to decide that one token within a response is “more important” than another based on token-wise importance ratios. Instead, the response as a whole is treated as the optimization unit, which matches the fact that the reward is also assigned to the whole response.

# How the sequence-level ratio modulates the whole response uniformly

The paper emphasizes that the sequence-level ratio has a clear interpretation: it measures how far the response $y_i$ sampled from the old policy deviates from the current policy. Because the ratio is computed on the entire response likelihood, it naturally aligns with sequence-level reward and sequence-level optimization.

This alignment matters not just conceptually but mathematically. Under GSPO, if a response receives a positive advantage, then the full response is encouraged proportionally to the same sequence-level weight. If it receives a negative advantage, the full response is discouraged, again under the same response-level weight. The update is therefore coherent across the entire sequence.

The paper also notes that length normalization is built into the definition of $s_i(\theta)$ through the exponent $1/|y_i|$. This is done to reduce variance and keep the ratio in a unified numerical range. Without this normalization, even a few token likelihood changes could create large fluctuations in the response-level importance ratio, and responses of different lengths would require different clipping ranges. The normalized sequence ratio therefore serves two purposes at once: it makes the optimization more stable, and it makes the importance ratio comparable across responses of varying length.

# Gradient derivation for the unclipped GRPO objective

GRPO starts from a similar group-based advantage construction, but it applies the importance ratio at the token level. For each response $y_i$ and token position $t$, the importance ratio is

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

With clipping ignored, the GRPO objective has token-wise terms of the form $w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_i$, averaged over tokens and responses. Differentiating that objective gives the gradient shown in the paper:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\widehat{A}_i
\cdot
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

This formula looks superficially similar to GSPO because it also contains token log-likelihood gradients. But there is a crucial difference: each token gradient is multiplied by its own token-specific importance ratio, rather than by a single response-level ratio shared across the entire sequence.

That difference is the core of the stability argument in the paper. In GRPO, the response is no longer updated uniformly. Instead, each token is scaled by a potentially different factor, and those factors are themselves derived from single token samples. The result is a much noisier gradient signal.

# Comparison of token weighting in GRPO and GSPO

The paper’s key observation is that GSPO and GRPO differ in how they weight the gradients of token log-likelihoods. In GSPO, all tokens in a response are weighted equally by the same response-level factor. In GRPO, each token receives its own importance weight.

This distinction is not just cosmetic. Because the importance ratios in GRPO are token-specific, the gradient contributions of different tokens within the same response can be unevenly amplified or suppressed. The paper points out that these weights can vary in ranges that depend on the sign of the advantage. For positive advantages, the weights can lie in $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$, while for negative advantages they can lie in $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$. As a result, the gradient signal within a response is not uniform.

GSPO avoids this internal imbalance. Since its ratio is computed at the sequence level, every token in a response is modulated equally. This means the response behaves as a single unit during optimization, which is much closer to how the reward is assigned. The paper argues that this alignment between reward unit and optimization unit is a central reason GSPO is more stable.

# Unequal token weighting in GRPO as an instability source

The paper identifies GRPO’s token-level importance weighting as a fundamental source of instability. The problem is not merely that the weights are different; it is that they are different for reasons that do not faithfully correct the sampling mismatch. Importance sampling is meant to reweight samples drawn from a behavior distribution so that they approximate expectations under a target distribution. But that logic requires averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution. In GRPO, the token-level ratio is computed from a single token sample at each position, which is too small a unit to reliably perform the distribution correction it is supposed to represent.

Because the token-level weights are misapplied in this way, they introduce high-variance noise into the gradients. The paper argues that this noise accumulates over long responses. Each token contributes a slightly distorted weighted gradient, and as the response grows longer, those distortions pile up. The clipping mechanism does not remove the underlying issue; instead, it can amplify the instability because it interacts with already noisy token-wise ratios.

This is why the paper describes GRPO’s objective as ill-posed in practice. The instability is not simply an optimization inconvenience. It is presented as a structural flaw that can eventually cause catastrophic model collapse.

# How clipping intervals interact with positive and negative advantages

Clipping is used in both PPO-style methods and GRPO-style methods to restrict overly off-policy updates. In the paper’s analysis, clipping matters because the effect of the importance ratio depends on the sign of the advantage.

When the advantage is positive, a larger ratio increases the update, but clipping prevents the ratio from becoming too large. When the advantage is negative, the ratio pushes the update in the opposite direction, and clipping again bounds the update. However, because GRPO applies this mechanism token by token, the clipping threshold is enforced separately at each token position rather than on the response as a whole.

That tokenwise clipping creates another layer of irregularity. The paper notes that the actual ranges of the importance ratios can be very different between GRPO and GSPO because their ratio definitions are different. In GSPO, the response-level ratio is already length-normalized and therefore numerically more controlled. In GRPO, the token-level ratios can fluctuate independently across positions, causing some token updates to be clipped while others are not. This uneven clipping contributes to the unpredictability of the total response gradient.

Thus, clipping is not inherently the problem; the problem is how it interacts with the unit of optimization. Sequence-level clipping in GSPO is coherent with sequence-level reward. Token-level clipping in GRPO is not, because it acts on a finer granularity than the signal being optimized.

# Accumulation of token-level weighting errors across long responses

The paper repeatedly emphasizes that the instability in GRPO becomes worse as response length grows. This is a natural consequence of token-wise weighting. Each token contributes its own weighted gradient term, and each of those terms may deviate from the ideal distribution-corrected signal. Over long sequences, the discrepancies accumulate.

This accumulation is especially problematic in the settings the paper cares about, such as large language models trained on long-response tasks. In such settings, the sampling mismatch between the old policy and the updated policy becomes more pronounced, and the token-level importance ratios become increasingly unreliable. Since the response has many tokens, even small distortions at each position can compound into a large gradient error.

GSPO avoids this by collapsing the response into a single importance ratio. The response-level ratio still reflects the degree of mismatch between the old and new policies, but it does so in a way that is tied to the whole trajectory rather than to individual token samples. That makes the gradient less sensitive to local fluctuations and therefore more robust over long outputs.

# Why equal per-response weighting improves robustness

The stability advantage of GSPO comes from a simple principle stated explicitly in the paper: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward. Since the reward is assigned to the whole response, the response should be the unit of clipping, weighting, and optimization.

Equal weighting within a response improves robustness because it avoids making the update depend on arbitrary token-by-token fluctuations. The sequence-level ratio captures the overall deviation of a response from the old policy, while the shared response-level factor ensures that all tokens move together. This reduces variance and prevents one part of the response from being disproportionately emphasized simply because its token-wise ratio happened to be extreme.

The paper’s gradient derivation shows that GSPO weights all the token log-likelihood gradients in a response equally. That is the key structural difference. Rather than producing a patchwork of token-specific updates, GSPO produces a coherent response-level update signal. The authors argue that this makes training more stable, more efficient, and more resistant to collapse.

# Practical interpretation of the theoretical gradient difference

The gradient formulas are not just mathematical details; they explain the empirical behavior reported in the paper. GSPO’s response-level gradient is simpler, more consistent, and more aligned with the reward structure. GRPO’s token-level gradient is noisier, because each token carries its own importance ratio and the resulting weights can fluctuate significantly as training progresses.

This difference helps explain why GSPO can continue improving with more compute, longer generations, and regularly updated query sets, while GRPO is more vulnerable to instability. It also helps explain why GSPO is especially effective in MoE training, where token-level likelihoods can fluctuate more sharply because the activated experts can change after each update. In such settings, token-wise importance ratios become even less reliable, which makes GSPO’s response-level formulation particularly valuable.

In practical terms, the gradient analysis says that GSPO trains on a cleaner learning signal. It does not try to over-interpret each token as its own importance-sampled event. Instead, it treats the whole generated response as the unit of optimization, which is exactly where the reward lives. That alignment is the theoretical reason the paper gives for GSPO’s improved robustness over GRPO.
