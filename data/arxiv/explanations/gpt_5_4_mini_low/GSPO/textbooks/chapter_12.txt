\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 12: 12. Infrastructure Implications: Toward Simpler RL Systems

# Training-engine and inference-engine precision mismatches

A central systems issue discussed in the paper is that the engines used for training and for inference are often not identical in practice. The paper specifically notes precision discrepancies between training engines such as Megatron and inference engines such as SGLang and vLLM. This matters because reinforcement learning for language models relies on likelihoods computed for sampled responses under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and those likelihoods are used inside the optimization objective.

The practical consequence is that the same sampled response may not receive exactly the same numerical likelihood when evaluated by different engines. The paper treats this as a precision mismatch problem rather than an algorithmic one. In a system that depends heavily on token-level likelihoods, even small numerical differences can become problematic because the optimization is sensitive to per-token values. GSPO is introduced partly as a response to this systems friction, because its sequence-level formulation is described as more tolerant to such discrepancies.

# Why old-policy likelihoods are often recomputed in practice

The paper explains that, in practice, likelihoods for sampled responses under the old policy are typically recomputed using the training engine. This is done because the training procedure needs those likelihoods for optimization, and if the rollout was produced elsewhere, the training system wants a consistent computation path. In other words, even though samples may come from an inference system, the training stack often recomputes the relevant quantities so that optimization can proceed using the training engine’s own numerical outputs.

This recomputation is a practical burden because it adds extra work to the RL pipeline. The paper presents it as something that becomes less necessary when the optimization objective is sequence-level rather than token-level. Since GSPO uses sequence-level likelihoods, it reduces the need to insist on exact token-by-token recomputation under the training engine.

# The burden of token-level likelihood dependence in existing methods

The paper’s critique of existing methods is not only statistical but also infrastructural. Methods like GRPO depend on token-level importance ratios, meaning that the training objective is tied directly to individual next-token likelihoods. This creates a strong dependency on fine-grained likelihood computations, which must be accurate at every token position.

That token-level dependence makes the system brittle. If the likelihoods differ slightly across engines, or if the routing behavior of a model changes between the old and new policies, the token-level ratios can fluctuate substantially. The paper argues that this is especially problematic for GRPO and even more so in Mixture-of-Experts settings, where token-level likelihoods can become unstable due to expert-activation volatility. From an infrastructure standpoint, token-level dependence forces the system to do more work and to maintain tighter numerical consistency.

# Why sequence-level optimization is more tolerant to precision discrepancies

GSPO replaces token-level importance ratios with a sequence-level importance ratio based on the full response likelihood, defined as $\frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)}$, with length normalization. The paper’s systems argument is that this sequence-level quantity is much more tolerant of precision discrepancies than token-level likelihoods.

The intuition given is straightforward: a single response-level likelihood aggregates over the entire sequence, so it is less sensitive to small numerical inconsistencies at individual token positions. In contrast, token-level objectives expose every local mismatch directly to the gradient. By operating at the sequence level, GSPO therefore makes the optimization signal more robust to the kinds of precision differences that arise when training and inference use different engines.

# Potential to use inference-engine likelihoods directly

One of the clearest infrastructure implications in the paper is that GSPO may allow practitioners to use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine directly for optimization. The paper emphasizes that this is possible because GSPO uses sequence-level likelihoods rather than token-level ones. Since the sequence-level quantity is more tolerant of precision discrepancies, the system does not necessarily need to recompute the old-policy likelihoods with the training engine.

This is presented as a meaningful simplification. If the inference engine can already provide the likelihoods needed by GSPO, then the training pipeline can avoid an extra recomputation step. The paper frames this as a direct benefit of the algorithmic design, not as an auxiliary trick. In this sense, GSPO does more than improve stability and efficiency; it also opens the door to a leaner implementation strategy.

# Benefits for partial rollout settings

The paper specifically mentions that this direct use of inference-engine likelihoods can be especially beneficial in partial rollout settings. Although the paper does not elaborate extensively on the mechanics of partial rollout, it highlights this setting as one where the infrastructure simplification becomes valuable. In such cases, the system may be assembling or processing only part of the rollout data at a time, which makes a clean separation between rollout generation and optimization particularly useful.

Because GSPO works at the sequence level, it aligns more naturally with these settings. The system can rely on the response-level likelihoods associated with sampled sequences rather than having to reconstruct or re-evaluate every token-level contribution in the training engine. This reduces friction in the rollout-to-training pipeline.

# Benefits for multi-turn RL

The paper also identifies multi-turn RL as a setting that can benefit from GSPO’s infrastructure properties. Multi-turn interaction naturally creates more complex rollout structures, because a response may be embedded in a longer interaction history and may require finer handling of how data is passed through the system. In such settings, the simplicity of sequence-level likelihood use becomes especially attractive.

The paper already introduces a token-level variant, GSPO-token, for cases where finer-grained advantage adjustment is desired. Even so, the broader infrastructure claim remains: GSPO’s sequence-level formulation makes the optimization side less dependent on precise token-level recomputation. That means multi-turn RL systems may be easier to organize if they can work directly with sequence likelihoods rather than maintaining a strict token-level training-engine computation path.

# Benefits for training-inference disaggregated architectures

The paper points out that sequence-level likelihoods are useful in training-inference disaggregated frameworks. The central reason is that these architectures separate the system that generates rollouts from the system that performs optimization. Once the rollout and training components are decoupled, the question of which engine computes the likelihoods becomes important.

GSPO reduces the pressure to recompute likelihoods in the training engine, because its optimization signal is built from sequence-level likelihoods that are less sensitive to precision mismatches. This makes it more compatible with a pipeline in which inference-time generation and training-time optimization are handled by different systems. In effect, GSPO helps bridge the gap between the rollout side and the optimization side without requiring exact low-level parity in every token probability computation.

# How GSPO may simplify RL infrastructure design

The paper’s broader infrastructure claim is that GSPO has the potential to simplify RL system design. This follows from several properties taken together. First, GSPO removes dependence on token-level importance ratios. Second, it reduces sensitivity to precision discrepancies between inference and training engines. Third, it may allow direct use of inference-engine likelihoods without recomputation. Fourth, it avoids some of the specialized workarounds that were needed for stability in previous methods, especially in MoE training.

In the paper’s view, these properties mean that GSPO is not only a better optimization algorithm but also a better systems primitive. A system built around sequence-level likelihoods can be simpler because it has fewer moving parts tied to exact token-level consistency. The paper closes this line of argument by suggesting that GSPO could serve as a robust and scalable foundation for RL infrastructure, helping future systems train large language models more cleanly and with less complexity.
