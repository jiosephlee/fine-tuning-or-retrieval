\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# Why Today’s AI Training Breaks: The Big Idea Behind GSPO

Reinforcement learning has become one of the key tools for pushing large language models beyond basic next-token prediction. It is what helps models get better at hard tasks like competition math, programming, and long-form reasoning. But as models get larger and responses get longer, training can become unstable. In the worst cases, the model does not just stop improving — it collapses.

The paper on Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO, is about fixing that failure mode.

## The problem: token-level training can become noisy and unstable

Many modern RL methods for language models update the model using samples generated by an older version of the policy. That means the training data is already slightly “off-policy” by the time the optimizer sees it. To handle this, methods like PPO and GRPO use clipping to prevent updates from drifting too far.

The issue, according to the paper, is that GRPO applies importance weighting at the token level. In other words, it tries to correct the mismatch between old and new policies one token at a time.

That sounds reasonable, but the paper argues this is the wrong unit of correction for language generation. A single token probability is only one small piece of a full response, and using token-level importance weights does not properly play the role that importance sampling is supposed to play. Instead of correcting distribution mismatch, it injects high-variance noise into the gradients. As responses get longer, that noise accumulates. With clipping on top, the instability can become severe enough to cause model collapse.

The core claim is simple: when the reward is assigned to the whole response, the optimization should also happen at the level of the whole response.

## GSPO’s main idea: optimize whole responses, not individual tokens

GSPO changes the unit of optimization from tokens to complete sequences.

Instead of computing an importance ratio for each token, GSPO computes a single sequence-level importance ratio based on the likelihood of the entire response under the new policy versus the old policy. That ratio is length-normalized, so responses of different lengths can be compared more consistently.

Then, rather than clipping each token independently, GSPO clips the whole response as one unit.

This matters because the reward in these RL setups is already sequence-level. A model gets scored on the quality of the full answer, not on whether each token was individually good. GSPO aligns the optimization with that reality.

In the paper’s framing, this is the key design principle: the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward.

## Why this helps training stability

The sequence-level view changes the gradient behavior in an important way.

Under GRPO, different tokens in the same response can receive different effective weights, which can create unpredictable training dynamics over time. Under GSPO, all tokens in a response are treated equally, since the response-level importance ratio applies uniformly.

That has two practical benefits:

First, it reduces instability caused by token-level noise. Instead of making many fine-grained corrections that may not be reliable, GSPO makes one correction per response.

Second, it makes clipping more meaningful. If a sampled response is too far from the current policy, GSPO can exclude it at the response level rather than trying to patch up individual tokens that may have very different behavior.

The paper reports that this leads to more stable training, better efficiency, and improved performance compared with GRPO.

## A particularly important benefit for Mixture-of-Experts models

The paper places a lot of emphasis on Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, models. These models are powerful, but their sparse activation patterns can make RL training even more fragile. After each update, the experts activated for the same response can change significantly, which makes token-level importance ratios even less reliable.

To work around that problem, previous training setups used a strategy called Routing Replay, which forced the old and new policies to reuse the same routing decisions. That helped GRPO converge, but it added extra complexity and overhead.

GSPO avoids that workaround. Because it operates at the sequence level, it is much less sensitive to expert-activation volatility. The paper argues that this lets MoE RL training proceed stably without Routing Replay.

## Why this is interesting beyond the algorithm itself

GSPO is not just a new loss function. It also points to a cleaner way to think about RL infrastructure for language models.

Because GSPO relies on sequence-level likelihood rather than token-level likelihood, it is more tolerant of small precision differences between training and inference systems. The paper suggests this could make it easier to use likelihoods produced by inference engines directly, instead of recomputing them with the training engine.

So the benefit is not only better optimization. It is also a simpler system design.

## The takeaway

The big idea behind GSPO is straightforward: if the reward is for the whole response, then the correction and optimization should also happen for the whole response.

That one shift from token-level to sequence-level optimization is what the paper argues makes GSPO more stable than GRPO. In the authors’ experiments, it improved training stability, efficiency, and benchmark performance, and it was important enough to help power the latest Qwen3 models.

For large language models, especially MoE models and long-response tasks, that is a practical lesson with broad implications: sometimes the way to make RL work better is not to add more machinery, but to align the math with the thing you actually care about.
