\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why do the authors say GRPO's token-level importance ratio is "invalid" if PPO also uses token-level ratios?
Question:
I'm trying to understand the main criticism of GRPO in this paper. The authors argue that GRPO's token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(	heta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}$ is a misapplication of importance sampling because there is only one sampled token at each position, so it does not really perform distribution correction.

But PPO is also presented with token-level ratios, and PPO is a standard RL method. So what exactly is the difference here?

Is the issue specifically that in GRPO the reward/advantage is sequence-level while the correction is token-level, whereas in PPO the token-level advantage $\widehat{A}_t$ is supposed to match the token-level objective? Or are the authors making a stronger claim that token-level ratios are fundamentally wrong in language-model RL?

I'm confused about whether this is:

1. a criticism of GRPO only,
2. a criticism of PPO-style language model RL more generally, or
3. a criticism only when all tokens share the same sequence-level advantage.
Answer:
The paper is **not** claiming that “token-level ratios are always invalid in every RL method.” The criticism is narrower: it is about the way **GRPO** uses token-level importance ratios together with a **sequence-level reward/advantage**.

In PPO, the ratio $w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}$ appears in the standard per-timestep policy-gradient surrogate because PPO is treating each token as an action in a sequential decision process. Crucially, PPO also has a token-level advantage $\widehat{A}_t$, which is meant to estimate the value of taking that token-action in that state. So the ratio and the advantage are matched at the same granularity: token by token. That is why the ratio has a clear role in PPO’s objective.

GRPO, as written in the paper, is different. It uses the same token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, but the advantage is not token-specific; it is the same sequence-level quantity $\widehat{A}_i$ for every token in the response. The paper’s argument is that this makes the ratio no longer play the usual “importance sampling correction” role, because there is no averaging over multiple samples from each next-token distribution to correct. Instead, each token’s ratio becomes an extra multiplicative factor on a shared sequence-level signal, which the authors say injects high-variance noise and gets worse for long responses.

So the distinction is mostly your option **(3)**, with a bit of **(1)** as a consequence: the criticism is mainly of **GRPO’s token-level weighting when all tokens share one sequence-level advantage**. The authors are not saying token-level ratios are fundamentally wrong in language-model RL. In fact, the paper explicitly acknowledges that a token-level objective can make sense when the advantages are token-specific, which is why they introduce the token-level GSPO variant. But when the reward is only sequence-level, they argue the optimization should also be sequence-level, which is why GSPO uses the sequence likelihood ratio
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}.
$$

In short: PPO’s token ratios are part of a token-level policy-gradient estimator; GRPO’s token ratios are what the paper calls “misapplied” because they are used with a sequence-level advantage. That is the paper’s central objection.

### Why is the GSPO importance ratio defined as $\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y|}$ instead of the plain sequence ratio?
Question:
In GSPO, the importance ratio is not the usual sequence ratio

$\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y \mid x)}$,

but instead its length-normalized version

$ s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}.$

The paper says this reduces variance and keeps the values in a unified numerical range. But I don't understand the theoretical status of this modification.

If importance sampling requires the exact density ratio, then doesn't replacing it with the geometric mean of token-wise ratios break the unbiasedness / correctness of the estimator? Is GSPO still doing importance sampling in the formal sense, or is this better viewed as a heuristic trust-region surrogate based on average log-likelihood shift per token?

In other words, what is the mathematical justification for taking the $1/|y|$ power here, beyond numerical stability?
Answer:
The short answer is that the $1/|y|$ power is **not** there because it is required by exact importance sampling. It is there because GSPO is using the sequence ratio as a **stability-controlled surrogate**, and the length normalization makes that surrogate numerically usable across responses of different lengths.

In exact importance sampling, if you want to rewrite an expectation under $\pi_\theta$ using samples from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, the correcting weight really is the full density ratio
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
That is the mathematically correct ratio for the whole sequence. Replacing it by
$$
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y|}
$$
does change the quantity being optimized, so it is not an unbiased importance-sampling estimator of the original sequence-level expectation. In that strict sense, yes: the normalized ratio is no longer “exact” importance sampling.

But the paper is not claiming a classical importance-sampling correction in the estimator-theory sense. What it argues is that the **sequence-level** ratio is the right object to use for sequence-level rewards, and then it further **normalizes that ratio by length** to avoid the pathological scaling you would otherwise get from autoregressive products. Since
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}
= \exp\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}\right),
$$
the plain sequence ratio grows or shrinks **exponentially with sequence length**. That means longer responses would naturally produce much larger variance in the ratio, and the clipping threshold would effectively mean something very different for short and long outputs. Taking the $1/|y|$ power turns this into
$$
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}\right),
$$
which is just the exponentiated **average log-likelihood shift per token**. Intuitively, it measures “how much the policy changed on average per token” rather than “how much the whole sequence probability changed in total.” That is why the paper says it reduces variance and keeps values in a unified numerical range.

So the right way to read GSPO is: the full sequence ratio is the theoretically natural off-policy correction at the sequence level, but the $1/|y|$-normalized version is a **practical surrogate/trust-region style scaling** of that ratio, chosen to make clipping and optimization stable across varying response lengths. It is not an exact unbiased IS estimator, and the paper does not present it as such. The justification is primarily stability and comparability across lengths, not preservation of the exact importance-sampling identity.

A useful intuition is that the plain ratio answers, “How much more probable is this entire response under the new policy?” while the normalized ratio answers, “How much did the policy change per token on average?” GSPO uses the second notion because, for long language-model responses, the first one is far too volatile to be a useful optimization signal.

### How does clipping work when GSPO says it clips "entire responses" but the gradient still sums over tokens?
Question:
The paper repeatedly says that GSPO performs sequence-level clipping and clips entire responses rather than individual tokens. But in the gradient formula,

$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_i \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)} \right)^{1/|y_i|} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) \right],$

the update is still a sum over token log-probability gradients.

So what does "clipping an entire response" concretely mean in implementation?

My current guess is: for a response $y_i$, if the scalar $s_i(\theta)$ falls outside $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$, then the same clipped scalar is applied to every token in that response. Is that correct? If so, are tokens from that response still contributing gradient after clipping, just with a capped shared multiplier, or are they completely dropped?
Answer:
Yes — your guess is essentially correct, with one important PPO-style nuance.

In GSPO, “clipping an entire response” means that the clipping decision is made on the single sequence-level ratio
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|},
$$
not on each token ratio separately. So every token in the response shares the same scalar multiplier for that update.

The key point is that GSPO uses the same clipped-surrogate form as PPO/GRPO:
$$
\min\!\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\big).
$$
This does **not** mean “multiply by the clipped scalar and then keep backpropagating through it” in all cases. It means that once the sequence-level ratio moves outside the clipping region in the direction that would improve the objective too much, the surrogate switches to the clipped branch, which is constant with respect to further changes in $s_i$ in that region. In the usual PPO interpretation, that response is therefore effectively **censored** from further policy-gradient pressure.

So concretely, for a given response $y_i$:

- if $s_i(\theta)$ is inside the clipping range, then all tokens in that response contribute to the gradient, all weighted by the same sequence-level scalar $s_i(\theta)$;
- if $s_i(\theta)$ is clipped, then that response’s contribution is truncated, and its gradient contribution is effectively zero in the clipped direction.

The paper’s gradient formula
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_i
s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i
\cdot
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right]
$$
is explicitly derived **with clipping omitted for brevity**. So that equation shows the underlying sequence-level weighting of token log-probability gradients, but not the truncation effect of the clip operator.

So the right mental model is: GSPO does **not** clip tokens independently; it clips the **response as a unit**. When unclipped, every token in that response shares the same scalar importance weight. When clipped, the response is effectively excluded from further gradient update in that clipping direction, rather than having each token separately clipped.

### Why does having only one sampled token per position make the token-level ratio useless for importance sampling?
Question:
The motivation section says that importance sampling relies on averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution, and that GRPO fails because at each token position there is only a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{i,<t})$.

I don't fully understand this argument. In Monte Carlo methods, we often estimate expectations using one sample per state/action occurrence and then average across many trajectories. Why isn't averaging across many sequences and many token positions enough here?

More concretely, suppose we want an expectation under the new policy over next-token events. Why can't the collection of all token occurrences across a batch provide the needed averaging, even if each individual context $ (x, y_{<t}) $ only contributes one sampled token?

Is the problem that each context is different, so there is no repeated sampling from the same conditional distribution? If that is the real issue, could someone explain it in a more formal way?
Answer:
Yes — the key issue is that the relevant random variable is not “a token at position $t$” in the abstract, but “a token drawn from the specific conditional distribution $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t})$ for this exact prefix.” In GRPO, the token-level ratio is

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

That looks like ordinary importance sampling, but importance sampling only works as a correction when you can average over many samples from the same behavior distribution. The paper’s point is that at each position $t$, you do not have many samples from the same conditional distribution $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x,y_{<t})$; you have exactly one sample, namely the sampled token $y_{i,t}$ from one realized prefix $y_{i,<t}$. So the “importance weight” is not correcting a Monte Carlo estimate of an expectation over that conditional distribution in the usual way. It is just a noisy multiplicative factor attached to one sample.

Your intuition about averaging across many sequences is reasonable, but it does not fix the problem, because those samples are not drawn from the same distribution. Across a batch, you have many different prefixes $y_{i,<t}$, so each token position corresponds to a different conditional distribution. Averaging one sample from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{i,<t})$ and one sample from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x, y'_{j,<s})$ is not like averaging repeated samples from one common state; it is averaging across many different states. That can be useful for estimating an overall gradient, but it does not make the token-wise ratio a good importance-sampling correction for each token-level term.

A formal way to say this is that importance sampling targets an identity of the form

$$
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
=
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}f(z)\right],
$$

where the weight is the density ratio for the *same* variable $z$. In language modeling, the natural “same variable” is the whole response $y$, whose density ratio is

$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}.
$$

That is exactly what GSPO uses. By contrast, the token-level term in GRPO is a ratio for one coordinate of a much larger sequential process, and the paper argues that applying it token by token does not give the intended distribution correction. Instead, it injects high-variance noise, and because there are many tokens per response, those noisy effects accumulate over length.

So the short answer is: yes, the issue is that each token position is a different conditional distribution, so you do not have repeated sampling from the same next-token distribution to make a token-level importance weight behave like a proper Monte Carlo correction. The paper’s remedy is to move the importance ratio to the sequence level, where the sampling unit and the reward unit match: one sampled response, one sequence-level reward, one sequence-level ratio. That is why GSPO is better aligned with importance sampling in the sense the paper is using.

### Is GSPO still optimizing the expected reward under the new policy, or only a surrogate objective?
Question:
The paper presents GSPO as being more theoretically grounded than GRPO because it uses a sequence-level importance ratio aligned with the sequence-level reward. However, because the ratio is length-normalized,

$ s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}, $

it no longer looks like the standard off-policy correction factor that would convert an expectation under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ into one under $\pi_{\theta}$.

So what objective is GSPO actually optimizing?

Is there an underlying expected reward maximization problem for which Equation (6) is a proper lower bound or trust-region surrogate, similar in spirit to PPO? Or is the claim only that GSPO gives a better-behaved gradient signal empirically, not that it is an unbiased or consistent estimator of any canonical RL objective?

I'm trying to place GSPO on the spectrum between "principled estimator" and "effective heuristic."
Answer:
GSPO is best understood as a **surrogate, PPO-style objective**, not as an unbiased importance-sampling estimator of a canonical expected-reward objective under the new policy.

The key point is exactly the one you noticed: if the paper used the true sequence-level importance ratio

$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y \mid x)},
$$

then the usual importance-sampling identity would connect an expectation under $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ to one under $\pi_\theta$. But GSPO does **not** use that ratio. It uses the length-normalized version

$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|},
$$

which the paper explicitly motivates as a variance-control device to keep the ratio in a “unified numerical range.” That normalization breaks the interpretation as exact off-policy correction. So if you are asking whether Equation (6) is a proper importance-sampling estimator of expected reward under the new policy, the answer is no.

What GSPO is doing instead is closer to the logic of PPO: it defines a **clipped surrogate objective** on samples drawn from the old policy and uses that as a stable local proxy for improving the policy. In other words, the objective in Equation (6) is not presented as the exact RL objective

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}[r(x,y)],
$$

nor as a lower bound on it in the way that, say, PPO is often heuristically justified. The paper’s own derivation treats GSPO as an optimization rule over rollouts from $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ with group-normalized advantages, and the clipping is there to exclude overly off-policy samples and stabilize training. The “theoretically grounded” claim in the paper is narrower: the authors argue that the **unit of weighting** should match the **unit of reward**, so if the reward is sequence-level then the importance ratio should also be sequence-level rather than token-level. They are not claiming an exact unbiased estimator of expected reward under $\pi_\theta$.

The length normalization makes this even clearer. If you write

$$
s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right),
$$

this is the geometric mean of token-level likelihood ratios, not their product. The geometric mean is useful as a smooth, length-normalized signal of how much the whole sequence has moved, but it is not the Radon-Nikodym derivative that would exactly convert expectations between policies.

So the right way to place GSPO on your spectrum is: **a principled surrogate heuristic**. It is more structured than an ad hoc trick, because it is designed around sequence-level reward and sequence-level likelihood, and the paper provides a gradient interpretation showing how it weights the log-likelihood terms. But it is still a surrogate objective, not a canonical off-policy estimator of the true expected reward. The paper’s empirical claim is that this surrogate behaves better than GRPO, especially for long responses and MoE models, because it avoids the noisy token-level importance ratios that they argue are ill-posed in that setting.

In short: GSPO is not “optimizing the expected reward under the new policy” in an exact importance-sampling sense. It is optimizing a **clipped sequence-level surrogate** that is intended to produce better-behaved gradients and more stable training.

### Why should matching the unit of reward and the unit of optimization matter mathematically?
Question:
A central statement in the paper is that "the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward." In this case, the reward $r(x,y)$ is given to the whole sequence, so the authors argue for sequence-level clipping and sequence-level weighting.

I understand the intuition, but I am not sure I see the formal reason. In policy gradient methods, a sequence-level return is often decomposed into token-level log-probability gradients:

$\nabla_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta}}[r(x,y)] = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta}}\left[r(x,y) \sum_t \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\right].$

That already mixes a sequence-level reward with token-level terms, and it is normally considered valid.

So what exactly fails in GRPO? Is it not the token decomposition itself, but the use of separate token-wise importance ratios and token-wise clipping? I'd appreciate a precise explanation distinguishing the valid decomposition above from the allegedly ill-posed GRPO objective.
Answer:
The key point is that the token-level decomposition in the policy-gradient identity is mathematically valid, but GRPO is not using that decomposition in the same way. Those are two different ideas.

The identity you wrote,
$$
\nabla_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta}}[r(x,y)]
=
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta}}\!\left[r(x,y)\sum_t \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\right],
$$
is just the usual log-derivative trick. It says that if the reward is a sequence-level function $r(x,y)$, then the gradient can be written as a sum of token-level score-function terms. That is perfectly fine, because the reward is still attached to the whole sampled sequence. The tokens only appear through the gradient of the joint log-likelihood,
$$
\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\sum_t \log \pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}).
$$
So this is a decomposition of the gradient of a sequence-level objective; it is not saying that the optimization problem has become token-level.

What the paper argues is problematic in GRPO is something more specific: it applies a separate importance ratio and a separate clipping operation to each token,
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$
while the reward signal is a single sequence-level scalar $\widehat A_i$. In other words, the objective is “sequence reward, token-wise off-policy correction.” That is the mismatch the authors are complaining about.

Why does that matter? Because importance sampling is only valid when the weight matches the variable whose distribution changed. If the random object you sampled is the whole sequence $y$, then the correct density ratio is the sequence ratio
$$
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)},
$$
not a collection of per-token ratios treated as if each token were an independent sample from its own marginal. The sequence ratio factors into token ratios algebraically, but that does not mean you can use each factor separately as an importance weight in the surrogate objective. The expectation you want to correct is over complete sequences, and the one-sample-per-token ratios do not correct the sequence distribution in the importance-sampling sense described in the paper.

This is why the paper says the GRPO weights “fail to perform the intended distribution-correction role.” The issue is not the token decomposition of $\nabla \log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ itself; that decomposition is exact. The issue is the use of token-wise ratios as if they were valid importance weights for a sequence-level reward, together with token-wise clipping, which distorts the surrogate and can inject high-variance noise that accumulates over long responses.

By contrast, GSPO keeps the reward and the importance ratio at the same granularity: whole response in, whole response out. Its weight
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
$$
is sequence-level, and the gradient then distributes that sequence-level weight across the token log-probability gradients. That is consistent with the paper’s principle: the reward is sequence-level, so the off-policy correction and clipping should also be sequence-level.

So the short answer is: the valid identity is the score-function decomposition of a sequence-level objective; what fails in GRPO is not that decomposition, but the attempt to use token-wise importance ratios and token-wise clipping as the importance-sampling correction for a sequence-level reward.

### Why does GSPO-token introduce a detach-based factor that is numerically equal to 1?
Question:
In the GSPO-token variant, the paper defines

$ s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})]}.$

Since

$ \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})]} $

has numerical value 1, this seems very strange at first glance.

Am I right that this is a "gradient trick" whose only purpose is to make the forward value equal to $s_i(\theta)$ while making the backward pass produce token-level score-function gradients weighted by the sequence scalar $s_i(\theta)$?

If so, could someone explain carefully how this detach construction leads to Equation (11)? I want to make sure I understand why the objective is numerically unchanged but the autograd path is different.
Answer:
Yes — this is exactly a gradient-trick, and the point is to make the *forward* value of the token-level ratio equal to the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$, while making the *backward* pass look like a token-level score-function gradient.

The key identity is that
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]}
$$
is numerically $1$, because the denominator is just a detached copy of the same scalar. So in the forward pass,
$$
s_{i,t}(\theta)=\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\cdot 1 = s_i(\theta)
$$
numerically. That is why the paper says GSPO-token is “numerically identical” to GSPO when all token advantages are the same.

But the gradient is not identical, because the detached terms are treated as constants by autograd. If you differentiate
$$
s_{i,t}(\theta)=\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]},
$$
then the only factor that contributes gradient is the numerator $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$. The detached denominator blocks the extra derivative you would normally get from a plain ratio. Since $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ is also constant for backprop, the derivative is
$$
\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta)
=
\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\cdot \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]}
=
s_i(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
$$
because the detached copies evaluate to the same numeric values as the original quantities.

That is the whole trick behind Equation (11). Starting from the objective with $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$,
$$
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t s_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t}\right],
$$
and differentiating through $s_{i,t}$ gives
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i s_i(\theta)\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right],
$$
which is exactly the paper’s Equation (11). So the detach construction does not change the objective value; it only changes which computational path autograd uses, turning the scalar sequence weight into a constant multiplier on each token’s policy-gradient term.

Intuitively, this is similar to the common “stop-gradient on the baseline” idea in policy gradients: you often want a quantity to affect the loss as a weight, but you do not want gradients to flow through the thing producing the weight itself. Here, the authors use that idea to reuse the sequence-level importance ratio as a frozen multiplier while still applying token-wise advantages.

### If GSPO and GSPO-token are numerically identical when $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$, why define both?
Question:
The paper states that GSPO-token and GSPO are numerically identical in the objective, clipping condition, and theoretical gradient when all tokens in a response share the same advantage, i.e. $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$.

If that is true, then why introduce GSPO-token at all instead of just saying "GSPO can also accept token-wise advantages"?

Is the practical reason simply implementation convenience in autograd frameworks, especially for multi-turn RL or partial-credit settings? Or is there some subtle distinction in how clipping or gradient flow behaves once token-wise advantages vary?

I'm asking because the construction currently feels redundant, and I suspect I'm missing the intended use case.
Answer:
They are not redundant for the reason the paper gives itself: GSPO is the main, sequence-level algorithm, while GSPO-token is only a variant for cases where you want token-dependent credit assignment.

The key point is that the equality the paper mentions is conditional. If every token in a response shares the same advantage, $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$, then GSPO-token collapses to GSPO numerically in the objective, the clipping condition, and the gradient. In that regime, GSPO-token buys you nothing except a different way of writing the same update. The paper is explicit about this: the token-level ratio $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is constructed so that its numerical value equals $s_i(\theta)$, because the extra factor uses $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ to stop gradients. So there is no hidden difference in gradient flow there; it is just a notational/device-level trick to let token-wise advantages be plugged in while keeping the same sequence-level ratio.

So why define it? Because the authors want a single framework that covers both the clean sequence-level case and more fine-grained settings. Their stated example is multi-turn RL, where different tokens may reasonably deserve different advantages, and more broadly any partial-credit or token-scoring setup. In those cases, GSPO-token is genuinely different from GSPO: the shared sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$ still scales the whole response, but each token’s policy-gradient term is now weighted by its own $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. That is the only real distinction. The clipping mechanism does not become subtly different; it still clips the same ratio, but the per-token advantage changes which token gradients matter more.

So the short answer is: yes, the practical reason is mostly flexibility, not a new optimization principle. GSPO-token is included because it lets the authors say, “our sequence-level formulation is the default, but if you want token-wise credit assignment, you can still use the same sequence-likelihood ratio machinery.” When $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ is constant across tokens, it is deliberately identical to GSPO; when it varies, it becomes a different objective with finer credit assignment.

### How can clipping more tokens by two orders of magnitude still improve sample efficiency?
Question:
The paper reports a "curious observation" that GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens than GRPO, by about two orders of magnitude, yet still achieves higher training efficiency and better performance.

I find this very counterintuitive. If clipped samples contribute less useful gradient information, then clipping dramatically more of them sounds like it should reduce sample efficiency, not improve it.

Is the right interpretation that GRPO is using many more token updates, but those updates are so noisy or misweighted that they hurt more than help? If so, is there any way to quantify this in terms of gradient variance, bias, or effective sample size?

I would especially appreciate an explanation of how "fewer active tokens" can still mean "more learning per rollout" in this setting.
Answer:
Yes — the paper’s intended interpretation is essentially that \emph{GRPO is not getting more useful learning signal from those extra token updates}. The key point is that GSPO and GRPO are not clipping “the same thing” at different rates: they are clipping at different levels of the policy, and the paper argues that the GRPO token-level importance ratios are fundamentally the wrong object for off-policy correction in this setting.

In GRPO, each token is weighted by a token-level ratio
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$
The paper’s claim is that this is a bad importance weight because it is trying to correct a \emph{sequence-level} learning problem using a \emph{single token sample} from each next-token distribution. That does not really estimate the distribution mismatch in the importance-sampling sense. Instead, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient, and the paper says this gets worse for long responses and can even lead to collapse.

GSPO fixes this by moving the ratio to the whole response:
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}.
$$
Now the clipping decision is based on whether the \emph{entire sampled response} is too far off-policy, which the paper argues is aligned with the fact that the reward is also assigned to the entire response. So when GSPO clips more “tokens” in the figure, what it is really doing is discarding more token positions because their \emph{parent response} is judged unreliable. That is not wasted learning; it is removing low-quality or misweighted gradient contributions that would otherwise inject variance.

This is why “fewer active tokens” can still mean “more learning per rollout.” In a noisy estimator, more samples are only helpful if they are approximately unbiased and not dominated by bad weights. If the weights themselves are mis-specified, then adding more token-level terms can make the gradient estimate look denser while actually making it less useful. The paper explicitly frames GSPO’s higher clipping fraction as evidence that GRPO’s token-level gradient estimates are “inherently noisy and inefficient for sample exploitation,” while GSPO provides a “more reliable and effective learning signal.”

So the answer to your variance question is: qualitatively, yes, the paper is saying GRPO has higher effective gradient noise because its token-wise ratios are invalid for the off-policy correction it is trying to do. But the paper does \emph{not} provide a formal variance decomposition, bias estimate, or effective-sample-size calculation. It makes the argument empirically and mechanistically: token-level clipping in GRPO is unstable and can accumulate harmful noise, whereas sequence-level clipping in GSPO preserves the signal that matters.

A good intuition is to think of GSPO as filtering at the level of a whole “trajectory”: if the rollout is too far from the behavior policy, you downweight the whole response. That can look like “throwing away more tokens,” but those are precisely the tokens whose gradient contributions are least trustworthy. In that sense, GSPO gets more learning per rollout not by using more updates, but by using \emph{better-targeted} updates.

### Why is GSPO supposedly less sensitive to MoE routing changes if sequence likelihood is still a product of token likelihoods?
Question:
In the MoE discussion, the paper says GSPO is not sensitive to individual token likelihood fluctuations and only cares about the sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)$, which supposedly does not fluctuate drastically even when expert routing changes.

But sequence likelihood is just

$\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x) = \prod_t \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}),$

so if routing changes can strongly perturb token-level probabilities, why doesn't that also strongly perturb the sequence likelihood ratio?

Is the answer mainly the length normalization,

$\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|},$

which averages the log-ratio over tokens? Or is there a deeper reason why MoE routing volatility harms per-token weighting much more than response-level weighting?

I'm looking for a more mechanistic explanation here, not just the empirical claim that GSPO works better.
Answer:
Yes, the length normalization is part of the story, but the paper is really making a broader point about *where the noise enters*.

A sequence likelihood is indeed a product of token likelihoods,
$$
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)=\prod_t \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t}),
$$
so in raw multiplicative form it can change a lot if a few token probabilities change. But GSPO does not use the raw product directly. It uses the normalized sequence ratio
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right),
$$
so what matters is the *average log-ratio per token*, not the raw product. That is already much less volatile than tokenwise ratios, because the sequence score aggregates many per-token perturbations into one scalar. A few routed tokens can be noisy, but unless the change is systematic across the whole response, their effect is diluted by the average.

The deeper reason, though, is that token-level GRPO is weighting each token by its own ratio
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$
and the paper argues this is exactly where MoE routing volatility hurts. In MoE models, after an update, the activated experts for the same token can change, so these per-token ratios can jump around sharply from one token to the next. That makes the gradient noisy because each token is being reweighted by a quantity that is already unstable and, crucially, is being used at the wrong granularity. The paper’s point in the “motivation” section is that importance sampling is supposed to correct a distribution mismatch at the level of the sample being optimized over; since the reward is sequence-level, applying the correction token-by-token is misaligned and high variance.

GSPO fixes both problems at once. First, it uses one importance weight per response, so routing changes affect only a single response-level scalar rather than many token-level scalars. Second, because the gradient becomes
$$
s_i(\theta)\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
$$
all tokens in a response are treated equally, instead of each token getting its own volatile weight. That is why the paper says GSPO is “not sensitive to the individual token likelihood”: the sensitivity is averaged away and no longer enters as tokenwise reweighting noise.

So the short answer is: it is not just length normalization, although that helps. The more important mechanism is that GSPO moves the correction from the token level to the sequence level, which matches the sequence reward and prevents MoE routing fluctuations from being injected independently into every token’s gradient contribution. The paper’s empirical claim about MoE stability is precisely that this response-level view avoids the routing replay machinery needed by GRPO.

### Does the group-normalized advantage become unstable when rewards in the group have very small standard deviation?
Question:
Both GRPO and GSPO use the group-based normalized advantage

$ \widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}. $

Since the reward is bounded in $[0,1]$, I can imagine cases where all sampled responses in a group receive almost the same reward, making the denominator very small.

Does this create instability due to extremely large advantages? In practice do implementations add an epsilon to the standard deviation, or clip advantages, or skip low-variance groups?

The paper focuses on instability from importance ratios, but I wonder whether some of the training noise could also come from this normalization step.
Answer:
Yes, in principle this normalization can be numerically fragile, but the paper itself does not identify it as the source of instability, and it does not specify any extra safeguard such as an $\epsilon$ in the denominator.

The advantage used by both GRPO and GSPO is just a groupwise $z$-score,

$$
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}.
$$

So if all rewards in a group are almost the same, then the numerator is also almost zero for every sample, because each reward is close to the group mean. In that case the normalized advantages do not automatically become huge; they are only large if the reward differences remain nontrivial while the group standard deviation becomes extremely small. The truly problematic case is the degenerate one where all rewards are exactly equal, in which case the denominator is zero and the expression is undefined.

That said, the paper is mainly arguing that the major instability in GRPO comes from the token-level importance ratios, not from the advantage normalization. In fact, the authors keep the same group-normalized advantage in GSPO and still report much better stability, which suggests that whatever noise this normalization introduces is not the dominant failure mode they are studying. Their instability story is about the mismatch between token-level ratios and sequence-level rewards, plus clipping effects, not about the reward standard deviation.

So the careful answer is: mathematically, very small within-group reward variance can make the normalization ill-conditioned, and a practical implementation would usually need some numerical protection; but this paper does not discuss such a fix, and its empirical claim is that the main instability comes from importance weighting, since GSPO retains the same advantage normalization while removing the problematic token-level ratios.

### How should I interpret the very different clipping ranges used in GSPO and GRPO?
Question:
In the experiments, GSPO uses very small clipping ranges like $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$, while GRPO uses much larger ranges like $0.2$ and $0.27$. The paper notes that the ranges differ in order of magnitude because the importance ratios are defined differently.

Could someone help me build intuition for these numbers?

Since GSPO uses

$ s_i(\theta) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_t \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}\right), $

it seems to measure an average per-token multiplicative change rather than a raw token ratio. Does that mean a clipping window of $\pm 10^{-4}$ already corresponds to a meaningful policy shift over long responses?

I'm looking for a concrete interpretation of what these clipping thresholds mean geometrically or statistically.
Answer:
The key is that GSPO is clipping a very different quantity from GRPO.

In GRPO, the ratio is a **token-level** importance weight
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$
so a clipping range like $0.2$ means “for this token, allow the new policy’s probability to be about $20\%$ lower or $27\%$ higher than before.” That is a fairly large local move.

In GSPO, by contrast, the ratio is the **length-normalized sequence likelihood ratio**
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
$$
So GSPO is not asking how much one token moved; it is asking how much the **geometric mean** of the per-token likelihood ratios moved. The clipping window $3\times 10^{-4}$ to $4\times 10^{-4}$ therefore means “keep the average per-token log-likelihood change extremely close to zero.”

For small deviations, $\log(1+\delta)\approx \delta$, so a GSPO clip of $s_i(\theta)\in[1-3\times 10^{-4},\,1+4\times 10^{-4}]$ is roughly
$$
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}
\in [-3\times 10^{-4},\,4\times 10^{-4}].
$$
That is a per-token average shift of only a few $10^{-4}$ nats in log-probability. Geometrically, this is a very narrow trust region around the old policy in the space of **average log-likelihood per token**, not in raw token probabilities.

Your intuition is exactly right that, over a long response, such a tiny per-token change can still correspond to a meaningful shift in the whole sequence. Because GSPO uses the $1/|y_i|$-th power, the sequence ratio itself is
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)} = s_i(\theta)^{|y_i|}.
$$
So if $s_i(\theta)=1.0004$ and $|y_i|=1000$, then the full-sequence ratio is about $(1.0004)^{1000}\approx e^{0.4}\approx 1.49$. That is not tiny at all. The clipping looks microscopic only because GSPO normalizes by length before taking the exponential.

So the clean interpretation is: GRPO clips how much an individual token can change; GSPO clips how much the **average token-level log-likelihood of the whole response** can change. The former is a local, tokenwise trust region; the latter is a sequencewise trust region expressed on a per-token scale. That is why the numerical ranges differ by orders of magnitude, and why the paper explicitly says the ranges are not directly comparable.

### Is the paper's argument against GRPO empirical, theoretical, or both?
Question:
After reading the paper, I'm unsure how strong the claim against GRPO is supposed to be.

On one hand, the authors use strong language like "ill-posed" and "invalidated importance sampling weights." On the other hand, the main evidence presented in the excerpt is empirical: instability, collapse on long sequences, and MoE problems.

Is there a formal theorem or counterexample showing that GRPO's token-level objective is biased in a way that cannot correspond to any valid importance-sampling estimator of a sequence-level reward objective? Or is the claim more like: "in practice this estimator has unacceptable variance/noise and therefore behaves as if ill-posed"?

I want to understand whether the objection is mathematically definitive or mainly an empirical diagnosis backed by intuition.
Answer:
The paper’s case against GRPO is **both theoretical and empirical, but not a formal impossibility theorem**.

On the theoretical side, the authors are making a fairly strong *modeling* argument: GRPO applies an importance ratio at the **token** level,
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$
even though the reward is assigned at the **sequence** level, $r(x,y_i)$. Their claim is that this is a mismatch of the unit of correction and the unit of reward. They explicitly say the token-level ratio “fails to perform the intended distribution-correction role” because it is based on a single sample from each next-token distribution, and they argue that this introduces high-variance noise rather than a meaningful importance-sampling correction. So the theoretical objection is: the tokenwise weights do not have the same clean interpretation as sequencewise importance sampling weights,
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}.
$$

However, the paper does **not** give a theorem of the form “GRPO is mathematically invalid” or “no unbiased estimator of the sequence objective can be written in token form.” In fact, the gradient derivations they show are mostly used to argue about **how the gradients are weighted**, not to prove an impossibility result. The paper’s language like “ill-posed” and “invalidated importance sampling weights” should be read as a conceptual critique: the estimator is not aligned with the underlying sequence-level RL objective in a principled way, and this misalignment is claimed to become unstable in practice.

That is why the empirical evidence matters so much in the paper. The authors support the theoretical story with observations of instability, collapse on long responses, and particular trouble for MoE routing, where token-level likelihoods fluctuate more sharply. Their strongest evidence is therefore **diagnostic and experimental**: GRPO behaves badly in the regimes they care about, while GSPO, which uses sequence-level importance ratios, behaves stably.

So the short answer is: the paper’s objection is **partly theoretical in motivation, but mainly empirical in support**. It argues that GRPO’s token-level weighting is conceptually misaligned with sequence-level rewards and likely high-variance, but it does **not** provide a formal counterexample proving that GRPO cannot correspond to any valid objective. The claim is closer to “this estimator is a poor and unstable surrogate for the intended sequence-level optimization” than to a strict mathematical impossibility.
