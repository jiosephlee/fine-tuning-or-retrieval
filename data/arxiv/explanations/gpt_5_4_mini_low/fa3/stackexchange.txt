\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### Why is attention still a memory bottleneck if the main cost looks like the two matrix multiplications?
Question:
I'm trying to understand the motivation of the paper. In standard attention, the expensive-looking operations are $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, so I would have expected the bottleneck to be pure compute. But the paper says FlashAttention speeds things up mainly by reducing reads/writes of the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM.

What exactly makes the standard implementation memory-bound rather than compute-bound here? Is the point that materializing $\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ and $\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ dominates bandwidth usage even though the arithmetic count is also large? A simple explanation in terms of GPU memory hierarchy would help.
Answer:
Yes — the key point is that in the standard GPU implementation, attention is not bottlenecked just because it does a lot of arithmetic; it is bottlenecked because it does a lot of *memory traffic to HBM* for data that are used only briefly.

The paper defines “standard attention” as the implementation that materializes the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM. That is the crucial distinction. Even though computing $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$ is expensive in FLOPs, the intermediate $N \times N$ matrices are enormous, and writing them out and reading them back costs a lot of bandwidth. On modern GPUs, HBM bandwidth is much more limited than on-chip compute throughput, so the kernel ends up waiting on memory movement rather than on arithmetic.

Intuitively, the GPU has a hierarchy: registers are fastest, then shared memory, then L2, and HBM is the slow off-chip memory. If you compute $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and then immediately store $\mathbf{S}$ to HBM, and later load it again for softmax, and then store and load $\mathbf{P}$ for $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, you are paying for large round-trips through the slowest level of the hierarchy. The arithmetic inside the matmuls may be dense, but the *reuse* of the intermediate matrices is poor: each element of $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ is used only a small number of times before being discarded. That gives low arithmetic intensity, which is exactly the regime where kernels become memory-bound.

This is why FlashAttention changes the algorithm rather than just the implementation. The paper’s main idea is to avoid ever materializing $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ in HBM. Instead, it computes attention in tiles, keeps partial results on-chip, and performs a local softmax update so that the intermediate scores are never written out as full $N \times N$ matrices. In the paper’s own wording, FlashAttention “eliminates intermediate reads/writes to slow global memory through fusing all of the attention operations into a single GPU kernel.” That is what turns the problem from a bandwidth problem into a much more efficient on-chip computation.

So the short answer is: yes, materializing $\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ and $\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ dominates HBM traffic. The expensive-looking matrix multiplications are not the whole story; on GPUs, if those operations are surrounded by large writes and reads of intermediate matrices, the overall runtime is often dominated by memory movement rather than FLOPs. FlashAttention improves performance mainly by cutting that memory traffic. FlashAttention-3 then pushes further by overlapping the remaining compute and data movement and by using lower-precision hardware more effectively.

### How does the 'local softmax' in FlashAttention give the same result as full softmax over the whole row?
Question:
I understand ordinary attention as computing one full row of scores, then applying
$\mathrm{softmax}$ to that full row. But FlashAttention processes keys/values in blocks, and the paper keeps running values $m_i$ and $\ell_i$ while updating $\mathbf{O}_i$ incrementally.

I am confused about why this blockwise procedure is still exactly equivalent to
$$\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top).$$

In particular, why do the updates
$$m_i = \max(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}, \mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}))$$
and
$$\ell_i = \exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i)\ell_i + \mathrm{rowsum}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)})$$
correctly maintain the normalization across blocks? An intuitive derivation would be very helpful.
Answer:
The key point is that FlashAttention is not approximating softmax block by block; it is computing the same row-wise softmax as if the whole row were materialized, but it does so in a numerically stable streaming form.

For a single query row, suppose the full score vector is split into blocks
$$s = [s^{(1)}, s^{(2)}, \dots, s^{(T_c)}].$$
If we define the usual softmax over the whole row, then for any entry $x$ in that row,
$$\mathrm{softmax}(s)_x = \frac{e^{x}}{\sum_t \sum_{y \in s^{(t)}} e^y}.$$
The only difficulty is that FlashAttention sees the blocks one at a time, so it cannot form the denominator all at once. The trick is to maintain two running quantities: the running maximum $m$ and the running sum of exponentials relative to that maximum, $\ell$.

After processing the first $j$ blocks, let
$$m^{(j)} = \max\{ \text{all scores seen so far} \}, \qquad \ell^{(j)} = \sum_{\text{seen }x} e^{x - m^{(j)}}.$$
These are exactly the quantities the paper stores in $m_i$ and $\ell_i$ row-wise. This is enough, because the true softmax denominator over the processed prefix is then
$$\sum_{\text{seen }x} e^x = e^{m^{(j)}} \ell^{(j)}.$$
So the pair $(m,\ell)$ is just a numerically stable reparameterization of the exact prefix sum.

Now suppose a new block $s^{(j)}$ arrives. If its row maximum is larger than the previous running max, the normalization must be changed to the new larger max. That is why the update is
$$m^{(j)} = \max\!\bigl(m^{(j-1)}, \mathrm{rowmax}(s^{(j)})\bigr).$$
If the max changes from $m^{(j-1)}$ to $m^{(j)}$, then every old exponential term must be rescaled from the old reference point to the new one:
$$e^{x - m^{(j)}} = e^{x - m^{(j-1)}} \, e^{m^{(j-1)} - m^{(j)}}.$$
Summing over all old entries gives
$$\sum_{\text{old }x} e^{x - m^{(j)}} = e^{m^{(j-1)} - m^{(j)}} \ell^{(j-1)}.$$
Then you add the contribution of the new block, already expressed relative to the new max:
$$\ell^{(j)} = e^{m^{(j-1)} - m^{(j)}} \ell^{(j-1)} + \sum_{x \in s^{(j)}} e^{x - m^{(j)}}.$$
This is exactly the paper’s update
$$\ell_i = \exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i)\ell_i + \mathrm{rowsum}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}).$$

So the recurrence is just the standard identity for combining two softmax-normalized chunks under a shared, updated maximum. Nothing is being approximated; the normalization is being rewritten in a way that is stable and updateable.

The same logic explains the output accumulation. The attention output for one row is
$$o = \frac{\sum_x e^x v_x}{\sum_x e^x}.$$
FlashAttention stores the numerator in the same shifted form. Whenever the max changes, the previously accumulated output must also be rescaled by $e^{m^{\mathrm{old}}-m}$ so that it remains expressed relative to the new normalization. After all blocks are processed, dividing by $\ell$ gives exactly the full softmax output.

Intuitively, you can think of it as streaming the row through a “moving reference level.” The reference level is the largest score seen so far. Every time a larger score appears, all previous exponentials are shifted down by the appropriate factor so that their relative weights remain correct. Because the same shift is applied consistently to both the numerator and denominator, the final ratio is identical to the full softmax over the entire row.

This is why the paper calls it “local softmax”: each block computes a softmax relative to the current local maximum, but the running $(m,\ell)$ state ensures that these local computations combine into the exact global softmax. The derivation is the same one used in earlier FlashAttention papers, and FlashAttention-3 keeps it unchanged; the new contribution in this paper is mainly in how that exact computation is overlapped with GEMMs and accelerated on Hopper hardware.

### What is the difference between a warp, a warpgroup, and a CTA in this paper's algorithm?
Question:
The paper discusses warp-specialization, producer warpgroups, consumer warpgroups, and gives a CTA-level view of the algorithm. I know a warp is 32 threads, but I am getting lost in the hierarchy.

Could someone explain, specifically in the context of FlashAttention-3 on Hopper, what roles are played by:

1. a warp,
2. a warpgroup,
3. a CTA,
4. the producer/consumer split?

I think the paper is saying that one set of warps mostly issues TMA loads and another set mostly issues WGMMA and softmax work, but I am not sure how these are grouped in practice.
Answer:
In the paper’s terminology, these are three different levels of GPU parallelism, and the cleanest way to think about them is “how many threads cooperate on one piece of work.”

A **warp** is the basic SIMT execution unit: 32 threads that execute in lockstep. In FlashAttention-3, a warp is not the main algorithmic unit by itself, but it is the smallest group the CUDA programming model exposes directly. The paper uses warps as building blocks for larger cooperative groups and, in the warp-specialized version, assigns warps different roles so that some mostly handle data movement and others mostly handle computation.

A **warpgroup** on Hopper is a group of 4 contiguous warps, so 128 threads total. This matters because Hopper’s `WGMMA` instruction is **warpgroup-wide**: the matmul is issued collectively by the whole warpgroup, not by a single warp. So when the paper says “consumer warpgroups,” it means groups of 4 warps that cooperate to do the attention math, especially the `WGMMA`-based GEMMs and the softmax work that follows them. Likewise, the “producer warpgroup” is another 4-warp group whose job is mainly to feed data into shared memory using TMA loads.

A **CTA** is the CUDA threadblock, i.e. the whole set of threads that are scheduled together on one SM. In the paper’s CTA-level view, one CTA contains the full attention tile computation for a block $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and produces the corresponding output block $\mathbf{O}_i$. The CTA is the unit that owns the shared-memory buffer and the synchronization between producer and consumer roles. So the CTA is the outer container, while warpgroups are the internal subteams.

The **producer/consumer split** is the key performance idea. The producer side is responsible for asynchronous data movement: loading $\mathbf{Q}_i$, then streaming in the $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ tiles from HBM to shared memory via TMA. The consumer side is responsible for the expensive compute: `WGMMA` for $\mathbf{Q}_i\mathbf{K}_j^\top$ and $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}\mathbf{V}_j$, plus the softmax rescaling and bookkeeping. The point is to overlap them so that while one warpgroup is waiting on or issuing memory transfers, another warpgroup is busy on Tensor Cores or softmax arithmetic. That is why the paper talks about warp-specialization and “pingpong” scheduling: the roles are swapped between warpgroups so memory traffic and compute can be hidden behind each other.

So the hierarchy is basically: a CTA contains multiple warpgroups; each warpgroup contains 4 warps; and within a CTA, FlashAttention-3 assigns one warpgroup to producer duties and one or more warpgroups to consumer duties. The important Hopper-specific detail is that `TMA` is the producer’s tool, `WGMMA` is the consumer’s tool, and both are designed to work asynchronously, which is what makes the split profitable.

### Why does warp-specialization help if the GPU already overlaps memory and compute automatically?
Question:
One thing I don't understand is why the paper needs an explicit producer-consumer design at all. GPUs are often described as latency-hiding machines already, so I thought the scheduler would naturally overlap memory operations and math operations whenever possible.

What extra benefit is gained by manually splitting warps into producer and consumer roles? Is it mainly about using Hopper-specific asynchronous units like TMA and WGMMA, or about reducing register pressure with `setmaxnreg`, or something else? I'm looking for the conceptual reason why this is better than a more uniform kernel.
Answer:
The short answer is that the GPU only overlaps work automatically when the work is already independent enough, and in attention it often is not. In \textsc{FlashAttention-3}, the point of warp-specialization is to make the overlap explicit in the algorithm so that Hopper’s asynchronous units can be kept busy despite the dependencies inside attention.

Conceptually, the kernel has two very different kinds of work: data movement and math. Hopper gives these different work types different hardware paths, namely TMA for moving tiles from global memory to shared memory and WGMMA for matrix multiplication. If every warp in a CTA were written as a uniform “do everything” warp, then each warp would have to alternate between loading, computing, waiting, and synchronizing. That is a much harder scheduling problem for the compiler and hardware, because the loads and the math are not independent in the obvious way: the softmax depends on the output of the first GEMM, and the second GEMM depends on softmax. So the GPU cannot simply “discover” a clean overlap unless the kernel is structured to expose one.

Warp-specialization does exactly that by assigning some warps the producer role and others the consumer role. The producers mostly issue TMA loads, while the consumers mostly issue WGMMA and softmax work. This helps in two ways. First, it lets the compiler and hardware overlap data movement with computation much more effectively, because while one warpgroup is waiting on or issuing asynchronous memory traffic, another can be actively using the Tensor Cores. Second, Hopper allows dynamic register reallocation with \verb|setmaxnreg|, so the compute warps can be given more registers and the producer warps can be kept lightweight. That matters because the consumer side of attention is register-hungry, while the producer side, as the paper notes, may only need a single thread for some of its work. So warp-specialization is not just about “being asynchronous”; it is also about making register use match the actual roles of the warps.

So the extra benefit is not merely that Hopper has TMA and WGMMA, but that the algorithm is redesigned to exploit them at the warpgroup level. In other words, the hardware can overlap memory and compute, but warp-specialization gives it a kernel structure where that overlap is actually possible and efficient. Without that structure, you leave performance on the table because the warps are entangled in mixed roles, more synchronization is needed, and the register file is used less efficiently.

### What does it mean that WGMMA is asynchronous, and what are the 'commit and wait' steps actually waiting for?
Question:
In the pseudocode, the GEMM operations are issued using WGMMA, followed by language like 'commit and wait' or 'commit but do not wait'. I think the paper is saying the instruction gets launched to the Tensor Cores and the warpgroup can continue doing other instructions before the result is needed.

But I am not sure what the dependency model is here. If the GEMM output is needed for softmax, then what state is available before the wait? Is 'commit' just enqueueing the operation, and 'wait' synchronizing on accumulator readiness? A more concrete explanation of what asynchronous WGMMA means at the programming level would help me read Algorithms 1 and 2.
Answer:
In the paper’s terminology, “asynchronous” means that a WGMMA instruction does not behave like an ordinary CUDA arithmetic instruction that must finish before the warpgroup can move on. Instead, issuing WGMMA launches the matrix multiply onto Hopper’s Tensor Cores, and the warpgroup can keep executing other instructions while that multiplication is still in flight. The important point is that the instruction is *logically* part of the program, but its execution is *decoupled* from the issuing warpgroup’s instruction stream.

The “commit and wait” language is about this decoupling. A `commit` means “the data or result associated with this stage is now ready to be advertised to the next consumer stage,” but it does not necessarily mean that all dependent computation has completed. A `wait` means “block until the specific asynchronous operation I need has finished and its output is safe to use.” In other words, `commit` advances the pipeline bookkeeping, while `wait` enforces the true data dependency.

In the attention pseudocode, the dependency is exactly what you suspected: the softmax needs the score tile $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, and the second GEMM needs the softmax probabilities $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$. So if you issue WGMMA for $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$, the result is not immediately usable. Before the `wait`, what is available is only the fact that the operation has been launched and is progressing asynchronously; the numerical values of $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ are not yet ready for the rowwise max, exp, or row sum. Likewise, for the second WGMMA, the output accumulator for $\mathbf{O}_i$ is not ready to be consumed until the wait.

The key trick in Algorithm 2 is that the paper intentionally overlaps independent work with those in-flight WGMMA operations. While the Tensor Cores are computing $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$, the warpgroup can do softmax work on the previous tile, or compute the other GEMM, as long as it does not touch the unfinished result. So “asynchronous WGMMA” does not mean the output is partially correct or streamable; it means the kernel can have multiple operations in flight, and the programmer decides when to synchronize on the specific one that is needed.

So, concretely, the sequence

\[
\text{issue WGMMA} \;\rightarrow\; \text{commit but do not wait}
\]

means “enqueue the GEMM on Tensor Cores and let it run while I do other work.” The later

\[
\text{wait for the WGMMA}
\]

means “stall until that GEMM’s accumulator tile is finished and can be read safely.” This is exactly why the paper describes WGMMA and TMA as enabling software pipelining: the computation is split into stages, and only the stage-to-stage dependencies are synchronized, not every instruction.

A useful way to read Algorithms 1 and 2 is that `commit` is about advancing the pipeline state, while `wait` is about respecting the real mathematical dependency graph. The algorithm is still exact attention; it is just carefully scheduled so that unrelated work fills the otherwise idle time between “launch a GEMM” and “use its result.”

### How can softmax be overlapped with GEMM if softmax depends on the GEMM output?
Question:
The main technical claim I am struggling with is the 2-stage pipeline. The paper says it overlaps non-GEMM softmax work with asynchronous GEMMs, even though softmax depends on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ and the second GEMM depends on $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}$.

I can follow the pseudocode line by line, but I do not have an intuitive mental model of why this is legal. Is the idea simply to pipeline across iterations so that during iteration $j$ you are:

- computing $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ for block $j$,
- computing $\mathbf{O}_i += \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ for block $j-1$,
- and doing softmax for the score block that has just become available?

If so, could someone restate the dependency graph carefully? Right now I keep thinking there is still a sequential chain that would prevent overlap.
Answer:
Yes — the key is that the overlap is *across iterations*, not within a single iteration’s true data dependence chain. The paper is explicit that “within one warpgroup, we can overlap some instructions in the softmax with some instructions in the GEMMs,” but only after “pipelining across iterations through additional buffers in registers.” In other words, they do **not** claim that softmax magically runs before the score block exists; they restructure the loop so that, while the current block is being softmaxed, the Tensor Cores are already computing the *next* score block and/or the previous value-product block.

The dependency graph is easiest to see if you separate the two GEMMs and the softmax state by iteration. For block $j$, the mathematical flow is still

$$
\mathbf{S}^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top,\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{(j)} = \mathrm{softmax\_local}(\mathbf{S}^{(j)}, m,\ell),\qquad
\mathbf{O} \mathrel{+}= \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j.
$$

So within a single block, yes, $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{(j)}$ depends on $\mathbf{S}^{(j)}$, and the output update depends on $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{(j)}$. That sequential dependence is real and cannot be removed. What FlashAttention-3 changes is the *schedule* of these operations across blocks. Because the first GEMM for block $j+1$ only needs $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and $\mathbf{K}_{j+1}$, it does **not** depend on the softmax of block $j$. Meanwhile, the second GEMM for block $j$ only needs the already-computed $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{(j)}$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$. These are independent enough that, once $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{(j)}$ has been formed, the Tensor Cores can start the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{(j)}\mathbf{V}_j$ update while the CUDA cores are doing the softmax work for block $j+1$.

That is exactly what the pseudocode is arranging. In the main loop, line $\ref{code:first_wgmma}$ launches the next score GEMM, line $\ref{code:second_wgmma}$ launches the previous value GEMM, and line $\ref{code:softmax}$ computes the softmax for the newly available score block. Because WGMMA is asynchronous, “commit but do not wait” means the Tensor Core work can be in flight while the regular FP/exp instructions run. The crucial point is that the two WGMMA calls and the softmax are not three independent computations of the *same* block; they belong to *different* blocks in a staggered pipeline.

Your mental model is therefore basically right, but with one small correction: in iteration $j$, the kernel is not simultaneously computing $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ and the softmax for the same block. Rather, it is computing $\mathbf{S}_{j+1}$, using $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_j$ to update $\mathbf{O}$, and then softmaxing $\mathbf{S}_{j+1}$. The “next” score block becomes the “current” one in the following step, which is why the code copies $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ to $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ at the end of the loop. The extra register buffer is what breaks the apparent serialization: one buffer holds the block whose softmax is currently being computed, while another buffer stores the next score block being produced by WGMMA.

So there is still a sequential chain, but it is only local to each block. Across blocks, the chain is rearranged into a pipeline:

$$
\text{GEMM}(j+1)\ \parallel\ \text{softmax}(j)\ \parallel\ \text{GEMM}(j)
$$

subject to the fact that each block’s softmax must still wait for its own score matrix, and each block’s value update must still wait for its own softmax probabilities. The overlap works because those two dependencies are on *different* blocks once the loop is shifted by one iteration. That is the legal trick FlashAttention-3 is exploiting.

### Why does the paper say exponential can take about 50% of the cycles when there are far fewer exponentials than matmul FLOPs?
Question:
In the pingpong scheduling discussion, the paper says that for FP16 attention with head dimension 128 there are $512\times$ more matmul FLOPs than exponential operations, but exponential has $256\times$ lower throughput, so exponential can take about 50% of the cycles relative to matmul.

I think I understand the arithmetic, but I do not understand what exactly is being compared. Are they comparing idealized lower bounds on execution time from hardware throughput numbers? Also, why are exponentials singled out rather than the whole softmax path, which also includes rowmax, rowsum, subtraction, and rescaling? I want to make sure I am not misreading this performance model.
Answer:
They are comparing a very simple hardware-level lower bound, not an exact end-to-end timing model.

The key idea in that paragraph is: if a kernel were perfectly balanced, the time contribution of each kind of operation would be proportional to

$$
\text{time} \approx \frac{\text{number of operations}}{\text{throughput of the hardware unit that executes them}}.
$$

So when the paper says there are $512\times$ more matmul FLOPs than exponential operations, but exponentials have $256\times$ lower throughput, it is doing the back-of-the-envelope comparison

$$
\frac{T_{\exp}}{T_{\text{matmul}}}
\approx
\frac{1}{512}\cdot 256
=
\frac{1}{2}.
$$

That is, although exponentials are far fewer in count, each exponential is much “more expensive” in throughput terms than a multiply-add on the Tensor Cores, so the total *time* can still be a large fraction of the matmul time. The paper is not claiming that the softmax path literally takes exactly half the wall-clock time in all cases; it is using this estimate to motivate why overlapping softmax with GEMM is worthwhile.

As for why exponentials are singled out: in that paragraph they are using exponential as the clearest representative of the non-matmul part of softmax, because the special-function throughput figure they quote is specifically for “special functions such as exponential” on the Hopper GPU. The rest of the softmax work — rowwise max, subtraction, rescaling, and rowwise sum — is mentioned elsewhere in the algorithm, but the paper’s performance argument there is about the part of softmax that runs on the comparatively slow special-function / general-purpose pipeline rather than on Tensor Cores. In other words, exponential is not being treated as the only non-matmul cost, but as the dominant and easiest-to-quantify one for a hardware-utilization estimate.

So your reading is basically right: it is a throughput-based, idealized cycle comparison meant to explain why softmax is not “free” just because it has fewer FLOPs than GEMM. The point of the pingpong schedule is to hide that slower softmax work under the much higher-throughput matmul work.

### Why does FP8 WGMMA only supporting k-major operands create a special problem for attention?
Question:
The FP8 section says that for FP8 WGMMA, only k-major input operands in shared memory are supported, unlike FP16 where both mn-major and k-major are allowed. I understand this as a layout restriction, but I am not seeing why attention is especially affected.

Could someone explain concretely for the two GEMMs in attention:

1. $\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$,
2. $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$,

which operand layouts are naturally produced by the previous steps, and which of those violate the FP8 WGMMA requirements? I think the issue is especially with $\mathbf{V}$ and with reusing the first GEMM's accumulator as the second GEMM's operand, but I would like to see this spelled out more explicitly.
Answer:
The special difficulty is not that attention is mathematically different from any other pair of GEMMs; it is that the two GEMMs in attention are *back-to-back* and the second one wants to consume the first one’s result immediately. In FP16 this is fairly painless because Hopper’s WGMMA accepts both mn-major and k-major shared-memory layouts, so you can usually arrange each operand in whatever layout is convenient. In FP8, however, the paper says that WGMMA only accepts k-major shared-memory operands, so the “natural” layouts produced by attention have to be massaged much more aggressively.

For the first GEMM, $\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, the paper says that $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ are “typically given as contiguous in the head dimension.” Since each tile is shaped like $B_r \times d$ for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and $B_c \times d$ for $\mathbf{K}_j$, being contiguous in the head dimension means they are already contiguous in the inner dimension $d$, i.e. k-major. So the first GEMM is not the problematic one: its inputs are naturally compatible with FP8 WGMMA’s restriction, at least in the layout sense the paper is discussing.

The second GEMM, $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$, is where the trouble starts. Here the right operand is a tile of $\mathbf{V}$, and those tiles are also naturally stored contiguous in the head dimension, because that is how the model tensors come in from upstream. But for this GEMM, the paper needs the $\mathbf{V}$ tile in shared memory to be contiguous in the *sequence-length* dimension so that it is k-major in the GEMM’s operand sense. That is the opposite of the usual layout, and the paper explicitly says that TMA cannot change the contiguous dimension on load. So $\mathbf{V}$ violates the FP8 WGMMA requirement unless you transpose it, either in GMEM beforehand or in-kernel after the load. This is why the paper singles out $\mathbf{V}$ as the first layout mismatch.

There is a second, subtler issue that is specific to attention and not just to $\mathbf{V}$ itself: the output of the first GEMM is an accumulator tile in FP32, and after softmax you want to reuse that data as the left operand of the second GEMM. In FP8, the paper points out that the FP32 accumulator layout produced by the first WGMMA is not the same as the register layout expected for operand $A$ of the second WGMMA. So even if you fix $\mathbf{V}$, you still cannot directly feed the first GEMM’s accumulator into the second GEMM. That is why the paper introduces a byte-permutation of the accumulator registers, and then arranges the in-kernel transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ to produce a matching row permutation. In other words, the two operands have to be made layout-compatible with each other, not just individually compatible with the hardware.

So the concrete picture is: $\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ is naturally fine because $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ already come in head-dimension-contiguous form, which matches k-major. The second GEMM is the hard one because $\mathbf{V}$ arrives in the “wrong” contiguous dimension for FP8 WGMMA, and because the intermediate $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ lives in an accumulator layout that does not match the next GEMM’s operand layout. That combination is exactly why attention, with its fused GEMM-softmax-GEMM structure, creates a special problem for FP8 WGMMA.

### Why is an in-kernel transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ preferable to transposing $\mathbf{V}$ once in global memory?
Question:
The paper considers two options for making $\mathbf{V}$ satisfy FP8 layout constraints: either transpose $\mathbf{V}$ in global memory as a preprocessing step, or load tiles normally and transpose them inside the kernel using shared-memory/register instructions like LDSM/STSM.

My first thought was that a one-time transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ should be simpler and maybe faster, since then the main kernel sees the right layout immediately. But the paper argues against that, especially for inference.

Why is the separate transpose kernel considered too wasteful? Is it because attention is memory-bound, so writing and rereading a transposed copy of $\mathbf{V}$ costs too much bandwidth compared with doing the tile-local transpose on chip? If possible, I'd appreciate a rough bandwidth-based explanation.
Answer:
Yes — the paper’s objection is fundamentally bandwidth, not arithmetic. In the FP8 forward pass, the value matrix $\mathbf{V}$ is already being streamed through the kernel once to compute $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. If you first run a standalone transpose kernel in global memory, you pay an extra full read and write of $\mathbf{V}$ in HBM, and then the attention kernel reads the transposed copy again. So for that operand alone you turn one global-memory pass into roughly three traffic events: read original, write transposed, read transposed. In a kernel that is already memory-sensitive, that is expensive.

The paper explicitly says this in the FP8 section: option (1b), a standalone preprocessing transpose kernel, is “too wasteful in a memory-bound situation such as inference.” The key point is that attention is not limited just by FLOPs; it is heavily limited by how fast you can move data between HBM and the chip. A transpose kernel does almost no useful computation, so its runtime is dominated by bandwidth. Even if the transpose kernel itself is optimized and “achieve[s] speed near the bandwidth of the device,” that still means it consumes a large fraction of the same scarce HBM bandwidth that the attention kernel needs.

A rough bandwidth argument is enough to see why. Suppose $\mathbf{V}$ has size $N \times d$ in FP8, so it occupies $Nd$ bytes. A separate transpose kernel must read those $Nd$ bytes and write another $Nd$ bytes, and then the attention kernel reads the transposed tensor again for another $Nd$ bytes. That is about $3Nd$ bytes of HBM traffic just to make $\mathbf{V}$ layout-compatible, before counting any other attention data movement. By contrast, doing the transpose inside the attention kernel with LDSM/STSM keeps the permutation on chip: the tile is loaded once from HBM into shared memory, transposed using shared-memory/register instructions, and then consumed by the FP8 WGMMA. So the transpose cost is paid with on-chip bandwidth, which is much higher than HBM bandwidth and, crucially, does not add an extra global-memory pass.

That is why the paper prefers the in-kernel approach. It is not that a global transpose is mathematically wrong or even hard to implement; it is that it duplicates the most expensive part of the work — moving bytes to and from HBM — in a workload whose performance is already dominated by memory movement. The in-kernel transpose is preferable because it preserves FlashAttention’s main design principle: keep intermediate transformations local and avoid extra global-memory traffic.

### What exactly is being permuted when the FP8 accumulator layout is transformed for the second WGMMA?
Question:
I got very confused in the paragraph about converting the first FP8 WGMMA's FP32 accumulator layout into a form suitable for the second WGMMA. The paper says the register order is changed to
$$\{\verb|d0 d1 d4 d5 d2 d3 d6 d7|\}$$
and that, logically, this permutes columns of the $\mathbf{P}$ tile, so the corresponding rows of the $\mathbf{V}$ tile must also be permuted.

I do not understand whether this is:

- a purely low-level register-layout fix that leaves the mathematical matrix unchanged,
- or an actual permutation of the tile indices that must be compensated mathematically,
- or both.

Could someone explain this at the matrix/tile level rather than at the byte/register level? I think I am missing how the same data can be 'reinterpreted' differently by the next WGMMA.
Answer:
It is best to think of this as a permutation of the *logical tile indexing induced by the register layout*, not as a mathematically new attention algorithm.

In the paper, the FP8 first WGMMA produces an FP32 accumulator tile \(\mathbf{P}\) in one register layout, but the second WGMMA expects its left operand to be laid out in a different register order. The authors therefore apply a byte-permute so that the registers holding the accumulator are rearranged from something like \(\{d0,d1,d2,d3,d4,d5,d6,d7\}\) to \(\{d0,d1,d4,d5,d2,d3,d6,d7\}\). That is a *physical* register-layout transformation, but it has a *logical* effect: if you now reinterpret those registers as a matrix tile, the columns of the tile have been permuted.

So the right way to read the paper is:

$$
\text{same numerical values, different tile interpretation.}
$$

More concretely, the data in registers is not “changed” in value, but the mapping from register positions to \((\text{row},\text{column})\) coordinates changes. Under the accumulator layout of the first WGMMA, a certain register ordering corresponds to one column order in \(\mathbf{P}\); after the permutation, the next WGMMA reads the same bytes as a tile with permuted columns. Since matrix multiplication depends on that ordering, this would change the computed result unless the other operand is adjusted too.

That is why the paper says the corresponding rows of \(\mathbf{V}\) must be permuted as well. This is exactly the compensation you would expect from the identity

$$
(\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{\Pi})(\mathbf{\Pi}^{-1}\mathbf{V}) = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V},
$$

where \(\mathbf{\Pi}\) is the permutation induced by the register remapping. In words: if you permute the columns of \(\mathbf{P}\), you must permute the rows of \(\mathbf{V}\) in the inverse way to keep the product unchanged.

So the answer is “both,” but at different levels. At the hardware level it is a low-level register-layout fix. At the mathematical tile level it is a permutation of the logical column/row indices that must be matched between the two operands. The reason this can be done safely is that the permutation is not meant to alter the attention computation; it is only there to make the accumulator layout produced by the first FP8 WGMMA compatible with the operand layout required by the second WGMMA.

Intuitively, the same block of numbers is being “viewed” through two different coordinate systems. The byte permute changes the coordinate system, and the matching transpose/permutation of \(\mathbf{V}\) restores the intended matrix product. Without that second step, you would genuinely compute a different \(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}\).

### Why does block quantization of $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}$ not change the exact attention formula in an inconsistent way across blocks?
Question:
The FP8 algorithm uses one scaling factor per block rather than one per tensor. I can see why this should reduce quantization error, but I am unsure how the scaling is incorporated into the actual attention computation.

Suppose $\mathbf{Q}_i$, $\mathbf{K}_j$, and $\mathbf{V}_j$ are each quantized with different block scales. Then the score block
$$\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$$
will implicitly depend on both the $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and $\mathbf{K}_j$ scales, and the output update also depends on the $\mathbf{V}_j$ scale.

How are these scale factors threaded through the softmax and output accumulation so that the computation still corresponds to a sensible approximation of full-precision attention? I am specifically worried that changing scale per block could distort relative magnitudes across different key blocks.
Answer:
The key point is that the block scales are not “ignored” or applied inconsistently; they are folded into each block’s matmul before that block participates in the online softmax. In other words, FlashAttention-3 is still computing attention on the dequantized values, just without ever materializing the full dequantized tensors in memory.

If a blockwise FP8 quantizer stores, say, a query block and a key block as
$$
\mathbf{Q}_i \approx a_i \,\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_i,\qquad \mathbf{K}_j \approx b_j \,\hat{\mathbf{K}}_j,\qquad \mathbf{V}_j \approx c_j \,\hat{\mathbf{V}}_j,
$$
where $\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_i,\hat{\mathbf{K}}_j,\hat{\mathbf{V}}_j$ are the FP8 values and $a_i,b_j,c_j$ are the per-block scales, then the mathematically correct score block is
$$
\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}=\alpha\,\mathbf{Q}_i\mathbf{K}_j^\top
\approx \alpha\,a_i b_j\,\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_i \hat{\mathbf{K}}_j^\top.
$$
So the block scale factors simply appear as a scalar multiplier on that block’s score matrix. The paper’s statement that “we can scale each block of $\mathbf{S}$ to account for this block quantization at no computation cost” is exactly this idea: once a $(i,j)$ block is formed, it is rescaled by the product of the corresponding $\mathbf{Q}$- and $\mathbf{K}$-block scales before being used in the softmax update.

This does not distort the relative magnitudes across different key blocks, because each block’s scores are brought back to the same physical scale before the running rowwise maximum $m_i$ and normalization factor $\ell_i$ are updated. FlashAttention’s online softmax is designed precisely for this kind of blockwise accumulation: it keeps track of the current row maximum and renormalizes the partial output as new blocks arrive. So as long as each block’s scores are correctly dequantized before contributing to $m_i$ and $\ell_i$, the comparisons across blocks are meaningful and consistent.

The same logic applies to $\mathbf{V}_j$. If a value block was quantized with scale $c_j$, then the contribution to the output is
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j \approx c_j\,\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \hat{\mathbf{V}}_j.
$$
Again, the scale is just folded into that block’s contribution to $\mathbf{O}_i$.

So the short answer is: block quantization does not change the attention formula in any inconsistent way because the algorithm never mixes “raw FP8 blocks” from different scales directly. Each block is dequantized on the fly, its scores are compared and normalized in the usual online-softmax way, and its value contribution is accumulated with the correct scale. The approximation is to full-precision attention only in the usual quantization sense, not in the sense of changing the attention definition.

### Why does multiplying both $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by the same orthogonal matrix preserve attention, but not affect $\mathbf{V}$?
Question:
The paper uses incoherent processing: multiply $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ before quantization, because
$$(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top.$$

I follow that algebraically, but I want to make sure I understand the limits of this trick.

1. Why is it sufficient to transform only $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$, not $\mathbf{V}$?
2. Does this preserve attention exactly only before quantization, with the benefit being that quantization error becomes smaller afterward?
3. Is there any interaction with rotary embeddings or head-wise structure that could break the argument?

An intuitive explanation of how this 'spreads out outliers' would also help.
Answer:
The key point is that attention has two conceptually separate parts: first you compute the weights from $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ via the score matrix $\mathbf{S}=\alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, then you use those weights to mix the values via $\mathbf{O}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. The incoherent processing trick in the paper is only meant to make the score computation numerically friendlier under FP8 quantization, so it only needs to act on $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$, not on $\mathbf{V}$.

Algebraically, multiplying both $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ on the right by the same orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ preserves the score matrix exactly because
$$
(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top
= \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top \mathbf{K}^\top
= \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top.
$$
Since the softmax depends only on these scores, the attention weights $\mathbf{P}$ are unchanged. And once $\mathbf{P}$ is unchanged, the output $\mathbf{O}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$ is unchanged as long as $\mathbf{V}$ itself is left alone. So there is no need to transform $\mathbf{V}$ for correctness. In fact, transforming $\mathbf{V}$ would only be useful if you were trying to change its memory layout for implementation reasons, which is a separate issue from incoherent processing.

On the quantization question: yes, the preservation is exact before quantization, and the benefit comes afterward. The paper’s idea is that after the rotation, the entries of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}$ and $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}$ are less likely to contain extreme outliers concentrated in a few coordinates. In other words, the random orthogonal transform “spreads” a large feature across many coordinates, so the dynamic range is more uniform. That helps FP8 because coarse quantizers suffer most when a few large-magnitude channels force the scale to be set too conservatively for the rest. The paper explicitly frames this as making each entry a “random sum” of the original entries, which reduces the impact of outlier features and hence the quantization error. The transform itself is mathematically lossless; the gain is purely that the subsequent low-precision rounding is less damaging.

Regarding rotary embeddings, the paper is careful to say that $\mathbf{M}$ can be fused with the rotary embedding “at no extra computation cost.” That is because both are linear transformations applied before attention, so they can be composed. The important requirement is simply that the same effective transform be applied consistently to both $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ before the dot product. If a rotary embedding is already part of the model, you can absorb $\mathbf{M}$ into that preprocessing step rather than doing an extra pass over the data. So rotary embeddings do not break the argument; they just change where you place the multiplication.

For head-wise structure, the argument is also local to each head. The paper presents the derivation for a single head, and multi-head attention is just a parallel collection of such heads. So you apply the same idea independently within each head’s $d$-dimensional subspace. What would break the logic is applying different transforms to $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$, or applying a transform that is not orthogonal, because then $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top\neq I$ and the scores would change. But using one shared orthogonal transform per head is exactly what makes the trick work.

The intuitive picture is that orthogonal mixing “rotates” the coordinate system without changing angles or inner products. That leaves attention scores invariant, since scores are just inner products. But the coordinates themselves become less spiky, which is precisely what you want before quantizing to a low-bit format like FP8.

### Why is the backward-pass FLOP count estimated as $2.5\times$ the forward-pass FLOP count?
Question:
In the benchmarking section, the paper says the forward FLOPs are computed as
$$4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head dimension} \cdot \text{number of heads},$$
and then the backward FLOPs are approximated by multiplying the forward count by $2.5$ because there are 2 matmuls in forward and 5 in backward due to recomputation.

I am unsure how exact this accounting is. Why does 5 backward matmuls over 2 forward matmuls imply a factor of $2.5$ for the whole kernel, given that softmax, masking, and rescaling also exist? Is this just a standard convention for reporting attention throughput, or is there a more careful derivation behind it?
Answer:
The short answer is that the paper is using a deliberately approximate, matmul-dominated FLOP accounting convention, not a literal count of every scalar operation in the kernel.

For a single head, the forward attention computation is

$$
\mathbf{S}=\alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top,\qquad \mathbf{P}=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}),\qquad \mathbf{O}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}.
$$

The dominant cost is the two matrix multiplications $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, each of which costs on the order of $N^2 d$ multiply-adds. That is why the paper estimates forward FLOPs as

$$
4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head dimension} \cdot \text{number of heads},
$$

which is just “two GEMMs times $2$ FLOPs per FMA” in the usual convention. The softmax, rowmax, masking, and rescaling are real work, but they are much smaller than the GEMMs in the long-sequence regime the benchmark cares about, so they are ignored in the headline FLOP count. This is standard practice when reporting attention throughput, because the goal is usually to compare implementations on the same approximate workload rather than to produce an exact instruction-level tally.

The backward pass is then multiplied by $2.5$ because, under the paper’s backward algorithm, the dominant work corresponds to five matmuls’ worth of FLOPs versus two matmuls in the forward pass. In the paper’s own notation, the backward formulas involve

$$
\mathbf{dV}=\mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO},\qquad
\mathbf{dP}=\mathbf{dO}\mathbf{V}^\top,\qquad
\mathbf{dQ}=\alpha \mathbf{dS}\mathbf{K},\qquad
\mathbf{dK}=\alpha \mathbf{dS}^\top \mathbf{Q},
$$

and with recomputation of the forward intermediates inside the backward kernel, the total matmul-equivalent work is counted as five matmuls rather than two. So the ratio is simply

$$
\frac{5}{2}=2.5.
$$

That said, this is not meant to imply that the entire kernel is literally “five times as much matrix multiplication” in a strict microarchitectural sense. It is an accounting shortcut for throughput reporting: count the expensive GEMM-like parts, treat the other elementwise operations as lower-order, and use the same convention consistently across methods. The paper is explicit about that wording: “we multiply the forward pass FLOPs by 2.5 (since there are 2 matmuls in the forward pass and 5 matmuls in the backward pass, due to recomputation).”

So the right way to read it is: this is a conventional, approximate benchmark normalization, not a careful derivation of exact kernel FLOPs including every softmax and masking operation. The approximation is good enough for comparing attention implementations because, at the sequence lengths they benchmark, the matmuls dominate both runtime and FLOP count.

### What is the real algorithmic novelty of FlashAttention-3 relative to Hopper-optimized FlashAttention-2 implementations?
Question:
The paper mentions that some of FlashAttention-2's lower utilization on H100 may come from implementation details, such as not using Hopper-specific instructions, and it compares against Triton and cuDNN implementations that already use newer hardware features.

So I am trying to separate 'algorithm' from 'better kernel engineering'. What parts of FlashAttention-3 are fundamentally new algorithmic ideas rather than just a more optimized Hopper implementation of FlashAttention-2?

For example, is the main novelty:

- explicit producer-consumer warp specialization,
- the pingpong / 2-stage overlap of softmax with asynchronous GEMMs,
- FP8 layout transformations,
- or all of the above together?

I'm looking for a precise way to describe what would remain novel even if FlashAttention-2 were rewritten perfectly for Hopper.
Answer:
The paper’s own framing is that FlashAttention-3 is not just “FlashAttention-2 written better for Hopper,” but a redesign of the attention kernel around Hopper’s asynchronous execution and FP8 support. So the right way to separate “algorithm” from “kernel engineering” is:

FlashAttention-2 already gives the basic exact-attention tiling idea: stream over $K,V$ blocks, keep the softmax state on-chip, and avoid materializing $S$ and $P$ in HBM. What FlashAttention-3 adds, at the algorithmic level, is a schedule that explicitly exploits Hopper’s asynchronous Tensor Cores and TMA, plus an FP8-aware variant of the attention computation.

More precisely, the paper identifies three main contributions. The first is producer-consumer warp specialization: one set of warps only issues data movement, while another set only issues computation. That is not merely a micro-optimization; it changes the execution model of the kernel so that memory transfer and compute can overlap in a way FlashAttention-2’s synchronous design does not expose. The paper even says FlashAttention-2 “makes no explicit use of asynchrony and low-precision in its design,” which is the key distinction.

The second is the 2-stage “pingpong” pipeline that overlaps the softmax of one block with the asynchronous GEMMs of the next block. This is probably the most clearly new algorithmic idea relative to a Hopper-optimized FlashAttention-2. The important point is that the paper does not just use Hopper instructions in the old control flow; it reorders the dependence structure so that the second GEMM from iteration $j$ can run while the softmax work from iteration $j+1$ is being computed. In other words, the novelty is not merely “use WGMMA,” but “refactor the loop so WGMMA and softmax can be overlapped.”

The third contribution is the FP8 version of the algorithm. Here the novelty is partly hardware-facing and partly numerical. The layout transformations for $V$ and the accumulator-to-operand register permutation are necessary to make Hopper’s FP8 WGMMA usable in this fused attention setting, but by themselves they are best thought of as enabling machinery. The algorithmic novelty in the FP8 path is really the combination of that layout handling with block quantization and incoherent processing, which make low-precision attention accurate enough to be practical. So if you are asking what remains novel “even if FlashAttention-2 were rewritten perfectly for Hopper,” the answer is not just “use Hopper instructions,” but “use an asynchronous, warp-specialized, pipelined attention schedule, and a separate FP8-aware formulation with blockwise quantization and incoherent preprocessing.”

So a precise summary would be: FlashAttention-3’s real novelty over a perfectly Hopper-optimized FlashAttention-2 is the explicit use of Hopper asynchrony in the algorithm design, via producer-consumer warp specialization and GEMM/softmax overlap, together with an FP8-specific attention formulation that includes layout transformations and accuracy-preserving quantization tricks. The low-level details matter, but they are in service of those higher-level algorithmic changes rather than being the novelty by themselves.
