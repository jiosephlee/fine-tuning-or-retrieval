\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

Why Attention Becomes the Bottleneck

# Quadratic Scaling of Self-Attention with Sequence Length

The paper begins from a simple but important observation: in the Transformer architecture, attention is expensive because the core self-attention scores between queries and keys scale quadratically with sequence length. If the input has length $N$, then the score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ has shape $\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$. This means that every query position interacts with every key position, so as $N$ grows, the amount of work grows much faster than linearly. The output of attention is then produced by applying softmax row-wise to $\mathbf{S}$ and multiplying by $\mathbf{V}$. Because the score matrix is so large, both computation and memory traffic become severe.

This quadratic growth is the central reason attention becomes a bottleneck in large Transformer models. The paper emphasizes that long-context applications are especially affected, because making the sequence longer does not merely add a little extra work; it dramatically increases the cost of forming and processing the attention matrix. In other words, the performance challenge is not a minor implementation detail but a consequence of the mathematical structure of attention itself.

# Why Long-Context Modeling Matters for Language, Code, Vision, Audio, and Video

The motivation for improving attention is not only efficiency in the abstract. The paper ties long-context attention directly to new capabilities across several domains. In language modeling, longer context enables a model to reason over multiple long documents and files in large codebases. The paper cites examples such as long-document modeling and code-related applications, underscoring that many useful tasks require a model to remember and use information from far earlier in the sequence than standard short-context settings allow.

The same argument extends beyond text. The paper notes that long-context attention is important for new modalities such as high-resolution images, audio, and video. In these settings, the input can naturally be represented as a long sequence of tokens or patches, and the model may need to connect distant parts of the input. It also points to applications involving user interaction with long histories and agent workflows with long horizons. The common thread is that the model must integrate information over long spans, and attention is the mechanism that supports this integration.

This is why the paper frames attention efficiency as enabling technology. Faster attention is not just a way to reduce runtime; it is a prerequisite for scaling models to tasks that require longer contexts and richer forms of reasoning.

# Attention as a Bottleneck in Large Language Models and Transformer Workloads

The paper describes attention as the primary computational bottleneck in the Transformer architecture. The key reason is that attention is central to both the expressiveness of the model and its computational cost. In large language models and other Transformer workloads, attention is repeatedly applied across many layers, heads, and batches, so any inefficiency is amplified across the whole model.

The bottleneck becomes even more visible in long-context settings. Since attention requires forming interactions between all query and key positions, the cost grows rapidly as sequence length increases. The paper also makes clear that this bottleneck is not merely theoretical: on modern GPUs, attention can underutilize available hardware even when optimized implementations are used. The authors report that prior FlashAttention-2 achieves only about $35\%$ utilization on the H100 GPU, while optimized matrix-multiplication kernels can reach much higher utilization. This gap shows that attention is not just expensive in principle; it is also hard to map efficiently onto modern hardware.

The result is a mismatch between the importance of attention in the model and the efficiency with which it runs on the device. This mismatch motivates the systems-level work in the paper. The goal is not to replace attention, but to make the exact attention computation much more efficient on current GPUs.

# Approaches to Speeding Up Attention: Approximation, Software Optimization, and Alternative Architectures

The paper situates its contribution within a broader effort to make attention faster. It identifies three major directions that have been explored in the literature. The first is approximation, where the attention computation is modified so that it is cheaper than exact self-attention. The paper cites several examples of this line of work. The second is software optimization, where the exact attention computation is preserved but implemented more efficiently. The third is the use of alternative architectures that avoid the standard attention mechanism altogether.

These three directions reflect different design philosophies. Approximation attempts to reduce the intrinsic cost by changing the computation. Software optimization keeps the computation but improves how it is executed on hardware. Alternative architectures seek a different model structure altogether. The paper places FlashAttention-3 squarely in the second category: it is an exact-attention method, and its purpose is to make exact attention faster by exploiting hardware more effectively.

The distinction matters because the paper is explicitly concerned with preserving the semantics of attention while improving performance. Rather than giving up exactness, it aims to redesign the implementation so that modern GPU capabilities are used to their fullest extent.

# The Case for Exact Attention Despite the Popularity of Approximations

Although approximation methods are prominent in the literature, the paper chooses to build on exact attention. This choice is motivated by the desire to retain the behavior of standard Transformers while improving efficiency. Exact attention keeps the original computation intact, which is attractive when one wants a drop-in improvement to existing models and training or inference pipelines.

The paper’s perspective is that recent hardware developments make this exact approach especially promising. FlashAttention already showed that careful algorithm design could dramatically reduce memory traffic by fusing attention into a single kernel and avoiding materialization of intermediate matrices in global memory. FlashAttention-2 extended this by improving parallelism over sequence length and restructuring the inner loop. FlashAttention-3 continues this line, but now with an even stronger emphasis on the specific features of Hopper GPUs, namely asynchrony and FP8 low precision.

In this view, exact attention remains valuable because the bottleneck is not only mathematical complexity but also how the computation is organized on hardware. If the algorithm is redesigned to fit the machine, exact attention can become much more practical even at long sequence lengths.

# From FlashAttention to FlashAttention-3: The Paper’s Core Motivation

FlashAttention-3 is presented as the next step in a sequence of hardware-aware exact-attention methods. The original FlashAttention introduced a tiling strategy that minimized reads and writes to slow global memory by fusing all attention operations into a single kernel. FlashAttention-2 then restructured the algorithm to parallelize over the sequence length dimension and improve occupancy and work distribution on the GPU. However, the paper observes that FlashAttention-2 still leaves substantial performance on the table on Hopper GPUs.

That observation is the immediate motivation for FlashAttention-3. The paper argues that newer hardware offers capabilities that the earlier algorithm did not exploit explicitly. In particular, Hopper provides asynchronous Tensor Core and memory movement support, as well as native FP8 computation. FlashAttention-3 is built to take advantage of those features through three main ideas: warp-specialized producer-consumer asynchrony, overlap between block-wise GEMMs and softmax, and hardware-accelerated low-precision GEMM with FP8. These ideas are not presented as isolated tricks, but as a response to the gap between the structure of FlashAttention-2 and the execution model of modern GPUs.

The motivation, then, is both practical and architectural. Practically, the authors want to close the utilization gap between FlashAttention and highly optimized GEMM kernels on H100. Architecturally, they want to show that exact attention can be redesigned around asynchrony and low precision without changing the underlying attention computation. FlashAttention-3 is the result of that effort: a faster, more hardware-conscious exact-attention algorithm intended to push attention closer to the performance limits of Hopper GPUs.

2. Mathematical Foundations of Multi-Head Attention

# Definitions of $Q$, $K$, $V$, attention scores, probabilities, and outputs

At the heart of Transformer models is the attention mechanism, which combines three sequences of vectors: queries, keys, and values. In the paper, these are written as $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$, each belonging to $\mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ for a single head. Here $N$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the head dimension. You can think of each row of these matrices as one token representation within the head. The query matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ encodes what each token is looking for, the key matrix $\mathbf{K}$ encodes what each token offers for matching, and the value matrix $\mathbf{V}$ encodes the information that will actually be aggregated.

The attention computation proceeds in three steps. First, the query and key matrices are multiplied to form a score matrix
\[
\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}.
\]
Each entry of $\mathbf{S}$ measures the compatibility between one query token and one key token. Second, these scores are normalized row-wise using softmax to obtain a probability matrix
\[
\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}.
\]
Each row of $\mathbf{P}$ is a probability distribution over the keys for one query. Third, these probabilities are used to form the output
\[
\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}.
\]
This final matrix $\mathbf{O}$ is the attention output, where each token receives a weighted sum of value vectors from the sequence.

The paper emphasizes that this is the standard mathematical form of attention, and that the challenge is not the definition itself but implementing it efficiently on a GPU without materializing large intermediate matrices in slow global memory.

# Scaled dot-product attention and the role of the factor $1/\sqrt{d}$

The score matrix is not simply $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ in the formulation used in the paper. It is scaled by a factor $\alpha$, and in practice one typically sets
\[
\alpha = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}.
\]
This scaling is part of the standard scaled dot-product attention formulation.

The reason for including this factor is tied to the dimension of the head. As $d$ grows, dot products between query and key vectors can become larger in magnitude. If these raw scores are too large, the softmax can become overly sharp, which is undesirable for both stability and learning behavior. Scaling by $1/\sqrt{d}$ keeps the values in a range where the softmax is better behaved. In the paper, this scaling is treated as part of the standard attention definition and carried through both the forward and backward formulas.

This detail matters because the FlashAttention algorithms preserve the exact attention computation, including the scaling factor, while changing only how the computation is scheduled and how intermediate results are handled.

# Row-wise softmax and numerical stabilization using row maxima

After computing the score matrix $\mathbf{S}$, attention applies softmax row-wise. That means each row of $\mathbf{S}$ is transformed into a row of probabilities whose entries sum to $1$. The row-wise nature is important because each query token independently chooses how to distribute its attention over all key tokens.

The paper also explicitly describes the standard numerical stabilization step used in attention: subtracting the row maximum before exponentiation. Concretely, for each row of $\mathbf{S}$, one subtracts
\[
\mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S})
\]
from that row before applying the exponential inside the softmax. This does not change the resulting softmax probabilities, because softmax is invariant to adding or subtracting the same constant from all entries in a row. But it does prevent large positive scores from causing numerical overflow in the exponential.

This stabilization is not just a theoretical detail. It is central to the attention algorithm used in the paper and becomes especially important in FlashAttention-style implementations, where the softmax is computed block by block rather than all at once. In the paper’s algorithms, the quantities $m_i$ and $\ell_i$ track row-wise maxima and normalization factors incrementally so that the exact softmax result can be recovered without materializing the full score matrix.

# Extension from single-head to multi-head attention

The paper first defines attention for a single head, but actual Transformer layers use multi-head attention. In multi-head attention, there are multiple sets of query, key, and value projections, one for each head. Each head performs the same attention computation independently on its own projected inputs.

This independence is what makes the computation naturally parallelizable. The paper notes that the attention computation parallelizes across multiple heads and batches. That means the same formulas for $\mathbf{S}$, $\mathbf{P}$, and $\mathbf{O}$ are applied separately to each head, and then the results are combined in the full Transformer layer. From the perspective of the mathematical derivation, nothing changes in the equations themselves; multi-head attention is simply multiple copies of the same single-head operation running in parallel.

This matters for understanding the GPU implementation, because the algorithmic redesign in FlashAttention-3 is built around tiling and parallel execution over the batch size, the number of heads, and the query sequence length. The math of a single head provides the core computation, while the hardware implementation exploits the fact that many such heads exist and can be processed concurrently.

# Backward pass derivation: $\mathbf{dV}$, $\mathbf{dP}$, $\mathbf{dS}$, $\mathbf{dQ}$, and $\mathbf{dK}$

To train a Transformer, one must differentiate the attention computation. The paper introduces a scalar loss function $\phi$ and uses the notation $\mathbf{d}(-) = \partial \phi / \partial(-)$ for gradients. Given the output gradient $\mathbf{dO} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, the backward pass computes gradients with respect to $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$.

The first step is the gradient with respect to $\mathbf{V}$:
\[
\mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}.
\]
This is the gradient flowing through the final matrix multiplication $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$.

Next, the gradient with respect to the probability matrix is
\[
\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO}\mathbf{V}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}.
\]
This comes from differentiating the same product with respect to $\mathbf{P}$.

The gradient must then pass through softmax. The paper denotes this as
\[
\mathbf{dS} = \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}.
\]
This is the gradient of the score matrix after accounting for the row-wise softmax transformation. Once $\mathbf{dS}$ is known, the gradients with respect to queries and keys follow from the score definition:
\[
\mathbf{dQ} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d},
\]
\[
\mathbf{dK} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}^\top \mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}.
\]

These formulas show the structure of the backward pass very clearly. The backward computation uses the same kinds of matrix multiplications as the forward pass, along with the derivative of softmax. This is why the paper later focuses heavily on how to schedule GEMMs and softmax efficiently in both forward and backward execution.

# The Jacobian of softmax and row-wise $\mathrm{dsoftmax}$ computation

The paper gives the derivative of softmax for a vector explicitly. If
\[
p = \mathrm{softmax}(s),
\]
then the differential is
\[
\mathbf{d}s = (\mathrm{diag}(p) - pp^\top)\mathbf{d}p.
\]
This is the Jacobian of the softmax function applied to a vector. The term $\mathrm{diag}(p)$ captures the contribution of each output probability to its own input, while $pp^\top$ captures the coupling among the entries in the same softmax row.

In attention, softmax is applied row-wise to the score matrix $\mathbf{S}$. The paper therefore writes $\mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP})$ to mean that the same vector formula is applied independently to each row of $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{dP}$. This row-wise treatment matches the forward definition of softmax and preserves the structure of the attention computation.

The significance of this formula is that it explains why the backward pass is not just another matrix multiplication. The gradient must account for the normalization performed by softmax, and this introduces row-wise interactions within each row of the score matrix. In implementation terms, this is one reason why attention involves a mix of matrix multiplications and elementwise or row-wise operations, rather than only GEMMs.

# Where the computational and memory costs arise in forward and backward passes

The paper presents attention as a bottleneck because the score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ has size $N \times N$. This quadratic dependence on sequence length is the main mathematical reason attention becomes expensive as context length grows. The forward pass must compute these pairwise similarities, normalize them, and then use them to mix the values. The backward pass repeats a similarly expensive pattern with additional gradient computations.

A key source of cost in standard attention is not only the arithmetic itself but also the memory traffic. In a standard GPU implementation, the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ may be materialized to high-bandwidth memory. Since these matrices are large, reading and writing them creates substantial overhead. The paper identifies this as the bottleneck that FlashAttention was originally designed to address, by fusing operations and keeping intermediate results on-chip whenever possible.

The backward pass is also expensive because it involves several matrix multiplications: $\mathbf{dV}$, $\mathbf{dP}$, $\mathbf{dQ}$, and $\mathbf{dK}$. The paper notes that the backward computation includes more matmuls than the forward pass, and that the total FLOP count is about $2.5$ times the forward-pass FLOPs in the benchmark accounting used in the paper, because the backward pass includes five matmuls compared with two in the forward pass, with recomputation. This highlights why attention remains a major performance target both during inference and training.

The mathematical structure therefore explains the engineering priorities in the rest of the paper. The same equations define both standard attention and FlashAttention-3, but the implementation strategy changes dramatically in order to reduce memory movement, overlap the matmul and softmax parts of the computation, and preserve numerical accuracy while using lower-precision arithmetic.

3. GPU Architecture for Attention Kernels

# GPU memory hierarchy: HBM/global memory, L2 cache, shared memory, and registers

FlashAttention-3 is designed around a simple but crucial fact about GPUs: different kinds of data live at different “distances” from the compute units, and those distances matter enormously for performance. The paper describes the GPU memory system as a hierarchy of data locales, each with different capacity and bandwidth. At the farthest and largest level is global memory, also called HBM. This off-chip DRAM is accessible to all streaming multiprocessors, but it is much slower than on-chip storage. Data from global memory is transparently cached into an on-chip L2 cache, which sits between HBM and the compute units. Closer still is shared memory, which is a small, programmer-managed, highly banked on-chip cache local to each streaming multiprocessor. Finally, each thread has access to its own register file, which is the closest and fastest storage but also the most limited.

This hierarchy is central to understanding why attention kernels are hard to optimize. Attention involves large matrix operations and repeated movement of query, key, and value tiles. If intermediate results are written out to HBM and later read back, performance is dominated by memory traffic rather than arithmetic. FlashAttention’s original insight, and FlashAttention-3’s continuation of it, is to keep intermediate data on chip whenever possible. In the paper’s algorithmic description, query, key, and value tiles are moved into shared memory, then consumed there by the compute steps. The outputs and normalization statistics are maintained on chip until the end of the block computation, and only the final results are written back to HBM.

The register file is especially important because some parts of the algorithm need to preserve intermediate state across iterations. In the consumer portion of the kernel, the algorithm maintains quantities such as the output accumulator $\mathbf{O}_i$, the running maximum $m_i$, and the running normalizer $\ell_i$. These are held in registers so that they can be updated efficiently across many small operations. The paper also uses register buffers to enable pipelining: when overlapping one operation with another, extra register space is needed to hold intermediate matrices such as $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ while the current work is still in flight. Thus, shared memory and registers are not just passive storage locations; they actively constrain how the kernel is structured.

# Bandwidth versus capacity across the memory hierarchy

A key organizing principle in the paper is that capacity and bandwidth move in opposite directions. The largest memory, HBM, has the greatest capacity but the lowest bandwidth relative to on-chip storage. Shared memory and registers are much smaller, but they are far faster to access. The paper explicitly notes that the memory hierarchy is organized so that capacity is inversely related to bandwidth. This is why attention kernels must be carefully tiled: the full matrices are too large to keep in the fastest memory levels, so the computation is broken into blocks that fit into shared memory and registers.

For attention, this tradeoff is particularly consequential because the score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ is $N \times N$, which grows quadratically in the sequence length. Materializing $\mathbf{S}$ and the softmax probabilities $\mathbf{P}$ in HBM, as in standard attention, creates a large bandwidth burden. The FlashAttention family avoids that by performing local softmax updates on blocks of the score matrix and by never storing the full intermediate matrices in global memory. Instead, it processes blocks of queries, keys, and values so that the working set stays within the fast on-chip memory hierarchy.

The paper’s implementation details reflect this bandwidth-capacity balance very directly. Query blocks are loaded into shared memory, key and value blocks are streamed through a circular shared-memory buffer, and partial outputs are accumulated on chip. The use of a staged buffer is a way of matching the flow of data to the finite capacity of shared memory. Because only a limited number of blocks can be resident at a time, the kernel must carefully coordinate when a stage is filled, consumed, and released. This interplay between movement and reuse is a defining feature of the architecture-aware design.

# GPU thread hierarchy: threads, warps, warpgroups, CTAs, clusters, and grids

The paper also emphasizes that GPU execution is organized through a hierarchy of thread groupings. From smallest to largest, these are threads, warps, warpgroups, threadblocks or CTAs, threadblock clusters, and grids. A warp contains $32$ threads. A warpgroup, in the Hopper model used in the paper, is a group of $4$ contiguous warps. CTAs are the workhorse unit of scheduling, and CTAs in the same cluster are co-scheduled on the same GPC in Hopper.

This hierarchy is more than a bookkeeping device; it determines how work is distributed in FlashAttention-3. The algorithm is parallel across batch elements, heads, and query blocks, so a CTA-level view is enough to understand one unit of work. Each CTA is responsible for a tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ of the query matrix and computes the corresponding tile $\mathbf{O}_i$ of the output. Within that CTA, the work is further divided across warps and warpgroups depending on whether they are producing data, consuming data, or performing asynchronous computation. The paper’s warp-specialized design is built on this hierarchy.

The hierarchy also shapes what kinds of data can be shared efficiently. Shared memory is directly addressable by all threads in a CTA, which makes it ideal for blockwise reuse across warps in the same CTA. Registers, by contrast, are private to each thread and therefore best suited for per-thread fragments and local intermediate values. This distinction is why the kernel uses shared memory for tiles of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$, but keeps certain intermediate accumulators and pipeline state in registers. Understanding the hierarchy is therefore essential to understanding why the algorithm is arranged in stages and why some data movement is done cooperatively while other data is kept private.

# How shared memory and registers shape kernel design

In FlashAttention-3, shared memory and registers do not merely store data; they determine the structure of the kernel itself. Shared memory is used as a circular buffer for query, key, and value tiles. The producer warpgroup loads tiles from HBM into shared memory, and consumer warpgroups read them back for computation. Because shared memory is programmer-managed, the kernel must explicitly coordinate when a buffer stage is available, when it is being filled, and when it can be reused. The paper’s pseudocode reflects this through wait and release steps, as well as through a pipeline object used to manage barrier synchronization.

Registers are equally important because the consumer side of the algorithm maintains running state across many blocks. In the standard FlashAttention update, each block contributes to a running maximum and a running normalizer for the softmax. These quantities are stored in registers so that the algorithm can rescale intermediate outputs without repeatedly moving data between memory levels. The paper also points out that additional pipeline stages require additional register space. For example, the two-stage overlapped algorithm keeps an extra matrix such as $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ in registers, which increases register pressure. This matters because register spilling would reduce performance, so the design must trade off deeper pipelining against available register capacity.

This is one reason the paper pays attention to dynamic register reallocation. If some warps are only issuing data movement, they do not need many registers, while warps performing matrix multiply operations need more. By rebalancing register usage according to role, the kernel can make better use of the available on-chip resources. The underlying lesson is that high performance on GPUs is not just about arithmetic throughput; it is about fitting the kernel’s live working set into the right level of the memory hierarchy and assigning resources to the right execution units.

# Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) for asynchronous memory movement

A major hardware feature exploited by FlashAttention-3 on Hopper is the Tensor Memory Accelerator, or TMA. The paper describes TMA as a dedicated hardware unit for asynchronous memory copies between global memory and shared memory. This matters because moving blocks of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ into shared memory is a recurring task in the attention kernel, and if those copies were done synchronously they would stall the compute pipeline.

TMA changes the design space because memory movement can proceed independently of other operations. In the paper’s warp-specialized kernel, producer warps issue TMA loads of the query, key, and value tiles, while consumer warps perform computation. Since TMA is asynchronous, issuing a load does not require the producer to wait for the corresponding copy to complete before continuing to plan the next one. This is what makes a circular shared-memory buffer useful: while one stage is being consumed, another stage can be filled in the background.

The paper stresses that this hardware capability is what enables the producer-consumer execution model. Instead of having one warpgroup do everything in lockstep, the kernel splits responsibilities so that data movement can be hidden behind computation. In the pseudocode, the producer warpgroup loads $\mathbf{Q}_i$, then repeatedly loads $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ into successive buffer stages. Meanwhile, the consumer warpgroup waits only when necessary and otherwise keeps the pipeline moving. The architectural insight is that memory copies are not merely preparatory work; on Hopper they can be scheduled as an active, overlapping part of the computation.

# Tensor Cores and asynchronous WGMMA on Hopper

FlashAttention-3 also exploits Hopper’s Tensor Cores through the warpgroup-wide WGMMA instruction. The paper highlights an important difference from prior architectures such as Ampere: on Hopper, WGMMA is asynchronous and can source inputs directly from shared memory. This makes it especially well suited to a kernel that continuously streams tiles through shared memory while computing on them.

The asynchronous nature of WGMMA is crucial. In a synchronous design, a warp would issue a matrix multiply and stall until it completed before moving on. In the Hopper model used by FlashAttention-3, a warpgroup can commit a WGMMA and continue with other work while the operation is still in flight. This allows the kernel to overlap arithmetic with other arithmetic or with memory movement. The paper uses WGMMA as a metonym for asynchronous GEMM, because the actual implementation is built around sequences of such matrix multiplications.

This capability is used in two places. First, the kernel uses WGMMA to perform the score computation $\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$. Second, it uses WGMMA again to apply the softmax probabilities to the value tile, computing $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$. Because both operations are substantial matrix multiplies, Hopper’s Tensor Cores are the natural units to execute them. The key design problem is to schedule them so that they can overlap with the softmax work and with the data movement handled by TMA. The paper’s answer is to combine WGMMA with warp specialization and pipelining.

# Warp specialization and producer-consumer execution models

Warp specialization is one of the paper’s central architectural ideas. The basic concept is to divide the warps of a CTA into distinct roles so that some warps act as producers, handling data movement, while others act as consumers, handling computation. This fits Hopper particularly well because TMA handles memory movement asynchronously and WGMMA handles matrix multiplications asynchronously. By separating the duties, the kernel can keep both kinds of hardware units busy.

The producer-consumer split also helps the compiler generate better instruction schedules. The paper notes that warp specialization generally improves the compiler’s ability to produce optimal instruction sequences. In the FlashAttention-3 kernel, producer warps issue loads into shared memory, while consumer warpgroups perform the GEMMs and softmax-related updates. This role separation is especially useful because different roles need different amounts of register space and have different latency-hiding responsibilities.

The paper goes further and combines warp specialization with “pingpong” scheduling. In that scheme, one warpgroup performs the softmax while another warpgroup is executing GEMMs, and then the roles swap. The motivation is that softmax involves much lower-throughput operations than matrix multiplication, especially exponential calculations. By moving these two kinds of work onto different warpgroups, the kernel can overlap them more effectively. This is a concrete example of how a hardware-aware execution model shapes the structure of the algorithm: the computation is not simply parallelized, but partitioned by hardware role and latency profile.

# Dynamic register reallocation with setmaxnreg

Hopper introduces another mechanism that FlashAttention-3 exploits: dynamic reallocation of registers between warpgroups using $\verb|setmaxnreg|$. The paper explains that this matters because the warps performing matrix multiplications need more register space than the warps that are only issuing TMA loads. In a warp-specialized kernel, not every warp has the same register demand, so a static equal split would be wasteful.

By using $\verb|setmaxnreg|$, the kernel can deallocate some registers from the producer-side warps and reallocate them to the consumer-side warps. This is particularly valuable because the consumer warps store intermediate values, maintain softmax statistics, and may hold extra buffers for pipelining. Meanwhile, the producer side, which mostly orchestrates memory copies, can operate with a smaller register budget. The paper describes this as a way of allowing the warps doing MMAs to obtain a larger share of register memory than those just issuing TMA.

This detail may sound implementation-specific, but it captures an important broader theme of the paper: modern GPU efficiency comes from matching resource allocation to functional role. Instead of treating all threads the same, FlashAttention-3 adapts register usage to the needs of each part of the pipeline. That flexibility is part of what lets the algorithm achieve better utilization on Hopper than the earlier FlashAttention designs.

# Low-precision formats on modern GPUs: FP16, BF16, FP8, and the trend toward lower precision

The paper places Hopper’s low-precision support in a broader historical trend. GPUs have steadily added specialized hardware for lower-precision arithmetic, moving from FP16 on Pascal in 2017 to BF16 on Ampere in 2020 and then FP8 on Hopper. The reason is straightforward: lower precision can deliver more throughput for the same power and chip area. The paper mentions that the FP8 Tensor Cores on Hopper can provide roughly twice the throughput per SM compared with FP16 or BF16.

However, lower precision is not just a performance opportunity; it is also a design constraint. FlashAttention-3 has to preserve numerical accuracy while taking advantage of FP8 throughput. The paper emphasizes that this is especially challenging in the presence of outlier features, which are common in large language models and can be much larger in magnitude than typical values. Quantization becomes difficult when a few large values dominate the scale of an entire tensor. That is why the paper does not simply “switch to FP8” but instead adapts the algorithm with block quantization and incoherent processing.

Another important low-precision detail in the paper is that precision affects layout requirements as well as numerical behavior. FP8 WGMMA on Hopper imposes stricter layout constraints than FP16 WGMMA, such as requiring k-major input operands for certain cases. This means that the move to lower precision is not only a matter of arithmetic format; it also changes how tiles must be arranged in shared memory and how the kernel must prepare operands for the Tensor Cores. Thus, the trend toward lower precision is tightly coupled to the hardware execution model and to the memory hierarchy described earlier.

Taken together, the paper’s treatment of low precision shows why attention kernels must be co-designed with hardware. Throughput gains from FP8 are real, but they come with layout restrictions and accuracy challenges. FlashAttention-3 addresses both by aligning its tile-based algorithm with Hopper’s execution units and by introducing quantization strategies that preserve the quality of the attention computation.

4. From Standard Attention to FlashAttention and FlashAttention-2

# Standard attention and the cost of materializing $S$ and $P$ in global memory

To understand why the FlashAttention line of work exists, it helps to start from the standard formulation of attention. For a single head, we have query, key, and value matrices $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, where $N$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the head dimension. The attention output is computed by first forming the score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, then applying row-wise softmax to obtain $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, and finally multiplying by the values to get $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. Usually $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$, and in practice one subtracts the row maximum of $\mathbf{S}$ before applying the exponential to improve numerical stability.

The key issue is that $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ are both $N \times N$ matrices. As sequence length grows, these intermediate matrices become very large. If a GPU implementation materializes them in global memory, then it must write them out and later read them back, which is expensive because global memory is the slowest and most bandwidth-limited level of the GPU memory hierarchy discussed in the paper. In other words, the naive implementation does not just perform a lot of arithmetic; it also performs a large amount of memory traffic on the very intermediates that are only needed transiently.

This is exactly why the paper describes standard attention as an implementation that materializes the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM. The problem is not only that these tensors are large, but also that they are not needed as standalone outputs of the layer. They are merely temporary quantities required to compute $\mathbf{O}$. From the perspective of performance, storing them in global memory is wasted work.

# The IO-aware design principle behind FlashAttention

FlashAttention was introduced to address this memory-traffic bottleneck directly. The paper describes it as an IO-aware approach: instead of treating attention as a purely algebraic expression, the algorithm is designed with the GPU memory hierarchy in mind. The central goal is to minimize reads and writes to slow global memory by keeping intermediate results on-chip whenever possible.

The intuition is simple but powerful. If an intermediate quantity is only useful for the next few instructions, then it should ideally live in shared memory or registers rather than being written out to HBM. FlashAttention reorganizes the computation so that the full score matrix $\mathbf{S}$ and probability matrix $\mathbf{P}$ never need to be stored in global memory. Instead, the algorithm processes attention in tiles, computes local partial results, and combines them in a way that reproduces the exact output.

This is what the paper means by integrating knowledge of the GPU execution model into the algorithmic design. Rather than computing the entire attention matrix at once, FlashAttention computes it in pieces that fit into on-chip storage. That change matters because the GPU is much faster when the working set stays on-chip and when the algorithm avoids unnecessary global-memory traffic. In the paper’s framing, FlashAttention is not an approximation; it is an exact attention algorithm that is reorganized to better match the hardware.

# Local softmax and fused-kernel execution

A central ingredient of FlashAttention is the local softmax reduction. Standard softmax over a row of scores requires knowing the row maximum for numerical stability, and then normalizing by the sum of exponentials. If the whole row is not stored in memory, one cannot simply compute softmax in the usual “all at once” way. FlashAttention solves this by maintaining running summaries as it processes blocks.

The paper explains that local softmax corresponds to the consumer-side operations in the FlashAttention-3 pseudocode, together with rescalings of blocks of $\mathbf{O}$. The idea is that each tile of scores contributes to the final output, but the algorithm keeps track of the row-wise maximum and the normalization factor incrementally. This allows the output to be assembled block by block while remaining mathematically equivalent to standard attention.

This local treatment of softmax is closely tied to kernel fusion. Instead of splitting attention into separate GPU kernels for matrix multiplication, softmax, and the final multiplication by $\mathbf{V}$, FlashAttention fuses these operations into a single kernel. That fusion is important because it removes the need to write intermediate matrices to HBM between stages. It also means the algorithm can keep intermediate quantities on chip and immediately reuse them, which is precisely what gives FlashAttention its efficiency advantage over standard attention.

# Tiling attention to avoid expensive intermediate reads and writes

The paper emphasizes that FlashAttention’s performance comes from a tiling strategy. Rather than computing attention over the full $N \times N$ score matrix, the algorithm splits the query sequence into blocks and processes each query tile against blocks of keys and values. This lets the implementation work with smaller subproblems that fit into shared memory and register storage.

The tiled computation changes how the algorithm is scheduled on the GPU. A block of queries can be paired with successive blocks of keys and values, and the algorithm maintains running state for the output tile $\mathbf{O}_i$ along with the softmax statistics $\ell_i$ and $m_i$. The paper’s pseudocode shows how each block of scores is used to update these values, and then how the partial output is rescaled and accumulated. This makes it possible to compute exact attention without ever materializing the full matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ in HBM.

This tiling strategy is not just a coding trick; it changes the fundamental cost profile of attention. The paper presents FlashAttention as a way to replace slow global-memory traffic with much cheaper on-chip computation and synchronization. The result is that the algorithm becomes far more suitable for long-sequence workloads, where the size of the score matrix would otherwise dominate the cost of the layer.

# FlashAttention-2: improved work partitioning and parallelization over sequence length

FlashAttention-2 built on FlashAttention by restructuring the algorithm to better use the GPU. According to the paper, FlashAttention-2 parallelizes over the sequence-length dimension and performs the inner loop of the forward pass over blocks of the key and value matrices. This improves occupancy and distributes work more effectively across the GPU.

This is an important step forward because FlashAttention-1 already reduced memory traffic, but it did not fully exploit the parallelism available in modern hardware. FlashAttention-2 addresses that by changing the organization of the computation so that more work can be carried out concurrently. The paper describes this as a reworking of the algorithm to improve the occupancy and distribution of work on the GPU. In other words, more of the hardware is kept busy.

However, FlashAttention-2 still uses a simplified synchronous model. It does not explicitly design around asynchrony or low-precision hardware features. That is why, even though it is more efficient than standard attention, the paper observes that it still falls short of what newer GPU architectures can do. FlashAttention-2 is a major improvement, but it is not the final step.

# Why FlashAttention-2 underutilizes Hopper GPUs

The paper’s central motivation for FlashAttention-3 is that FlashAttention-2 underutilizes Hopper GPUs relative to optimized GEMM kernels. On the H100 GPU, FlashAttention-2 achieves only about $35\%$ utilization, while optimized matrix-multiplication kernels can reach about $80$--$90\%$. This gap is large enough that it cannot be dismissed as a minor implementation detail.

Part of the issue is implementation-level: FlashAttention-2 did not use Hopper-specific instructions where they would have helped. The paper notes that newer work and libraries have shown that Hopper-specific instructions and tile-based abstractions can improve attention performance and simplify implementation. But the deeper issue is algorithmic. FlashAttention-2 is built around a synchronous execution model that does not explicitly exploit the fact that Hopper separates data movement and matrix multiplication into specialized asynchronous hardware units.

In the paper’s view, this is the key limitation. FlashAttention-2 improves the organization of the work, but it still assumes a more conventional execution model than Hopper actually provides. As a result, it does not fully hide memory and instruction latencies, and it does not make full use of the Tensor Cores and TMA in the way a Hopper-native algorithm can.

# Measured utilization gap between attention kernels and optimized GEMM kernels

The paper repeatedly compares FlashAttention-2 to optimized GEMM kernels because GEMM is the kind of workload that modern GPUs handle especially well. On H100, optimized matrix-multiplication kernels can achieve very high utilization, while FlashAttention-2 remains far lower. The paper gives the specific comparison of $35\%$ utilization for FlashAttention-2 versus roughly $80$--$90\%$ for optimized GEMM kernels.

This comparison is important because it tells us that the bottleneck is not simply “attention is hard.” Rather, it reveals that attention, as implemented in FlashAttention-2, is not yet aligned with the hardware’s best execution paths. Since the GPU is built to accelerate matrix multiplication extremely efficiently, an attention kernel that leaves that capability underused is effectively leaving performance on the table.

The paper’s later results reinforce this point. FlashAttention-3 reaches up to $740$ TFLOPs/s on H100, corresponding to about $75\%$ utilization, and FP8 performance approaches $1.2$ PFLOPs/s. These numbers show that much more of the GPU can be used when the algorithm is redesigned to better match the architecture. The fact that FlashAttention-2 sits far below these levels is exactly what motivates the new design.

# Why new hardware features require a redesign rather than a simple port

A natural question is why one cannot simply port FlashAttention-2 to Hopper and expect large gains. The paper’s answer is that Hopper introduces hardware features that change what a good algorithm looks like. In particular, Hopper provides asynchronous Tensor Cores via WGMMA and a Tensor Memory Accelerator for asynchronous data movement. It also supports low-precision FP8 computation. These are not just faster versions of old operations; they introduce new possibilities and new constraints.

Asynchrony changes the structure of the computation. If matrix multiplication and memory movement can proceed asynchronously, then the algorithm can overlap them rather than waiting for each stage to finish before starting the next. But to exploit this, the algorithm must be reorganized so that data producers and consumers can be separated, and so that the software can pipeline different parts of the work. That is a design problem, not a simple replacement of one instruction with another.

Low precision also requires redesign. FP8 can provide roughly double the throughput of FP16 or BF16 on Hopper, but it comes with reduced numeric range and precision. In attention, this matters because the computation can be sensitive to outliers in the input features. The paper therefore uses block quantization and incoherent processing to control error. None of this is handled by the original FlashAttention-2 design, which was not built to target FP8 Tensor Cores or to manage the layout restrictions they impose.

So the paper’s larger point is that Hopper is not merely “faster hardware.” It is hardware with new capabilities that require the algorithm to be rethought around asynchrony, specialized memory movement, and low-precision execution. FlashAttention-2 was a major step forward, but FlashAttention-3 is needed to fully exploit those features.

5. The Core Ideas of FlashAttention-3

# Overview of FlashAttention-3

FlashAttention-3 is the next step in a line of exact attention algorithms that are designed around the way modern GPUs actually execute work. The paper’s central observation is that attention is still a bottleneck for Transformer models, especially in long-context settings, and that earlier FlashAttention methods already improved performance by reducing slow reads and writes to global memory. FlashAttention-3 keeps the same basic goal, but it goes further by explicitly taking advantage of two Hopper GPU capabilities that earlier designs did not fully exploit: asynchronous execution and FP8 low precision.

At a high level, the paper presents FlashAttention-3 as a redesign of FlashAttention-2 rather than a completely new attention formula. It still computes exact attention, not an approximation. The improvement comes from how the computation is scheduled and how the data is represented. The algorithm is organized so that memory movement, matrix multiplication, and softmax do not sit idle waiting for one another any longer than necessary. In parallel, the FP8 version changes the layout and quantization strategy so that the Hopper Tensor Cores can be used efficiently without sacrificing too much numerical accuracy.

The conceptual picture is therefore one of coordination. FlashAttention-3 is not simply “faster because of better kernels,” but faster because it maps the structure of attention onto the structure of the hardware. Its three main ideas are: producer-consumer asynchrony through warp specialization, overlapping GEMM and softmax to hide non-matmul costs, and hardware-aware FP8 attention with block quantization and incoherent processing. The paper’s core claim is that these ideas are complementary: together they raise utilization and also preserve accuracy.

# Producer-consumer asynchrony through warp specialization

The first major idea is to divide the work inside a CTA into producer and consumer roles. This is called warp specialization. The paper’s intuition is that GPUs are throughput machines, and throughput is improved when different hardware units are kept busy with different kinds of work at the same time. On Hopper, data movement from global memory to shared memory can be done by TMA, while matrix multiplication can be done by asynchronous WGMMA instructions on Tensor Cores. FlashAttention-3 uses this separation by assigning some warps the job of producing data and others the job of consuming it.

The producer warps are responsible for moving tiles of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ into shared memory. The consumer warps wait for those tiles, then perform the actual attention computation on them. This organization matters because loading and computation no longer have to be treated as one monolithic synchronous step. Instead, the algorithm turns the CTA into a pipeline. While one set of warps is loading the next block, another set of warps can already be computing on the current block.

The paper emphasizes that Hopper makes this style of design especially effective because the Tensor Memory Accelerator and WGMMA are both asynchronous. That means the producer does not have to block the consumer in the same way a purely synchronous algorithm would. The kernel can issue loads, continue with other work, and then synchronize only when a specific tile is needed. The result is better latency hiding and better use of the machine’s execution resources. Hopper also supports dynamic register reallocation with $\verb|setmaxnreg|$, which lets the consumer warps hold more registers than the producer warps, since the producers need relatively little register space. This is part of the paper’s broader idea that the kernel should adapt its internal resource allocation to the roles the warps play.

The algorithmic effect of warp specialization is easiest to see as a pipeline over the query block $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and the key/value blocks $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$. The producer fills shared memory with the needed tiles, while the consumer performs the blockwise GEMMs and softmax updates. Because the buffer is circular, the producer can keep moving forward as soon as a stage becomes free. That circular structure gives the algorithm a way to reuse on-chip memory without materializing large intermediate matrices in HBM.

# Overlapping GEMM and softmax to hide non-matmul costs

The second major idea is to hide the cost of softmax behind matrix multiplication. This is important because the paper shows that non-matmul operations, especially the exponential used in softmax, are much slower than matmul on Hopper in throughput terms. Even though softmax is essential for attention, its arithmetic intensity is much lower than GEMM, so if the kernel executes it in a strictly sequential way, it can become a bottleneck.

FlashAttention-3 solves this by changing how the inner loop is scheduled. In the usual structure, one first computes a score block $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, then applies softmax operations to that block, and then uses the result to form the contribution to $\mathbf{O}_i$ through multiplication with $\mathbf{V}_j$. In a synchronous design, those steps are serialized. FlashAttention-3 instead breaks this dependency chain across iterations and uses extra register buffering so that the next matrix multiplication can begin before all softmax work from the current iteration is done.

The paper’s two-stage pipeline captures this idea clearly. In one stage, the consumer issues WGMMA for the next score block. In the other stage, it uses the previous block’s softmax result to multiply by $\mathbf{V}$. While the Tensor Cores are busy with one WGMMA, the softmax operations for another block can run in parallel on other hardware units. This is described in the paper as a kind of pingpong scheduling: one warpgroup performs GEMMs while another performs softmax, and then they swap roles. The goal is not perfect simultaneity in a literal sense, but a structured overlap that keeps different units occupied at the same time.

This overlap matters because the paper argues that, on Hopper, GEMM is so fast relative to exponential and other softmax-related operations that leaving them separate wastes performance. By scheduling the softmax “under” the matmul, the algorithm reduces visible non-matmul cost. The paper also notes that the overlap is not automatic. The compiler may reorder instructions, and the extra buffering increases register pressure. So the algorithm is carefully designed as a balance: enough staging to expose overlap, but not so much that register use destroys the performance gains. The paper even discusses a possible 3-stage extension, showing that the design space is really about how much asynchrony can be exploited before resource limits become too costly.

The conceptual takeaway is that FlashAttention-3 treats softmax not as a separate phase, but as a task to be hidden inside the broader matmul pipeline. That is why the algorithm can improve utilization beyond what FlashAttention-2 achieved, even though both still compute exact attention.

# Low-precision FP8 attention with hardware-aware design

The third major idea is to make FP8 work well in attention. The paper’s point is not simply that FP8 is faster because it uses fewer bits. Instead, FP8 is useful because Hopper has Tensor Cores that can execute FP8 WGMMA at roughly twice the throughput of FP16 or BF16. But to use those Tensor Cores effectively, the algorithm must respect their layout constraints and must avoid large numerical errors.

The first challenge is layout. In the FP8 setting, the second GEMM in attention has stricter requirements on how the $\mathbf{V}$ tile must be laid out in shared memory. The paper explains that while the inputs are often contiguous in the head dimension, FP8 WGMMA wants certain tiles to be k-major rather than mn-major. Since the TMA load cannot itself change the contiguous dimension, FlashAttention-3 performs an in-kernel transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ tiles after loading them into shared memory. It uses LDSM and STSM instructions for this. The point is that the algorithm does not treat layout as an afterthought; it is built around the layout restrictions of the hardware so that FP8 execution can stay on the fast path.

The second layout issue is subtler. The paper notes that the FP32 accumulator layout of FP8 WGMMA is different from the operand layout expected when the result is fed into the next WGMMA. That means the output of the first GEMM cannot simply be reused as-is. FlashAttention-3 resolves this by using byte permutes so that the accumulated result is rearranged into a format suitable for the next operation. This also aligns with the transposed layout of the $\mathbf{V}$ tile, making the whole FP8 pipeline consistent. In other words, the algorithm is not just quantized; it is layout-aware at every step of the computation.

Accuracy is the other challenge. FP8 has fewer mantissa bits than FP16 or BF16, and large language models often contain outlier values that are hard to quantize well. The paper addresses this with two techniques: block quantization and incoherent processing. With block quantization, the tensor is not scaled by one global factor, but by one factor per block. Since FlashAttention already operates on blocks, this fits naturally into the algorithm and can be fused with nearby preprocessing such as rotary embedding. This reduces quantization error because different blocks can adapt to different ranges of values.

Incoherent processing attacks the outlier problem from another angle. The paper multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ before quantization. Since $\mathbf{M}$ is orthogonal, the attention scores do not change:
\[
(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top.
\]
What changes is the distribution of the values being quantized. The random transformation spreads out large entries so that the quantizer sees a more balanced signal. The paper follows a structured choice of $\mathbf{M}$ using random diagonal sign matrices and a Hadamard matrix, which can be applied efficiently. This is a key theme of FlashAttention-3: hardware efficiency and numerical robustness are designed together, not separately.

# How the three ideas interact to improve performance

The three ideas in FlashAttention-3 are not independent tricks. They reinforce one another because they address different kinds of inefficiency in the attention kernel. Warp specialization improves the movement of data into and out of on-chip memory. GEMM-softmax pipelining improves the use of compute units within the consumer loop. FP8 support improves the throughput of the GEMM itself, provided the layout and quantization issues are handled correctly.

This interaction is especially important because attention has both memory pressure and compute structure. A kernel can be slowed down by waiting for memory, by wasting Tensor Core cycles, or by spending too much time on softmax and other scalar operations. FlashAttention-3 attacks all three. The producer-consumer design keeps TMA and the Tensor Cores busy. The two-stage pipeline keeps softmax from sitting on the critical path. The FP8 path doubles matmul throughput, which raises the payoff from the other scheduling optimizations. The paper’s empirical results show that these gains accumulate into significant end-to-end speedups.

The paper also makes clear that the interaction is not always straightforward. FP8 improves GEMM throughput but does not speed up exponentials, so the relative importance of hiding softmax becomes even greater in FP8. At the same time, FP8 introduces extra layout and register-handling complexity, which must be absorbed into the same pipelined design. This is why the FP8 version is not just a lower-precision copy of the FP16 algorithm. It is a hardware-aware variant whose efficiency depends on carefully aligning tile layout, transposition, and pipelined execution.

In this sense, FlashAttention-3 is best understood as a single coordinated strategy rather than three separate optimizations. The core strategy is to expose as much parallelism as possible between data movement, GEMM, and softmax, while also exploiting the higher throughput of FP8 when accuracy can be preserved.

# What makes FlashAttention-3 both faster and numerically robust

What makes FlashAttention-3 distinctive is that it improves speed without giving up the exactness of attention, and it improves FP8 accuracy without abandoning the hardware advantages of low precision. The paper’s FP16 results show that FlashAttention-3 reaches much higher utilization on H100 than FlashAttention-2, with forward-pass speedups of about $1.5$ to $2.0 \times$ and backward-pass speedups of about $1.5$ to $1.75 \times$. It also reaches up to $740$ TFLOPs/s, which the paper reports as about $75\%$ utilization. These gains come from a kernel that is more fully matched to Hopper’s asynchronous execution model.

At the same time, numerical robustness is maintained through careful algorithmic choices. In FP16, the paper reports that FlashAttention-3 has the same numerical error as FlashAttention-2 and is better than standard attention because intermediate results such as softmax rescaling are kept in FP32. That is an important point: speed improvements do not require sacrificing the stable accumulation strategy that made FlashAttention attractive in the first place.

For FP8, the paper goes further and validates that the block quantization and incoherent processing strategy reduces numerical error by a factor of $2.6$ compared to a baseline FP8 attention method with per-tensor scaling. This result matters because it shows that low precision can be made more reliable when the quantization scheme is aware of the structure of the data and the structure of the computation. The algorithm does not assume that a single scale is good enough for every tensor or every block. Instead, it uses the block structure of attention itself to localize scaling and reduce the damage from outliers.

So the deeper lesson of FlashAttention-3 is that speed and accuracy need not be opposing goals. By treating the GPU as a collection of specialized, asynchronous units and by treating low precision as a design problem rather than a mere storage optimization, the paper builds an attention kernel that is both faster and more robust. That combination is what makes FlashAttention-3 the paper’s core contribution.

6. Warp-Specialized Producer-Consumer Attention

# CTA-Level Decomposition Over Query Blocks

The first major algorithmic idea in \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is to reorganize the forward pass at the level of a threadblock, or CTA, so that each CTA is responsible for one block of queries and the corresponding block of outputs. This is the same basic computational view used in \textsc{FlashAttention-2}: the overall attention computation is embarrassingly parallel across batches, heads, and query positions, so one can focus on a single CTA that processes a tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ of the query matrix and produces the matching output tile $\mathbf{O}_i$.

This decomposition matters because it makes the algorithm compatible with the GPU execution model. Rather than thinking about attention as a single large matrix operation, the implementation treats it as a sequence of smaller blockwise operations, each executed on-chip as much as possible. The query block $\mathbf{Q}_i$ stays associated with one CTA, while the keys and values are streamed through that CTA in blocks. In this way, the algorithm combines parallelism across CTAs with locality inside each CTA.

The paper emphasizes that this CTA-level view is the right abstraction for describing the producer-consumer design. A CTA does not compute the entire attention matrix at once. Instead, it repeatedly consumes blocks of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$, updates local softmax state, and accumulates the output $\mathbf{O}_i$. This organization is what enables the later use of asynchronous data movement and warp specialization.

# Query and Key-Value Tiling with Block Sizes $B_r$ and $B_c$

The CTA-level algorithm divides the query matrix into blocks of size $B_r$, so that the full sequence of length $N$ is split into $T_r = \lceil N / B_r \rceil$ blocks $\mathbf{Q}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{Q}_{T_r}$. Each CTA is assigned one such query block $\mathbf{Q}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{B_r \times d}$, where $d$ is the head dimension. The keys and values are also blocked, but with a possibly different block size $B_c$, giving $T_c = \lceil N / B_c \rceil$ blocks $\mathbf{K}_0, \ldots, \mathbf{K}_{T_c-1}$ and $\mathbf{V}_0, \ldots, \mathbf{V}_{T_c-1}$.

This asymmetry between $B_r$ and $B_c$ is a core part of the implementation. The query block is fixed for the CTA, while the key-value blocks are streamed through the CTA one at a time. For each key block $\mathbf{K}_j$ and value block $\mathbf{V}_j$, the CTA computes the partial score matrix $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, uses it to update softmax statistics, and then updates the running output with $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$.

This blocked formulation is important because it makes the algorithm compatible with the GPU memory hierarchy. The full attention matrix is never materialized in global memory. Instead, each block is brought into shared memory, processed, and then released. The paper presents this as the foundation for reducing memory traffic while still computing exact attention.

# Circular Shared-Memory Buffers and Multi-Stage Pipelines

To support this blockwise processing, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} uses a circular shared-memory buffer with $s$ stages. The shared-memory buffer holds the query block and a rotating set of key-value tiles. The producer warpgroup fills the buffer stage by stage, and the consumer warpgroups process entries from the buffer as soon as they become available.

The circular structure is what allows the algorithm to reuse shared-memory capacity efficiently. Instead of allocating a separate buffer for every key and value tile, the implementation cycles through a fixed number of buffer stages. For each iteration $j$, the producer waits until the stage indexed by $j \% s$ is free, then loads $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ into that stage. After the consumer finishes with a stage, it releases it so the producer can reuse it later.

This design is paired with a pipeline object that manages barrier synchronization between stages. The paper presents this as a software mechanism for coordinating asynchronous loads and computation. The first $s$ iterations do not require producer waits because the buffer is still being filled, which helps overlap the initial data movement with computation. Once the pipeline is running, the CTA can keep a steady flow of data through shared memory without stalling unnecessarily.

# Producer Warpgroups for Issuing TMA Loads

In the warp-specialized design, some warpgroups act as producers. Their role is to move data from global memory to shared memory using Hopper's Tensor Memory Accelerator, or TMA. The producer side first loads the query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ from HBM to shared memory. After that, it streams the key and value tiles $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ into the circular buffer.

A key point is that these loads are asynchronous. Issuing a TMA load does not require the producer to wait for previous computation to complete, and conversely computation in the consumer warpgroups does not block the producer from scheduling future loads. This allows the producer to keep the data pipeline full while the consumers work on the tiles already resident in shared memory.

The producer side is also lightweight from a register perspective. The paper notes that TMA loads for the producer can be issued with only a single thread when appropriate, which is one reason why the producer warpgroups can be assigned fewer registers. This makes it possible to reallocate registers away from the producer and toward the consumer, where the arithmetic work is concentrated.

# Consumer Warpgroups for Executing GEMMs and Softmax

The consumer warpgroups are responsible for the core attention computation. Once $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and the current key block $\mathbf{K}_j$ are present in shared memory, the consumers compute the score block
\[
\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top.
\]
They then perform the local softmax update by maintaining running row-wise statistics $m_i$ and $\ell_i$, and they use these to rescale the output accumulator $\mathbf{O}_i$ as needed. After the softmax quantities are updated, they wait for the corresponding value block $\mathbf{V}_j$ and compute the second GEMM, updating
\[
\mathbf{O}_i \leftarrow \mathrm{diag}\!\big(\mathrm{exp}(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i)\big)^{-1} \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j.
\]

The paper describes these GEMMs as being executed with WGMMA on Hopper. In the non-overlapped version of the algorithm, the consumer loop is simple: compute the score block, update softmax state, compute the output update, and then release the shared-memory stage back to the producer. The important point is that the consumers are doing the high-throughput work, while the producer keeps feeding them new tiles.

This division of labor is what makes the algorithm “producer-consumer” attention. The producer warpgroups produce data tiles, and the consumer warpgroups consume them through GEMM and softmax operations. The separation is not just conceptual; it is used to exploit Hopper's asynchronous execution model.

# Synchronization Through Pipeline Objects and Barriers

Although the producer and consumer warpgroups operate asynchronously, the algorithm still requires careful synchronization so that each side observes the correct data at the correct time. The paper uses a pipeline object to manage this coordination, together with barrier synchronization and explicit wait/commit operations.

In the producer mainloop, a load is issued and then committed to notify the consumers when it completes. In the consumer mainloop, the warpgroups wait until the relevant shared-memory tile has been loaded before using it. Similarly, after the consumers finish with a shared-memory stage, they release it so the producer can reuse that stage later in the circular buffer.

The paper also points out that synchronization is not only about correctness but also about controlling scheduling. In the later pingpong scheme, barriers are used to force a desirable order of GEMM execution across warpgroups so that softmax from one warpgoup can be overlapped with GEMMs from another. Even in the simpler producer-consumer scheme, the synchronization primitives are what make asynchronous execution manageable and deterministic enough to implement.

# Register Deallocation and Reallocation Across Producer and Consumer Roles

A distinctive feature of the warp-specialized design is that the producer and consumer warpgroups do not need the same number of registers. The producer's job is relatively light: it mainly issues TMA loads and manages buffer stages. The consumer's job is heavier because it must hold intermediate results, softmax statistics, and partial outputs while executing GEMMs.

To reflect this imbalance, the algorithm uses `setmaxnreg` to deallocate registers from the producer and reallocate them to the consumer. The paper explains that this dynamic register redistribution is important on Hopper because it lets the warpgroups doing MMAs receive a larger share of the register file, while the warpgroups issuing TMA need far fewer registers.

This is not just an implementation detail. It is part of why warp specialization is effective. By specializing roles, the compiler can generate better schedules, and the hardware resources can be matched more closely to the actual work performed by each warpgroup. In other words, the algorithm does not treat all warps as interchangeable; it deliberately assigns different jobs to different warps to improve utilization.

# Step-by-Step Reading of the Non-Overlapped FlashAttention-3 Forward Algorithm

The paper first presents a non-overlapped version of the forward pass to isolate the effect of warp specialization and circular buffering before introducing more aggressive overlap. Reading that algorithm step by step makes the control flow easier to understand.

The producer warpgroup begins by reducing its register allocation, loading the query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ into shared memory, and notifying the consumers that the query tile is ready. Then it loops over the key-value blocks. For each block $j$, it waits until the shared-memory stage indexed by $j \% s$ has been released, loads $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ into that stage, and commits the load so the consumers can observe it.

The consumer warpgroup starts by increasing its register allocation, initializing the output accumulator $\mathbf{O}_i$ to zero, and setting the softmax state variables $\ell_i$ and $m_i$ to $(0)$ and $(-\infty)$, respectively. It waits until the query tile is ready, then processes each key-value block in order. For each block, it waits for $\mathbf{K}_j$, computes $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$, updates the softmax statistics, waits for $\mathbf{V}_j$, computes the output update, and releases the shared-memory stage. At the end, it performs an epilogue that normalizes $\mathbf{O}_i$ with $\ell_i$, computes the log-sum-exp quantity $L_i = m_i + \log(\ell_i)$, and writes both $\mathbf{O}_i$ and $L_i$ back to HBM.

This version already shows the core producer-consumer structure: the producer streams tiles into shared memory, and the consumer processes them as they arrive. The algorithm is exact attention, but implemented in a way that respects the GPU's asynchronous hardware structure.

# How Local Softmax and Output Rescaling Are Maintained On-Chip

A central difficulty in blockwise attention is that softmax is not naturally local. The softmax of one block of scores depends on the maximum and sum over the entire row, not just the current block. The paper solves this by maintaining running row-wise variables $m_i$ and $\ell_i$ on-chip throughout the loop.

For each score block $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$, the consumer stores the old row maximum $m_i^{\mathrm{old}}$, updates
\[
m_i = \max\!\big(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}, \mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)})\big),
\]
and then computes the rescaled local probabilities
\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \exp(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i).
\]
It updates the running softmax denominator with
\[
\ell_i = \exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i)\,\ell_i + \mathrm{rowsum}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}).
\]
At the same time, the running output $\mathbf{O}_i$ is rescaled to stay consistent with the changing normalization.

This is how the algorithm preserves correctness while processing the attention matrix block by block. The softmax never needs to be materialized globally. Instead, the necessary normalization statistics are carried through the loop in on-chip state. That is why the paper describes the algorithm as using a local softmax: each block is processed locally, but the running state ensures the final result matches exact attention.

# Why Warp Specialization Improves Latency Hiding and Compiler Scheduling

The main advantage of warp specialization is that it creates natural opportunities to hide latency. Because producer warpgroups handle data movement and consumer warpgroups handle computation, the two roles can proceed concurrently. TMA loads can be in flight while WGMMA instructions execute, and the consumers can keep working on one tile while the producer prepares the next one.

This matters especially because attention contains operations with very different throughputs. The paper emphasizes that non-matmul operations, such as the exponential in softmax, have much lower throughput than the Tensor Cores. By separating the roles of different warpgroups, the algorithm can schedule the relatively slow softmax work in parallel with matmul work, rather than forcing one to wait for the other.

Warp specialization also helps the compiler. When warps have fixed roles, the compiler has a simpler job generating schedules that respect those roles. The paper notes that this can improve instruction scheduling, though the exact behavior still depends on the compiler. In practice, the authors observe that the design leads to meaningful performance gains because it better overlaps memory traffic, Tensor Core work, and softmax computation.

Taken together, these ideas explain why the producer-consumer design is the first major step in \textsc{FlashAttention-3}. The algorithm does not merely make attention faster by reducing memory traffic, as in earlier FlashAttention work. It also reorganizes the work inside a CTA so that Hopper's asynchronous hardware can be used more effectively.

7. Hiding Softmax Under GEMM: Pingpong Scheduling and Intra-Warpgroup Overlap

# Why softmax becomes a bottleneck on modern accelerators

The paper argues that attention on modern GPUs is not limited only by the amount of arithmetic it requires, but also by the imbalance between different kinds of arithmetic. In the attention forward pass, the main mathematical work is split between matrix multiplications and the softmax-related operations that normalize the attention scores. The matrix multiplications map well to Tensor Cores, which are designed for very high throughput. By contrast, the softmax step depends on lower-throughput operations such as floating-point multiply-add, exponentials, and reductions like row-wise maxima and row-wise sums.

This matters because the attention computation is not just “one big matmul.” Even when the matrix multiplications are accelerated heavily, the softmax still has to be carried out, and its operations are not free. The paper emphasizes that on Hopper, the Tensor Core side of attention is much faster than the exponential side, so if the two are executed in a purely sequential way, the Tensor Cores can sit idle while the softmax runs. In other words, the softmax becomes a bottleneck not because it is mathematically large in the same way as the GEMMs, but because its hardware execution rate is much lower.

# Throughput asymmetry between Tensor Core matmul and exponential/special-function units

A central motivation for the overlap technique is the large throughput gap between matrix multiplication and special functions on Hopper GPUs. The paper gives a concrete example: on the H100 SXM5, FP16 matmul throughput is 989 TFLOPS, while special functions such as exponential are only 3.9 TFLOPS. The special-function throughput is calculated from the hardware limit of 16 special-function operations per SM per clock cycle, scaled by the number of SMs and the clock speed.

The paper then compares this asymmetry to the actual work in attention. For FP16 attention with head dimension 128, there are 512 times more matmul FLOPs than exponential FLOPs, but the exponential operations run at 256 times lower throughput. The result is that the exponential portion can consume roughly half of the cycle time compared with the matmul portion, even though the matmuls dominate the arithmetic count. The situation is even more unfavorable in FP8, because the matmul throughput doubles while the exponential throughput stays the same. That means the arithmetic imbalance becomes even sharper as the compute side gets faster.

The important intuition is that a fast accelerator does not necessarily make every part of the kernel equally fast. A kernel can still be limited by a slower functional unit if its schedule forces the slow work to happen while the fast unit is idle. FlashAttention-3 addresses this by deliberately arranging for the softmax work to occur while the Tensor Cores are busy.

# Scheduling softmax during Tensor Core execution

The paper’s core idea in this section is to treat softmax as something that should be hidden behind matmul execution whenever possible. Since the exponential and related operations use a different hardware path than the Tensor Cores, the kernel can, in principle, do useful work on both paths at the same time. The challenge is that the softmax depends on the output of the first GEMM, and the second GEMM depends on the softmax output. So the dependency graph is sequential by default.

FlashAttention-3 exploits the fact that the GPU can issue work asynchronously. The paper’s design uses this to arrange that while one warpgroup is running softmax, another warpgroup is executing GEMMs. The high-level picture is that the slow non-matmul work is scheduled into the “shadow” of Tensor Core execution. This is what the paper calls pingpong scheduling.

This does not eliminate softmax; rather, it hides much of its latency. The key achievement is not making softmax faster in isolation, but preventing it from blocking the Tensor Cores. That distinction is important, because the paper is concerned with end-to-end throughput of attention rather than the throughput of any single operation.

# Pingpong scheduling across two consumer warpgroups

The paper introduces pingpong scheduling as a way to alternate responsibilities between two consumer warpgroups. Each warpgroup has a role in the attention pipeline, and the two warpgroups are coordinated so that one can focus on GEMMs while the other carries out softmax. The scheduling resembles a back-and-forth exchange: one warpgroup performs the matrix multiplications for the current or next block, while the other executes the softmax for the previous block.

Concretely, the paper describes using synchronization barriers to force the GEMMs of one warpgroup to be scheduled before the GEMMs of the other. This creates a structured alternation. While warpgroup 1 is engaged in GEMM work, warpgroup 2 is scheduled to perform softmax; then the roles switch, so warpgroup 2 does GEMM work while warpgroup 1 does softmax. That is why the method is called pingpong scheduling: the active role “bounces” between warpgroups.

The paper notes that the schedule is not perfectly clean in practice, but still improves performance. In the reported example, this kind of overlap increases FP16 forward throughput for head dimension 128 and sequence length 8192 from 570 TFLOPS to 620-640 TFLOPS. That improvement comes specifically from better use of the hardware execution units, not from changing the underlying attention math.

# Using barriers to enforce beneficial execution order

Because the GPU compiler and runtime may otherwise schedule instructions in ways that weaken the intended overlap, the paper uses synchronization barriers to shape execution order. The role of the barriers is to ensure that the GEMM activity and the softmax activity appear in the correct relative order so that the softmax can run while another warpgroup is keeping the Tensor Cores busy.

This is especially important because the overlap is not just a matter of launching two independent tasks. The two tasks are dependent in the attention algorithm, and if the schedule is not controlled carefully, the compiler could serialize them in an unfavorable way or reorder instructions so that the intended hiding effect is lost. The barriers help create a disciplined pipeline across warpgroups, so that the machine has a chance to execute the exponential work concurrently with matmul work.

The paper’s broader point is that performance on modern GPUs is not only about arithmetic intensity. It is also about execution order. By managing that order with barriers, the kernel can align the slower softmax work with the faster matmul work, instead of letting them compete for time in a purely sequential manner.

# Breaking sequential dependencies by pipelining across iterations

Within a single attention iteration, the softmax depends on the score matrix $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, and the second GEMM depends on the softmax output $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$. This creates a dependency chain that would normally force the kernel to finish one step before starting the next. The paper’s main insight is to break this apparent seriality by pipelining across iterations.

Instead of computing everything for one block before moving on, FlashAttention-3 keeps one block’s GEMM result around while the next block’s GEMM is already underway. That allows the kernel to overlap the second GEMM from one iteration with the softmax computation from another iteration. The dependency is not removed mathematically; rather, it is moved so that the waiting time can be covered by other useful work.

This idea requires additional buffering and careful state management. The paper describes using extra buffers in registers so that the kernel can hold both the current and next score matrices, and thereby begin computing on the next block before the previous one has fully finished its softmax and value-matrix update. This is the basis of the two-stage pipelined algorithm.

# The two-stage GEMM-softmax overlapping algorithm

The paper presents a two-stage consumer warpgroup algorithm that replaces the simpler sequential consumer path. The key change is that the kernel no longer treats the two GEMMs and the softmax as strictly serial within each iteration. Instead, it splits the work into a current stage and a next stage.

At the beginning, the consumer warpgroup waits for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and $\mathbf{K}_0$ to be available in shared memory, then computes the first score block $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_0^\top$ using WGMMA. It then computes the softmax quantities $m_i$, $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, and $\ell_i$ based on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, and rescales $\mathbf{O}_i$. That establishes the initial current state.

Inside the main loop, the algorithm launches the next score computation while simultaneously consuming the previous softmax output. Specifically, it computes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ and commits it without waiting, then computes $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ and also commits that without waiting. Afterward, it waits for the score result, performs the softmax update for the next block, then waits for the value-product WGMMA and rescales $\mathbf{O}_i$. In this way, the algorithm overlaps the second GEMM from one iteration with the softmax of the next.

The paper is explicit that this is an overlap across iterations, not within a single operation. The two-stage structure makes it possible to keep both the Tensor Cores and the softmax-related compute units active more of the time.

# Managing $S_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{next}}$ buffers in registers

A crucial implementation detail is that the overlap requires extra register storage. The consumer warpgroup must preserve $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ while $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ is being computed, because the softmax for the current block and the GEMM for the next block are both in flight. The paper explains that the next score block must be kept in registers until it becomes the current block on the next iteration.

This is why the algorithm uses the names $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$. They are not merely conceptual labels; they represent the staged data needed to connect one iteration to the next without forcing the kernel to wait. After the next block has been processed, $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ is copied into $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, and the loop continues.

The paper also notes that this buffering increases register pressure. Since registers are a limited resource, holding additional state can conflict with other performance choices such as larger block sizes. So the benefit of overlap comes with a practical cost: the kernel must balance pipeline depth against register usage.

# Overlapping $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, and softmax across consecutive blocks

The two-stage pipeline is easiest to understand by tracking how the three major pieces of the attention forward pass move across iterations. The first GEMM computes the score block $\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$. The softmax then transforms those scores into $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$. Finally, the second GEMM multiplies $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ by $\mathbf{V}_j$ to update the output block $\mathbf{O}_i$.

FlashAttention-3 arranges these so that the first GEMM for block $j$ is launched while the second GEMM for block $j-1$ is still in progress, and the softmax for block $j$ is executed while that second GEMM is happening. This creates a pipeline across consecutive blocks: current block softmax is hidden under next block GEMM. The code in the paper makes this explicit by computing $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ first, then launching the output update for $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$, and then handling the softmax for the next block.

This is the main technical move behind intra-warpgroup overlap. It is not simply a matter of overlapping arbitrary instructions, but of reorganizing the attention loop so that the next block’s score computation and the previous block’s value update can coexist with the current block’s softmax transformation. The pipeline is therefore both a scheduling trick and a structural rewrite of the forward algorithm.

# Consumer main loop walkthrough for the overlapped FP16 algorithm

The paper’s overlapped FP16 algorithm can be understood by following the consumer main loop step by step. After initialization, the kernel waits for the initial query and key block to arrive. It computes the first score matrix, commits it, and then forms the first softmax state. From there, each iteration of the loop handles three things at once: preparing the next score matrix, consuming the current softmax output into the output accumulator, and updating the softmax statistics for the next block.

The most important detail is that the second WGMMA of iteration $j$ is not isolated from the softmax work of iteration $j+1$. Instead, the kernel begins the next score GEMM, then uses the time while that asynchronous GEMM is in flight to perform the current output GEMM and the softmax computations. Only after the score result is ready does it finalize the softmax update for that next block. This arrangement is what allows the softmax to be hidden behind the GEMM work rather than stretching the critical path.

At the end of the loop, after the last value block has been loaded, the kernel performs the final output update and then rescales $\mathbf{O}_i$ one last time using the accumulated softmax normalizer $\ell_i$ and log-sum statistic $m_i$. The result is then written back to HBM. Thus, the overlap affects the entire middle portion of the computation, while the epilogue completes the final normalization.

# Practical effects of overlap on utilization and throughput

The paper’s empirical results show that the overlap strategy has a measurable impact on throughput. In the ablation study, adding warp-specialization and WGMMA-softmax pipelining improves the performance of non-causal FP16 FlashAttention-3 from 570 TFLOPS to 661 TFLOPS for the tested configuration. More broadly, the paper reports that FlashAttention-3 reaches up to 740 TFLOPS on H100, corresponding to about $75\%$ utilization of the theoretical peak.

The practical interpretation is that the kernel spends less time waiting on the softmax path and more time keeping the Tensor Cores occupied. Since the softmax is relatively low-throughput, the main benefit is not that it becomes faster on its own, but that it stops interrupting the matmul pipeline. This is exactly the kind of gain that hardware-aware scheduling is meant to capture.

The paper also emphasizes that the effect depends on implementation details. Compiler reordering can disrupt the intended schedule, and register pressure can constrain how aggressively overlap is used. So while the principle is straightforward, the performance result depends on careful kernel design. In the paper’s setting, though, the overlap is clearly beneficial: it raises utilization, improves throughput, and helps FlashAttention-3 close much of the gap between the original FlashAttention designs and the optimized GEMM kernels available on Hopper.

8. Engineering Trade-offs in Asynchronous Attention Kernels

# Compiler instruction reordering and its effect on intended overlap

The idealized picture behind \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is that the expensive parts of attention can be arranged so that one kind of work happens “in the shadow” of another. In particular, the algorithm is designed so that the Tensor Cores perform a GEMM while the softmax-related work runs at the same time, or so that data movement overlaps with computation. This is the whole point of introducing asynchrony and warp-specialization. However, the paper makes clear that the intended overlap is not guaranteed simply because the pseudocode is written that way. The compiler, specifically NVCC, may reorder instructions in the interest of optimization, and that can disturb the carefully planned schedule.

This matters because the overlap is not just a high-level mathematical property; it is a property of execution order on hardware. If the compiler moves instructions around, then a GEMM that was supposed to run concurrently with softmax may instead be separated from it, reducing the amount of useful latency hiding. In other words, the algorithmic structure says “these operations can overlap,” but the implementation must still persuade the compiler and hardware to actually preserve that overlap. The paper explicitly notes that the pseudocode is an idealization, not a literal guarantee of runtime behavior.

# Reading SASS to verify actual execution order

Because compiler transformations can change the actual instruction schedule, the paper uses SASS analysis to check what is really happening. SASS is the lower-level machine code generated for the GPU, and inspecting it provides a way to verify whether the intended interleaving of WGMMA and non-WGMMA operations survives compilation. This step is essential precisely because the performance gains depend on the overlap being real, not merely conceptual.

The paper’s point is that one cannot validate an asynchronous kernel only by reading the high-level source. The source expresses dependencies and barriers, but the compiler may still alter the arrangement of instructions. By examining the SASS, the authors confirm that the overlapped code is generated as expected. For a student learning to interpret this work, the key lesson is that performance engineering on GPUs often requires moving between abstraction levels: the algorithmic idea is expressed in pseudocode, but the correctness of the overlap must be checked in the compiled machine code.

# Register pressure from multi-stage pipelines

The paper emphasizes that deeper overlap is not free, because pipelining consumes registers. In the 2-stage design, the consumer warpgroup has to keep additional intermediate state in registers so that one stage of computation can proceed while another stage is still pending. Concretely, the algorithm needs to retain an extra $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ in registers, which adds register usage of size $B_r \times B_c \times \text{sizeof}(\text{float})$ per threadblock. This is a direct cost of turning a sequential dependence into a pipelined one.

The importance of this cost is that registers are one of the most limited resources on the GPU. If a kernel uses too many registers, then occupancy can fall or register spilling can occur, both of which can reduce performance. So while pipelining can increase utilization of the Tensor Cores, it can simultaneously put pressure on the register file. The paper presents this as a practical engineering tension: the most aggressive overlap may be limited not by the abstract algorithm but by how much state can be held on-chip.

# Trade-offs between larger tiles and deeper pipelining

The paper also makes clear that register pressure interacts with tile size. Larger tiles are attractive because they can improve computational efficiency, but they are also register-hungry. At the same time, deeper pipelining requires additional buffering and thus additional registers. These two strategies therefore compete for the same resource budget.

This creates a design trade-off rather than a one-directional optimization. If tile sizes are increased too much, there may be insufficient registers left to support the pipeline depth needed for overlap. Conversely, if the pipeline is made deeper, there may be less freedom to choose larger tiles. The paper does not present a universal best choice; instead, it stresses that in practice one should make trade-offs based on profiling. That is a recurring theme in hardware-aware kernel design: the best configuration is not determined by theory alone but by the interaction of algorithm structure, compiler behavior, and hardware limits.

# Why extra buffering consumes registers

A useful way to understand the register cost is to think about what pipelining really means. The kernel cannot simply overwrite values as soon as they are computed, because later stages still need them. In the 2-stage algorithm, one stage computes the next block of scores while another stage uses the current softmax probabilities to update the output. That means the current and next intermediates must coexist in registers long enough for the pipeline to advance safely.

This extra buffering is what makes overlap possible, but it is also what makes the design expensive. Each additional stage stores more live data, and live data must sit somewhere on the chip. In this paper’s implementation, that “somewhere” is the register file. Thus, buffering is not an abstract software convenience; it is a hardware resource commitment. This is why the paper repeatedly frames pipeline depth as a resource allocation problem, not just a performance trick.

# The 3-stage pipeline idea and its benefits and costs

The paper proposes a 3-stage variant as a natural extension of the 2-stage pipeline. The motivation is straightforward: if the 2-stage design can overlap one GEMM with softmax, then an additional stage may allow even more of the second GEMM to be hidden under the softmax work. In principle, this could further improve Tensor Core utilization by increasing the amount of computation that can proceed concurrently.

At the same time, the paper is careful to present the 3-stage idea as a trade-off rather than an unconditional improvement. A deeper pipeline requires still more registers because one more stage of state must be kept alive. This makes the balance between pipeline depth and tile size even harder to manage. The paper therefore treats the 3-stage design as promising but more demanding, and notes that its detailed description and evaluation are given separately. The takeaway is that additional overlap can be beneficial, but every new stage raises the cost in live state and implementation complexity.

# When more overlap stops helping

A central lesson of the chapter is that overlap is only useful until another bottleneck takes over. The paper’s discussion of softmax versus matmul throughput makes this especially clear. Softmax-related operations such as exponential are much lower throughput than Tensor Core GEMMs. This means that if overlap is not arranged well, the softmax can become the limiting factor and reduce the benefit of asynchronous matmul execution.

But even when overlap is arranged successfully, there are limits. One limit is the cost of register pressure, which can offset the gains of deeper pipelining. Another is the fact that compilers and instruction scheduling are not perfectly predictable. A third is that the hardware resources themselves are finite: only so much can be hidden behind the Tensor Cores, and only so much state can be buffered efficiently. The paper’s practical view is that better overlap helps, but the performance curve eventually flattens because one resource bottleneck is replaced by another.

# Support for multi-query attention (MQA)

The paper states that for multi-query attention, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} follows the approach used in \textsc{FlashAttention-2} by adjusting tensor indexing so that $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ do not need to be duplicated in HBM. This is important because MQA changes how key and value tensors are shared across heads, and a naive implementation would waste memory bandwidth and storage by copying these tensors unnecessarily.

The point here is that the new asynchronous and low-precision techniques are built on top of a broader attention implementation strategy rather than replacing it. The kernel still needs to respect the structural differences of attention variants. By reusing the indexing strategy from \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, the authors preserve compatibility with MQA while focusing their innovation on the new Hopper-specific execution features. For students, the lesson is that performance work often depends on carefully preserving the right tensor-sharing semantics while changing the low-level execution plan.

# Support for grouped-query attention (GQA)

The same approach is used for grouped-query attention. The paper again says that the implementation adjusts tensor indexing in the style of \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, avoiding duplication of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ in HBM. This makes the kernel applicable to GQA without changing the core strategy of tiled, fused attention.

What is notable here is that these attention variants are not treated as separate algorithmic inventions in the paper. Instead, they are supported by adapting the indexing scheme while keeping the main execution ideas intact. That is consistent with the paper’s broader engineering philosophy: once the kernel is built around the correct tiled and asynchronous execution model, variant support becomes a matter of mapping the logical attention structure onto the same hardware-efficient dataflow.

# General lessons in hardware-aware kernel engineering

The overarching lesson of this chapter is that high performance comes from balancing algorithmic structure against hardware constraints. \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is not merely a faster version of attention in the abstract. It is a carefully engineered kernel whose speed depends on making the hardware work in a very specific way: producers and consumers are separated into different warps, TMA and WGMMA are overlapped, softmax is hidden behind asynchronous matmuls, and the entire schedule is constrained by registers, compiler behavior, and buffer depth.

The paper shows that one should not think of a “better algorithm” as automatically faster. The 2-stage and 3-stage designs illustrate that more overlap can help, but only when the additional buffering and scheduling complexity do not outweigh the gains. It also shows that correctness and performance are not the same thing: a pseudocode pipeline is only valuable if the compiled instruction stream preserves the intended order. Finally, the support for MQA and GQA underscores that hardware-aware engineering must still respect the logical structure of the model.

Taken together, these design choices demonstrate a central principle of modern GPU kernel optimization: the best implementation is often the one that aligns the algorithm with the machine’s execution model, while carefully managing the costs of that alignment.

9. FP8 Attention on Hopper: Layout Constraints and Kernel Design

# Why FP8 promises major throughput gains

One of the central motivations for moving attention to FP8 is that modern GPU hardware has specialized support for low-precision arithmetic. In the paper, FP8 is presented as part of a continuing trend in GPU number formats: FP16 on Pascal, BF16 on Ampere, and FP8 on Hopper. The key promise is simple: if the hardware can do the same kind of matrix multiplication using fewer bits, then it can achieve higher throughput for the same power and chip area. For attention, this matters because the attention mechanism is already the main bottleneck in Transformer models, and improving its throughput directly improves the performance of large language models and long-context applications.

The paper emphasizes that Hopper’s FP8 support is not just a minor numerical format change. It is tied to a hardware path that can nearly double the measured TFLOPs/s compared to FP16. In the paper’s empirical results, FP8 FlashAttention-3 reaches close to $1.2$ PFLOPs/s, which shows how large the performance upside can be when the kernel is adapted correctly to the hardware. At the same time, the paper is careful to note that FP8 introduces accuracy challenges, especially in the presence of outlier features, so the performance benefit comes with design constraints that the kernel must handle explicitly.

# Hopper FP8 WGMMA versus FP16 WGMMA

The paper’s discussion of Hopper centers on WGMMA, the warpgroup-wide matrix-multiply instruction. For FP16 WGMMA, the hardware is comparatively flexible: it accepts both mn-major and k-major input operands when the data lives in shared memory. That flexibility makes FP16 easier to integrate into fused attention kernels, because the kernel can more readily reuse layouts that naturally arise from standard tensor storage and intermediate computations.

FP8 WGMMA is different. The paper states that on Hopper, FP8 WGMMA only supports k-major input operands. This means that, unlike FP16, the operand layout must be arranged so that the data is contiguous in the inner $K$ dimension rather than the outer $M$ or $N$ dimension. This difference is not merely an implementation detail. It affects how attention must move data between global memory, shared memory, registers, and the Tensor Cores, because the kernel must present each operand in a layout that the FP8 instruction expects.

The consequence is that a fused attention kernel can no longer treat the GEMM stages as layout-agnostic. The paper’s FP8 design has to account for the exact layout conformance rules of WGMMA. In other words, the kernel must be redesigned not only for arithmetic precision but also for how the data is physically organized at the moment the Tensor Core consumes it.

# mn-major and k-major operand layouts

To describe the layout requirements of WGMMA, the paper uses the terms mn-major and k-major. In a GEMM call that multiplies $A \times B^\top$, where $A$ is an $M \times K$ matrix and $B$ is an $N \times K$ matrix, an operand is mn-major if it is contiguous in the outer $M$ or $N$ dimension. It is k-major if it is contiguous in the inner $K$ dimension. This distinction captures whether the data is laid out so that adjacent memory locations correspond to rows or columns of the logical matrix.

The point of introducing this vocabulary is that the correct layout depends on the specific WGMMA variant. For FP16, the kernel can work with either format in shared memory. For FP8, the operand must be k-major. That requirement becomes especially important in attention, because attention is not a single GEMM but a composition of GEMMs and softmax, and the intermediate results must be arranged so that the next operation can consume them without a costly rearrangement.

This is why the paper frames layout as a hardware constraint rather than a superficial storage preference. The layout of a tensor affects whether it can be fed efficiently into FP8 WGMMA at all. In FP8 FlashAttention-3, the design must therefore coordinate the layout of the key, value, and accumulator fragments so that the sequence of GEMMs remains executable on the FP8 Tensor Cores.

# Why FP8 WGMMA requires k-major operands in shared memory

The paper’s FP8 constraint is specifically about operands in shared memory. The text explains that for FP8 WGMMA, only the k-major format is supported for inputs in shared memory. This matters because FlashAttention-style kernels keep tiles in shared memory to avoid repeated movement through global memory. If the shared-memory tile is not laid out in the format the Tensor Core expects, the kernel cannot directly invoke the instruction on that tile.

This is particularly important in attention because the kernel is built around block processing. Each block of $K$ and $V$ must be loaded into shared memory, used by WGMMA, and then released so that the next block can be processed. If the layout in shared memory is wrong, the kernel either has to transform the data or abandon the ability to use FP8 WGMMA efficiently. So the layout restriction is not an incidental inconvenience; it directly shapes the kernel structure.

The paper also notes that the problem becomes harder when one wants to fuse back-to-back GEMMs in a single kernel. In such a fused design, the output of one GEMM becomes the input of the next. If the output is stored in one layout and the next instruction expects a different one, the kernel must bridge that mismatch somehow. That is exactly what happens in FP8 FlashAttention-3, and it is the reason the paper devotes substantial attention to transpose and register-layout transformations.

# The challenge of back-to-back GEMMs in fused attention kernels

Attention is not just a matrix multiplication; it is a sequence of operations. In the forward pass, the kernel computes scores with $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, applies softmax, and then multiplies by $\mathbf{V}$. In a fused kernel, these stages are kept on-chip as much as possible so that intermediate matrices do not have to be written to global memory. This is the core FlashAttention idea, and FlashAttention-3 builds on it.

The challenge in FP8 is that the output of the first GEMM and the operand requirements of the second GEMM do not naturally align. In the paper’s discussion, the accumulator layout produced by the first FP8 WGMMA does not match the operand layout expected by the second FP8 WGMMA when that operand is held in registers. This means that even if the attention computation is mathematically straightforward, the hardware-level data representation is not. The kernel has to convert between representations while preserving the performance advantage of fusion.

This creates a tension that is central to the FP8 design. On one hand, the kernel wants to keep everything fused so that attention stays memory efficient. On the other hand, the hardware’s layout constraints mean that the intermediate representations must sometimes be transformed before the next WGMMA can run. FlashAttention-3 resolves this tension by combining layout-aware transpose work with careful register permutation.

# Why V must be transformed for the second GEMM

In the FP8 forward pass, the paper identifies $\mathbf{V}$ as the key tensor that must be transformed for the second GEMM. The reason is that the second GEMM in attention multiplies the softmax probabilities by the value matrix, and FP8 WGMMA requires the operand to be in k-major format. However, tensors such as $\mathbf{V}$ are typically laid out contiguous in the head dimension, which is not the layout needed by the FP8 instruction when the tile is in shared memory.

The paper explains that because the TMA load itself cannot change the contiguous dimension, simply loading $\mathbf{V}$ from global memory does not solve the problem. The tile must either be transposed before it reaches shared memory or transposed after it has been loaded there. Without this transformation, the second GEMM cannot be executed in the desired FP8 format.

This is an especially important point in fused attention, because the second GEMM depends on the softmax output from the first stage. If the kernel has to pause and move data inefficiently, the benefits of low precision are reduced. FlashAttention-3 therefore treats the transformation of $\mathbf{V}$ as a first-class part of the kernel design rather than as a separate preprocessing step.

# Options for meeting layout constraints: preprocessing transpose versus in-kernel transpose

The paper considers two broad ways to satisfy the FP8 layout requirement for $\mathbf{V}$. The first is to transpose $\mathbf{V}$ ahead of time in global memory. This could be done either by fusing the transpose into the epilogue of a preceding step, such as rotary embedding, or by running a standalone preprocessing transpose kernel. The second is to perform the transpose inside the attention kernel, after loading tiles of $\mathbf{V}$ into shared memory.

The paper explains why the first approach is awkward. Fusing the transpose into an earlier operation is difficult to integrate into a standard library workflow, and a standalone transpose kernel is unattractive in a memory-bound setting. Since attention, especially in inference, is often limited by memory traffic, adding an extra global-memory pass just to rearrange $\mathbf{V}$ would waste bandwidth and undermine the efficiency gains of the fused kernel.

Because of this, FlashAttention-3 chooses the second approach: in-kernel transpose. This decision keeps the transformation on-chip and avoids introducing a separate global-memory-intensive preprocessing phase. The kernel can then reorganize the data exactly where and when it is needed, which aligns with the overall FlashAttention philosophy of minimizing slow memory traffic.

# Why standalone transposes are unattractive for memory-bound inference

The paper’s reasoning against a standalone transpose is rooted in memory bandwidth. An optimized transpose kernel can run near device bandwidth, but that is precisely the problem: if the system is already memory-bound, then spending bandwidth on a separate transpose is expensive. In inference, where the goal is often to maximize throughput and minimize latency, such extra traffic can erase much of the benefit of moving the actual attention computation to FP8.

The issue is not that transpose kernels are slow in an absolute sense. Rather, they consume a significant amount of the same scarce resource that attention is trying to save: memory bandwidth. FlashAttention-3 is designed to reduce reads and writes to global memory, so introducing a separate kernel that does little besides rearranging bytes would go against the central design principle.

This is why the paper treats in-kernel transposition as the more suitable choice. By keeping the transformation local to the attention kernel, the method preserves the fused execution model and reduces the chance that layout adaptation becomes a bottleneck in its own right.

# Using LDSM/ldmatrix and STSM/stmatrix for in-kernel transpose

To perform the in-kernel transpose, the paper uses LDSM and STSM instructions, exposed as $ldmatrix$ and $stmatrix$. These instructions let a warp collectively load data from shared memory into registers and then store data back from registers to shared memory at a granularity of 128 bytes. The paper notes that although the PTX documentation describes these as copying $8 \times 8$ matrices with 16-bit entries, the same machinery can be adapted to packed 8-bit data, which is the relevant case for FP8.

The important property of these instructions is that they are register efficient and naturally suited to warp-level cooperation. That makes them a good fit for the producer side of the FlashAttention-3 kernel. By using LDSM to pull a tile into registers, rearranging the register contents as needed, and then using STSM to write the data back in a transposed form, the kernel can convert the layout without leaving the chip. This avoids the cost of moving the tile through global memory just to change its orientation.

The paper also notes a subtlety: the transpose versions of LDSM/STSM cannot split packed 8-bit entries, so the kernel needs certain register movements between the load and store to actually accomplish the tile-wise transpose. The paper does not detail every low-level step, but the high-level takeaway is clear: the transpose is implemented as a carefully orchestrated on-chip register-and-shared-memory transformation.

# Performing transpose work in the producer warpgroup

In FlashAttention-3, the transpose is performed in the producer warpgroup. This is a natural place for it because the producer is already responsible for data movement. The paper’s warp-specialized design divides work into producer and consumer warpgroups, with the producer handling loading and buffer management and the consumer handling the matmul-heavy computation. Since the transpose belongs to the data-movement side of the kernel, it can be assigned to the producer without interfering with the consumer’s computation.

This choice also fits the asynchronous execution model of Hopper. The producer can execute the transpose work while the consumer is busy with GEMMs and softmax-related computation. In this way, the transpose becomes part of the data-flow pipeline instead of a separate serial step. The kernel can continue feeding data to the consumer while the producer prepares the next $\mathbf{V}$ tile in the correct layout.

The paper stresses that this placement makes the transpose much more practical. A producer warpgroup is already the locus of TMA loads and buffer staging, so adding transpose logic there preserves the division of labor that warp-specialization is designed to achieve. The producer becomes responsible not only for fetching data but also for shaping it into the exact form needed by FP8 WGMMA.

# Overlapping V-tile transpose with prior GEMMs

The final refinement in the paper’s FP8 design is that the transpose of the next $\mathbf{V}$ tile can be overlapped with the two WGMMA operations that involve the preceding $\mathbf{V}$ tile and the current $\mathbf{K}$ tile. This is one of the reasons the in-kernel approach is effective: the transpose does not have to appear as a standalone phase that stalls the kernel. Instead, after the first iteration, the kernel can hide the transpose behind ongoing GEMM work.

This overlap is consistent with the paper’s broader strategy of exploiting asynchrony. Hopper’s Tensor Cores and TMA permit data movement and computation to proceed independently enough that the kernel can interleave them. In the FP8 case, the producer can prepare the next transposed $\mathbf{V}$ tile while the consumer is still processing the current attention block. That means the layout conversion is amortized across useful work rather than paid as a visible pause.

Seen together, these design choices explain how FlashAttention-3 makes FP8 practical on Hopper. The kernel does not merely “use FP8”; it adapts the layout of the data, the placement of work across warpgroups, and the timing of the transpose so that the hardware can actually execute the fused attention pipeline efficiently.

10. Making FP8 Accurate: Block Quantization and Incoherent Processing

# Numerical limitations of FP8 $e4m3$

The paper’s FP8 attention method is built around the $e4m3$ format, and its starting point is a simple fact: FP8 is much less numerically precise than FP16 or BF16. In the paper’s discussion, this lower precision comes from the limited number of bits available to represent numbers, with only $3$ bits for the mantissa and $4$ bits for the exponent. Because of this restricted representation, FP8 introduces larger quantization error than the higher-precision formats that are more commonly used in attention kernels.

This matters especially in attention, where the computation is not just one matrix multiplication but a sequence of operations that depends on intermediate values being accurate enough to preserve the final distribution produced by softmax. The paper emphasizes that moving from FP16 to FP8 is not just a matter of using the same algorithm at lower precision. The lower-precision format changes the numerical behavior of the computation, so the algorithm must be adapted to keep the error under control.

# Why outlier activations are problematic in large language models

A major difficulty identified in the paper is the presence of outlier values in large models. These are entries whose magnitude is much larger than the typical values in the same tensor. The paper notes that large language models often contain such outliers, and that these outlier features make quantization difficult. The reason is intuitive: if most values are moderate but a few are very large, then a quantization scheme must choose a scale that can represent the large values without overflow or severe distortion. That choice often reduces the effective resolution available for the many ordinary values.

In attention, these outliers are especially problematic because the query, key, and value matrices all participate in a computation that is sensitive to how their entries are represented. If a few unusually large activations dominate the scale, then the rest of the tensor may be represented too coarsely. The paper treats this as a central obstacle to using FP8 directly for attention.

# Baseline per-tensor scaling and its weaknesses

The paper describes the standard baseline approach to FP8 quantization as per-tensor scaling. In this method, one scalar is kept for each tensor, such as one scale for $\mathbf{Q}$, one for $\mathbf{K}$, and one for $\mathbf{V}$. This is a natural first strategy because it is simple and easy to apply: each tensor is quantized using a single global scale.

However, the paper presents this baseline as too coarse for attention with outlier features. A single scale must cover the entire tensor, which means that unusual large values can force the scale to be set conservatively. That reduces precision for the rest of the values, since the quantizer has to preserve the extreme entries too. In the paper’s numerical evaluation, this baseline serves as the comparison point for the more accurate FP8 FlashAttention-3 method.

The paper’s main message here is that per-tensor scaling ignores the structure of attention. Attention is already organized around blocks, and the distribution of values can vary significantly across those blocks. Using one scale for the whole tensor therefore throws away useful locality information that could otherwise improve accuracy.

# Block quantization with one scale per $Q$, $K$, and $V$ block

To address this, the paper introduces block quantization. Instead of using one scalar for an entire tensor, the tensor is split into blocks and each block gets its own scale. For $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$, the paper describes splitting them into blocks of size $B_r \times d$ or $B_c \times d$, depending on the dimension being tiled, and quantizing each block separately.

The intuition is that values within a smaller block are more likely to have a similar scale than values across the whole tensor. By reducing the scope of each scale, block quantization makes the quantizer more adaptive. A large outlier in one block no longer needs to distort the representation of values in another block. This is exactly the kind of locality-aware design that fits the paper’s broader philosophy for FlashAttention-3.

The paper also points out that block quantization can be fused with an operation that happens right before attention, such as rotary embedding, with no additional slowdown. This is important because it means the accuracy improvement does not necessarily come with a separate overhead step. The quantization can be inserted into existing preprocessing work rather than added as a new cost.

# How block quantization aligns naturally with tiled attention

A key reason block quantization works well in FlashAttention-3 is that the attention algorithm itself is already tiled. FlashAttention processes the matrices in blocks rather than materializing the full attention matrix in high precision. The paper exploits this fact directly: because the algorithm naturally operates on blocks, it is easy to attach a separate scale to each block.

This alignment is more than a convenience. It means the same block structure used to improve memory efficiency can also be used to improve numerical fidelity. The paper emphasizes that the algorithm can scale each block of the score matrix $\mathbf{S}$ to account for block quantization “at no computation cost.” In other words, the blockwise organization of the algorithm allows the quantization correction to be absorbed into the existing tiled processing logic.

This is an important design principle in the paper: accuracy improvements are most effective when they are integrated into the same tiled pipeline that already defines the kernel. Rather than treating quantization as an external preprocessing step, FlashAttention-3 makes it part of the attention computation itself.

# Scaling score blocks without extra compute cost

The paper explains that once $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ are block quantized, the score blocks in attention can be adjusted accordingly. Since attention computes scores through $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, the effect of the per-block quantization scales must be reflected in the score computation. The paper states that this can be done without extra computation cost because the algorithm already processes the matrices block by block.

This is an important subtlety. The paper is not merely proposing a more accurate way to store inputs; it is showing how the blockwise structure of the entire attention kernel lets the scale factors be handled naturally inside the computation. This makes the method practical, because accuracy improvements that require major extra work would be far less attractive in a performance-critical kernel.

# Incoherent processing as outlier spreading

Block quantization improves the scale granularity, but the paper also introduces a second technique called incoherent processing. The goal here is to reduce the impact of outliers before quantization happens. The paper’s idea is to multiply $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ prior to quantization. This does not change the attention output, but it changes how the information is distributed across coordinates.

The paper describes this as “spreading out” the outliers. Instead of a few coordinates carrying very large values, the transformed vectors become sums of many entries, so the magnitude is more evenly distributed across dimensions. This makes quantization easier because the extreme values are less concentrated in specific positions. The result is lower quantization error after FP8 rounding.

The term “incoherent processing” reflects this goal of making the representation less aligned with the original outlier structure. The transform does not remove information; it redistributes it so that the FP8 representation can preserve it more faithfully.

# Orthogonal transforms that preserve $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$

A crucial property of the incoherent-processing transform is that it preserves the attention scores. The paper uses the fact that $\mathbf{M}$ is orthogonal, so $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = I$. Therefore,
\[
(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top\mathbf{K}^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top.
\]
This means multiplying both $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by the same orthogonal matrix changes the intermediate representation but not the final dot products that drive attention.

This identity is the mathematical reason incoherent processing is safe. The paper is careful to preserve the exact attention computation while changing only the coordinate system in which the tensors are represented. That is why the method can improve numerical behavior without altering the model’s output in principle.

# Using random diagonal sign matrices and Hadamard transforms

The paper follows prior work and chooses $\mathbf{M}$ to be a product of random diagonal matrices of $\pm 1$ and a Hadamard matrix. This choice is important because it gives the benefits of a random orthogonal transform while remaining computationally efficient. The paper notes that this form of transform can be multiplied in $O(d \log d)$ time rather than $O(d^2)$ time.

The random diagonal sign matrices introduce randomness in the signs of coordinates, and the Hadamard transform mixes the values across dimensions. Together, they provide a structured way to spread out the outliers. The paper’s emphasis is not merely on randomness for its own sake, but on using a transform that is both orthogonal and fast enough to be practical in a GPU kernel.

This specific construction is also convenient for integration with the rest of the attention pipeline. The paper points out that it can be fused with rotary embedding at no extra computation cost, which helps keep the overall method efficient.

# Fast $O(d \log d)$ incoherent transforms

One reason the paper chooses the random-sign-plus-Hadamard construction is that it is much faster than a dense orthogonal transform. A general orthogonal matrix would be too expensive to apply inside an attention kernel, especially in a setting where performance matters. The Hadamard-based approach keeps the transform lightweight enough to be used alongside FP8 attention.

The paper frames this efficiency as necessary for practical deployment. Incoherent processing is not meant to be a separate expensive preprocessing stage; it is meant to be a low-overhead modification that supports the FP8 pipeline. The $O(d \log d)$ complexity is what makes that possible.

This efficiency also matters because the paper’s broader contribution is about using hardware aggressively. Since FlashAttention-3 already aims to maximize throughput through asynchronous execution and tiling, the accuracy-preserving transform must fit into that same high-performance design philosophy.

# Fusion opportunities with rotary embedding

The paper notes that both block quantization and incoherent processing can be fused with rotary embedding. This is significant because rotary embedding is already a preprocessing step in many attention pipelines. By fusing the FP8-related transforms with it, FlashAttention-3 avoids introducing an extra pass over the data.

The paper repeatedly emphasizes this kind of fusion as a practical advantage. In a memory-bound setting, any additional kernel or additional movement of data can be costly. Fusing the transform with an existing step reduces that overhead and helps the FP8 path remain efficient.

This also reinforces the paper’s overall methodology: numerical accuracy is improved not by adding isolated corrective stages, but by integrating the corrections into the existing structure of the attention computation.

# Why these methods improve FP8 attention accuracy

The paper’s overall argument is that FP8 attention becomes accurate when two sources of error are addressed together. First, block quantization reduces the damage done by using a single scale for a large tensor. By assigning a scale to each block, the method adapts to local variation and keeps outliers from dominating the entire tensor’s representation. Second, incoherent processing reduces the concentration of outliers by redistributing the energy across dimensions before quantization.

These methods are complementary. Block quantization improves the granularity of scaling, while incoherent processing makes the values easier to quantize in the first place. The paper validates their combined effect experimentally and reports that FP8 FlashAttention-3 is up to $2.6\times$ more accurate than a baseline FP8 attention method that uses per-tensor quantization in the presence of outlier features.

The key takeaway is that low precision does not have to imply poor accuracy, but it does require algorithmic support. In FlashAttention-3, the paper uses the structure of tiled attention and the mathematical invariance of orthogonal transforms to make FP8 practical for attention while preserving much better numerical behavior than a naive baseline.

11. FlashAttention-3 Backward Pass and Training Considerations

# Why backward attention matters for training efficiency

Although much of the paper focuses on the forward pass, the backward pass is equally important in training because it is part of every gradient step. In a Transformer, attention is not just used to produce outputs during inference; it also needs to propagate gradients back through the query, key, and value projections during optimization. That means any speedup in backward attention directly affects overall training throughput, not just inference latency.

The paper frames attention as a bottleneck because self-attention has quadratic scaling in sequence length, and this cost appears in both the forward and backward passes. As sequence lengths grow, the cost of training becomes increasingly dominated by attention computation, so improvements to backward attention are necessary if one wants the speed benefits of FlashAttention-style kernels to translate into practical training gains. This is why the paper reports not only forward performance but also backward performance and numerical behavior.

# Relationship between forward and backward attention computations

The paper gives the standard forward formulas for a single head as
$\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, and $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$.
The backward pass is then derived by applying the chain rule to these same quantities. Given the output gradient $\mathbf{dO}$, the gradients are computed as
$\mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO}$,
$\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO}\mathbf{V}^\top$,
$\mathbf{dS} = \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP})$,
$\mathbf{dQ} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}\mathbf{K}$, and
$\mathbf{dK} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}^\top \mathbf{Q}$.

This structure reveals an important point: backward attention is not a completely separate computation, but rather a reuse of the same ingredients that appear in the forward pass. The same matrices $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{V}$, and the softmax probabilities $\mathbf{P}$ all play a role, and the backward computation is organized around matrix multiplications and the derivative of softmax. In the paper’s background section, the derivative of softmax is written row-wise as
$\mathbf{d}s = (\mathrm{diag}(p) - p p^\top)\mathbf{d}p$
for a vector $p = \mathrm{softmax}(s)$.

Because the forward and backward passes share this structure, the same kinds of GPU-aware design choices matter for both. In particular, the paper’s emphasis on minimizing slow global memory traffic and exploiting asynchronous execution is relevant not only to producing the forward output, but also to efficiently moving through the gradient computations needed during training.

# Recomputation trade-offs in FlashAttention-style kernels

The paper explicitly notes that the backward-pass FLOPs are larger than those of the forward pass because of recomputation. In the benchmarking section, the backward FLOPs are computed as $2.5$ times the forward FLOPs, since there are $2$ matmuls in the forward pass and $5$ matmuls in the backward pass, due to recomputation.

This recomputation is a key training-time trade-off. Rather than saving all intermediate attention matrices in high-bandwidth memory, FlashAttention-style kernels reorganize the computation so that they can recover what they need during backward execution. The paper’s broader motivation is to reduce reads and writes to slow global memory, and this same philosophy extends to training. Recomputation increases arithmetic work, but it is often a favorable trade if it avoids materializing large intermediate matrices such as $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$.

The paper’s description of standard attention makes clear what is being avoided: in standard implementations, the intermediate matrices are materialized to HBM. FlashAttention instead uses a local softmax reduction and fuses the attention operations into a single kernel. In training, the backward pass must still recover the needed quantities, but the design goal remains the same: preserve efficiency by carefully balancing recomputation against memory traffic. This is why the paper treats recomputation as part of the performance story rather than as an incidental detail.

# Backward-pass matmul structure versus forward-pass structure

The forward pass consists of two matrix multiplications surrounding the softmax: first $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, then $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. The paper’s FP16 algorithm is organized around this two-GEMM structure, and its performance improvements come from overlapping these GEMMs with softmax work using asynchronous hardware features.

The backward pass has a different algebraic structure. Instead of two matmuls in the simple forward description, the paper notes that the backward pass involves five matmuls due to recomputation. This means that backward attention is even more matmul-heavy than the forward pass. That matters because the paper’s hardware discussion shows that matmul throughput on Hopper is far higher than the throughput of special functions such as exponential, but it also means that the backward pass offers more opportunity for throughput-oriented scheduling when the kernel is designed well.

At the same time, the different structure of backward computation means that the exact forward-pass pipeline cannot be copied over without modification. The paper does not fully spell out the backward algorithm in the main text, but it does state that the backward pass is described separately and that the reported backward performance is strong. The key conceptual point is that both passes are based on tiled GEMM and softmax-like operations, yet the backward pass includes additional gradient-specific matmuls and recomputation steps that make it computationally heavier.

# Reported backward speedups of FlashAttention-3 over FlashAttention-2

The paper reports that FlashAttention-3 achieves a backward-pass speedup of about $1.5$ to $1.75\times$ over FlashAttention-2 on the H100 SXM5 GPU. This is slightly smaller than the forward-pass speedup, which is reported as about $1.5$ to $2.0\times$, but it is still a substantial improvement.

These speedups are important because training time is determined by both passes. A kernel that accelerates only inference would be incomplete for training workloads, but the reported backward gains show that the benefits of FlashAttention-3 extend beyond the forward path. The paper presents these numbers as evidence that the algorithmic changes—warp-specialization, asynchronous overlap, and Hopper-specific execution choices—are useful not just for producing outputs quickly, but for reducing the cost of gradient computation as well.

The paper also emphasizes that these measurements were taken over a range of sequence lengths and attention settings. That matters because training workloads often involve different context lengths and head dimensions, and the reported backward speedups indicate that the design is robust enough to provide consistent benefits across those settings.

# How kernel design choices affect training throughput

The paper’s central message is that training throughput is shaped not only by the mathematical form of attention, but by how that computation is mapped onto GPU hardware. Three design choices in particular matter.

First, producer-consumer asynchrony through warp-specialization helps hide memory and instruction issue latency. The paper explains that warps can be divided into producers and consumers, with producers handling data movement and consumers handling computation. This makes it easier to overlap loading with useful work. In training, where attention kernels are repeatedly executed, better latency hiding can have an outsized impact on total step time.

Second, the paper’s pingpong scheduling overlaps the softmax computation of one warpgroup with GEMM execution in another warpgroup. Since exponentials and other softmax-related operations have much lower throughput than matmuls, scheduling them to run concurrently with Tensor Core work improves hardware utilization. The paper reports that this kind of algorithmic reordering improved performance in the forward pass, and the same general principle is relevant to training throughput more broadly.

Third, the paper’s Hopper-specific use of asynchronous WGMMA and TMA improves the ability to overlap computation and data movement. This is especially important for attention, where the kernel repeatedly moves blocks of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ while also performing matrix multiplications. Because training invokes both forward and backward kernels, the benefits of this scheduling discipline compound over many iterations.

# What remains challenging for low-precision training-time attention

The paper makes clear that low precision introduces additional difficulty, especially for training. It notes that low precision such as FP8 is attractive because it can provide higher throughput, but the technical challenge is to minimize quantization error, particularly in the presence of outlier features in large language models.

For the FP8 version of FlashAttention-3, the paper addresses this with block quantization and incoherent processing. Block quantization uses one scale per block rather than one scale per tensor, and incoherent processing multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix before quantization to spread out outliers. These techniques reduce numerical error in the forward pass, and the paper validates that FP8 FlashAttention-3 is $2.6\times$ more accurate than a baseline FP8 attention method with per-tensor quantization.

However, the paper’s discussion also makes clear that low-precision training-time attention remains challenging. FP8 requires careful handling of layout constraints, additional transformations between accumulator and operand layouts, and accuracy-preserving quantization strategies. The paper also notes limitations for low-precision use: for benchmarks, FP16 FlashAttention-3 uses a persistent kernel and load balancing strategy, whereas FP8 FlashAttention-3 does not, which partly explains weaker performance for small sequence lengths and causal masking compared to cuDNN FP8 kernels. The discussion also points to open questions about the effects of low-precision attention in large-scale training.

So, while FlashAttention-3 shows that low-precision attention can be fast and accurate, the paper does not present low-precision training as a solved problem. Instead, it treats the FP8 result as a major step forward that still leaves room for further work, especially in the training regime where both performance and numerical stability must be maintained across many iterations.

12. Benchmarking Methodology and Performance Results

# Implementation with CUTLASS primitives

The paper validates \textsc{FlashAttention-3} by implementing it with the low-level primitives provided by CUTLASS, including the Hopper-specific abstractions for WGMMA and TMA. This choice is important because the algorithm is designed around Hopper’s asynchronous execution features, so the implementation needs direct access to those hardware-aware building blocks rather than a generic matrix-multiplication interface.

Using CUTLASS lets the authors express the kernel in terms of the same kinds of operations discussed in the algorithm section: asynchronous matrix multiplication, asynchronous data movement, and the careful coordination of producer and consumer roles. In other words, the empirical results are not based on an abstract simulation of the algorithm, but on a concrete GPU implementation that maps the paper’s proposed scheduling and layout ideas onto actual Hopper execution mechanisms.

# Hardware platform: H100 80GB SXM5

All of the main benchmarking results are reported on an NVIDIA H100 80GB SXM5 GPU. This is the specific Hopper platform used throughout the paper to evaluate whether the new asynchronous and low-precision techniques translate into real performance gains on modern hardware.

The paper uses this platform because Hopper is precisely the architecture for which the algorithm was designed. The H100 exposes the relevant Tensor Core and TMA features, and the paper’s central claim is that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} better exploits these capabilities than \textsc{FlashAttention-2}. The reported utilization numbers and throughput values are therefore tied to this hardware environment, rather than to a general theoretical model.

# Baselines: PyTorch standard attention, FlashAttention-2, Triton FlashAttention-2, and cuDNN

The main comparisons in the paper are against four baselines. The first is a standard attention implementation in PyTorch, which materializes intermediate attention matrices and serves as the conventional reference point. The second is \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, the immediate predecessor method. The third is \textsc{FlashAttention-2} implemented in Triton, which the paper notes uses H100-specific instructions. The fourth is a vendor implementation of attention from NVIDIA’s cuDNN library, which is presented as a strong optimized baseline for H100.

These comparisons are designed to separate different questions. Comparing against standard PyTorch attention shows the benefit of the overall FlashAttention approach. Comparing against \textsc{FlashAttention-2} isolates the effect of the new algorithmic and hardware-aware ideas in \textsc{FlashAttention-3}. Comparing against Triton and cuDNN shows whether the method is competitive with other highly optimized Hopper-targeted implementations, not just with earlier academic kernels.

# Benchmark settings: sequence lengths, batch sizes, hidden size, head dimensions, and masking

The paper benchmarks attention over sequence lengths from 512 up to 16k, including intermediate values such as 1k and above. The batch size is chosen so that the total number of tokens is 16k. The hidden dimension is fixed at 2048. The head dimension is varied among 64, 128, and 256, which correspond respectively to 32, 16, and 8 heads.

The benchmarks are run both without masking and with causal masking. This matters because masking changes the amount of useful work in attention, and the paper reports results under both settings to show how the kernels behave in realistic transformer configurations. The paper also evaluates FP8 forward-pass performance under similar settings, with a detailed figure for head dimension 256 and fuller results reported separately.

# How forward-pass FLOPs are computed

To compare performance across configurations, the paper computes forward-pass FLOPs using the formula

$$4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head dimension} \cdot \text{number of heads}.$$

This formula reflects the quadratic dependence of attention on sequence length and scales with both the per-head feature dimension and the number of heads. It provides a common accounting convention for converting runtime measurements into throughput in TFLOPs/s.

The use of this formula is central to the paper’s performance claims, because the throughput numbers are not just raw runtimes. They are normalized by the estimated work done, allowing the authors to say how efficiently the GPU is being used for a given attention problem size.

# How causal masking changes effective FLOPs

When causal masking is enabled, the paper divides the forward-pass FLOP count by 2. The reason given is that approximately only half of the attention entries are computed in the causal case. Since causal attention only permits each token to attend to earlier positions, the effective amount of work is lower than in the full dense case.

This adjustment matters for interpreting the throughput numbers fairly. Without it, causal and non-causal runs would not be measured against the same effective workload. The paper therefore changes the FLOP accounting to reflect the reduced computation implied by the causal structure.

# How backward-pass FLOPs are estimated

For the backward pass, the paper estimates FLOPs by multiplying the forward-pass FLOPs by 2.5. The explanation given is that the backward computation involves 5 matmuls compared to 2 matmuls in the forward pass, due to recomputation.

This is a deliberately simple accounting rule that lets the authors convert backward-pass runtime into a throughput number comparable to the forward-pass metric. The exact factor is not meant to be a universal property of attention in all settings; rather, it is the paper’s practical estimate for the benchmark configurations under study.

# Forward-pass speedups of FlashAttention-3 in FP16

The paper reports that in FP16, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is about 1.5--2.0$\times$ faster than \textsc{FlashAttention-2} in the forward pass. In the detailed benchmark plots, the method is also shown to be up to 3--16$\times$ faster than a standard attention implementation, depending on the sequence length and configuration.

These improvements are consistent with the paper’s algorithmic story: warp-specialization helps hide memory and issue latency, and the overlapping of softmax with WGMMA reduces idle time inside the kernel. The benchmarking results therefore serve as empirical evidence that the asynchronous scheduling ideas do in fact translate into real speedup on Hopper.

# Backward-pass speedups of FlashAttention-3 in FP16

The paper also reports backward-pass gains in FP16. Here, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is about 1.5--1.75$\times$ faster than \textsc{FlashAttention-2}. This is an important result because the backward pass is a major part of training cost, and a speedup in the backward pass indicates that the benefits of the new kernel are not limited to inference-like forward computation.

Although the paper focuses its algorithmic exposition primarily on the forward pass, the backward result shows that the overall approach is broadly useful for transformer training workloads as well. The improvement is smaller than in some forward settings, but it is still substantial and consistent across the tested range.

# Peak throughput results: up to 740 TFLOPs/s and 75% utilization

One of the headline findings is that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} reaches up to 740 TFLOPs/s in FP16 on the H100 SXM5. The paper describes this as about 75\% utilization of the theoretical maximum TFLOPs/s on the GPU.

This is significant because the abstract and introduction emphasize that previous methods were leaving a large amount of Hopper’s capability unused. Reaching 740 TFLOPs/s suggests that the new asynchronous design closes much of that gap. The throughput number is therefore not only a speed claim, but also a utilization claim: the kernel is making much better use of the available Tensor Core performance.

# FP8 throughput results approaching 1.2 PFLOPs/s

The paper also evaluates the FP8 version of \textsc{FlashAttention-3}. In this setting, the kernel reaches close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s. This is one of the clearest demonstrations of the benefit of Hopper’s low-precision hardware support, because FP8 nearly doubles the throughput relative to FP16 for the GEMM-heavy parts of the kernel.

The paper ties this result to the FP8 Tensor Cores and to the algorithmic changes needed to use them correctly. The throughput increase is not obtained simply by changing the data type; it depends on layout transformations, in-kernel transposes, and the quantization strategies described earlier in the paper. The benchmark confirms that these ingredients work together to produce very high measured performance.

# Comparisons with vendor-optimized cuDNN attention

The paper also compares \textsc{FlashAttention-3} with a vendor-optimized attention implementation from cuDNN. The main conclusion is that for large sequence lengths, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} in FP16 outperforms this cuDNN implementation, and FP8 is competitive with it.

This is an important result because cuDNN is not an academic baseline; it is a highly optimized vendor library designed for NVIDIA hardware. The fact that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} can match or exceed it indicates that the paper’s algorithmic restructuring is competitive even against production-quality kernels.

# When FlashAttention-3 wins most clearly: medium and long sequences

The paper emphasizes that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is especially strong for medium and long sequences. In the benchmark section, the authors note that for sequence lengths of 1k and above, the method can surpass the cuDNN implementation. The overall speedups compared with \textsc{FlashAttention-2} are also reported across a wide range of sequence lengths, but the gains become most meaningful as the sequence length grows.

This pattern is consistent with the motivation for the work. Attention becomes more expensive as sequence length increases, so better scheduling and better use of Hopper’s asynchronous hardware matter more in the long-context regime. The benchmarks therefore support the paper’s central thesis: that careful algorithm-hardware co-design produces the biggest payoff when attention is the dominant bottleneck.

13. Ablation Studies: Why the Speedup Happens

# Purpose of ablation studies in systems and ML papers

Ablation studies are the part of an experimental section where the authors deliberately turn off or isolate individual design choices to see how much each one contributes to the final result. In a systems paper like this one, that matters because performance improvements often come from several ideas working together: better parallelization, better use of hardware instructions, reduced memory traffic, or improved numerical formats. If one only reports the final speedup, it is hard to tell which idea actually mattered and whether the gains came from a single major change or from many smaller ones.

In \textsc{FlashAttention-3}, the ablation study serves exactly this purpose. The paper does not merely claim that the new algorithm is faster than \textsc{FlashAttention-2}; it also shows that the speedup comes from the specific mechanisms introduced in the paper, especially warp-specialization and the 2-stage GEMM-softmax pipeline. This is important for understanding the paper as a research contribution. The ablation results show that the algorithmic redesign is not just an implementation detail, but a direct source of the observed throughput improvement.

Ablation studies are also useful because they connect the architecture-level story to the measured numbers. The paper’s broader argument is that Hopper’s asynchronous Tensor Cores and TMA can be exploited by rethinking the execution schedule of attention. The ablation study tests whether that reasoning is correct. In other words, it checks whether the claimed overlap between memory movement, GEMM, and softmax is actually responsible for the gain that is observed in practice.

# Experimental configuration for the paper’s ablation

The paper performs the ablation study on non-causal FP16 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} with a fixed configuration: batch size $4$, sequence length $8448$, number of heads $16$, and head dimension $128$. These fixed settings matter because they control the workload so that the comparison focuses on algorithmic differences rather than changes in the model shape or input size. By holding the problem size constant, the authors can compare versions of the kernel more fairly.

The study specifically examines the effects of two ideas: warp-specialization and 2-stage WGMMA-softmax pipelining. The benchmark is designed to determine how much performance each contributes when applied to the forward pass. The paper reports performance in TFLOPs/s, which is a useful summary because attention is dominated by matrix multiplication work, and the GPU’s effectiveness can be described in terms of how much of its theoretical compute capability is being used.

This setting also matters because the paper’s kernel is built around a block-structured execution model. The sequence length is large enough that there are many blocks, so there is enough opportunity for overlapping operations and hiding latencies. That makes the benchmark suitable for exposing the practical value of the scheduling changes introduced by \textsc{FlashAttention-3}.

# Isolating the effect of warp specialization

Warp-specialization is one of the main algorithmic ideas in the paper. The basic idea is to split the roles within a CTA into producer warps and consumer warps. The producer warps handle data movement, such as moving $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ tiles into shared memory using TMA, while the consumer warps perform the compute-heavy parts, namely the GEMMs and softmax-related work. The goal is to let these activities proceed asynchronously instead of forcing the whole CTA to wait for one step before starting the next.

The reason this helps is that different hardware units operate independently. On Hopper, TMA handles data movement and WGMMA handles matrix multiplication, while the remaining CUDA core activity can proceed in parallel where possible. Warp-specialization makes it easier for the compiler and hardware to schedule these different kinds of work efficiently, because the warps are no longer all trying to do the same thing at once. Some warps can stay focused on issuing loads, while others stay focused on computation.

In the paper’s ablation discussion, warp-specialization is treated as one of the two major ingredients behind the speedup. The point is not simply that the kernel becomes “more parallel” in an abstract sense. Rather, the structure of the code is changed so that memory movement and compute are issued by different warps, which improves the ability to hide latency. The paper emphasizes that this mechanism helps the algorithm exploit Hopper’s asynchronous execution model, especially when combined with register reallocation through $\verb|setmaxnreg|$ and a circular shared-memory buffer.

# Isolating the effect of 2-stage GEMM-softmax pipelining

The second major ablation target is the 2-stage GEMM-softmax pipeline. In the base warp-specialized version, softmax and the two GEMMs in each iteration are still largely serialized: the algorithm computes $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, then performs softmax work, and only after that computes $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. That structure leaves some performance on the table because the softmax computations are much lower throughput than the matrix multiplications.

The paper’s key observation is that the second GEMM of one iteration can be overlapped with the softmax computation of the next iteration. This is made possible because WGMMA is asynchronous on Hopper. In the 2-stage pipeline, the algorithm keeps one block of scores in registers while beginning the next matrix multiplication, allowing the softmax for one tile to run while the Tensor Cores work on the next tile. The paper explains this as a way to hide the relatively slow non-GEMM operations under the much more expensive matmul work.

This pipelining is more subtle than simple loop unrolling. The algorithm has to manage dependencies carefully, since softmax depends on the scores, and the output GEMM depends on the softmax result. The paper’s solution is to carry intermediate results across iterations using additional register buffers, so that one stage can be computing on the “current” tile while another stage has already issued the next WGMMA. The ablation study therefore tests whether this overlapping really produces a measurable benefit, rather than just increasing code complexity.

# Measured improvement from 570 to 661 TFLOPs

The paper reports that, in the ablation study, the throughput increases from $570$ TFLOPs/s to $661$ TFLOPs/s when the algorithmic improvements are enabled. This is a substantial gain, and it is important because it shows that the speedup is not only visible in relative terms against older baselines, but also within the design space of the new method itself.

The numbers support the paper’s claim that the performance improvement comes from the combination of warp-specialization and GEMM-softmax overlap. The increase is not a small tuning effect; it indicates that the scheduling changes materially improve how the GPU is utilized. Since attention is a latency-sensitive kernel with a mix of heavy compute and lighter scalar work, even moderate reductions in idle time can produce a meaningful throughput increase.

It is also worth noting that the ablation is performed on a specific fixed configuration, so the result should be interpreted as evidence about that workload class rather than a universal constant. Still, the paper uses this result to support a broader conclusion: Hopper’s asynchronous execution features matter, and when they are exploited carefully, they can produce a visible throughput improvement over a synchronous design.

# How overlap and asynchrony contribute jointly

A central lesson of the ablation study is that the gains do not come from one mechanism in isolation. Warp-specialization by itself improves the organization of work across the CTA, but the larger benefit appears when asynchrony is used to overlap different kinds of operations. Likewise, GEMM-softmax pipelining is only possible because the hardware can issue WGMMA asynchronously, instead of blocking the entire consumer path until each matmul completes.

This is why the paper frames the improvement as a joint effect of overlap and asynchrony. The producer warps keep the pipeline fed with data, while the consumer warps can proceed in a staged fashion. Then, within the consumer path, the algorithm hides softmax work behind the next matmul. The two ideas reinforce each other: warp-specialization creates the structural separation needed for clean scheduling, and asynchronous WGMMA allows the schedule to actually overlap useful work.

For students reading the paper, this is an important systems principle. The speedup is not just about reducing arithmetic operations, and it is not only about reducing memory traffic. It is about organizing the kernel so that the GPU’s specialized functional units are doing useful work at the same time. The ablation study is the evidence that this organization matters in practice.

# Interpreting utilization improvements beyond raw runtime numbers

The paper does not just emphasize lower runtime; it also reports higher TFLOPs/s and better utilization of the GPU. This distinction matters. Runtime is the end result, but utilization tells us *why* the runtime improved. If a kernel reaches only a small fraction of the hardware’s theoretical compute capability, then the bottleneck may lie in memory stalls, instruction scheduling, or idle periods between operations rather than in raw arithmetic throughput.

The paper notes in the abstract that \textsc{FlashAttention-2} achieved only $35\%$ utilization on the H100 GPU, while \textsc{FlashAttention-3} reaches up to $75\%$ utilization for FP16. The ablation study helps explain this gap. By overlapping TMA loads with computation through warp-specialization, and by overlapping softmax with asynchronous WGMMA, the kernel spends less time waiting and more time using the Tensor Cores.

This is why raw TFLOPs/s should be interpreted together with the paper’s scheduling story. A higher TFLOPs/s value suggests that the compute units are being kept busier, but the ablation shows that this comes from reducing exposed latency. In other words, the utilization improvement is evidence that the kernel is better matched to the hardware execution model. For a reader learning to interpret systems papers, that is the key lesson: performance gains are often best understood as a result of better overlap, better scheduling, and better use of asynchronous hardware features, not just as a single speed number.

14. Numerical Accuracy Evaluation

# Why numerical error matters for attention kernels

The paper emphasizes that improving attention speed is not the only goal when designing a new kernel. Attention is used inside Transformer models, and if a faster implementation changes the computed values too much, then the speedup may come at the cost of degraded model behavior. This concern becomes especially important in low-precision settings, where arithmetic is intentionally compressed to gain throughput. In \textsc{FlashAttention-3}, the authors therefore treat numerical accuracy as a first-class evaluation criterion alongside performance.

This is particularly relevant because the paper’s main algorithmic advances target Hopper GPUs and include an FP8 implementation. FP8 provides much higher throughput, but it also increases the risk of quantization error. The authors frame their accuracy study as a way to confirm that the performance improvements do not come from sacrificing too much numerical fidelity. In other words, the kernel should still behave like attention, not just like a fast approximation that happens to run on the same inputs.

# Using FP64 attention as a reference

To evaluate accuracy, the paper compares \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, \textsc{FlashAttention-3}, and a standard attention implementation against a reference implementation in FP64. This reference serves as the high-precision baseline from which numerical error is measured. Using FP64 is a standard way to isolate the effect of finite-precision computation: if a method differs from the FP64 result, that difference can be attributed to numerical error introduced by the algorithm and datatype choices.

The paper does not present FP64 as a practical deployment format; rather, it is used as the anchor for the error analysis. By comparing all methods to the same reference, the authors can make a controlled statement about how much precision is lost in each implementation. This is especially useful because the different attention kernels vary not only in precision but also in how intermediate values are stored and rescaled.

# Synthetic outlier distribution used to stress-test quantization

To simulate the kinds of outlier features and activations that appear in large language models, the paper constructs a synthetic distribution for the entries of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$. Each entry is sampled from

$$
\mathcal{N}(0, 1) + \mathcal{N}(0, 100) \cdot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001).
$$

This means that most entries come from a normal distribution with standard deviation $1$, but with probability $0.001$ an additional independent term with much larger variance is added. The effect is that a tiny fraction of entries become much larger in magnitude than the rest. The paper uses this setup to mimic the “outlier” behavior that makes quantization difficult in real large models.

The reason this is a meaningful stress test is that quantization schemes often work well when values are relatively uniform, but can behave poorly when a few extreme values dominate the dynamic range. By injecting rare large-magnitude values, the authors can measure whether their FP8 method remains stable under conditions that resemble the difficult cases encountered in practice.

# RMSE as the reported accuracy metric

The paper reports root mean squared error, or RMSE, as the main numerical accuracy metric. RMSE compares the computed output from each attention implementation to the FP64 reference and summarizes the average magnitude of the error. A lower RMSE indicates that the output is closer to the high-precision baseline.

This choice is appropriate for the paper’s goal because it provides a direct scalar summary of output deviation. Rather than focusing on a single element or a qualitative comparison, RMSE measures the overall discrepancy across the full attention output. The paper uses this metric in its table of numerical error results to compare standard attention, \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, and \textsc{FlashAttention-3} under both FP16 and FP8 settings.

# Why FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3 improve FP16 accuracy over standard attention

In FP16, the paper reports that both \textsc{FlashAttention-2} and \textsc{FlashAttention-3} achieve $1.7\times$ lower RMSE than the standard attention implementation. The key reason given is that intermediate results, especially in the softmax computation, are kept in FP32. This matters because the softmax is numerically sensitive: rescaling and exponentiation can amplify small errors if the intermediate values are stored in lower precision.

The standard attention implementation materializes intermediate matrices and does not benefit from the same fused structure. In contrast, the FlashAttention family performs the attention computation in a fused way that preserves higher-precision intermediate values where they matter most. As a result, the paper concludes that FlashAttention’s design is not only faster but also more numerically stable in FP16 than the standard baseline.

The important point is that this accuracy gain is not attributed to the new Hopper-specific asynchrony in \textsc{FlashAttention-3}. Instead, the paper states that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} matches the FP16 numerical error of \textsc{FlashAttention-2}. The accuracy improvement comes from the FlashAttention approach itself, especially from the way it handles intermediate computations.

# Role of FP32 intermediates in stable softmax computation

A central reason for the strong FP16 accuracy results is that intermediate softmax-related computations are kept in FP32. The paper specifically points to the softmax rescaling steps as examples of quantities that remain in higher precision. This matters because softmax involves exponentials and normalization, both of which can be sensitive to rounding and underflow or overflow if handled too aggressively in low precision.

In the paper’s framing, maintaining FP32 intermediates allows the kernel to compute the local softmax more stably while still using FP16 inputs and outputs. This is one reason that both FlashAttention methods outperform the standard attention implementation in numerical error. The authors present this as a design advantage of the FlashAttention family: it reduces memory traffic while also improving the stability of the core attention computation.

# FP8 baseline with per-tensor scaling

For FP8, the paper compares \textsc{FlashAttention-3} against a baseline attention implementation that uses per-tensor scaling. In that baseline, a single scaling factor is used for each tensor, such as one for $\mathbf{Q}$, one for $\mathbf{K}$, and one for $\mathbf{V}$. The paper notes that this is the standard way to manage FP8 quantization, but it can be vulnerable when the tensor contains outlier values.

The baseline also keeps the matmul accumulator in FP32 and the intermediate softmax results in FP16. Even with these protections, the paper reports that the baseline is less accurate than \textsc{FlashAttention-3} in the presence of outliers. This shows that the main challenge in FP8 is not simply having a high-precision accumulator; it is also the quality of the quantization strategy used for the inputs.

# Accuracy gains from block quantization and incoherent processing

To reduce FP8 error, the paper introduces two techniques: block quantization and incoherent processing. Block quantization replaces a single scale per tensor with one scale per block. Instead of quantizing all of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, or $\mathbf{V}$ using one global scale, the tensor is split into blocks of size $B_r \times d$ or $B_c \times d$, and each block is quantized separately. The authors note that this fits naturally with the block structure of \textsc{FlashAttention-3}, so the required scaling can be incorporated without additional computation cost.

The second technique, incoherent processing, multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ before quantization. Because $\mathbf{M}$ is orthogonal, multiplying both sides by $\mathbf{M}$ does not change the attention scores:

$$
(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top.
$$

The purpose of this transformation is to “spread out” outliers so that no single coordinate dominates the quantization error. The paper follows prior work in choosing $\mathbf{M}$ as a product of random diagonal sign matrices and a Hadamard matrix, which can be applied efficiently. These two techniques together make FP8 quantization much more robust in the presence of large-magnitude entries.

# The reported 2.6x reduction in FP8 numerical error

The paper’s main numerical accuracy claim for FP8 is that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is $2.6\times$ more accurate than the baseline FP8 attention with per-tensor quantization. This improvement is reported in the RMSE results and is attributed specifically to block quantization and incoherent processing. The synthetic outlier experiment is designed to make this comparison meaningful, since the outlier-heavy input distribution is exactly where quantization methods differ most.

This result is important because it shows that FP8 does not have to be synonymous with poor accuracy. The paper does not claim that FP8 matches FP16 in error, but it does show that careful algorithmic design can significantly narrow the gap relative to a straightforward per-tensor quantization baseline.

# Practical implications for low-precision deployment

The accuracy results support the paper’s broader claim that low-precision attention can be practical if it is designed with the right numerical safeguards. In FP16, the FlashAttention family already improves accuracy over standard attention by keeping key intermediate values in FP32. In FP8, the more aggressive quantization would normally increase error, but the use of block quantization and incoherent processing substantially reduces that penalty.

For deployment, this means that low-precision attention is not merely a speed optimization. According to the paper, it can be made accurate enough to be a realistic option, especially when paired with techniques that address outlier-heavy data. The overall message of the accuracy study is that performance and numerical fidelity need not be in opposition: with the right kernel design, the same attention implementation can be both fast and stable.

15. Limitations, Open Problems, and Broader Lessons

# Current limitations for LLM inference workloads

The paper ends by emphasizing that, although FlashAttention-3 substantially improves both speed and accuracy, it is not presented as a complete solution for every attention workload. One explicit limitation is that the authors still hope to optimize the method further for LLM inference. This matters because inference places different demands on the implementation than the benchmark settings emphasized in the paper. Inference workloads often care not just about peak throughput, but also about consistent latency, efficient handling of small or irregular batch sizes, and integration into larger serving systems. The paper’s conclusion makes clear that these aspects remain open.

This limitation is especially important because the paper’s main contribution is to show that careful use of Hopper’s asynchronous execution and FP8 support can materially improve attention performance. However, the authors do not claim that this automatically resolves all practical bottlenecks in deployment settings. Instead, they present FlashAttention-3 as a strong primitive whose design still leaves room for future work aimed specifically at inference-oriented constraints.

# Persistent kernel design in FP16 versus FP8 implementations

A concrete engineering difference highlighted in the paper is that the FP16 and FP8 versions of FlashAttention-3 do not share the same kernel strategy. The authors note that, for their benchmarks, the FP16 version has a persistent kernel and a load balancing strategy, whereas the FP8 version does not. This is a significant distinction because a persistent kernel can help keep the GPU occupied more efficiently by maintaining work on-chip and reducing overheads associated with launching or scheduling many small pieces of work.

The absence of a persistent kernel in FP8 is not framed as a conceptual limitation of FP8 itself, but as a practical implementation limitation in the current system. The paper makes clear that this difference is part of why FP8 FlashAttention-3 does not always match the performance of the best cuDNN kernels in certain regimes. In other words, the FP8 kernel is already benefiting from low-precision acceleration, but it is not yet as fully optimized in all aspects of kernel organization as the FP16 version.

# Why FP8 underperforms in some small-sequence and causal-mask settings

The paper is careful to report that FP8 FlashAttention-3 is not uniformly best across all settings. In particular, for small sequence lengths and in cases with causal masking, the FP8 kernel does not always perform as well as the FP8 cuDNN kernels. The authors connect this partly to the implementation difference just discussed: the lack of a persistent kernel in FP8 FlashAttention-3.

More broadly, this observation reflects an important practical lesson from the paper. High-performance GPU kernels are not improved by one idea alone. The gains from FP8 arithmetic and asynchronous execution still depend on the surrounding kernel structure, including how work is scheduled and how well the implementation handles the amount of available parallelism. For short sequences, there may be less work to amortize overheads, and causal masking can further change the amount and pattern of computation. The paper does not claim to eliminate these effects; instead, it reports them as part of the current performance envelope.

# Load balancing issues in low-precision kernels

The conclusion also points to load balancing as an open engineering issue, especially for FP8. This connects to the observation that the FP16 version already includes a persistent kernel and load balancing strategy, while the FP8 version does not. In a GPU kernel, load balancing refers to how evenly the available work is distributed across execution resources. If some warps or threadblocks finish early while others remain busy, the hardware cannot be used as efficiently as possible.

This matters particularly in the context of the paper because FlashAttention-3 is built around careful coordination among producer and consumer warps, asynchronous loads, and overlapped GEMMs and softmax operations. Once the kernel becomes more complex, ensuring that all of those pieces stay balanced becomes harder. The paper’s discussion suggests that future work could improve FP8 performance by bringing its scheduling and load-balancing machinery closer to the FP16 implementation.

# Open questions about large-scale training with low-precision attention

Another limitation named explicitly by the authors is that they would like to better understand the effects of low-precision attention in large-scale training. This is an important open question because the paper’s accuracy analysis focuses on numerical error under synthetic outlier-heavy inputs and compares against reference and baseline implementations. Those experiments demonstrate that block quantization and incoherent processing significantly reduce error, but they do not fully settle how such methods behave when deployed during full-scale training of large models.

The paper therefore leaves open a broader systems-and-learning question: how does low-precision attention interact with the dynamics of training at scale? Since attention is a central component of Transformer models, any numerical approximation or quantization strategy can potentially affect optimization behavior, stability, or downstream model quality. The paper does not answer that question, but it clearly identifies it as a next step for the field.

# Portability of the techniques beyond Hopper GPUs

Although the implementation and evaluation are grounded in NVIDIA Hopper, the authors explicitly state that the algorithm is operative for any GPU architecture with sufficiently robust asynchronous execution and low-precision capabilities. This is an important broader lesson: the ideas are not tied only to one vendor or one chip generation, even if Hopper provides the concrete feature set used in the paper.

At the same time, the paper’s techniques are deeply shaped by Hopper-specific mechanisms such as TMA, WGMMA, and register reallocation via \verb|setmaxnreg|. That means the exact implementation is not directly portable in a mechanical sense. What is portable is the design philosophy: exploit the hardware’s asynchronous execution model, use low-precision pathways when available, and adapt the kernel structure to match the architecture rather than treating the GPU as a generic black box. The paper’s conclusion suggests that these principles should transfer, even if the details must be rewritten for other accelerators.

# What FlashAttention-3 teaches about co-design across algorithms, compilers, and hardware

One of the strongest broader lessons of the paper is that achieving high performance requires co-design across multiple layers of the stack. FlashAttention-3 is not just a mathematical reformulation of attention, and it is not just a compiler trick. It is an algorithm that is explicitly structured around the GPU execution model, and it depends on an implementation that respects instruction scheduling, memory hierarchy behavior, and low-precision layout constraints.

The paper repeatedly shows that progress comes from making these layers interact well. Warp-specialization matches the division of labor between producers and consumers. Pingpong scheduling uses asynchronous Tensor Core execution to hide softmax work. The FP8 design handles layout constraints by combining in-kernel transposition with register permutation. Even compiler behavior matters, since the authors note that NVCC may reorder instructions and that one must inspect SASS to verify the intended overlap. The lesson is that high-performance ML systems are built by aligning algorithm design, compiler output, and hardware features, not by optimizing any one of them in isolation.

# How faster exact attention may enable future long-context applications

The paper frames its contribution in the context of a much larger goal: making exact attention fast enough to support longer contexts. In the introduction and conclusion, the authors connect efficient attention to applications such as long-document modeling, codebase reasoning, high-resolution images, audio, video, long user histories, and long-horizon agent workflows. The motivation is that attention’s quadratic cost in sequence length is a major barrier to these uses.

FlashAttention-3 does not change the asymptotic complexity of attention, but it makes the exact computation substantially faster and more hardware-efficient. That matters because practical systems often become useful not only when their big-$O$ behavior improves, but when the constant factors become small enough to make large input sizes feasible. The paper’s view is that faster exact attention can unlock longer contexts in situations where approximation would otherwise be required or where existing exact implementations would be too slow.

# Final synthesis of the paper’s contributions and impact

The final synthesis of the paper is that FlashAttention-3 advances the state of the art by combining three ingredients: producer-consumer asynchrony through warp-specialization, overlap of softmax with asynchronous block-wise GEMMs, and hardware-accelerated FP8 computation with block quantization and incoherent processing. The empirical results show that these ideas are not merely elegant in theory; they deliver measured gains on H100 GPUs, including up to $740$ TFLOPs/s in FP16 and close to $1.2$ PFLOPs/s in FP8.

Just as importantly, the paper argues that the significance of this result is broader than one kernel. It shows that modern GPU features can be used to improve both performance and numerical behavior if the algorithm is redesigned around them. The open problems—better inference kernels, improved FP8 load balancing, and deeper understanding of training-time effects—make clear that the story is not finished. But the overall impact is already substantial: FlashAttention-3 demonstrates that exact attention can continue to scale by becoming more deeply integrated with the realities of the hardware on which it runs.
