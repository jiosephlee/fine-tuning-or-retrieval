\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

Chapter 12: 12. Benchmarking Methodology and Performance Results

# Implementation with CUTLASS primitives

The paper validates \textsc{FlashAttention-3} by implementing it with the low-level primitives provided by CUTLASS, including the Hopper-specific abstractions for WGMMA and TMA. This choice is important because the algorithm is designed around Hopper’s asynchronous execution features, so the implementation needs direct access to those hardware-aware building blocks rather than a generic matrix-multiplication interface.

Using CUTLASS lets the authors express the kernel in terms of the same kinds of operations discussed in the algorithm section: asynchronous matrix multiplication, asynchronous data movement, and the careful coordination of producer and consumer roles. In other words, the empirical results are not based on an abstract simulation of the algorithm, but on a concrete GPU implementation that maps the paper’s proposed scheduling and layout ideas onto actual Hopper execution mechanisms.

# Hardware platform: H100 80GB SXM5

All of the main benchmarking results are reported on an NVIDIA H100 80GB SXM5 GPU. This is the specific Hopper platform used throughout the paper to evaluate whether the new asynchronous and low-precision techniques translate into real performance gains on modern hardware.

The paper uses this platform because Hopper is precisely the architecture for which the algorithm was designed. The H100 exposes the relevant Tensor Core and TMA features, and the paper’s central claim is that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} better exploits these capabilities than \textsc{FlashAttention-2}. The reported utilization numbers and throughput values are therefore tied to this hardware environment, rather than to a general theoretical model.

# Baselines: PyTorch standard attention, FlashAttention-2, Triton FlashAttention-2, and cuDNN

The main comparisons in the paper are against four baselines. The first is a standard attention implementation in PyTorch, which materializes intermediate attention matrices and serves as the conventional reference point. The second is \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, the immediate predecessor method. The third is \textsc{FlashAttention-2} implemented in Triton, which the paper notes uses H100-specific instructions. The fourth is a vendor implementation of attention from NVIDIA’s cuDNN library, which is presented as a strong optimized baseline for H100.

These comparisons are designed to separate different questions. Comparing against standard PyTorch attention shows the benefit of the overall FlashAttention approach. Comparing against \textsc{FlashAttention-2} isolates the effect of the new algorithmic and hardware-aware ideas in \textsc{FlashAttention-3}. Comparing against Triton and cuDNN shows whether the method is competitive with other highly optimized Hopper-targeted implementations, not just with earlier academic kernels.

# Benchmark settings: sequence lengths, batch sizes, hidden size, head dimensions, and masking

The paper benchmarks attention over sequence lengths from 512 up to 16k, including intermediate values such as 1k and above. The batch size is chosen so that the total number of tokens is 16k. The hidden dimension is fixed at 2048. The head dimension is varied among 64, 128, and 256, which correspond respectively to 32, 16, and 8 heads.

The benchmarks are run both without masking and with causal masking. This matters because masking changes the amount of useful work in attention, and the paper reports results under both settings to show how the kernels behave in realistic transformer configurations. The paper also evaluates FP8 forward-pass performance under similar settings, with a detailed figure for head dimension 256 and fuller results reported separately.

# How forward-pass FLOPs are computed

To compare performance across configurations, the paper computes forward-pass FLOPs using the formula

$$4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head dimension} \cdot \text{number of heads}.$$

This formula reflects the quadratic dependence of attention on sequence length and scales with both the per-head feature dimension and the number of heads. It provides a common accounting convention for converting runtime measurements into throughput in TFLOPs/s.

The use of this formula is central to the paper’s performance claims, because the throughput numbers are not just raw runtimes. They are normalized by the estimated work done, allowing the authors to say how efficiently the GPU is being used for a given attention problem size.

# How causal masking changes effective FLOPs

When causal masking is enabled, the paper divides the forward-pass FLOP count by 2. The reason given is that approximately only half of the attention entries are computed in the causal case. Since causal attention only permits each token to attend to earlier positions, the effective amount of work is lower than in the full dense case.

This adjustment matters for interpreting the throughput numbers fairly. Without it, causal and non-causal runs would not be measured against the same effective workload. The paper therefore changes the FLOP accounting to reflect the reduced computation implied by the causal structure.

# How backward-pass FLOPs are estimated

For the backward pass, the paper estimates FLOPs by multiplying the forward-pass FLOPs by 2.5. The explanation given is that the backward computation involves 5 matmuls compared to 2 matmuls in the forward pass, due to recomputation.

This is a deliberately simple accounting rule that lets the authors convert backward-pass runtime into a throughput number comparable to the forward-pass metric. The exact factor is not meant to be a universal property of attention in all settings; rather, it is the paper’s practical estimate for the benchmark configurations under study.

# Forward-pass speedups of FlashAttention-3 in FP16

The paper reports that in FP16, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is about 1.5--2.0$\times$ faster than \textsc{FlashAttention-2} in the forward pass. In the detailed benchmark plots, the method is also shown to be up to 3--16$\times$ faster than a standard attention implementation, depending on the sequence length and configuration.

These improvements are consistent with the paper’s algorithmic story: warp-specialization helps hide memory and issue latency, and the overlapping of softmax with WGMMA reduces idle time inside the kernel. The benchmarking results therefore serve as empirical evidence that the asynchronous scheduling ideas do in fact translate into real speedup on Hopper.

# Backward-pass speedups of FlashAttention-3 in FP16

The paper also reports backward-pass gains in FP16. Here, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is about 1.5--1.75$\times$ faster than \textsc{FlashAttention-2}. This is an important result because the backward pass is a major part of training cost, and a speedup in the backward pass indicates that the benefits of the new kernel are not limited to inference-like forward computation.

Although the paper focuses its algorithmic exposition primarily on the forward pass, the backward result shows that the overall approach is broadly useful for transformer training workloads as well. The improvement is smaller than in some forward settings, but it is still substantial and consistent across the tested range.

# Peak throughput results: up to 740 TFLOPs/s and 75% utilization

One of the headline findings is that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} reaches up to 740 TFLOPs/s in FP16 on the H100 SXM5. The paper describes this as about 75\% utilization of the theoretical maximum TFLOPs/s on the GPU.

This is significant because the abstract and introduction emphasize that previous methods were leaving a large amount of Hopper’s capability unused. Reaching 740 TFLOPs/s suggests that the new asynchronous design closes much of that gap. The throughput number is therefore not only a speed claim, but also a utilization claim: the kernel is making much better use of the available Tensor Core performance.

# FP8 throughput results approaching 1.2 PFLOPs/s

The paper also evaluates the FP8 version of \textsc{FlashAttention-3}. In this setting, the kernel reaches close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s. This is one of the clearest demonstrations of the benefit of Hopper’s low-precision hardware support, because FP8 nearly doubles the throughput relative to FP16 for the GEMM-heavy parts of the kernel.

The paper ties this result to the FP8 Tensor Cores and to the algorithmic changes needed to use them correctly. The throughput increase is not obtained simply by changing the data type; it depends on layout transformations, in-kernel transposes, and the quantization strategies described earlier in the paper. The benchmark confirms that these ingredients work together to produce very high measured performance.

# Comparisons with vendor-optimized cuDNN attention

The paper also compares \textsc{FlashAttention-3} with a vendor-optimized attention implementation from cuDNN. The main conclusion is that for large sequence lengths, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} in FP16 outperforms this cuDNN implementation, and FP8 is competitive with it.

This is an important result because cuDNN is not an academic baseline; it is a highly optimized vendor library designed for NVIDIA hardware. The fact that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} can match or exceed it indicates that the paper’s algorithmic restructuring is competitive even against production-quality kernels.

# When FlashAttention-3 wins most clearly: medium and long sequences

The paper emphasizes that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is especially strong for medium and long sequences. In the benchmark section, the authors note that for sequence lengths of 1k and above, the method can surpass the cuDNN implementation. The overall speedups compared with \textsc{FlashAttention-2} are also reported across a wide range of sequence lengths, but the gains become most meaningful as the sequence length grows.

This pattern is consistent with the motivation for the work. Attention becomes more expensive as sequence length increases, so better scheduling and better use of Hopper’s asynchronous hardware matter more in the long-context regime. The benchmarks therefore support the paper’s central thesis: that careful algorithm-hardware co-design produces the biggest payoff when attention is the dominant bottleneck.
