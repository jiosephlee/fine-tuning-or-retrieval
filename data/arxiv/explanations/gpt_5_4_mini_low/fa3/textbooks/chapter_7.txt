\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

Chapter 7: 7. Hiding Softmax Under GEMM: Pingpong Scheduling and Intra-Warpgroup Overlap

# Why softmax becomes a bottleneck on modern accelerators

The paper argues that attention on modern GPUs is not limited only by the amount of arithmetic it requires, but also by the imbalance between different kinds of arithmetic. In the attention forward pass, the main mathematical work is split between matrix multiplications and the softmax-related operations that normalize the attention scores. The matrix multiplications map well to Tensor Cores, which are designed for very high throughput. By contrast, the softmax step depends on lower-throughput operations such as floating-point multiply-add, exponentials, and reductions like row-wise maxima and row-wise sums.

This matters because the attention computation is not just “one big matmul.” Even when the matrix multiplications are accelerated heavily, the softmax still has to be carried out, and its operations are not free. The paper emphasizes that on Hopper, the Tensor Core side of attention is much faster than the exponential side, so if the two are executed in a purely sequential way, the Tensor Cores can sit idle while the softmax runs. In other words, the softmax becomes a bottleneck not because it is mathematically large in the same way as the GEMMs, but because its hardware execution rate is much lower.

# Throughput asymmetry between Tensor Core matmul and exponential/special-function units

A central motivation for the overlap technique is the large throughput gap between matrix multiplication and special functions on Hopper GPUs. The paper gives a concrete example: on the H100 SXM5, FP16 matmul throughput is 989 TFLOPS, while special functions such as exponential are only 3.9 TFLOPS. The special-function throughput is calculated from the hardware limit of 16 special-function operations per SM per clock cycle, scaled by the number of SMs and the clock speed.

The paper then compares this asymmetry to the actual work in attention. For FP16 attention with head dimension 128, there are 512 times more matmul FLOPs than exponential FLOPs, but the exponential operations run at 256 times lower throughput. The result is that the exponential portion can consume roughly half of the cycle time compared with the matmul portion, even though the matmuls dominate the arithmetic count. The situation is even more unfavorable in FP8, because the matmul throughput doubles while the exponential throughput stays the same. That means the arithmetic imbalance becomes even sharper as the compute side gets faster.

The important intuition is that a fast accelerator does not necessarily make every part of the kernel equally fast. A kernel can still be limited by a slower functional unit if its schedule forces the slow work to happen while the fast unit is idle. FlashAttention-3 addresses this by deliberately arranging for the softmax work to occur while the Tensor Cores are busy.

# Scheduling softmax during Tensor Core execution

The paper’s core idea in this section is to treat softmax as something that should be hidden behind matmul execution whenever possible. Since the exponential and related operations use a different hardware path than the Tensor Cores, the kernel can, in principle, do useful work on both paths at the same time. The challenge is that the softmax depends on the output of the first GEMM, and the second GEMM depends on the softmax output. So the dependency graph is sequential by default.

FlashAttention-3 exploits the fact that the GPU can issue work asynchronously. The paper’s design uses this to arrange that while one warpgroup is running softmax, another warpgroup is executing GEMMs. The high-level picture is that the slow non-matmul work is scheduled into the “shadow” of Tensor Core execution. This is what the paper calls pingpong scheduling.

This does not eliminate softmax; rather, it hides much of its latency. The key achievement is not making softmax faster in isolation, but preventing it from blocking the Tensor Cores. That distinction is important, because the paper is concerned with end-to-end throughput of attention rather than the throughput of any single operation.

# Pingpong scheduling across two consumer warpgroups

The paper introduces pingpong scheduling as a way to alternate responsibilities between two consumer warpgroups. Each warpgroup has a role in the attention pipeline, and the two warpgroups are coordinated so that one can focus on GEMMs while the other carries out softmax. The scheduling resembles a back-and-forth exchange: one warpgroup performs the matrix multiplications for the current or next block, while the other executes the softmax for the previous block.

Concretely, the paper describes using synchronization barriers to force the GEMMs of one warpgroup to be scheduled before the GEMMs of the other. This creates a structured alternation. While warpgroup 1 is engaged in GEMM work, warpgroup 2 is scheduled to perform softmax; then the roles switch, so warpgroup 2 does GEMM work while warpgroup 1 does softmax. That is why the method is called pingpong scheduling: the active role “bounces” between warpgroups.

The paper notes that the schedule is not perfectly clean in practice, but still improves performance. In the reported example, this kind of overlap increases FP16 forward throughput for head dimension 128 and sequence length 8192 from 570 TFLOPS to 620-640 TFLOPS. That improvement comes specifically from better use of the hardware execution units, not from changing the underlying attention math.

# Using barriers to enforce beneficial execution order

Because the GPU compiler and runtime may otherwise schedule instructions in ways that weaken the intended overlap, the paper uses synchronization barriers to shape execution order. The role of the barriers is to ensure that the GEMM activity and the softmax activity appear in the correct relative order so that the softmax can run while another warpgroup is keeping the Tensor Cores busy.

This is especially important because the overlap is not just a matter of launching two independent tasks. The two tasks are dependent in the attention algorithm, and if the schedule is not controlled carefully, the compiler could serialize them in an unfavorable way or reorder instructions so that the intended hiding effect is lost. The barriers help create a disciplined pipeline across warpgroups, so that the machine has a chance to execute the exponential work concurrently with matmul work.

The paper’s broader point is that performance on modern GPUs is not only about arithmetic intensity. It is also about execution order. By managing that order with barriers, the kernel can align the slower softmax work with the faster matmul work, instead of letting them compete for time in a purely sequential manner.

# Breaking sequential dependencies by pipelining across iterations

Within a single attention iteration, the softmax depends on the score matrix $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, and the second GEMM depends on the softmax output $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$. This creates a dependency chain that would normally force the kernel to finish one step before starting the next. The paper’s main insight is to break this apparent seriality by pipelining across iterations.

Instead of computing everything for one block before moving on, FlashAttention-3 keeps one block’s GEMM result around while the next block’s GEMM is already underway. That allows the kernel to overlap the second GEMM from one iteration with the softmax computation from another iteration. The dependency is not removed mathematically; rather, it is moved so that the waiting time can be covered by other useful work.

This idea requires additional buffering and careful state management. The paper describes using extra buffers in registers so that the kernel can hold both the current and next score matrices, and thereby begin computing on the next block before the previous one has fully finished its softmax and value-matrix update. This is the basis of the two-stage pipelined algorithm.

# The two-stage GEMM-softmax overlapping algorithm

The paper presents a two-stage consumer warpgroup algorithm that replaces the simpler sequential consumer path. The key change is that the kernel no longer treats the two GEMMs and the softmax as strictly serial within each iteration. Instead, it splits the work into a current stage and a next stage.

At the beginning, the consumer warpgroup waits for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and $\mathbf{K}_0$ to be available in shared memory, then computes the first score block $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_0^\top$ using WGMMA. It then computes the softmax quantities $m_i$, $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, and $\ell_i$ based on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, and rescales $\mathbf{O}_i$. That establishes the initial current state.

Inside the main loop, the algorithm launches the next score computation while simultaneously consuming the previous softmax output. Specifically, it computes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ and commits it without waiting, then computes $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ and also commits that without waiting. Afterward, it waits for the score result, performs the softmax update for the next block, then waits for the value-product WGMMA and rescales $\mathbf{O}_i$. In this way, the algorithm overlaps the second GEMM from one iteration with the softmax of the next.

The paper is explicit that this is an overlap across iterations, not within a single operation. The two-stage structure makes it possible to keep both the Tensor Cores and the softmax-related compute units active more of the time.

# Managing $S_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{next}}$ buffers in registers

A crucial implementation detail is that the overlap requires extra register storage. The consumer warpgroup must preserve $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ while $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ is being computed, because the softmax for the current block and the GEMM for the next block are both in flight. The paper explains that the next score block must be kept in registers until it becomes the current block on the next iteration.

This is why the algorithm uses the names $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$. They are not merely conceptual labels; they represent the staged data needed to connect one iteration to the next without forcing the kernel to wait. After the next block has been processed, $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ is copied into $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, and the loop continues.

The paper also notes that this buffering increases register pressure. Since registers are a limited resource, holding additional state can conflict with other performance choices such as larger block sizes. So the benefit of overlap comes with a practical cost: the kernel must balance pipeline depth against register usage.

# Overlapping $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, and softmax across consecutive blocks

The two-stage pipeline is easiest to understand by tracking how the three major pieces of the attention forward pass move across iterations. The first GEMM computes the score block $\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$. The softmax then transforms those scores into $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$. Finally, the second GEMM multiplies $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ by $\mathbf{V}_j$ to update the output block $\mathbf{O}_i$.

FlashAttention-3 arranges these so that the first GEMM for block $j$ is launched while the second GEMM for block $j-1$ is still in progress, and the softmax for block $j$ is executed while that second GEMM is happening. This creates a pipeline across consecutive blocks: current block softmax is hidden under next block GEMM. The code in the paper makes this explicit by computing $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ first, then launching the output update for $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$, and then handling the softmax for the next block.

This is the main technical move behind intra-warpgroup overlap. It is not simply a matter of overlapping arbitrary instructions, but of reorganizing the attention loop so that the next block’s score computation and the previous block’s value update can coexist with the current block’s softmax transformation. The pipeline is therefore both a scheduling trick and a structural rewrite of the forward algorithm.

# Consumer main loop walkthrough for the overlapped FP16 algorithm

The paper’s overlapped FP16 algorithm can be understood by following the consumer main loop step by step. After initialization, the kernel waits for the initial query and key block to arrive. It computes the first score matrix, commits it, and then forms the first softmax state. From there, each iteration of the loop handles three things at once: preparing the next score matrix, consuming the current softmax output into the output accumulator, and updating the softmax statistics for the next block.

The most important detail is that the second WGMMA of iteration $j$ is not isolated from the softmax work of iteration $j+1$. Instead, the kernel begins the next score GEMM, then uses the time while that asynchronous GEMM is in flight to perform the current output GEMM and the softmax computations. Only after the score result is ready does it finalize the softmax update for that next block. This arrangement is what allows the softmax to be hidden behind the GEMM work rather than stretching the critical path.

At the end of the loop, after the last value block has been loaded, the kernel performs the final output update and then rescales $\mathbf{O}_i$ one last time using the accumulated softmax normalizer $\ell_i$ and log-sum statistic $m_i$. The result is then written back to HBM. Thus, the overlap affects the entire middle portion of the computation, while the epilogue completes the final normalization.

# Practical effects of overlap on utilization and throughput

The paper’s empirical results show that the overlap strategy has a measurable impact on throughput. In the ablation study, adding warp-specialization and WGMMA-softmax pipelining improves the performance of non-causal FP16 FlashAttention-3 from 570 TFLOPS to 661 TFLOPS for the tested configuration. More broadly, the paper reports that FlashAttention-3 reaches up to 740 TFLOPS on H100, corresponding to about $75\%$ utilization of the theoretical peak.

The practical interpretation is that the kernel spends less time waiting on the softmax path and more time keeping the Tensor Cores occupied. Since the softmax is relatively low-throughput, the main benefit is not that it becomes faster on its own, but that it stops interrupting the matmul pipeline. This is exactly the kind of gain that hardware-aware scheduling is meant to capture.

The paper also emphasizes that the effect depends on implementation details. Compiler reordering can disrupt the intended schedule, and register pressure can constrain how aggressively overlap is used. So while the principle is straightforward, the performance result depends on careful kernel design. In the paper’s setting, though, the overlap is clearly beneficial: it raises utilization, improves throughput, and helps FlashAttention-3 close much of the gap between the original FlashAttention designs and the optimized GEMM kernels available on Hopper.
