\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

Chapter 11: 11. FlashAttention-3 Backward Pass and Training Considerations

# Why backward attention matters for training efficiency

Although much of the paper focuses on the forward pass, the backward pass is equally important in training because it is part of every gradient step. In a Transformer, attention is not just used to produce outputs during inference; it also needs to propagate gradients back through the query, key, and value projections during optimization. That means any speedup in backward attention directly affects overall training throughput, not just inference latency.

The paper frames attention as a bottleneck because self-attention has quadratic scaling in sequence length, and this cost appears in both the forward and backward passes. As sequence lengths grow, the cost of training becomes increasingly dominated by attention computation, so improvements to backward attention are necessary if one wants the speed benefits of FlashAttention-style kernels to translate into practical training gains. This is why the paper reports not only forward performance but also backward performance and numerical behavior.

# Relationship between forward and backward attention computations

The paper gives the standard forward formulas for a single head as
$\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, and $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$.
The backward pass is then derived by applying the chain rule to these same quantities. Given the output gradient $\mathbf{dO}$, the gradients are computed as
$\mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO}$,
$\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO}\mathbf{V}^\top$,
$\mathbf{dS} = \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP})$,
$\mathbf{dQ} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}\mathbf{K}$, and
$\mathbf{dK} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}^\top \mathbf{Q}$.

This structure reveals an important point: backward attention is not a completely separate computation, but rather a reuse of the same ingredients that appear in the forward pass. The same matrices $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{V}$, and the softmax probabilities $\mathbf{P}$ all play a role, and the backward computation is organized around matrix multiplications and the derivative of softmax. In the paper’s background section, the derivative of softmax is written row-wise as
$\mathbf{d}s = (\mathrm{diag}(p) - p p^\top)\mathbf{d}p$
for a vector $p = \mathrm{softmax}(s)$.

Because the forward and backward passes share this structure, the same kinds of GPU-aware design choices matter for both. In particular, the paper’s emphasis on minimizing slow global memory traffic and exploiting asynchronous execution is relevant not only to producing the forward output, but also to efficiently moving through the gradient computations needed during training.

# Recomputation trade-offs in FlashAttention-style kernels

The paper explicitly notes that the backward-pass FLOPs are larger than those of the forward pass because of recomputation. In the benchmarking section, the backward FLOPs are computed as $2.5$ times the forward FLOPs, since there are $2$ matmuls in the forward pass and $5$ matmuls in the backward pass, due to recomputation.

This recomputation is a key training-time trade-off. Rather than saving all intermediate attention matrices in high-bandwidth memory, FlashAttention-style kernels reorganize the computation so that they can recover what they need during backward execution. The paper’s broader motivation is to reduce reads and writes to slow global memory, and this same philosophy extends to training. Recomputation increases arithmetic work, but it is often a favorable trade if it avoids materializing large intermediate matrices such as $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$.

The paper’s description of standard attention makes clear what is being avoided: in standard implementations, the intermediate matrices are materialized to HBM. FlashAttention instead uses a local softmax reduction and fuses the attention operations into a single kernel. In training, the backward pass must still recover the needed quantities, but the design goal remains the same: preserve efficiency by carefully balancing recomputation against memory traffic. This is why the paper treats recomputation as part of the performance story rather than as an incidental detail.

# Backward-pass matmul structure versus forward-pass structure

The forward pass consists of two matrix multiplications surrounding the softmax: first $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, then $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. The paper’s FP16 algorithm is organized around this two-GEMM structure, and its performance improvements come from overlapping these GEMMs with softmax work using asynchronous hardware features.

The backward pass has a different algebraic structure. Instead of two matmuls in the simple forward description, the paper notes that the backward pass involves five matmuls due to recomputation. This means that backward attention is even more matmul-heavy than the forward pass. That matters because the paper’s hardware discussion shows that matmul throughput on Hopper is far higher than the throughput of special functions such as exponential, but it also means that the backward pass offers more opportunity for throughput-oriented scheduling when the kernel is designed well.

At the same time, the different structure of backward computation means that the exact forward-pass pipeline cannot be copied over without modification. The paper does not fully spell out the backward algorithm in the main text, but it does state that the backward pass is described separately and that the reported backward performance is strong. The key conceptual point is that both passes are based on tiled GEMM and softmax-like operations, yet the backward pass includes additional gradient-specific matmuls and recomputation steps that make it computationally heavier.

# Reported backward speedups of FlashAttention-3 over FlashAttention-2

The paper reports that FlashAttention-3 achieves a backward-pass speedup of about $1.5$ to $1.75\times$ over FlashAttention-2 on the H100 SXM5 GPU. This is slightly smaller than the forward-pass speedup, which is reported as about $1.5$ to $2.0\times$, but it is still a substantial improvement.

These speedups are important because training time is determined by both passes. A kernel that accelerates only inference would be incomplete for training workloads, but the reported backward gains show that the benefits of FlashAttention-3 extend beyond the forward path. The paper presents these numbers as evidence that the algorithmic changes—warp-specialization, asynchronous overlap, and Hopper-specific execution choices—are useful not just for producing outputs quickly, but for reducing the cost of gradient computation as well.

The paper also emphasizes that these measurements were taken over a range of sequence lengths and attention settings. That matters because training workloads often involve different context lengths and head dimensions, and the reported backward speedups indicate that the design is robust enough to provide consistent benefits across those settings.

# How kernel design choices affect training throughput

The paper’s central message is that training throughput is shaped not only by the mathematical form of attention, but by how that computation is mapped onto GPU hardware. Three design choices in particular matter.

First, producer-consumer asynchrony through warp-specialization helps hide memory and instruction issue latency. The paper explains that warps can be divided into producers and consumers, with producers handling data movement and consumers handling computation. This makes it easier to overlap loading with useful work. In training, where attention kernels are repeatedly executed, better latency hiding can have an outsized impact on total step time.

Second, the paper’s pingpong scheduling overlaps the softmax computation of one warpgroup with GEMM execution in another warpgroup. Since exponentials and other softmax-related operations have much lower throughput than matmuls, scheduling them to run concurrently with Tensor Core work improves hardware utilization. The paper reports that this kind of algorithmic reordering improved performance in the forward pass, and the same general principle is relevant to training throughput more broadly.

Third, the paper’s Hopper-specific use of asynchronous WGMMA and TMA improves the ability to overlap computation and data movement. This is especially important for attention, where the kernel repeatedly moves blocks of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ while also performing matrix multiplications. Because training invokes both forward and backward kernels, the benefits of this scheduling discipline compound over many iterations.

# What remains challenging for low-precision training-time attention

The paper makes clear that low precision introduces additional difficulty, especially for training. It notes that low precision such as FP8 is attractive because it can provide higher throughput, but the technical challenge is to minimize quantization error, particularly in the presence of outlier features in large language models.

For the FP8 version of FlashAttention-3, the paper addresses this with block quantization and incoherent processing. Block quantization uses one scale per block rather than one scale per tensor, and incoherent processing multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix before quantization to spread out outliers. These techniques reduce numerical error in the forward pass, and the paper validates that FP8 FlashAttention-3 is $2.6\times$ more accurate than a baseline FP8 attention method with per-tensor quantization.

However, the paper’s discussion also makes clear that low-precision training-time attention remains challenging. FP8 requires careful handling of layout constraints, additional transformations between accumulator and operand layouts, and accuracy-preserving quantization strategies. The paper also notes limitations for low-precision use: for benchmarks, FP16 FlashAttention-3 uses a persistent kernel and load balancing strategy, whereas FP8 FlashAttention-3 does not, which partly explains weaker performance for small sequence lengths and causal masking compared to cuDNN FP8 kernels. The discussion also points to open questions about the effects of low-precision attention in large-scale training.

So, while FlashAttention-3 shows that low-precision attention can be fast and accurate, the paper does not present low-precision training as a solved problem. Instead, it treats the FP8 result as a major step forward that still leaves room for further work, especially in the training regime where both performance and numerical stability must be maintained across many iterations.
