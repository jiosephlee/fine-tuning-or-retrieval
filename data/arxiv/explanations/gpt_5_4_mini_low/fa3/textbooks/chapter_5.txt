\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

Chapter 5: 5. The Core Ideas of FlashAttention-3

# Overview of FlashAttention-3

FlashAttention-3 is the next step in a line of exact attention algorithms that are designed around the way modern GPUs actually execute work. The paper’s central observation is that attention is still a bottleneck for Transformer models, especially in long-context settings, and that earlier FlashAttention methods already improved performance by reducing slow reads and writes to global memory. FlashAttention-3 keeps the same basic goal, but it goes further by explicitly taking advantage of two Hopper GPU capabilities that earlier designs did not fully exploit: asynchronous execution and FP8 low precision.

At a high level, the paper presents FlashAttention-3 as a redesign of FlashAttention-2 rather than a completely new attention formula. It still computes exact attention, not an approximation. The improvement comes from how the computation is scheduled and how the data is represented. The algorithm is organized so that memory movement, matrix multiplication, and softmax do not sit idle waiting for one another any longer than necessary. In parallel, the FP8 version changes the layout and quantization strategy so that the Hopper Tensor Cores can be used efficiently without sacrificing too much numerical accuracy.

The conceptual picture is therefore one of coordination. FlashAttention-3 is not simply “faster because of better kernels,” but faster because it maps the structure of attention onto the structure of the hardware. Its three main ideas are: producer-consumer asynchrony through warp specialization, overlapping GEMM and softmax to hide non-matmul costs, and hardware-aware FP8 attention with block quantization and incoherent processing. The paper’s core claim is that these ideas are complementary: together they raise utilization and also preserve accuracy.

# Producer-consumer asynchrony through warp specialization

The first major idea is to divide the work inside a CTA into producer and consumer roles. This is called warp specialization. The paper’s intuition is that GPUs are throughput machines, and throughput is improved when different hardware units are kept busy with different kinds of work at the same time. On Hopper, data movement from global memory to shared memory can be done by TMA, while matrix multiplication can be done by asynchronous WGMMA instructions on Tensor Cores. FlashAttention-3 uses this separation by assigning some warps the job of producing data and others the job of consuming it.

The producer warps are responsible for moving tiles of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ into shared memory. The consumer warps wait for those tiles, then perform the actual attention computation on them. This organization matters because loading and computation no longer have to be treated as one monolithic synchronous step. Instead, the algorithm turns the CTA into a pipeline. While one set of warps is loading the next block, another set of warps can already be computing on the current block.

The paper emphasizes that Hopper makes this style of design especially effective because the Tensor Memory Accelerator and WGMMA are both asynchronous. That means the producer does not have to block the consumer in the same way a purely synchronous algorithm would. The kernel can issue loads, continue with other work, and then synchronize only when a specific tile is needed. The result is better latency hiding and better use of the machine’s execution resources. Hopper also supports dynamic register reallocation with $\verb|setmaxnreg|$, which lets the consumer warps hold more registers than the producer warps, since the producers need relatively little register space. This is part of the paper’s broader idea that the kernel should adapt its internal resource allocation to the roles the warps play.

The algorithmic effect of warp specialization is easiest to see as a pipeline over the query block $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and the key/value blocks $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$. The producer fills shared memory with the needed tiles, while the consumer performs the blockwise GEMMs and softmax updates. Because the buffer is circular, the producer can keep moving forward as soon as a stage becomes free. That circular structure gives the algorithm a way to reuse on-chip memory without materializing large intermediate matrices in HBM.

# Overlapping GEMM and softmax to hide non-matmul costs

The second major idea is to hide the cost of softmax behind matrix multiplication. This is important because the paper shows that non-matmul operations, especially the exponential used in softmax, are much slower than matmul on Hopper in throughput terms. Even though softmax is essential for attention, its arithmetic intensity is much lower than GEMM, so if the kernel executes it in a strictly sequential way, it can become a bottleneck.

FlashAttention-3 solves this by changing how the inner loop is scheduled. In the usual structure, one first computes a score block $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, then applies softmax operations to that block, and then uses the result to form the contribution to $\mathbf{O}_i$ through multiplication with $\mathbf{V}_j$. In a synchronous design, those steps are serialized. FlashAttention-3 instead breaks this dependency chain across iterations and uses extra register buffering so that the next matrix multiplication can begin before all softmax work from the current iteration is done.

The paper’s two-stage pipeline captures this idea clearly. In one stage, the consumer issues WGMMA for the next score block. In the other stage, it uses the previous block’s softmax result to multiply by $\mathbf{V}$. While the Tensor Cores are busy with one WGMMA, the softmax operations for another block can run in parallel on other hardware units. This is described in the paper as a kind of pingpong scheduling: one warpgroup performs GEMMs while another performs softmax, and then they swap roles. The goal is not perfect simultaneity in a literal sense, but a structured overlap that keeps different units occupied at the same time.

This overlap matters because the paper argues that, on Hopper, GEMM is so fast relative to exponential and other softmax-related operations that leaving them separate wastes performance. By scheduling the softmax “under” the matmul, the algorithm reduces visible non-matmul cost. The paper also notes that the overlap is not automatic. The compiler may reorder instructions, and the extra buffering increases register pressure. So the algorithm is carefully designed as a balance: enough staging to expose overlap, but not so much that register use destroys the performance gains. The paper even discusses a possible 3-stage extension, showing that the design space is really about how much asynchrony can be exploited before resource limits become too costly.

The conceptual takeaway is that FlashAttention-3 treats softmax not as a separate phase, but as a task to be hidden inside the broader matmul pipeline. That is why the algorithm can improve utilization beyond what FlashAttention-2 achieved, even though both still compute exact attention.

# Low-precision FP8 attention with hardware-aware design

The third major idea is to make FP8 work well in attention. The paper’s point is not simply that FP8 is faster because it uses fewer bits. Instead, FP8 is useful because Hopper has Tensor Cores that can execute FP8 WGMMA at roughly twice the throughput of FP16 or BF16. But to use those Tensor Cores effectively, the algorithm must respect their layout constraints and must avoid large numerical errors.

The first challenge is layout. In the FP8 setting, the second GEMM in attention has stricter requirements on how the $\mathbf{V}$ tile must be laid out in shared memory. The paper explains that while the inputs are often contiguous in the head dimension, FP8 WGMMA wants certain tiles to be k-major rather than mn-major. Since the TMA load cannot itself change the contiguous dimension, FlashAttention-3 performs an in-kernel transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ tiles after loading them into shared memory. It uses LDSM and STSM instructions for this. The point is that the algorithm does not treat layout as an afterthought; it is built around the layout restrictions of the hardware so that FP8 execution can stay on the fast path.

The second layout issue is subtler. The paper notes that the FP32 accumulator layout of FP8 WGMMA is different from the operand layout expected when the result is fed into the next WGMMA. That means the output of the first GEMM cannot simply be reused as-is. FlashAttention-3 resolves this by using byte permutes so that the accumulated result is rearranged into a format suitable for the next operation. This also aligns with the transposed layout of the $\mathbf{V}$ tile, making the whole FP8 pipeline consistent. In other words, the algorithm is not just quantized; it is layout-aware at every step of the computation.

Accuracy is the other challenge. FP8 has fewer mantissa bits than FP16 or BF16, and large language models often contain outlier values that are hard to quantize well. The paper addresses this with two techniques: block quantization and incoherent processing. With block quantization, the tensor is not scaled by one global factor, but by one factor per block. Since FlashAttention already operates on blocks, this fits naturally into the algorithm and can be fused with nearby preprocessing such as rotary embedding. This reduces quantization error because different blocks can adapt to different ranges of values.

Incoherent processing attacks the outlier problem from another angle. The paper multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ before quantization. Since $\mathbf{M}$ is orthogonal, the attention scores do not change:
\[
(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top.
\]
What changes is the distribution of the values being quantized. The random transformation spreads out large entries so that the quantizer sees a more balanced signal. The paper follows a structured choice of $\mathbf{M}$ using random diagonal sign matrices and a Hadamard matrix, which can be applied efficiently. This is a key theme of FlashAttention-3: hardware efficiency and numerical robustness are designed together, not separately.

# How the three ideas interact to improve performance

The three ideas in FlashAttention-3 are not independent tricks. They reinforce one another because they address different kinds of inefficiency in the attention kernel. Warp specialization improves the movement of data into and out of on-chip memory. GEMM-softmax pipelining improves the use of compute units within the consumer loop. FP8 support improves the throughput of the GEMM itself, provided the layout and quantization issues are handled correctly.

This interaction is especially important because attention has both memory pressure and compute structure. A kernel can be slowed down by waiting for memory, by wasting Tensor Core cycles, or by spending too much time on softmax and other scalar operations. FlashAttention-3 attacks all three. The producer-consumer design keeps TMA and the Tensor Cores busy. The two-stage pipeline keeps softmax from sitting on the critical path. The FP8 path doubles matmul throughput, which raises the payoff from the other scheduling optimizations. The paper’s empirical results show that these gains accumulate into significant end-to-end speedups.

The paper also makes clear that the interaction is not always straightforward. FP8 improves GEMM throughput but does not speed up exponentials, so the relative importance of hiding softmax becomes even greater in FP8. At the same time, FP8 introduces extra layout and register-handling complexity, which must be absorbed into the same pipelined design. This is why the FP8 version is not just a lower-precision copy of the FP16 algorithm. It is a hardware-aware variant whose efficiency depends on carefully aligning tile layout, transposition, and pipelined execution.

In this sense, FlashAttention-3 is best understood as a single coordinated strategy rather than three separate optimizations. The core strategy is to expose as much parallelism as possible between data movement, GEMM, and softmax, while also exploiting the higher throughput of FP8 when accuracy can be preserved.

# What makes FlashAttention-3 both faster and numerically robust

What makes FlashAttention-3 distinctive is that it improves speed without giving up the exactness of attention, and it improves FP8 accuracy without abandoning the hardware advantages of low precision. The paper’s FP16 results show that FlashAttention-3 reaches much higher utilization on H100 than FlashAttention-2, with forward-pass speedups of about $1.5$ to $2.0 \times$ and backward-pass speedups of about $1.5$ to $1.75 \times$. It also reaches up to $740$ TFLOPs/s, which the paper reports as about $75\%$ utilization. These gains come from a kernel that is more fully matched to Hopper’s asynchronous execution model.

At the same time, numerical robustness is maintained through careful algorithmic choices. In FP16, the paper reports that FlashAttention-3 has the same numerical error as FlashAttention-2 and is better than standard attention because intermediate results such as softmax rescaling are kept in FP32. That is an important point: speed improvements do not require sacrificing the stable accumulation strategy that made FlashAttention attractive in the first place.

For FP8, the paper goes further and validates that the block quantization and incoherent processing strategy reduces numerical error by a factor of $2.6$ compared to a baseline FP8 attention method with per-tensor scaling. This result matters because it shows that low precision can be made more reliable when the quantization scheme is aware of the structure of the data and the structure of the computation. The algorithm does not assume that a single scale is good enough for every tensor or every block. Instead, it uses the block structure of attention itself to localize scaling and reduce the damage from outliers.

So the deeper lesson of FlashAttention-3 is that speed and accuracy need not be opposing goals. By treating the GPU as a collection of specialized, asynchronous units and by treating low precision as a design problem rather than a mere storage optimization, the paper builds an attention kernel that is both faster and more robust. That combination is what makes FlashAttention-3 the paper’s core contribution.
