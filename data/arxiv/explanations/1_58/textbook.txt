# Introduction: Motivation for Low-bit Large Language Models

In recent years, the field of artificial intelligence has witnessed a remarkable surge in the development and deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs). These models, often based on the Transformer architecture, have grown exponentially in size and capability, enabling breakthroughs in natural language understanding, generation, and a wide array of downstream tasks. However, this rapid growth in model scale has brought with it significant computational and environmental challenges. This chapter introduces the motivation behind low-bit quantization of LLMs, culminating in the presentation of BitNet b1.58, a novel 1.58-bit ternary-weight LLM that achieves parity with full-precision models while drastically reducing computational costs. We also outline the pedagogical goals of this textbook to guide readers through this exciting frontier.

## Growth of LLMs and Computational Trends

The past decade has seen a dramatic increase in the size of language models, from millions to hundreds of billions of parameters. This growth has been driven by the observation that larger models, trained on more data, tend to perform better across a variety of language tasks. For example, models like GPT-3, PaLM, and LLaMA have demonstrated that scaling up parameters and training tokens leads to improved perplexity and zero-shot task performance.

However, this scaling comes at a steep computational cost. Training and inference for these models require massive amounts of floating-point operations (FLOPs), memory bandwidth, and energy. The dominant operations in LLMs are matrix multiplications within linear layers, which scale roughly with the square of the model dimension and linearly with the number of layers. As models grow, the demand for compute resources grows superlinearly, making it increasingly difficult and expensive to train and deploy these models.

Moreover, the environmental impact of training large models is non-trivial. The energy consumption associated with training and serving LLMs contributes to carbon emissions, raising concerns about sustainability. These factors motivate the search for more efficient model representations and computation paradigms that can maintain or improve performance while reducing resource consumption.

## Costs of Scaling: Latency, Memory, Throughput, and Energy

When deploying LLMs, several key cost metrics come into play:

- **Latency:** The time it takes to generate a single token during inference. High latency can degrade user experience in interactive applications.

- **Memory:** The amount of GPU or accelerator memory required to store model parameters and intermediate activations. Larger models and higher precision weights consume more memory, limiting batch sizes and sequence lengths.

- **Throughput:** The number of tokens generated per unit time, often constrained by memory and compute resources.

- **Energy Consumption:** The total electrical energy required to perform inference or training. This is closely tied to the number and type of arithmetic operations, memory accesses, and data movement.

Scaling up model size tends to increase all these costs, often disproportionately. For example, the matrix multiplications in linear layers dominate both latency and energy consumption. Additionally, transferring model parameters from slower DRAM to faster on-chip memory (e.g., SRAM) during inference can be a bottleneck, especially for large models.

Reducing these costs without sacrificing model quality is a central challenge in making LLMs more accessible and environmentally friendly.

## Quantization as a Solution: Overview and Taxonomy

Quantization is a widely studied technique to reduce the precision of model parameters and activations, thereby lowering memory footprint and computational complexity. By representing weights and activations with fewer bits, quantization can significantly reduce the size of models and accelerate inference.

There are several types of quantization:

- **Post-Training Quantization (PTQ):** Applying quantization to a pre-trained full-precision model without retraining. This is convenient but often suboptimal in accuracy.

- **Quantization-Aware Training (QAT):** Incorporating quantization effects during training to adapt the model to low-bit representations, often yielding better accuracy.

- **Uniform vs. Non-Uniform Quantization:** Uniform quantization uses evenly spaced quantization levels, while non-uniform schemes allocate levels adaptively.

- **Symmetric vs. Asymmetric Quantization:** Symmetric quantization centers quantization levels around zero, whereas asymmetric includes a zero-point offset.

- **Weight vs. Activation Quantization:** Weights are typically quantized once and stored, while activations are quantized dynamically during inference.

Quantization levels vary from 16-bit floating point (FP16) down to 8-bit, 4-bit, and even 2-bit or 1-bit representations. Lower bit-widths offer greater efficiency but pose challenges in maintaining model accuracy.

## From FP16 to 4-bit and Beyond: Industry Trends

Industry practice has evolved from using 16-bit floating-point precision (FP16 or BF16) to lower-bit quantization to improve efficiency. For example, 8-bit integer quantization is now common in many inference pipelines, offering a good balance between speed and accuracy.

More aggressive quantization to 4-bit or even 2-bit weights has been explored in research and some production systems. Techniques like GPTQ and AWQ have demonstrated that 4-bit quantization can approach full-precision performance with careful calibration and quantization-aware training.

However, post-training quantization methods often remain suboptimal, and further reducing bit-widths to 1-bit or ternary weights has been challenging due to accuracy degradation and training instability.

Recent research, such as BitNet, has introduced architectures designed from the ground up to operate with 1-bit weights, enabling a new computation paradigm that replaces floating-point multiplications with integer additions. This shift promises orders of magnitude improvements in energy efficiency and latency.

## Introducing BitNet b1.58: Claims and High-Level Contributions

Building on the foundation of BitNet, the paper "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits" introduces BitNet b1.58, a novel variant where every parameter is ternary, taking values in \{-1, 0, 1\}. This ternary representation corresponds to approximately 1.58 bits per parameter in the binary system, hence the name.

BitNet b1.58 achieves several key advances:

- **Accuracy Parity:** It matches full-precision (FP16 or BF16) Transformer LLMs of the same size and training tokens in terms of perplexity and zero-shot end-task performance, starting from model sizes of 3 billion parameters.

- **Computational Efficiency:** By replacing floating-point multiplications with integer additions, BitNet b1.58 drastically reduces energy consumption, latency, and memory bandwidth requirements.

- **Memory Footprint:** The ternary weights reduce the model size and bandwidth needed to load parameters from DRAM, enabling faster and more cost-effective inference.

- **New Scaling Law:** The paper proposes a new scaling law and training recipe for 1.58-bit LLMs, showing that larger ternary models can outperform smaller full-precision models at a fraction of the cost.

- **Hardware Implications:** The 1-bit computation paradigm opens the door for designing specialized hardware optimized for ternary-weight LLMs, potentially revolutionizing LLM deployment.

These contributions position BitNet b1.58 as a Pareto improvement over existing full-precision LLMs, offering a compelling path forward for efficient, high-performance language models.

# Technical Background: Transformers, LLM Architectures, and Computation Profile

In this chapter, we provide a foundational understanding of the Transformer architecture, which underpins most modern Large Language Models (LLMs), including the BitNet b1.58 model introduced in the paper. We will explore the key components of Transformers, their computational characteristics, and how these relate to the challenges of scaling LLMs. This background is essential to appreciate why low-bit quantization, such as the ternary weights in BitNet b1.58, can yield significant efficiency gains without sacrificing performance.

## Recap of the Transformer Block: Self-Attention and Feed-Forward

The Transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017), revolutionized natural language processing by enabling models to capture long-range dependencies in sequences efficiently. A Transformer block consists primarily of two sublayers: the self-attention mechanism and the feed-forward network (FFN).

### Self-Attention

Self-attention allows the model to weigh the importance of different tokens in the input sequence relative to each other. Given an input sequence of token embeddings, the self-attention mechanism computes three matrices: queries \( Q \), keys \( K \), and values \( V \), each obtained by linear projections of the input embeddings. The attention scores are computed as scaled dot-products between queries and keys:

\[
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V,
\]

where \( d_k \) is the dimensionality of the keys. This operation produces a weighted sum of the values, where the weights reflect the relevance of each token to the query token.

Multi-head attention extends this by computing multiple attention heads in parallel, allowing the model to attend to information from different representation subspaces.

### Feed-Forward Network (FFN)

Following the self-attention layer, the Transformer block includes a position-wise feed-forward network, which applies two linear transformations with a non-linear activation in between, typically GELU or SwiGLU:

\[
\mathrm{FFN}(x) = \mathrm{Activation}(x W_1 + b_1) W_2 + b_2,
\]

where \( W_1, W_2 \) are weight matrices, and \( b_1, b_2 \) are biases. This component increases the model's capacity to learn complex transformations.

Each sublayer is followed by normalization and residual connections, which help stabilize training and improve gradient flow.

## Linear Layers, Matrix Multiplication, and Where FLOPs Go

The core computational operation in Transformers is matrix multiplication, primarily within the linear layers that produce \( Q, K, V \) matrices in self-attention and the two linear layers in the FFN. These linear layers are implemented as fully connected layers (e.g., PyTorch's `nn.Linear`), which perform matrix multiplications between input activations and weight matrices.

The computational cost of these matrix multiplications dominates the overall FLOPs (floating-point operations) during both training and inference. Specifically, the number of multiply-accumulate operations scales with the product of the input dimension, output dimension, and sequence length.

For example, in self-attention, computing \( Q = X W_Q \) involves multiplying the input embedding matrix \( X \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d} \) (where \( L \) is sequence length and \( d \) is embedding dimension) by the weight matrix \( W_Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_k} \). Similar operations occur for \( K \) and \( V \), and in the FFN layers.

Because these matrix multiplications are repeated many times across layers and tokens, they constitute the bulk of the computational workload and energy consumption in LLMs.

## Parameter Distribution: Embeddings, Attention, MLPs

Understanding how parameters are distributed across different components of the Transformer helps identify where quantization and compression efforts can be most impactful.

- **Embeddings:** The input token embeddings and output embeddings typically consume a significant portion of the model's parameters, especially for large vocabularies. These embeddings are often kept in full precision (e.g., FP16) because they are accessed frequently and quantizing them can degrade performance.

- **Attention Layers:** The weight matrices for query, key, and value projections, as well as the output projection, contain a substantial number of parameters. These matrices are involved in the self-attention computations.

- **MLP (Feed-Forward) Layers:** The two linear layers in the FFN usually have the largest parameter count in the Transformer block. The intermediate dimension in the FFN is often several times larger than the embedding dimension, making these layers parameter-heavy.

In total, the attention and MLP layers dominate the parameter count and computational cost, making them prime targets for quantization and optimization.

## Common Normalization and Nonlinear Components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU)

Normalization and nonlinear activation functions are critical for the stability and expressiveness of Transformer models.

### RMSNorm

Root Mean Square Layer Normalization (RMSNorm) is a variant of layer normalization that normalizes inputs based on their root mean square rather than mean and variance. It is defined as:

\[
\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x) = \frac{x}{\mathrm{RMS}(x)} \odot g,
\]

where \( \mathrm{RMS}(x) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{d} \sum_{i=1}^d x_i^2} \), and \( g \) is a learned gain parameter. RMSNorm is computationally simpler and has been shown to perform comparably to standard LayerNorm in LLMs like LLaMA.

### SwiGLU

SwiGLU is a gated linear unit activation function combining the Swish activation and gating mechanism. It is defined as:

\[
\mathrm{SwiGLU}(a, b) = a \odot \mathrm{SiLU}(b),
\]

where \( a \) and \( b \) are linear projections of the input, and SiLU (Swish) is the activation function \( \mathrm{SiLU}(x) = x \cdot \sigma(x) \), with \( \sigma \) being the sigmoid function. SwiGLU has been found to improve model performance and training stability compared to traditional activations like ReLU or GELU.

These components are part of the LLaMA architecture and are adopted in BitNet b1.58 to maintain compatibility and performance.

## The LLaMA Architecture and Why It Is a Popular Backbone

LLaMA (Large Language Model Meta AI) is a family of Transformer-based LLMs that have become a popular open-source backbone for research and deployment. Its design choices balance performance and efficiency, making it a natural foundation for innovations like BitNet b1.58.

Key features of LLaMA include:

- Use of RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm for normalization.
- SwiGLU activation in the feed-forward layers.
- Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) for encoding token positions.
- Removal of biases in linear layers to simplify the model.
- A well-optimized architecture that scales effectively from billions to tens of billions of parameters.

By adopting LLaMA-alike components, BitNet b1.58 benefits from a proven architecture and can integrate smoothly with popular open-source frameworks such as HuggingFace Transformers, vLLM, and llama.cpp.

## Profiling Compute and Memory: Which Operations Dominate Inference and Training

Profiling the computational and memory demands of LLMs reveals where optimization efforts should focus.

- **Compute Dominance:** Matrix multiplications in linear layers (self-attention projections and FFN) dominate the floating-point operations. These operations involve both multiplications and additions, which are energy-intensive, especially in full precision (FP16 or BF16).

- **Memory Footprint:** The largest memory consumers are the model parameters (weights) and activations. Embeddings occupy a significant portion of parameter memory, while activations, especially key-value caches during inference, consume substantial runtime memory.

- **Latency Bottlenecks:** The time to perform matrix multiplications grows with model size and sequence length. Additionally, transferring weights from DRAM to on-chip memory (e.g., SRAM) can be a bottleneck due to bandwidth limitations.

- **Energy Consumption:** Multiplications in floating-point arithmetic consume significantly more energy than integer additions. Therefore, reducing precision and replacing multiplications with additions can yield large energy savings.

This profiling motivates the design of BitNet b1.58, which replaces full-precision weights with ternary values \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\), enabling matrix multiplications to be performed with integer additions only. This shift reduces latency, memory bandwidth, and energy consumption, especially as model size scales.

---

In summary, the Transformer architecture's reliance on large matrix multiplications in linear layers, combined with the distribution of parameters and activations, creates a computational profile where low-bit quantization can have outsized benefits. The LLaMA architecture provides a strong, efficient backbone that BitNet b1.58 builds upon, adopting its normalization and activation components while innovating in weight quantization and computation paradigms. Understanding these technical foundations is crucial for grasping the significance of the 1.58-bit LLMs introduced in the paper.

# Quantization Fundamentals and Low-bit Neural Networks

In this chapter, we delve into the fundamental concepts of quantization as applied to neural networks, with a particular focus on low-bit quantization techniques that enable efficient Large Language Models (LLMs) such as BitNet b1.58. Understanding these concepts is crucial for grasping how models can be compressed to use fewer bits per parameter and activation without sacrificing performance. We will explore the mathematical and practical aspects of quantization, the different schemes used, and the challenges involved in training and evaluating low-bit neural networks.

---

## Quantization Basics: Scale, Zero Point, and Dynamic Range

Quantization is the process of mapping continuous-valued parameters or activations in a neural network to a discrete set of values that can be represented with fewer bits. This reduces the memory footprint and computational cost, enabling faster and more energy-efficient inference and training.

At its core, quantization involves two key components: a **scale** and a **zero point**. These parameters define how floating-point values are mapped to integers and vice versa.

Consider a floating-point value \( x \) that we want to quantize to an integer \( q \) in the range \([q_{\min}, q_{\max}]\), where \( q_{\min} \) and \( q_{\max} \) depend on the bitwidth. For example, for 8-bit unsigned integers, \( q_{\min} = 0 \) and \( q_{\max} = 255 \).

The quantization and dequantization processes are typically defined as:

\[
q = \mathrm{round}\left(\frac{x}{s}\right) + z,
\]

\[
\hat{x} = s \times (q - z),
\]

where:

- \( s > 0 \) is the **scale**, representing the step size between quantized levels in the floating-point domain.
- \( z \) is the **zero point**, an integer offset that aligns zero in floating-point to an integer value in the quantized domain.
- \( \mathrm{round}(\cdot) \) denotes rounding to the nearest integer.

The **dynamic range** of the quantized values is the range of floating-point values that can be represented after quantization. It is determined by the scale and zero point:

\[
x_{\min} = s \times (q_{\min} - z), \quad x_{\max} = s \times (q_{\max} - z).
\]

The choice of scale and zero point is critical to minimize quantization error. For example, if the floating-point values are mostly positive, asymmetric quantization with a non-zero zero point can better represent the distribution. Conversely, symmetric quantization sets \( z = 0 \) and centers the quantized range around zero, which simplifies computations but may be less precise for skewed distributions.

In low-bit quantization, such as ternary or binary networks, the quantization levels are very limited (e.g., \{-1, 0, 1\} for ternary), so careful design of scale and zero point is essential to preserve model accuracy.

---

## Uniform vs Non-uniform and Per-channel vs Per-tensor Quantization

Quantization schemes can be broadly categorized based on how the quantization levels are distributed and how the scale and zero point are applied.

### Uniform vs Non-uniform Quantization

- **Uniform quantization** divides the floating-point range into equal-sized intervals. Each quantized level corresponds to a fixed step size \( s \). This is simple to implement and efficient for hardware, especially when the quantization levels are evenly spaced.

- **Non-uniform quantization** uses quantization levels that are not evenly spaced. This can better approximate distributions with heavy tails or non-linear characteristics. Examples include logarithmic quantization or learned quantization levels. Non-uniform quantization can achieve lower quantization error but is more complex to implement and less hardware-friendly.

BitNet b1.58 uses a form of uniform quantization with ternary weights \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\), but the scale is dynamically computed per weight matrix using the average absolute value (absmean), which adapts the quantization to the distribution of weights.

### Per-channel vs Per-tensor Quantization

- **Per-tensor quantization** applies a single scale and zero point to the entire tensor (e.g., a weight matrix or activation tensor). This is simpler but may be suboptimal if different channels or rows have different value distributions.

- **Per-channel quantization** assigns separate scales and zero points for each channel (e.g., each output feature in a convolution or linear layer). This allows finer granularity and typically reduces quantization error, improving accuracy.

In practice, per-channel quantization is often used for weights, while activations are quantized per-tensor or per-token (as in BitNet b1.58) for efficiency.

---

## Activation Quantization: Ranges and Token-wise Scaling

Activations in neural networks are the intermediate outputs of layers and can have different statistical properties than weights. Quantizing activations is challenging because their distributions can vary dynamically during inference.

A common approach is to quantize activations to unsigned integers (e.g., 8-bit) with a zero point to represent zero activation. However, this introduces complexity in hardware and software due to zero-point offsets.

BitNet b1.58 adopts a different approach for activation quantization. Instead of scaling activations to the range \([0, Q_b]\) (where \(Q_b\) is the maximum quantized value, e.g., 255 for 8-bit), it scales activations to the symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) on a **per-token** basis. This means that for each token processed, the activations are scaled individually to fit within this symmetric range, eliminating the need for zero-point quantization.

This **token-wise scaling** has several advantages:

- It simplifies implementation by removing zero-point offsets.
- It aligns well with the symmetric ternary weight quantization.
- It introduces negligible performance degradation in practice.

By scaling activations per token, BitNet b1.58 achieves efficient and accurate low-bit activation quantization, which is crucial for maintaining model performance.

---

## Post-training Quantization (PTQ) vs Quantization-aware Training (QAT)

Quantization can be applied at different stages of the model lifecycle:

### Post-training Quantization (PTQ)

PTQ is applied after a model has been fully trained in full precision (e.g., FP16 or FP32). The trained weights and activations are quantized without further training or fine-tuning.

Advantages:

- Simple and fast to apply.
- Does not require access to training data or retraining.

Disadvantages:

- Often suboptimal, especially for very low-bit quantization (e.g., 1-bit or ternary).
- Can cause significant accuracy degradation if the quantization error is large.

PTQ is widely used in industry for 4-bit or 8-bit quantization but struggles to maintain accuracy at extremely low bitwidths.

### Quantization-aware Training (QAT)

QAT incorporates quantization into the training process itself. During training, weights and activations are quantized in the forward pass, and gradients are computed with respect to the quantized values.

Advantages:

- Allows the model to adapt to quantization noise.
- Achieves higher accuracy at very low bitwidths.

Disadvantages:

- Requires retraining or fine-tuning.
- More complex training pipeline.

BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with quantization-aware training, enabling it to achieve parity with full-precision models even with ternary weights and 8-bit activations.

---

## Ternary and Binary Networks: Historical Perspective and Key Techniques

Ternary and binary neural networks represent the extreme end of low-bit quantization, where weights and sometimes activations are constrained to very few discrete values.

- **Binary networks** restrict weights to \(\{-1, +1\}\), representing 1 bit per weight.
- **Ternary networks** extend this to \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\), effectively using about 1.58 bits per weight (since \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.58\)).

Historically, binary and ternary networks have been studied to reduce model size and computation, especially for deployment on resource-constrained devices.

Key techniques developed include:

- **Weight binarization/ternarization functions** that map continuous weights to discrete sets.
- **Scaling factors** to compensate for the reduced representational capacity.
- **Straight-through estimators (STE)** to enable gradient-based training despite discrete weights.
- **Sparsity via zero weights** in ternary networks, which can improve expressiveness and efficiency.

BitNet b1.58 builds on these ideas by using ternary weights with an absmean scaling function and 8-bit activations, achieving a balance between efficiency and accuracy.

---

## Straight-through Estimator and Gradient Handling for Discrete Weights

A fundamental challenge in training networks with discrete weights (binary or ternary) is that the quantization function is non-differentiable. For example, the rounding operation used to map continuous weights to \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\) has zero gradients almost everywhere, preventing standard backpropagation.

The **Straight-through Estimator (STE)** is a widely used technique to address this. It approximates the gradient of the quantization function by pretending it is the identity function during backpropagation. Formally, if the forward quantization is:

\[
\widetilde{W} = Q(W),
\]

where \(Q(\cdot)\) is the quantization function (e.g., rounding and clipping), then the STE defines the gradient as:

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W} \approx \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \widetilde{W}},
\]

ignoring the non-differentiability of \(Q\).

This allows gradients to flow through the quantization step, enabling gradient-based optimization methods to train discrete-weight networks effectively.

In BitNet b1.58, STE is used to train the ternary weights from scratch, combined with the absmean scaling and clipping functions, ensuring stable and effective training.

---

## Error Metrics for Quantization: Signal-to-Quantization-Noise and Perplexity Impact

Evaluating the quality of quantization involves measuring how much the quantized model deviates from the full-precision baseline.

### Signal-to-Quantization-Noise Ratio (SQNR)

SQNR measures the ratio of the power of the original signal (weights or activations) to the power of the quantization error (difference between original and quantized values):

\[
\mathrm{SQNR} = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{\mathbb{E}[x^2]}{\mathbb{E}[(x - \hat{x})^2]} \right) \text{ dB},
\]

where \(x\) is the original value and \(\hat{x}\) is the quantized value.

Higher SQNR indicates less quantization noise and better fidelity.

### Impact on Perplexity and Downstream Tasks

For language models, **perplexity** is a primary metric reflecting how well the model predicts the next token. Quantization noise can degrade perplexity, indicating loss of model quality.

In practice, the goal is to minimize the increase in perplexity due to quantization. BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that ternary quantization with proper training can match the perplexity of full-precision models starting from 3B parameters.

Similarly, zero-shot task accuracy on benchmarks like ARC, Hellaswag, and Winogrande is used to assess the impact of quantization on downstream performance.

# Prior Work: 1-bit Models, BitNet and Related Efforts

In this chapter, we explore the landscape of prior research that has paved the way for the development of BitNet b1.58, a 1.58-bit Large Language Model (LLM) with ternary weights. Understanding these foundational works is crucial to appreciate the innovations and contributions of BitNet b1.58. We will cover the original BitNet architecture, various post-training quantization methods, low-bit kernel optimizations, hardware initiatives tailored for low-bit computation, and the limitations of previous approaches that motivated the move toward ternary weight models.

---

## Overview of BitNet: Architecture and Core Ideas

BitNet represents a significant milestone in the quest to reduce the computational and memory costs of LLMs by fundamentally rethinking how weights are represented and how matrix multiplications are performed. The core idea behind BitNet is to quantize the model weights to 1-bit values, specifically binary weights taking values in \(\{-1, +1\}\), and to replace the standard floating-point matrix multiplications with integer addition operations.

In a typical Transformer-based LLM, the bulk of computation lies in matrix multiplications within linear layers (e.g., \(\text{nn.Linear}\) modules). These operations are traditionally performed in 16-bit floating-point precision (FP16 or BF16), which, while efficient compared to 32-bit floating point, still consume significant energy and memory bandwidth. BitNet replaces these floating-point multiplications with integer additions by constraining weights to binary values. This leads to a new computation paradigm where matrix multiplication is effectively reduced to a series of additions and subtractions, which are far less energy-intensive and faster on many hardware platforms.

The BitNet architecture modifies the Transformer by substituting all linear layers with a custom \(\text{BitLinear}\) layer that operates on these binary weights. Activations are quantized to 8 bits, striking a balance between precision and efficiency. BitNet is trained from scratch with quantization-aware training techniques to maintain model accuracy despite the aggressive weight quantization.

The key benefits of BitNet include:

- **Energy Efficiency:** Integer addition consumes orders of magnitude less energy than floating-point multiplication.
- **Memory Savings:** Binary weights require only 1 bit per parameter, drastically reducing the model's memory footprint.
- **Latency Reduction:** Simplified arithmetic operations enable faster inference.

However, the original BitNet’s binary weight representation limits its expressiveness. The absence of zero in the weight set means the model cannot explicitly "turn off" certain connections, which can hinder its ability to filter features and represent sparse patterns effectively. This limitation motivates the extension to ternary weights in BitNet b1.58.

---

## Post-Training Quantization Methods: GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant

Before BitNet and its ternary extension, a dominant approach to reducing LLM size and computational cost was **post-training quantization (PTQ)**. PTQ methods quantize a pre-trained full-precision model without retraining or with minimal fine-tuning, making them attractive for practical deployment.

Several notable PTQ methods include:

- **GPTQ (Gradient-based Post-Training Quantization):** GPTQ applies a second-order error minimization technique to quantize weights to low-bit representations (e.g., 4-bit) while minimizing the impact on model accuracy. It uses Hessian information to guide quantization, achieving impressive results on large models without retraining.

- **AWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization):** AWQ improves upon GPTQ by considering the distribution of activations during quantization, leading to better accuracy retention. It also targets 4-bit weight quantization and is designed to be efficient for inference.

- **SmoothQuant:** SmoothQuant focuses on jointly quantizing weights and activations by smoothing the quantization error across layers. It enables 4-bit quantization of both weights and activations with minimal accuracy loss.

While PTQ methods have been widely adopted in industry due to their ease of use and compatibility with existing models, they have inherent limitations:

- **Suboptimality:** Since PTQ does not involve full retraining, it cannot fully adapt the model to the quantized representation, often resulting in accuracy degradation, especially at very low bitwidths (e.g., below 4 bits).

- **Limited Bitwidth Reduction:** PTQ methods typically struggle to maintain accuracy below 4 bits, making 1-bit or ternary quantization challenging.

- **Hardware Constraints:** PTQ methods often rely on floating-point operations during inference, limiting the potential energy and latency gains.

These limitations highlight the need for architectures designed from the ground up for low-bit quantization, such as BitNet and its ternary extension.

---

## Low-bit Kernels and System-Level Optimizations: Ladder and 2-bit Kernels

Achieving practical speedups and efficiency gains from low-bit quantization requires not only algorithmic advances but also system-level optimizations and specialized kernels that can exploit the reduced precision.

One such effort is the development of **Ladder kernels**, which provide optimized implementations for 2-bit matrix multiplication on GPUs. These kernels pack low-bit weights efficiently and perform matrix multiplications using integer arithmetic, enabling significant speedups over full-precision baselines.

The Ladder 2-bit kernel is integrated into the BitNet b1.58 system to accelerate inference. Although BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights (1.58 bits on average), the 2-bit kernel can efficiently handle the ternary representation by encoding the three possible weight values within 2 bits. This kernel supports:

- **Efficient Memory Layout:** Compact storage of low-bit weights reduces memory bandwidth requirements.
- **Integer Arithmetic:** Replacing floating-point multiplications with integer additions and bitwise operations.
- **Parallelism:** Leveraging GPU parallelism to maximize throughput.

# BitNet b1.58: Design Principles and Architecture

In this chapter, we delve into the design and architectural innovations behind BitNet b1.58, a novel large language model (LLM) variant that operates with ternary weights and low-bit activations. BitNet b1.58 represents a significant departure from traditional full-precision Transformer models by adopting a 1.58-bit weight representation and an integer-add matrix multiplication paradigm. This design achieves remarkable efficiency gains in memory, latency, throughput, and energy consumption, while maintaining accuracy on par with FP16 baselines. We will explore the meaning of "1.58 bits," the core design goals, the BitLinear layer that replaces standard linear layers, the computational implications of the integer-add paradigm, activation quantization strategies, the adoption of LLaMA-style architectural components, and the treatment of embeddings.

## What Does "1.58 Bits" Mean? Ternary Weights and Entropy Calculation

The term "1.58 bits" in BitNet b1.58 refers to the effective bit-width used to represent each model parameter (weight). Unlike conventional floating-point representations such as FP16 (16 bits) or BF16, BitNet b1.58 constrains each weight to one of three discrete values: \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\). This ternary set introduces a quantization scheme that is more expressive than binary weights \(\{-1, +1\}\) but still extremely compact compared to full-precision floating-point numbers.

To understand why this corresponds to approximately 1.58 bits, consider the entropy of a ternary distribution. If the weights are uniformly distributed over the three values, the entropy \(H\) in bits per weight is:

\[
H = -\sum_{i=1}^3 p_i \log_2 p_i,
\]

where \(p_i\) is the probability of each weight value. For a uniform distribution, \(p_i = \frac{1}{3}\), so

\[
H = -3 \times \frac{1}{3} \log_2 \frac{1}{3} = \log_2 3 \approx 1.58 \text{ bits}.
\]

This entropy represents the theoretical minimum average number of bits needed to encode each weight without loss, assuming uniform distribution. In practice, the actual bit usage may be slightly higher due to encoding overhead, but the ternary representation drastically reduces storage compared to 16-bit floating-point.

The inclusion of zero as a weight value is crucial. It allows the model to explicitly "turn off" certain connections, introducing sparsity and enabling feature filtering. This added expressiveness improves the model's representational capacity compared to binary weights, which can only flip signs but cannot nullify connections.

## Design Goals: Accuracy Parity with FP16, Reduced Energy & Memory

The primary design goals of BitNet b1.58 are twofold: first, to achieve accuracy comparable to full-precision FP16 LLMs, and second, to drastically reduce computational cost, memory footprint, latency, and energy consumption during inference and training.

Achieving accuracy parity is challenging because quantizing weights to ternary values inherently reduces the representational precision of the model. However, BitNet b1.58 overcomes this by carefully designing the quantization function, training from scratch with quantization-aware techniques, and leveraging architectural components that maintain expressiveness.

On the efficiency side, the ternary weights enable a new computation paradigm that replaces expensive floating-point multiplications with simple integer additions. This shift reduces the arithmetic energy cost by over 70 times compared to FP16 matrix multiplications on modern chips. Additionally, the memory required to store model parameters shrinks by a factor of roughly 10, enabling faster weight loading from DRAM and reduced bandwidth requirements.

Together, these design goals position BitNet b1.58 as a Pareto improvement over traditional LLMs: it matches or exceeds accuracy while significantly lowering inference latency, memory usage, throughput bottlenecks, and energy consumption.

## BitLinear: Replacing nn.Linear with Low-bit Multiply-Free Operations

At the heart of BitNet b1.58's architecture is the replacement of the standard fully connected linear layers (commonly implemented as \texttt{nn.Linear} in PyTorch) with a specialized layer called \texttt{BitLinear}. This layer is designed to operate on ternary weights and 8-bit activations, enabling efficient integer-add matrix multiplication.

In a conventional Transformer, the \texttt{nn.Linear} layer performs matrix multiplication between a floating-point weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}\) and an input activation matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times b}\), where \(b\) is the batch size or sequence length. This involves costly floating-point multiplications and additions.

In contrast, \texttt{BitLinear} stores weights as ternary values \(\widetilde{W} \in \{-1, 0, +1\}^{n \times m}\). The matrix multiplication is then computed as:

\[
Y = \widetilde{W} \times X,
\]

where the multiplication by \(\pm 1\) reduces to either adding or subtracting the corresponding activation vector, and multiplication by zero effectively skips the addition. This eliminates the need for floating-point multiplications entirely within the weight matrix multiplication.

This design leads to a multiply-free computation paradigm, where the dominant arithmetic operation is integer addition and subtraction. The \texttt{BitLinear} layer thus achieves significant speedups and energy savings, especially on hardware optimized for integer operations.

---

## Int-Add Matrix Multiplication Paradigm and Computational Implications

The integer-add matrix multiplication paradigm enabled by ternary weights is a fundamental shift from traditional floating-point matrix multiplication. Instead of performing multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations, the computation reduces to a series of conditional additions and subtractions.

Concretely, for each output element \(y_i\) in the product \(Y = \widetilde{W} X\), the calculation is:

\[
y_i = \sum_{j=1}^m \widetilde{W}_{ij} \cdot X_j,
\]

where \(\widetilde{W}_{ij} \in \{-1, 0, +1\}\). Since multiplication by \(\pm 1\) is equivalent to sign flipping, and multiplication by zero is skipping, the operation simplifies to:

- If \(\widetilde{W}_{ij} = +1\), add \(X_j\) to \(y_i\).
- If \(\widetilde{W}_{ij} = -1\), subtract \(X_j\) from \(y_i\).
- If \(\widetilde{W}_{ij} = 0\), do nothing.

This paradigm has several computational implications:

1. **Energy Efficiency:** Integer addition consumes significantly less energy than floating-point multiplication. On 7nm chips, this can translate to over 70x energy savings for matrix multiplication.

2. **Hardware Simplification:** The absence of multiplications allows for simpler arithmetic units, potentially enabling specialized hardware accelerators optimized for ternary matrix operations.

3. **Memory Bandwidth:** The ternary weights require fewer bits to store and transfer, reducing memory bandwidth bottlenecks during inference.

4. **Latency Reduction:** Integer-add operations can be parallelized and pipelined efficiently, leading to lower latency per token during autoregressive generation.

5. **Sparsity Exploitation:** The presence of zeros in weights introduces sparsity, which can be leveraged to skip computations and further accelerate inference.

Overall, the int-add matrix multiplication paradigm is a key enabler of BitNet b1.58's efficiency gains without sacrificing model quality.

## Activation Quantization Choices: 8-bit Activations and Token-wise Scaling

While BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights, it employs 8-bit quantization for activations. This choice balances the need for reduced precision with maintaining sufficient dynamic range and expressiveness in intermediate representations.

The activation quantization scheme in BitNet b1.58 differs from traditional zero-point quantization. Instead of scaling activations to the range \([0, Q_b]\) (where \(Q_b\) is the maximum quantized value, e.g., 255 for 8-bit), BitNet b1.58 scales activations per token to the symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\). This symmetric scaling removes the need for zero-point offsets, simplifying implementation and system-level optimization.

Formally, for each token's activation vector \(a\), the quantized activation \(\widetilde{a}\) is computed as:

\[
\widetilde{a} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{a}{s}, -Q_b, Q_b\right),
\]

where \(s\) is a scaling factor computed per token to normalize the activation range, and \(\mathrm{RoundClip}\) rounds and clips values to the quantization range.

This token-wise scaling approach has several advantages:

- **Simplicity:** Avoids the complexity of zero-point handling in quantization and dequantization.

- **Performance:** Empirically, it introduces negligible degradation in model accuracy.

- **System Optimization:** Facilitates efficient kernel implementations and hardware acceleration.

By quantizing activations to 8 bits, BitNet b1.58 reduces memory bandwidth and storage requirements for intermediate activations, which is critical for scaling to large models and long sequences.

## LLaMA-alike Components: RMSNorm, SwiGLU, Rotary Embeddings, Bias Removal

To align with popular open-source LLM architectures and facilitate community adoption, BitNet b1.58 adopts several architectural components inspired by the LLaMA family of models. These choices also contribute to the model's efficiency and performance.

1. **RMSNorm (Root Mean Square Normalization):** Instead of the more common LayerNorm, BitNet b1.58 uses RMSNorm, which normalizes activations based on their root mean square. RMSNorm is simpler, has fewer parameters, and has been shown to perform well in Transformer architectures. It also synergizes well with low-bit quantization.

2. **SwiGLU Activation:** The SwiGLU activation function is a variant of the gated linear unit (GLU) that uses the SiLU (Swish) nonlinearity. SwiGLU has been demonstrated to improve model expressiveness and training stability. Its use in BitNet b1.58 helps maintain accuracy despite quantization.

3. **Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE):** Rotary embeddings encode positional information by rotating query and key vectors in self-attention. They are computationally efficient and have become standard in LLaMA models. BitNet b1.58 incorporates RoPE to preserve positional awareness without adding significant overhead.

4. **Bias Removal:** BitNet b1.58 removes all bias terms from linear layers. This simplification reduces parameter count and memory usage, and empirical results show it does not harm performance in this context.

By adopting these LLaMA-alike components, BitNet b1.58 benefits from a well-validated architectural backbone that is compatible with existing open-source frameworks such as HuggingFace, vLLM, and llama.cpp. This compatibility lowers the barrier for integration and deployment.

## Where Embeddings Live: Full-Precision Embeddings and Why

While BitNet b1.58 quantizes all Transformer weights to ternary values, it retains the input and output embeddings in full precision (FP16 or BF16). This design choice is motivated by several considerations:

4. **Implementation Simplicity:** Embeddings are often stored and accessed differently from Transformer layers, making it practical to keep them in higher precision.

By maintaining full-precision embeddings, BitNet b1.58 strikes a balance between aggressive quantization for compute-heavy layers and preserving critical representational components. This approach contributes to the model's ability to match FP16 baselines in perplexity and downstream tasks.

# Quantization Mechanics in BitNet b1.58

In this chapter, we delve into the core quantization mechanism that underpins the BitNet b1.58 model: the absmean quantizer. Understanding this quantization function is crucial because it enables the model to represent weights in a ternary set \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\) while maintaining performance comparable to full-precision models. We will explore the mathematical formulation of the quantizer, the intuition behind its design choices, and how it is applied both to weights and activations. We will also discuss implementation details that ensure numerical stability and effective gradient propagation during training. Finally, we will contrast this approach with alternatives and explain why absmean quantization was selected.

---

## Mathematical Definition of Absmean Quantization

At the heart of BitNet b1.58’s quantization scheme is the absmean quantizer, which maps continuous-valued weights \(W\) to discrete ternary values \(\widetilde{W} \in \{-1, 0, +1\}\). The quantization process involves two main steps: scaling and rounding with clipping.

Formally, given a weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}\), the quantized weights \(\widetilde{W}\) are computed as:

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]

where \(\gamma\) is a scale parameter defined as the average absolute value of the weights:

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m |W_{ij}|,
\]

and \(\epsilon\) is a small positive constant added for numerical stability to avoid division by zero.

The function \(\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b)\) rounds \(x\) to the nearest integer and then clips the result to lie within the interval \([a, b]\):

\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\left(a, \min\left(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\right)\right).
\]

This quantization function ensures that each weight is mapped to one of three discrete values: \(-1\), \(0\), or \(+1\).

---

## Computing the Scale Parameter \(\gamma\) and the Role of \(\epsilon\)

The scale parameter \(\gamma\) plays a critical role in normalizing the weight matrix before quantization. By dividing each weight by \(\gamma\), the quantizer effectively rescales the weight distribution so that the average magnitude of the scaled weights is approximately 1. This normalization centers the quantization thresholds around the typical magnitude of the weights, making the ternary quantization more balanced and representative.

Intuitively, \(\gamma\) captures the "typical" absolute weight magnitude in the matrix. Without this scaling, weights with large magnitudes would be clipped excessively, and weights with small magnitudes would be quantized mostly to zero, leading to poor representation.

The small constant \(\epsilon\) is added to the denominator to prevent division by zero in pathological cases where the weight matrix might be all zeros or have extremely small values. This ensures numerical stability during training and inference.

---

## RoundClip Function: Rounding Behavior and Clipping Rationale

The \(\mathrm{RoundClip}\) function is a simple yet effective mechanism to discretize scaled weights into the ternary set. It first rounds the scaled weight to the nearest integer, which would naturally be one of \(\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\). However, since the ternary quantization only allows values in \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\), the function clips any values outside this range to the nearest boundary.

This clipping is essential because it prevents outliers or extreme weight values from being quantized to values outside the ternary set, which would violate the model’s design constraints. The rounding step ensures that weights close to zero are quantized to zero, while weights with sufficiently large magnitude are quantized to \(\pm 1\).

The combined rounding and clipping operation can be viewed as a projection of the scaled weights onto the ternary set, preserving as much information as possible given the limited bit budget.

---

## Why Include Zero? Role of Sparsity and Feature Filtering

A key innovation in BitNet b1.58 compared to the original 1-bit BitNet is the inclusion of zero as a possible quantized weight value, expanding the weight set from binary \(\{-1, +1\}\) to ternary \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\). This seemingly small change has profound implications for the model’s representational power and efficiency.

Including zero allows the model to explicitly "turn off" certain connections or features, effectively introducing sparsity into the weight matrices. This sparsity acts as a form of feature filtering, where unimportant or noisy features can be suppressed by assigning zero weights. This selective pruning improves the model’s ability to focus on salient information and reduces interference from irrelevant signals.

From a computational perspective, zero weights mean that certain multiplications can be skipped or simplified, further reducing computation and energy consumption. Moreover, the ternary representation still only requires about 1.58 bits per weight (since \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.58\)), maintaining a very low memory footprint.

Empirically, the inclusion of zero has been shown to significantly improve the performance of 1-bit LLMs, enabling BitNet b1.58 to match full-precision baselines in perplexity and downstream tasks starting from 3B model sizes.

---

## Activation Scaling to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) Per Token vs Zero-Point Schemes

In addition to weight quantization, BitNet b1.58 applies quantization to activations, which are represented in 8 bits. Unlike some quantization schemes that scale activations to the range \([0, Q_b]\) with a zero-point offset, BitNet b1.58 scales activations to the symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) on a per-token basis.

Here, \(Q_b\) denotes the maximum quantized value representable in the chosen bitwidth (for 8 bits, \(Q_b = 127\) or \(255\) depending on signedness).

The per-token scaling means that for each token’s activation vector, the values are linearly scaled so that the minimum and maximum values map to \(-Q_b\) and \(Q_b\), respectively. This approach eliminates the need for zero-point quantization, simplifying the quantization and dequantization process.

The advantages of this symmetric, per-token scaling include:

- **Simplified implementation:** No need to store or compute zero-points, which reduces overhead.
- **Better numerical stability:** Symmetric ranges avoid biasing activations towards positive or negative values.
- **Negligible performance impact:** Experiments show that this scaling scheme does not degrade model accuracy.

This design choice aligns well with the ternary weight quantization, maintaining consistency in the quantization framework.

---

## Implementation Details: Numerical Stability and Backpropagation

Implementing the absmean quantizer in a training pipeline requires careful attention to numerical stability and gradient flow.

- **Numerical Stability:** The addition of \(\epsilon\) in the denominator of the scaling step prevents division by zero or extremely small values, which could cause exploding gradients or NaNs during training.

- **Gradient Propagation:** Since the quantization function involves non-differentiable operations (rounding and clipping), BitNet b1.58 employs the Straight-Through Estimator (STE) technique during backpropagation. STE approximates the gradient of the quantization function as identity or a clipped identity, allowing gradients to flow through the discrete quantization step.

- **Clamping and Regularization:** To prevent weights from drifting too far outside the quantization range, clamping or regularization techniques may be applied during training. This ensures that the weights remain well-distributed around the scale \(\gamma\), facilitating stable quantization.

- **Per-layer or Per-matrix Scaling:** The scale \(\gamma\) is computed per weight matrix (e.g., per linear layer), which balances granularity and computational overhead. This local scaling adapts to the distribution of weights in each layer, improving quantization fidelity.

These implementation details collectively ensure that the ternary quantization is both stable and effective during training from scratch.

---

## Alternatives and Why Absmean Was Chosen: Empirical and Theoretical Reasons

Several alternative quantization schemes exist for low-bit neural networks, including:

- **Uniform Quantization:** Dividing the range of weights into equal intervals and mapping weights accordingly.
- **Non-uniform Quantization:** Using learned or heuristic quantization levels that better fit the weight distribution.
- **Binary Quantization:** Restricting weights to \(\{-1, +1\}\) without zero.
- **Learned Thresholds:** Optimizing quantization thresholds during training.

BitNet b1.58’s absmean quantizer was chosen for several reasons:

1. **Simplicity and Efficiency:** The absmean quantizer is straightforward to implement, requiring only the computation of the average absolute weight magnitude and simple rounding/clipping operations.

2. **Adaptivity:** By scaling weights with \(\gamma\), the quantizer adapts to the distribution of weights in each layer, improving representational accuracy compared to fixed thresholds.

3. **Inclusion of Zero:** The ternary set with zero allows for sparsity and feature filtering, which binary quantization lacks.

4. **Empirical Performance:** Experiments demonstrate that absmean quantization achieves parity with full-precision models starting from 3B parameters, outperforming other low-bit quantization methods.

5. **Compatibility with Multiply-Free Computation:** The ternary weights enable matrix multiplication to be implemented with integer additions only, significantly reducing energy consumption.

6. **Stability in Training:** The absmean quantizer combined with STE and per-layer scaling leads to stable training dynamics.

In summary, the absmean quantizer strikes a balance between theoretical soundness, practical implementation, and empirical effectiveness, making it the quantization method of choice for BitNet b1.58.

## Quantization-Aware Training Techniques: STE, Clamping, and Regularization

Training with discrete ternary weights \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\) introduces non-differentiability, which complicates gradient-based optimization. BitNet b1.58 addresses this challenge using quantization-aware training (QAT) techniques, which simulate quantization effects during training to allow the model to adapt.

- **Straight-Through Estimator (STE):** The STE is a popular method to approximate gradients through discrete quantization functions. During the forward pass, weights are quantized to ternary values. During the backward pass, gradients are computed as if the quantization function were the identity, effectively passing gradients through unchanged. This approximation enables gradient descent despite the non-differentiable quantization step.

- **Clamping:** To maintain numerical stability and prevent weights from drifting outside the representable range, weights are clamped before quantization. Clamping restricts weights to a bounded interval, ensuring that the quantization function behaves predictably.

- **Regularization:** Additional regularization terms can encourage sparsity (promoting zeros in weights) or penalize large deviations from quantized values. This helps the model learn weight distributions that are more amenable to ternary quantization.

- **Scaling Factors:** The absmean quantization function scales weights by their average absolute value \(\gamma\) before rounding. This dynamic scaling adapts to the weight distribution, improving quantization fidelity.

Together, these techniques allow BitNet b1.58 to train ternary weights from scratch effectively, preserving model capacity and generalization.

## Checkpointing and Mixed-Precision Considerations

Training large LLMs requires careful management of memory and compute resources. Checkpointing strategies and mixed-precision training are essential tools.

- **Checkpointing:** Saving intermediate model states periodically allows recovery from interruptions and enables evaluation of model performance at different training stages. For BitNet b1.58, checkpointing also facilitates experimentation with quantization parameters and training schedules.

- **Mixed-Precision Training:** While BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights (1.58 bits) and 8-bit activations, other parts of the model, such as embeddings, may remain in full precision (e.g., FP16 or BF16). Mixed-precision training leverages lower precision where possible to save memory and speed up computation, while maintaining higher precision where necessary for stability.

- **Gradient Accumulation:** To simulate large batch sizes without exceeding memory limits, gradient accumulation over multiple smaller batches is often employed.

These practices ensure efficient and stable training of BitNet b1.58 models on available hardware.

## Training-from-Scratch vs Finetuning: Trade-offs and When to Use Each

The paper trains BitNet b1.58 models from scratch rather than applying post-training quantization or finetuning. This choice has important implications:

- **Training-from-Scratch:** Allows the model to learn representations optimized for ternary weights and quantized activations from the beginning. This often leads to better performance and stability, as the model adapts to the constraints of low-bit precision during all training phases.

- **Finetuning:** Starting from a pretrained full-precision model and then quantizing or finetuning can be faster and require less compute. However, it may suffer from accuracy degradation due to mismatch between the pretrained weights and the quantized representation.

- **Post-Training Quantization (PTQ):** Applying quantization after training without further optimization is simpler but often suboptimal, especially for aggressive quantization like ternary weights.

BitNet b1.58’s success in training from scratch demonstrates that ternary LLMs can achieve parity with full-precision models when given sufficient training budget and appropriate optimization. For practitioners, training from scratch is recommended when accuracy is paramount and compute resources allow. Finetuning or PTQ may be considered for rapid deployment or resource-constrained scenarios but may require additional techniques to mitigate accuracy loss.

## BitLinear Kernel Design: Packing Ternary Weights and Int-Add Routines

At the heart of BitNet b1.58 lies the BitLinear layer, a replacement for the standard \texttt{nn.Linear} layer in Transformers. The key innovation is that the weights are ternary, constrained to the set \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\), which allows matrix multiplication to be performed using integer addition operations rather than floating-point multiplications. This shift fundamentally changes the computational paradigm and enables significant energy and latency savings.

### Packing Ternary Weights

To efficiently store and process ternary weights, the BitLinear kernel packs multiple ternary values into compact bit representations. Since ternary values require \(\log_2(3) \approx 1.58\) bits per weight, the implementation uses 2 bits per weight to simplify packing and alignment on hardware. Each 2-bit segment encodes one of the three possible values:

- `00` for 0
- `01` for +1
- `10` for -1

(One bit pattern remains unused or reserved for error checking.)

This packing reduces the memory footprint by approximately 8x compared to 16-bit floating-point weights (FP16), as each FP16 weight uses 16 bits, while ternary weights use only 2 bits.

### Integer Addition Matrix Multiplication

The matrix multiplication in BitLinear is implemented as a series of integer additions and subtractions, exploiting the ternary nature of weights. Specifically, for a weight matrix \( \widetilde{W} \in \{-1,0,1\}^{n \times m} \) and an activation vector \( a \in \mathbb{R}^m \), the product \( \widetilde{W} a \) can be computed as:

\[
(\widetilde{W} a)_i = \sum_{j=1}^m \widetilde{W}_{ij} a_j = \sum_{j: \widetilde{W}_{ij} = +1} a_j - \sum_{j: \widetilde{W}_{ij} = -1} a_j
\]

This eliminates the need for multiplications entirely, replacing them with conditional additions and subtractions. The kernel implementation leverages bitwise operations to quickly identify the positions of +1 and -1 weights and accumulates the corresponding activations accordingly.

### Kernel Optimization

To maximize throughput and minimize latency, the BitLinear kernel is carefully optimized for GPU architectures. Key optimizations include:

- **Vectorized loads and stores:** Activations and packed weights are loaded in aligned blocks to maximize memory bandwidth utilization.
- **Warp-level parallelism:** Threads within a warp cooperate to process multiple output elements simultaneously.
- **Shared memory buffering:** Partial sums are accumulated in fast shared memory before being written back to global memory.
- **Loop unrolling and instruction-level parallelism:** Critical loops are unrolled to reduce branch overhead and increase pipeline utilization.

These optimizations ensure that the BitLinear kernel can outperform traditional FP16 matrix multiplication kernels, especially as model size grows.

## Memory Layout and DRAM-to-SRAM Bandwidth Considerations

Efficient memory management is critical for achieving the latency and throughput gains promised by BitNet b1.58. The memory hierarchy in modern GPUs and accelerators includes off-chip DRAM, on-chip SRAM (shared memory), and registers. The cost of moving data between these levels often dominates computation time and energy.

### Memory Footprint Reduction

By quantizing weights to ternary values, BitNet b1.58 reduces the model size by approximately 8x compared to FP16 models. This reduction has two major effects:

1. **Lower DRAM capacity requirements:** Smaller models fit more easily into limited GPU memory, enabling larger batch sizes or longer sequences.
2. **Reduced DRAM bandwidth demand:** Less data needs to be transferred per inference step, alleviating a common bottleneck.

### DRAM-to-SRAM Transfer

During inference, weights are loaded from DRAM into on-chip SRAM for fast access during matrix multiplication. The reduced size of ternary weights means that more weights can be cached on-chip, reducing the frequency of expensive DRAM accesses.

Moreover, the bandwidth savings translate into lower latency because the time to load weights is a significant portion of the total inference time, especially for large models.

### Memory Layout Strategies

To maximize efficiency, the memory layout of weights and activations is carefully designed:

- **Contiguous packing:** Ternary weights are packed contiguously in memory to enable coalesced memory accesses.
- **Alignment:** Data structures are aligned to cache line boundaries to avoid unaligned memory penalties.
- **Blocking:** Matrices are partitioned into blocks that fit into shared memory, allowing reuse of data and reducing global memory traffic.

These strategies ensure that the BitLinear kernel and Ladder kernel can operate at peak memory bandwidth and compute efficiency.

## Perplexity as a Primary Measure for Language Model Quality: WikiText2 and C4

Perplexity is a fundamental metric for evaluating language models, quantifying how well a model predicts a sequence of tokens. Intuitively, perplexity measures the uncertainty of the model when predicting the next token in a text. A lower perplexity indicates that the model assigns higher probability to the correct next token, reflecting better language understanding and generation capabilities.

Formally, for a sequence of tokens \( t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_N \), the perplexity \( PP \) is defined as:

\[
PP = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log p(t_i | t_1, \ldots, t_{i-1})\right),
\]

where \( p(t_i | t_1, \ldots, t_{i-1}) \) is the probability assigned by the model to token \( t_i \) given the preceding context.

In the paper, perplexity is evaluated on two widely used benchmark datasets:

- **WikiText2**: A dataset consisting of over 100 million tokens extracted from Wikipedia articles. It is a standard benchmark for language modeling, providing a clean and diverse corpus for evaluation.

- **C4 (Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus)**: A large-scale dataset derived from web crawl data, cleaned and filtered to remove noise. It represents a more challenging and diverse distribution of natural language.

Evaluating perplexity on these datasets allows the authors to measure the intrinsic language modeling capability of BitNet b1.58 compared to FP16 LLaMA models. Since perplexity directly reflects the model's predictive accuracy, it serves as a primary quantitative measure to assess whether the ternary-weight model can match or approach the performance of full-precision counterparts.

# Ablation Studies and Design Insights

In this chapter, we delve into the ablation studies and design choices that underpin the success of BitNet b1.58, a ternary-weight large language model (LLM) variant. Ablation studies are critical for understanding which components and design decisions contribute most to the model’s performance and efficiency. By systematically varying or removing certain elements, we can isolate their effects and gain intuition about the trade-offs involved in building low-bit LLMs.

BitNet b1.58’s key innovation is the use of ternary weights \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\), which correspond to 1.58 bits per parameter in the binary information-theoretic sense. This design choice, along with activation quantization, scaling methods, and kernel optimizations, enables the model to match full-precision (FP16) baselines in perplexity and downstream tasks while drastically reducing memory, latency, and energy consumption. We now analyze the impact of these design choices in detail.

---

## Effect of Including Zero in the Ternary Set vs Binary Weights

One of the most fundamental design decisions in BitNet b1.58 is the choice to use ternary weights \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\) rather than strictly binary weights \(\{-1, +1\}\). This seemingly small addition of the zero value has profound implications for model capacity and performance.

### Intuition and Role of Zero

Including zero in the weight set introduces sparsity and the ability to explicitly "turn off" certain connections in the network. This acts as a form of feature filtering or gating, allowing the model to selectively ignore irrelevant or noisy features. In contrast, binary weights force every connection to be either positively or negatively weighted, which can limit expressiveness and lead to noisy or less stable representations.

From an information perspective, ternary weights have higher entropy than binary weights, allowing for a richer representation of the learned parameters. The effective bit-width increases from 1 bit (binary) to approximately 1.58 bits (ternary), reflecting this additional representational capacity.

### Empirical Findings

Ablation experiments comparing binary and ternary weight sets show that ternary weights significantly improve perplexity and downstream task accuracy. The zero value enables the model to better approximate the full-precision weight distributions, leading to better generalization and robustness.

Moreover, ternary weights maintain the computational advantages of 1-bit models because the matrix multiplication can still be implemented with integer additions and sign flips, avoiding costly floating-point multiplications. The zero weights simply skip additions, which can be efficiently handled in optimized kernels.

### Summary

Including zero in the weight quantization set is a crucial design choice that balances model capacity and computational efficiency. It enables explicit sparsity and feature filtering, which improves performance without sacrificing the low-bit computational paradigm.

---

## Activation Bitwidth Ablations: 8-bit vs Lower/Higher

While BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights, the activations are quantized to 8 bits. The choice of activation bitwidth is another important design parameter that affects model accuracy, latency, and memory.

### Why 8-bit Activations?

Activations in neural networks tend to have a wider dynamic range and more complex distributions than weights. Using too low a bitwidth for activations can introduce significant quantization noise, harming model performance. On the other hand, higher bitwidth activations increase memory usage and computational cost.

The choice of 8-bit activations in BitNet b1.58 strikes a balance: it reduces memory and bandwidth requirements by a factor of two compared to FP16 activations, while maintaining sufficient precision to preserve model accuracy.

### Ablation Results

Experiments varying activation bitwidth show that reducing activations below 8 bits (e.g., 4 bits) leads to noticeable degradation in perplexity and downstream task performance. Increasing activation precision beyond 8 bits (e.g., 16 bits) yields marginal accuracy improvements but at a significant cost in memory and latency, negating the benefits of low-bit weights.

Thus, 8-bit activations are an effective sweet spot for BitNet b1.58, enabling efficient inference with minimal accuracy loss.

### Token-wise Scaling

Another subtlety is the use of token-wise scaling of activations to the symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\), where \(Q_b = 2^{b-1} - 1\) for \(b\)-bit activations. This approach avoids zero-point quantization and simplifies implementation, while introducing negligible performance impact. It also facilitates system-level optimizations.

---

## Quantizer Variants: Absmean vs Alternatives and Their Effects

The quantization function used to map full-precision weights to ternary values is critical for stable training and final model quality. BitNet b1.58 employs an "absmean" quantizer, which scales the weight matrix by its average absolute value before rounding and clipping to \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\).

### Mathematical Formulation Recap

Given a weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}\), the quantized weights \(\widetilde{W}\) are computed as:

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm} \sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|
\]

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)
\]

where \(\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x)))\) and \(\epsilon\) is a small constant for numerical stability.

### Intuition Behind Absmean Quantization

Scaling by the average absolute weight magnitude normalizes the weight distribution, ensuring that the quantization thresholds are adaptive to the scale of the weights. This prevents systematic bias in quantization and helps maintain the relative importance of weights.

The rounding and clipping step enforces the ternary constraint while minimizing quantization error.

### Comparison to Alternatives

Alternative quantizers might use max-absolute scaling, learned scaling factors, or non-uniform quantization schemes. However, these can introduce instability or require additional parameters and complexity.

Empirical ablations show that absmean quantization provides a good trade-off between simplicity, stability, and accuracy. It is easy to implement, computationally cheap, and effective in practice.

### Activation Quantization

For activations, BitNet b1.58 uses a similar quantization scheme but scales activations per token to a symmetric range, avoiding zero-point offsets. This design choice simplifies hardware implementation and reduces quantization artifacts.

---

## Embedding Precision Ablation: Impact of Keeping Embeddings in FP16

Embeddings constitute a significant portion of the parameter count in LLMs, but their quantization is often treated differently from the rest of the model.

### Why Keep Embeddings in Full Precision?

Embeddings map discrete tokens to continuous vectors and are critical for capturing rich semantic information. Quantizing embeddings aggressively can degrade the quality of token representations, harming downstream performance.

Moreover, embeddings are accessed frequently but do not dominate the compute cost compared to matrix multiplications in Transformer layers. Therefore, keeping embeddings in FP16 or BF16 precision is a reasonable trade-off.

### Ablation Findings

Experiments quantizing embeddings to ternary or low-bit formats show a drop in perplexity and task accuracy. Conversely, maintaining embeddings in FP16 preserves model quality while still achieving large overall memory savings due to the quantization of the bulk of the model weights.

### Practical Implications

This design choice simplifies integration with existing frameworks and hardware, as embeddings can be stored and accessed in standard floating-point formats, while the rest of the model benefits from low-bit quantization.

---

## Kernel Optimizations and Their Marginal Gains for Latency

The computational efficiency of BitNet b1.58 depends not only on the quantization scheme but also on the implementation of the core matrix multiplication kernels.

### BitLinear Kernel and Ladder 2-bit Kernel

BitNet b1.58 replaces standard \texttt{nn.Linear} layers with \texttt{BitLinear}, which performs matrix multiplication using integer additions and sign flips, avoiding floating-point multiplications.

The Ladder 2-bit kernel is integrated to further optimize the ternary weight operations, packing weights efficiently and exploiting hardware parallelism.

### Ablation on Kernel Optimizations

Profiling experiments show that kernel-level optimizations yield significant latency reductions, especially for large models where \texttt{nn.Linear} layers dominate runtime.

However, the marginal gains from further kernel tuning diminish beyond a point, as other components (e.g., embedding lookups, normalization) become bottlenecks.

### System-Level Considerations

Optimizing kernels for ternary weights enables BitNet b1.58 to achieve 2.4x to 4.1x speedups over FP16 baselines, depending on model size. These gains scale with model size because matrix multiplication cost grows superlinearly with parameter count.

---

## Sensitivity to Training Tokens and Regularization Choices

Training low-bit LLMs from scratch requires careful consideration of training data volume and regularization to ensure convergence and generalization.

### Training Token Budget

BitNet b1.58 models trained on 100 billion tokens match FP16 baselines starting from 3B parameters. Increasing the training tokens to 2 trillion, following the StableLM-3B recipe, further improves zero-shot task performance, demonstrating strong scalability.

### Regularization and Quantization-Aware Training

Regularization techniques such as weight decay, gradient clipping, and straight-through estimators (STE) for discrete weights are essential to stabilize training.

Ablations indicate that insufficient regularization or token budget leads to degraded performance, highlighting the importance of training recipe tuning.

---

## Practical Takeaways for Model Designers

From the ablation studies and design insights, several practical guidelines emerge for designing ternary LLMs like BitNet b1.58:

1. **Include zero in the weight quantization set** to enable sparsity and feature filtering, improving accuracy without sacrificing efficiency.

2. **Use 8-bit activations** as a sweet spot for balancing precision and resource usage; lower bitwidths harm performance, higher bitwidths increase cost.

3. **Adopt absmean quantization** for weights to ensure stable and effective scaling, avoiding complex learned quantizers.

4. **Keep embeddings in full precision** to preserve semantic richness and avoid accuracy degradation.

5. **Invest in kernel optimizations** for ternary matrix multiplication to realize latency and throughput gains, especially for large models.

6. **Train with sufficient tokens and regularization** to ensure convergence and generalization in low-bit regimes.

7. **Leverage token-wise symmetric activation scaling** to simplify implementation and maintain performance.

By following these principles, model designers can build low-bit LLMs that achieve parity with full-precision models while dramatically reducing inference cost, enabling new applications and hardware designs.

---

## Profiling Breakdown: Compute vs Memory-Bound Stages

Large Language Models, particularly Transformer-based architectures like LLaMA, spend the majority of their inference time and energy budget on matrix multiplications within linear layers. These layers dominate both the floating-point operations (FLOPs) and memory bandwidth requirements. Specifically, the \texttt{nn.Linear} modules, which perform dense matrix multiplications, are the primary computational bottleneck.

Profiling a typical FP16 LLaMA model reveals two main categories of resource consumption:

1. **Compute-bound stages:** These include matrix multiplications in the attention and feed-forward networks (FFNs). The cost here is dominated by floating-point multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations, which are energy-intensive and latency-critical.

2. **Memory-bound stages:** These involve loading model parameters (weights) and activations from DRAM to on-chip memory (e.g., SRAM or caches). Memory bandwidth and latency can limit throughput, especially for large models where weights do not fit entirely on-chip.

In FP16 models, each weight and activation is represented with 16 bits, leading to significant memory traffic and energy consumption. The arithmetic operations require floating-point multiplications and additions, which are more costly than integer operations.

In contrast, BitNet b1.58 replaces the floating-point weights with ternary weights \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\), encoded in approximately 1.58 bits per parameter. This enables matrix multiplications to be performed using integer additions and subtractions only, eliminating the need for floating-point multiplications. The activations are quantized to 8 bits, further reducing memory bandwidth and storage requirements.

This fundamental shift changes the profiling landscape:

- **Compute cost:** The expensive floating-point multiplications are replaced by integer additions, which consume significantly less energy and have lower latency.

- **Memory cost:** The model size shrinks by a factor of roughly 10x compared to FP16 (16 bits to 1.58 bits), reducing DRAM bandwidth requirements and enabling more weights to fit in faster on-chip memory.

Thus, BitNet b1.58 shifts the bottleneck from compute-bound to a more balanced or even memory-bound regime, but with much lower absolute costs in both dimensions.

## Why Speedups Grow with Model Size: Analysis of \texttt{nn.Linear} Scaling

The speedup of BitNet b1.58 over FP16 LLaMA models increases as the model size grows. This phenomenon can be understood by analyzing how the cost of \texttt{nn.Linear} layers scales with model size.

In Transformer architectures, the number of parameters and FLOPs in linear layers grows roughly linearly with model size. Larger models have more layers and wider hidden dimensions, leading to more matrix multiplications. Since \texttt{nn.Linear} operations dominate the inference time, their cost becomes more pronounced as models scale.

For FP16 models, each matrix multiplication involves many floating-point multiplications and additions, which are costly in terms of latency and energy. As the model size increases, the total number of these operations grows, causing inference latency to increase significantly.

In BitNet b1.58, the matrix multiplications are replaced by integer additions and subtractions, which are much cheaper. The cost per operation is lower, and the memory footprint is smaller, allowing more efficient data movement and caching.

Because the cost of \texttt{nn.Linear} layers grows with model size, the relative advantage of BitNet b1.58’s low-bit integer operations becomes more significant for larger models. This explains the observed speedups:

- At 3B parameters, BitNet b1.58 is approximately 2.7 times faster than FP16 LLaMA.

- At 70B parameters, the speedup increases to about 4.1 times.

This scaling behavior highlights that BitNet b1.58’s benefits compound with model size, making it especially attractive for very large models where inference cost is a critical bottleneck.

## Memory Footprint Trends and the Role of Embedding Size

Memory consumption during inference is a critical factor affecting latency, throughput, and energy. The total memory footprint includes:

- **Model weights:** Stored in DRAM and transferred to on-chip memory during inference.

- **Activations:** Intermediate outputs stored during computation, including key-value (KV) caches for attention.

- **Embeddings:** Input token embeddings and output projection layers.

In BitNet b1.58, the weights are quantized to ternary values, reducing their storage requirement to approximately 1.58 bits per parameter. This leads to a substantial reduction in the memory needed to store and transfer weights compared to FP16 (16 bits per parameter).

However, embeddings remain in full precision (FP16 or BF16) in BitNet b1.58. This is because embeddings are critical for model expressivity and are relatively small compared to the total number of parameters in large models. As model size increases, the proportion of memory occupied by embeddings decreases, making the ternary quantization of the bulk of the model more impactful.

The memory footprint trends observed are:

- For smaller models, embeddings constitute a larger fraction of total memory, so the relative memory savings from ternary weights are somewhat less pronounced.

- For larger models (e.g., 70B parameters), embeddings are a smaller fraction, and the memory savings from ternary weights dominate, leading to up to 3.5 times reduction in GPU memory usage.

Additionally, activations in BitNet b1.58 are quantized to 8 bits, halving the memory required compared to FP16 activations. This further reduces the memory footprint, especially for KV caches in long-context inference.

Overall, the memory footprint reduction enables faster weight loading from DRAM, better caching, and the possibility of fitting larger models or longer contexts within the same hardware constraints.

## Throughput Analysis under Batching and Pipeline Parallelism

Throughput, measured as tokens processed per second, is a key metric for practical LLM deployment. It depends on both latency per token and the ability to process multiple tokens or sequences in parallel (batching).

BitNet b1.58’s reduced memory footprint and lower computational cost enable larger batch sizes before hitting GPU memory limits. This is particularly important for large models, where memory constraints often limit batch size and thus throughput.

In experiments with the 70B parameter model on two 80GB A100 GPUs using pipeline parallelism, BitNet b1.58 supports batch sizes up to 11 times larger than FP16 LLaMA. This translates to an 8.9 times higher throughput.

Pipeline parallelism splits the model across multiple devices, allowing concurrent processing of different layers or micro-batches. BitNet b1.58’s smaller memory footprint reduces inter-device communication overhead and memory pressure, improving pipeline efficiency.

Batching also amortizes fixed overheads such as kernel launches and memory transfers, further enhancing throughput. The ability to increase batch size without exceeding memory limits is a direct consequence of BitNet b1.58’s compact weight and activation representations.

Thus, BitNet b1.58 not only reduces per-token latency but also enables significantly higher throughput in practical multi-GPU inference setups.

## Energy Model: Arithmetic Energy per Operation and Chip-Level Estimates

Energy consumption is a critical concern for deploying LLMs at scale, both economically and environmentally. The energy cost of inference is dominated by arithmetic operations and memory accesses.

The paper estimates energy consumption focusing on matrix multiplications, which dominate compute cost. The key points are:

- **FP16 LLaMA:** Matrix multiplications involve floating-point multiplications and additions, which are energy-intensive.

- **BitNet b1.58:** Matrix multiplications are replaced by integer additions and subtractions (INT8), which consume significantly less energy.

This dramatic reduction arises because:

- The ternary weights allow multiply-free matrix multiplication, eliminating the most expensive operations.

The total end-to-end energy consumption for inference with 512 tokens also shows increasing efficiency gains as model size grows. This is because the proportion of compute spent in \texttt{nn.Linear} layers increases with model size, amplifying the benefits of BitNet b1.58’s low-bit arithmetic.

Memory access energy is also reduced due to smaller weight and activation sizes, though the paper focuses primarily on arithmetic energy.

These energy savings have profound implications for the environmental impact and operational cost of deploying large-scale LLMs.

## 1-bit Mixture-of-Experts: Memory and Communication Benefits

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures have emerged as a powerful approach to scale LLMs efficiently by activating only a subset of model parameters (experts) per input token. This conditional computation reduces the total floating-point operations (FLOPs) required during inference, enabling larger models without proportional increases in compute cost. However, MoE models face significant challenges in deployment, primarily due to their high memory footprint and the communication overhead involved in routing activations between experts distributed across multiple devices.

BitNet b1.58’s ternary weight representation directly addresses these challenges by drastically reducing the memory requirements of each expert. Since each parameter is stored in approximately 1.58 bits instead of 16 bits (FP16), the total memory footprint of an MoE model shrinks by nearly an order of magnitude. This reduction means that more experts can fit into the memory of a single device or fewer devices are needed to host the entire MoE model. Consequently, the inter-device communication overhead, which is a major bottleneck in MoE inference, is significantly alleviated.

Moreover, the communication of activations between experts—often a costly operation in distributed MoE systems—benefits from the reduced precision of activations in BitNet b1.58. With activations quantized to 8 bits and weights ternarized, the bandwidth required for transferring intermediate data is lowered, reducing latency and energy consumption. In an ideal scenario, the entire MoE model could be deployed on a single chip, eliminating inter-chip communication overhead altogether.

In summary, BitNet b1.58 enables a new generation of 1-bit MoE LLMs that are not only computationally efficient but also more practical to deploy at scale, overcoming key limitations of traditional MoE architectures.

This enhanced capacity for long-context inference is particularly valuable in real-world applications where context length is a bottleneck. # Hardware and Future Directions

The advent of 1-bit Large Language Models (LLMs), exemplified by BitNet b1.58, marks a transformative moment not only in model design but also in the hardware landscape that supports these models. This chapter explores the hardware implications of 1-bit LLMs, the opportunities for specialized accelerator design, and the promising avenues for future research. We also discuss the open challenges that remain in training and deploying such models, and the need for standardized benchmarks to evaluate low-bit LLMs effectively.

## Why New Hardware Matters: LPUs and Multiply-Free Compute Units

Traditional hardware accelerators for neural networks, such as GPUs and TPUs, are optimized for floating-point matrix multiplications, which dominate the computation in full-precision LLMs. These devices rely heavily on multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations, which consume significant energy and silicon area. However, BitNet b1.58 introduces a fundamentally different computation paradigm: matrix multiplications are replaced by integer additions with ternary weights \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\), eliminating the need for multiplications altogether.

This shift opens the door for designing specialized hardware units known as **Logic Processing Units (LPUs)** or multiply-free compute units. LPUs focus on efficient integer addition and logical operations rather than floating-point multiplication. Because addition is significantly less energy-intensive than multiplication, LPUs can achieve orders of magnitude improvements in energy efficiency and throughput.

For example, the BitNet b1.58 model’s matrix multiplication involves only integer additions, which can be implemented with simpler arithmetic circuits, reducing power consumption and heat dissipation. This is crucial because power and thermal constraints are often the bottleneck in scaling LLM inference and training. By moving to LPUs, hardware designers can build accelerators that are not only faster but also more cost-effective and environmentally friendly.

Moreover, the reduced complexity of the arithmetic units allows for denser packing of compute cores on a chip, increasing parallelism and throughput. This is especially beneficial for large-scale LLMs where matrix multiplications dominate runtime. The new hardware paradigm aligns perfectly with the BitNet b1.58 architecture, enabling a symbiotic relationship between model design and hardware capabilities.

## Hardware Design Primitives: Int-Add Arrays, Memory Hierarchies, and Bandwidth

Designing hardware optimized for 1-bit LLMs requires rethinking the fundamental building blocks of accelerators. The key primitives include:

1. **Integer-Add Arrays:** Instead of floating-point MAC arrays, the core compute units consist of large arrays of integer adders capable of efficiently summing ternary-weighted inputs. These arrays can be simpler and faster, as they avoid the complexity of floating-point multipliers.

2. **Memory Hierarchies Optimized for Low-bit Data:** Since BitNet b1.58 weights are ternary (1.58 bits per parameter), the memory system can be designed to store and transfer these weights in highly compressed formats. This reduces the bandwidth requirements between off-chip DRAM and on-chip SRAM or register files. The smaller memory footprint also allows for larger portions of the model to be cached on-chip, reducing latency and energy consumption.

3. **Bandwidth Considerations:** Memory bandwidth is often a limiting factor in LLM inference. The ternary representation reduces the amount of data that must be moved per operation, alleviating bandwidth bottlenecks. Hardware can be designed with narrower buses and optimized data paths tailored to the 1.58-bit format, further improving efficiency.

4. **Support for Sparse Computation:** The inclusion of zero in the ternary weight set introduces sparsity, which can be exploited by hardware to skip unnecessary computations and memory accesses. Specialized sparse matrix multiplication units can leverage this to reduce energy and latency.

5. **Activation Storage and Processing:** BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit activations, which are more compact than full-precision activations. Hardware can be designed to efficiently handle these lower-bit activations, including support for per-token scaling as used in the model, to maintain accuracy while minimizing resource usage.

Together, these design primitives form the foundation for a new class of accelerators that are tightly coupled with the computational characteristics of 1-bit LLMs, enabling unprecedented efficiency gains.

## Opportunities for Further Compressing Activations and KV Caches

While BitNet b1.58 achieves ternary weights and 8-bit activations, there is potential to push compression even further, especially for activations and key-value (KV) caches used in transformer attention mechanisms.

### Activation Compression

Activations in BitNet b1.58 are quantized to 8 bits with per-token scaling to the range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\), which simplifies zero-point handling and maintains performance. However, future work could explore:

- **Lower-bit activations:** Reducing activations to 4 bits or fewer could further decrease memory and bandwidth requirements. This is challenging because activations are dynamic and sensitive to quantization noise, but careful quantization-aware training and calibration may enable this.

- **Lossless or near-lossless compression:** Techniques such as entropy coding or learned compression schemes could be applied to activations to reduce storage without degrading accuracy.

### KV Cache Compression

The KV cache stores intermediate key and value vectors for each token in the context window during autoregressive inference. This cache grows linearly with sequence length and is a major contributor to memory usage in long-context LLMs.

BitNet b1.58’s reduced activation bitwidth already halves the memory needed for KV caches compared to FP16 models. Further opportunities include:

- **Quantizing KV caches to lower bits:** Similar to activations, KV caches could be quantized to 4 bits or even ternary representations, provided that the model can tolerate the induced noise.

- **Sparse or structured KV caches:** Leveraging sparsity or low-rank approximations in KV caches could reduce memory and computation.

- **Dynamic compression:** Compressing KV caches on-the-fly during inference and decompressing as needed could balance memory savings and latency.

These compression strategies would enable native support for longer sequences on the same hardware resources, a critical capability for many real-world applications.

## Research Directions: 1-bit MoE, Adaptive Precision, and Mixed-Precision Kernels

The success of BitNet b1.58 opens several exciting research directions that combine low-bit quantization with other model and system innovations.

Mixture-of-Experts architectures route inputs dynamically to specialized expert subnetworks, reducing computation by activating only a subset of experts per token. While MoE reduces FLOPs, it often increases memory usage and communication overhead, limiting scalability.

Integrating 1-bit LLMs like BitNet b1.58 into MoE architectures could:

- **Reduce memory footprint:** Ternary weights and low-bit activations shrink expert model sizes, enabling more experts to fit on-chip or on fewer devices.

Research is needed to develop training algorithms and routing mechanisms that maintain accuracy with ternary weights and low-bit activations in MoE settings.

### Adaptive Precision and Mixed-Precision Kernels

Not all parts of an LLM require the same precision. Some layers or parameters may be more sensitive to quantization than others. Future work could explore:

- **Adaptive precision schemes:** Dynamically adjusting bitwidths per layer, token, or even parameter during training and inference to optimize the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency.

- **Mixed-precision kernels:** Combining 1-bit, 2-bit, 4-bit, and full-precision operations within a single model or inference pipeline, leveraging hardware that supports multiple precisions efficiently.

These approaches could further improve the efficiency and performance of low-bit LLMs beyond uniform ternary quantization.

Addressing these challenges is critical for scaling 1-bit LLMs to even larger sizes and diverse tasks.

### Sparsity Exploitation

The ternary weight set \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\) introduces sparsity via zeros, which can be leveraged for efficiency gains. However:

- **Hardware support for sparse computation:** Efficiently skipping zero-weight multiplications or additions requires specialized hardware or software support.

- **Dynamic sparsity patterns:** The distribution of zeros may vary during training and inference, complicating optimization.

- **Balancing sparsity and accuracy:** Excessive sparsity can degrade model performance, so methods to control sparsity levels are needed.

Research into algorithms and hardware that exploit ternary sparsity without sacrificing accuracy is essential.

### Model Interpretability

Low-bit quantization changes the representational properties of LLMs. Understanding how ternary weights affect:

- **Feature representations and learned embeddings**

- **Attention patterns and internal dynamics**

- **Robustness to adversarial inputs and distribution shifts**

is important for building trustworthy and explainable AI systems. Interpretability studies tailored to low-bit LLMs are an emerging research frontier.

As 1-bit and other low-bit LLMs become more prevalent, the community requires standardized benchmarks and evaluation protocols to:

- **Compare models fairly:** Ensuring that accuracy, latency, memory, energy, and throughput are measured consistently across different hardware and software stacks.

Developing open-source tools and datasets for low-bit LLM evaluation will accelerate research and adoption, guiding both model designers and hardware architects.

## Converting an FP16 Model to Ternary vs Training-from-Scratch

One practical question is whether to convert an existing FP16 LLM to a ternary BitNet b1.58 model or to train the ternary model from scratch. Both approaches have pros and cons.

### Conversion (Post-Training Quantization)

- **Advantages:**
  - Faster deployment since no retraining is required.
  - Leverages existing high-quality FP16 checkpoints.
- **Challenges:**
  - Post-training quantization (PTQ) is often sub-optimal for 1-bit or ternary weights.
  - Performance degradation is common due to the limited representational capacity of ternary weights.
  - Requires careful calibration and possibly fine-tuning to recover accuracy.
- **Applicability:**
  - May be feasible for smaller models or when training resources are limited.
  - Less effective for very low-bit quantization like ternary weights compared to 4-bit or 8-bit.

### Training-from-Scratch

- **Advantages:**
  - Allows the model to learn representations optimized for ternary weights.
  - Achieves parity with FP16 baselines in perplexity and downstream tasks, as demonstrated by BitNet b1.58.
  - Enables the use of quantization-aware training techniques such as straight-through estimators.
- **Challenges:**
  - Requires significant compute resources and training time.
  - Needs careful hyperparameter tuning and quantization function implementation.
- **Recommendation:**
  - For best performance and scalability, training BitNet b1.58 models from scratch is preferred.
  - Conversion may be used as a quick baseline or for smaller-scale experiments.

In summary, while conversion from FP16 to ternary weights is possible, the BitNet b1.58 approach strongly favors training from scratch to fully realize the benefits of 1.58-bit LLMs.

## Measuring Latency, Memory, Throughput, and Energy Reliably

Accurate measurement of performance metrics is critical to validate the benefits of BitNet b1.58 and to guide system optimization.

### Latency Measurement

- **Definition:** Time taken to generate a single output token during inference.
- **Methodology:**
  - Use high-resolution timers (e.g., CUDA events for GPU) to measure per-token latency.
  - Warm up the model with several tokens before measurement to avoid cold-start overhead.
  - Average latency over multiple runs to reduce noise.
- **Considerations:**
  - Measure latency under realistic batch sizes and sequence lengths (e.g., 512 tokens).
  - Account for data loading and preprocessing overhead separately.

### Memory Usage

- **Definition:** Peak GPU or CPU memory consumption during inference.
- **Methodology:**
  - Use profiling tools such as NVIDIA’s `nvidia-smi` or PyTorch’s memory profiler.
  - Measure both model parameters and activation memory.
  - Include memory used by intermediate buffers and workspace allocations.
- **Considerations:**
  - Memory usage depends on batch size and sequence length; report these parameters.
  - Distinguish between DRAM and on-chip memory usage if possible.

### Throughput

- **Definition:** Number of tokens generated per second, often measured at maximum batch size.
- **Methodology:**
  - Increase batch size until memory limits are reached.
  - Measure total tokens generated over a fixed time interval.
  - Calculate throughput as tokens per second.
- **Considerations:**
  - Use pipeline parallelism or model parallelism as needed for large models.
  - Report hardware configuration and software versions.

### Energy Estimation

- **Definition:** Energy consumed by arithmetic operations during inference.
- **Methodology:**
  - Use energy models from literature (e.g., Horowitz 2014, Pokebnn 2023) to estimate energy per operation.
  - Focus on matrix multiplication energy, as it dominates LLM inference.
  - Calculate total energy as the product of operation counts and per-operation energy.
- **Considerations:**
  - Include both addition and multiplication energy costs.
  - Account for energy savings from reduced precision (e.g., INT8 addition vs FP16 multiply-add).
  - For end-to-end energy, include memory access and data transfer costs if possible.

By following these measurement protocols, practitioners can reliably quantify the efficiency gains of BitNet b1.58 and compare them fairly to FP16 baselines.

# Theoretical Considerations and the New Scaling Law

In this chapter, we delve into the theoretical underpinnings of the new scaling law introduced by the BitNet b1.58 model, a 1.58-bit Large Language Model (LLM) variant. We aim to formalize how model precision, size, and training compute budgets interact to determine model performance, and how these relationships differ from conventional full-precision LLMs. This understanding is crucial for guiding future research and practical deployment of low-bit LLMs, especially those using ternary weights as in BitNet b1.58.

---

## Review of Conventional Scaling Laws for LLMs

Scaling laws for LLMs have been a foundational concept in understanding how model performance improves as we increase model size, dataset size, and compute. Seminal works such as Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022) have empirically demonstrated that the test loss (or perplexity) of LLMs follows smooth power-law relationships with respect to three main factors:

1. **Model size ($N$):** Number of parameters in the model.
2. **Training tokens ($D$):** Number of tokens the model is trained on.
3. **Compute budget ($C$):** Total floating-point operations used during training.

These scaling laws typically take the form:

\[
L(N, D) \approx L_\infty + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}
\]

where $L$ is the loss (e.g., cross-entropy), $L_\infty$ is the irreducible loss floor, and $A, B, \alpha, \beta$ are empirically fitted constants.

The key insight is that increasing model size or training data yields diminishing returns, but the combined scaling of both leads to predictable improvements. These laws have guided the design of ever-larger LLMs, but they assume full-precision (e.g., FP16 or BF16) weights and activations.

---

## Defining Effective Bits and Model Capacity for Quantized Networks

When moving from full-precision to low-bit quantized models, such as BitNet b1.58 with ternary weights \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\), the effective representational capacity of the model changes. This affects the scaling behavior and thus requires a refined notion of model capacity that accounts for precision.

### Effective Bits per Parameter

BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights, which can be encoded with \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.58\) bits per parameter, compared to 16 bits in FP16. However, the raw bit count alone does not fully capture the model's expressive power because:

- The quantization scheme imposes discrete constraints on weights.
- The inclusion of zero allows for sparsity and feature filtering, enhancing representational flexibility beyond pure binary weights.
- Activations are quantized to 8 bits, which also affects the effective precision of computations.

Thus, we define an **effective bit-width** \(b_{\text{eff}}\) that reflects the model's true capacity to represent functions, which is influenced by both weight precision and architectural design.

### Effective Model Capacity

We can think of the effective model capacity \(C_{\text{eff}}\) as a function of the number of parameters \(N\) and the effective bits per parameter \(b_{\text{eff}}\):

\[
C_{\text{eff}} = N \times b_{\text{eff}}
\]

This capacity is a proxy for the model's ability to fit data and generalize. For full-precision models, \(b_{\text{eff}} \approx 16\), while for BitNet b1.58, \(b_{\text{eff}} \approx 1.58\).

However, the empirical results in the paper show that BitNet b1.58 models with larger \(N\) can match or exceed the performance of smaller full-precision models, suggesting that the effective capacity is not simply linear in bits but also depends on architectural and training factors.

## Empirical Scaling Observations in BitNet b1.58 and Mapping Rationale

The paper presents empirical evidence that BitNet b1.58 models follow a new scaling law that relates model size and precision to performance and inference cost. Key observations include:

- A 13B parameter BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient (in latency, memory, and energy) than a 3B FP16 LLaMA model, while matching or exceeding its performance.
- Similarly, 30B BitNet b1.58 outperforms 7B FP16, and 70B BitNet b1.58 outperforms 13B FP16, in terms of both efficiency and accuracy.
- The performance gap between BitNet b1.58 and FP16 narrows as model size increases, with parity reached around 3B parameters.

These observations suggest an **equivalence mapping** between model sizes at different precisions:

\[
N_{\text{b1.58}} \approx k \times N_{\text{FP16}}
\]

where \(k\) is an empirical scaling factor greater than 1, reflecting how many more parameters a low-bit model needs to match a smaller full-precision model.

This mapping is a practical manifestation of the new scaling law, indicating that lower precision can be compensated by increasing model size, but with significant gains in efficiency.

## A Toy Theoretical Model Connecting Precision, Parameter Count, and Perplexity

To gain intuition, consider a simplified model where the test loss \(L\) depends on the effective capacity \(C_{\text{eff}}\) and the number of training tokens \(D\):

\[
L \approx L_\infty + \frac{A}{C_{\text{eff}}^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}
\]

Substituting \(C_{\text{eff}} = N \times b_{\text{eff}}\), we get:

\[
L \approx L_\infty + \frac{A}{(N b_{\text{eff}})^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}
\]

For a fixed training token budget \(D\), to achieve the same loss \(L\), a low-bit model with \(b_{\text{eff}}^{\text{low}}\) must have:

\[
N_{\text{low}} = N_{\text{high}} \times \left(\frac{b_{\text{eff}}^{\text{high}}}{b_{\text{eff}}^{\text{low}}}\right)
\]

raised to the power \(1/\alpha\), i.e.,

\[
N_{\text{low}} = N_{\text{high}} \times \left(\frac{b_{\text{eff}}^{\text{high}}}{b_{\text{eff}}^{\text{low}}}\right)^{1/\alpha}
\]

Given that \(b_{\text{eff}}^{\text{high}} = 16\) (FP16) and \(b_{\text{eff}}^{\text{low}} = 1.58\) (BitNet b1.58), and assuming \(\alpha \approx 0.5\) (typical empirical value), the factor is roughly:

\[
\left(\frac{16}{1.58}\right)^{2} \approx (10.13)^2 = 102.6
\]

This naive calculation suggests a very large increase in parameters needed, which contradicts empirical findings. The discrepancy arises because:

- The effective bit-width \(b_{\text{eff}}\) is not simply the raw bit count; ternary weights with zero allow more expressive power than pure binary.
- Architectural modifications and training techniques improve the effective capacity beyond raw bits.
- The exponent \(\alpha\) may differ for low-bit models.

Thus, the toy model highlights that precision reduction can be compensated by increasing model size, but the relationship is more nuanced and depends on effective capacity, not just raw bits.

## Implications for Optimal Resource Allocation under Cost Constraints

The new scaling law for BitNet b1.58 has practical implications for how to allocate resources when training and deploying LLMs:

1. **Model Size vs Precision Trade-off:** Lower precision models require larger parameter counts to match performance, but the overall compute, memory, and energy cost can be significantly lower due to efficient integer-add operations and reduced memory bandwidth.

2. **Training Token Budget:** Since BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 performance starting at 3B parameters with the same training tokens, it suggests that training budgets can be reused without modification, simplifying adoption.

3. **Inference Efficiency:** The equivalence mappings (e.g., 13B b1.58 ~ 3B FP16) mean that practitioners can choose larger low-bit models to gain accuracy while reducing inference latency and energy consumption.

4. **Hardware Design:** The new scaling law motivates hardware optimized for low-bit operations, enabling further cost reductions and scaling.

5. **Compute-Optimal Model Design:** Given a fixed compute budget \(C\), the optimal model size \(N^*\) and precision \(b_{\text{eff}}^*\) can be chosen to minimize loss \(L\), balancing the trade-offs between parameter count and bit precision.

## Limitations of the Scaling Law and Where It May Break Down

While the new scaling law for BitNet b1.58 provides valuable guidance, it has limitations:

- **Empirical Basis:** The law is derived from experiments on specific model sizes (3B to 70B) and datasets (RedPajama). Extrapolation beyond these regimes may not hold.

- **Architecture Dependence:** The scaling behavior depends on the BitNet architecture and training recipe; other low-bit models may exhibit different scaling.

- **Precision Floor:** There may be a lower bound on precision below which increasing model size cannot compensate for loss in representational power.

- **Training Stability:** Very large low-bit models may face optimization challenges not captured by scaling laws.

- **Task and Domain Specificity:** The scaling law is based on language modeling and zero-shot tasks; other tasks may have different sensitivity to precision and size.

- **Hardware Constraints:** Real-world hardware limitations, such as memory bandwidth and parallelism, may affect the practical gains predicted by the scaling law.

## Derivation Details for Absmean Quantizer and Related Formulas

The core quantization function used in BitNet b1.58 is the **absmean quantizer**, which maps full-precision weights to ternary values \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\) by scaling and rounding. This section elaborates on the mathematical formulation and intuition behind this quantizer.

### Quantization Function Definition

Given a weight matrix \( W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m} \), the quantized weight matrix \(\widetilde{W}\) is computed as:

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]

where

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m |W_{ij}|,
\]

and \(\epsilon\) is a small positive constant added for numerical stability to avoid division by zero.

The function \(\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b)\) is defined as:

\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\left(a, \min\left(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\right)\right),
\]

where \(\mathrm{round}(x)\) rounds \(x\) to the nearest integer.

### Intuition and Rationale

- **Scaling by \(\gamma\):** The scale \(\gamma\) is the average absolute value of the weights. Dividing by \(\gamma\) normalizes the weight distribution so that the quantizer operates on a roughly standardized range. This normalization ensures that the ternary quantization captures the relative magnitude of weights effectively.

- **Inclusion of zero:** Unlike binary quantization \(\{-1, +1\}\), ternary quantization includes zero, allowing weights to be pruned or "turned off." This sparsity can improve model expressiveness by enabling feature filtering and reducing noise.

- **Clipping:** The clipping ensures that after rounding, the quantized values remain within the ternary set \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\).

- **Numerical stability:** The small \(\epsilon\) prevents division by zero in rare cases where \(\gamma\) might be extremely small.

### Entropy and Bit Representation

The ternary quantization corresponds to approximately 1.58 bits per weight because:

\[
\log_2(3) \approx 1.58496,
\]

reflecting the information content of three discrete states.

### Activation Quantization

Activations are quantized to 8 bits, scaled per token to the symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\), where \(Q_b = 2^{b-1} - 1\) for \(b=8\). This symmetric scaling avoids zero-point quantization, simplifying implementation and system optimization.

## Energy Model Computations and Data Sources

Energy consumption is a critical metric for evaluating the efficiency of LLMs. BitNet b1.58 achieves significant energy savings by replacing floating-point multiply-accumulate operations with integer additions.

### Energy Cost Breakdown

The energy model focuses on the arithmetic operations involved in matrix multiplication, which dominates LLM inference cost.

- **FP16 LLaMA LLM:** Uses floating-point 16-bit (FP16) addition and multiplication.

- **BitNet b1.58:** Uses integer 8-bit (INT8) addition only, eliminating multiplications.

### Energy per Operation Estimates

Based on prior work~\cite{energycost, pokebnn}, the approximate energy costs on 7nm chips are:

| Operation           | Energy per Operation (pJ) |
|---------------------|---------------------------|
| FP16 Addition       | ~0.9                      |
| FP16 Multiplication | ~3.7                      |
| INT8 Addition       | ~0.05                     |

### Energy Savings Calculation

For matrix multiplication of size \(N \times M\), the number of multiply-accumulate operations is proportional to \(N \times M \times K\).

- FP16 LLaMA requires both FP16 multiplications and additions.

- BitNet b1.58 replaces multiplications with additions, reducing energy by a factor of approximately:

\[
\frac{0.9 + 3.7}{0.05} = 91.2,
\]

but considering overheads and other factors, the paper reports a conservative estimate of **71.4 times** energy savings.

### End-to-End Energy Estimation

Energy consumption for inference with 512 tokens is estimated by summing the energy of all arithmetic operations, including matrix multiplications and other components. The proportion of matrix multiplication energy grows with model size, making BitNet b1.58 increasingly efficient at larger scales.

## Example Kernel Pseudo-code and Packing Formats for Ternary Weights

Efficient implementation of BitNet b1.58 requires specialized kernels for matrix multiplication with ternary weights.

### Ternary Weight Packing

- Each weight is represented by 2 bits to encode \(-1, 0, +1\).

- Packing multiple weights into 32-bit or 64-bit words reduces memory bandwidth.

- Example encoding scheme:

  - `00` → 0

  - `01` → +1

  - `10` → -1

  - `11` → unused or reserved

### Pseudo-code for BitLinear Matrix Multiplication

```python
def bitlinear_matmul(input_activations, packed_ternary_weights, scale):
    """
    input_activations: int8 tensor of shape (batch_size, input_dim)
    packed_ternary_weights: packed 2-bit ternary weights of shape (output_dim, input_dim)
    scale: scaling factor gamma for weights

    Returns:
        output: int32 tensor of shape (batch_size, output_dim)
    """
    output = zeros(batch_size, output_dim, dtype=int32)
    for out_idx in range(output_dim):
        for in_idx in range(input_dim):
            weight = unpack_ternary(packed_ternary_weights[out_idx, in_idx])
            # weight in {-1, 0, +1}
            output[:, out_idx] += weight * input_activations[:, in_idx]
    # Rescale output by scale factor
    output = output * scale
    return output
```

### Notes

- Multiplications by \(\pm 1\) are implemented as conditional additions or subtractions, avoiding costly multiply instructions.

- Zero weights skip computation, enabling sparsity exploitation.

- The Ladder 2-bit kernel~\cite{ladder} is an optimized GPU kernel implementing this logic.
