According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', the dominant factor explaining why BitNet's relative inference speed-up improves with larger model sizes is the dominance of nn.Linear layers. In Transformer LLMs, most compute and memory traffic come from the linear projections in attention and the two large feed-forward matrices, not from embeddings or small nonlinearities. The paper emphasizes that “the time cost for nn.Linear grows with the model size” and that “the percentage of nn.Linear grows with the model size,” while other components (e.g., embeddings kept in full precision) form a shrinking proportion of the total as width and depth increase. Concretely, the FLOPs of the linear layers scale roughly as \(O(T \cdot N \cdot d^2)\) where \(T\) is sequence length, \(N\) is the number of layers, and \(d\) is the hidden width; by contrast, components like layer norms or elementwise activations scale as \(O(T \cdot N \cdot d)\). As \(d\) increases, the quadratic term makes nn.Linear increasingly dominant, so any optimization that specifically targets linear layers—like BitNet’s \(\mathrm{BitLinear}\) replacing FP16 multiplications with low-bit integer additions—yields a larger fraction of total runtime savings, hence a larger relative speed-up.

This dominance also manifests in bandwidth and energy. The paper notes that matrix multiplications account for the majority of arithmetic energy, and that BitNet b1.58 shifts them to INT8 additions, saving \(71.4\times\) arithmetic energy on 7nm for matmuls. Since the parameter and activation movement for linear layers scales with the number of weights and activations—both proportional to \(O(N \cdot d^2)\) and \(O(T \cdot N \cdot d)\), respectively—the bit-width reduction from 16-bit to 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations amortizes more of the memory and bandwidth cost as models grow. Meanwhile, fixed-cost components (e.g., full-precision embeddings) occupy a smaller share at larger scales, so the end-to-end latency and energy speed-ups, which are driven by the transformed nn.Linear workload, increase with model size. This is exactly what the paper’s empirical trends show: at 70B, BitNet b1.58 is \(4.1\times\) faster than the FP16 baseline, a larger gain than at 3–13B, because the inference is progressively more dominated by the nn.Linear layers that BitNet optimizes.
