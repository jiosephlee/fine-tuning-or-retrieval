According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', the experimental practice reported to ensure a fair comparison between BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA baseline is using the same pretraining data. The authors explicitly state that “to ensure a fair comparison, we pre-trained the models on the RedPajama dataset for 100 billion tokens,” thereby holding the data distribution and token count constant across variants. By fixing the corpus (RedPajama) and the token budget at \(10^{11}\), any observed differences in perplexity, zero-shot accuracy, latency, memory, throughput, or energy are attributable to the model architecture and precision (ternary \( \{-1, 0, 1\} \) weights with 8-bit activations versus FP16), rather than confounded by shifts in data quality, domain composition, or scale.

This alignment is reinforced by the paper’s broader parity measures: BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, no biases) and matches configurations such as model size and training tokens, while reporting results on identical evaluation suites. Using the same pretraining data removes dataset-induced variability in gradient statistics and representation learning, which would otherwise impact metrics like validation perplexity on WikiText2 and C4 and end-task accuracies on ARC, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, and BoolQ. In short, by fixing the pretraining distribution and scale, the study isolates the effect of 1.58-bit quantization and the BitLinear computation paradigm, making claims like “BitNet b1.58 starts to match full precision at 3B” and the reported speedups (\(\sim 2.4\times\)–\(4.1\times\)) credibly attributable to the precision and architecture rather than to data differences.
