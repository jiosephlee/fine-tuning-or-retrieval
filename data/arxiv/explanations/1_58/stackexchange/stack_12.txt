### How is 'matching full precision starting from 3B parameters' measured, and is the effect consistent across tasks?
Question:
The paper states BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA baselines in perplexity and downstream performance from 3B parameters, trained on 100B tokens. Which exact model configs (depth/width, vocab, tokenizer) were used for the matched comparisons? Are there tasks where the ternary model still lags notably even at 3B? Could you provide confidence intervals or multiple training runs to show robustness of 'matching' claims?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s “matches FP16 starting from $3$B” claim is based on models trained from scratch on RedPajama ($100$B tokens) using LLaMA-like architecture choices and evaluated with lm-evaluation-harness on the handful of zero-shot tasks listed (ARC-easy, ARC-challenge, HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ) plus perplexity on WikiText2 and C4. The paper gives architecture-level details (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, no biases; embeddings kept full precision, activations $8$-bit, weights ternary $\{-1,0,1\}$) and reports numbers for model sizes ($3$B, $3.9$B, $7$B, $13$B, $70$B). However, it does not publish the low-level hyperparameters you asked for (explicit depth × width per size, vocab size, tokenizer version) nor any multi-seed runs or confidence intervals. It therefore supports the matching claim empirically on the datasets reported, but with the usual caveats about single-run experiments.

What the paper does report and what that implies
The paper says: models were trained on RedPajama for $100$B tokens, the BitNet b1.58 design adopts LLaMA-alike components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary, bias removal), activations are $8$-bit, embeddings are full precision, and BitLinear replaces nn.Linear so weights are ternary $\{-1,0,1\}$. Perplexity comparisons are on WikiText2 and C4; downstream zero-shot evaluations use the lm-evaluation-harness pipeline on the tasks listed above. Tables in the paper claim that at the $3$B scale the BitNet b1.58 perplexity and averaged zero-shot accuracies are essentially indistinguishable from the reproduced FP16 LLaMA baselines, and larger b1.58 models (e.g., $3.9$B) outperform the FP16 $3$B baseline while being cheaper to run.

What is missing (and why that matters)
The paper does not publish:
- Exact transformer depth and hidden-dimension (layer count, hidden size, MLP sizes, attention heads) for each “$3$B” / “$3.9$B” / etc configuration, nor the tokenizer/vocabulary size/version used (bpe/spm and vocab size), nor the detailed optimizer/hyperparameter seeds. These are important because small differences in width/depth or vocabulary can change perplexity and some task accuracies.
- Any multiple independent runs (different random seeds) or standard errors / confidence intervals. The results appear to be single-run point estimates.
- Per-task breakdowns with statistical significance testing to show where differences are consistently negligible versus where they fluctuate.

Consequences for the “matching” claim
Given those omissions, the statement “matches full precision starting from $3$B” is supported by the reported single-run experiments on the listed benchmarks, but it is not demonstrated with statistical robustness. Without multiple seeds / CI / per-task significance tests, you cannot be certain the effect is robust across random initialization, data ordering, or modest architecture/tokenizer variants. The paper’s reported tasks show the performance gap narrows with size and that for the set they evaluated the $3$B b1.58 is comparable to their FP16 baseline, but the paper does not claim exhaustive equivalence across all possible tasks or datasets.

Are there tasks where the ternary model still lags at $3$B?
The paper’s Tables (per the Results section) do not highlight any specific downstream task where BitNet b1.58 at $3$B “notably” lags the FP16 baseline; the narrative is that the gap narrows and matching is observed from $3$B on the reported tasks. Because the authors only evaluated the finite list of zero-shot benchmarks, this does not rule out tasks outside that list (for example, long-horizon reasoning, compositional generalization, or domain-specific QA) where differences might still appear. The paper itself does not present examples of such persistent gaps.

How to make the matching claim stronger (recommended analyses)
To convince skeptics and demonstrate robustness, the natural next steps are: (1) publish exact model dimension tables and tokenizer/vocab details; (2) run multiple independent training runs (at least $3$–$5$ seeds) and report mean ± standard deviation or $95\%$ confidence intervals for perplexity and each downstream task; (3) report per-task differences and run paired statistical tests (e.g., paired t-test or bootstrap over examples) to see whether observed deltas are statistically indistinguishable from zero; (4) provide ablations that vary depth vs width and evaluate sensitivity to tokenizer/vocab choices; (5) report calibration and failure modes (e.g., examples where the ternary model confidently gives wrong answers compared to FP16) and per-dataset sample-level bootstrap curves. These are standard reproducibility steps and would directly address the robustness question.

Practical advice if you want to reproduce or probe the claim now
If you plan to reproduce or test how consistent the match is, ensure you (a) use the exact RedPajama split and tokenizer/vocab the authors used (contact authors if not published), (b) try $3$–$5$ seeds, (c) evaluate the same evaluation harness and per-example predictions so you can bootstrap confidence intervals, and (d) compare not just averaged accuracy but per-task and per-example differences and perplexity curves during training. For statistical comparison, compute $95\%$ bootstrap confidence intervals over test examples and/or report standard deviations across seeds.

Summary
The paper reports matching between BitNet b1.58 and FP16 LLaMA starting at $3$B on the reported perplexity metrics and a set of zero-shot tasks when both are trained for $100$B tokens on RedPajama, with LLaMA-like architectural components and ternary weights. The exact low-level model dimensions, tokenizer/vocabulary, and multiple-run statistics are not provided, so the claim is empirically supported for the presented single-run experiments but not yet demonstrated with statistical robustness or exhaustiveness across tasks. Running multiple seeds, publishing exact config details, and adding confidence intervals or per-task significance tests would make the claim much stronger.
