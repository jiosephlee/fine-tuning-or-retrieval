\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits}

### Why choose ternary {-1, 0, +1} rather than a 2-bit set like {-2, -1, +1, +2} or learned ternary thresholds?
Question:
The paper uses RoundClip on W/(gamma+epsilon) to snap to {-1,0,+1}. Did you compare against other low-bit alphabets with 2 bits of dynamic range, or ternary with learned thresholds and scales per channel? What guided the choice of the absmean normalization and uniform ternary thresholds versus per-channel learned scales that might better match weight distributions?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper chooses symmetric ternary $\{-1,0,+1\}$ with a single absmean scale because it gives a practical balance of representational power (zero enables feature filtering), training/hardware simplicity, and good empirical performance when training from scratch. Per-channel learned thresholds or a different 2-bit alphabet (e.g. $\{-2,-1,+1,+2\}$) would add storage/compute complexity and implementation friction without a clear win; the paper uses absmean + uniform rounding as a simple, stable recipe that matches the observed weight statistics well enough for the model sizes where they report parity with FP16.

Why ternary $\{-1,0,+1\}$ is attractive (intuitions tied to the paper)
The paper frames BitNet b1.58 as a 1-bit/ternary architecture designed for two practical goals: extreme compute/memory efficiency and competitive modeling ability. Adding 0 (so weights can be exactly zero) is an important design choice: it enables explicit feature filtering/sparsity in the weight matrices, which is a powerful and hardware-friendly form of pruning built into the representation. A symmetric ternary alphabet is also easy to implement in integer-only kernels and leads to very simple multiplication behavior: a multiply by +1, -1 or 0 is just sign, negation, or zeroing—cheap on hardware.

Why absmean scaling + uniform thresholds were chosen
The quantizer in the paper scales each weight matrix by the average absolute value
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|
\]
and then applies RoundClip to map to $\{-1,0,+1\}$. This “absmean” normalization is attractive for a few reasons that the paper emphasizes: it is extremely cheap to compute (one scalar per weight matrix), it is robust (average absolute value tracks the overall scale of the weight distribution), it preserves symmetry around zero (no zero-point fiddling), and it avoids per-channel bookkeeping. The paper also points out that activations are scaled per token to a symmetric range to avoid a zero-point, again leaning on symmetry and simplicity. In short, absmean + uniform thresholds is a minimal, stable recipe that requires only one extra scalar per matrix and integrates cleanly with the BitLinear operator and system-level optimizations.

Why the authors likely did not use per-channel learned thresholds or a 4-value alphabet
The paper does not report a direct comparison with per-channel learned thresholds/scales or alternate 2-bit alphabets, so we can only infer the rationale from their design goals and the empirical results they present. Per-channel learned scales or thresholds would increase complexity in three ways: extra parameters (one scale/threshold per output channel or per row/column), extra storage and memory traffic (scalars must be stored and loaded), and extra compute to apply non-uniform scaling or dequantization at runtime. For extreme-efficiency hardware and for integration into simple INT-add kernels, those costs matter.

A 4-value symmetric alphabet like $\{-2,-1,+1,+2\}$ (or any non-uniform spacing) provides higher dynamic range per weight but also increases quantization granularity and potentially the variance of the effective weight distribution after quantization. That may require different training hyperparameters or more elaborate scale estimation. Crucially, the paper trains from scratch with the ternary scheme and demonstrates parity with FP16 at reasonably large model sizes; that empirical success reduces the motivation to add complexity. Also, using $\{-1,0,+1\}$ preserves a small integer set that maps nicely to efficient integer accumulation and simple bit-packed storage; more distinct magnitudes break some of those hardware advantages unless you pay for additional encoding/scale logic.

Broader conceptual trade-offs
This is the familiar trade-off in quantization: per-tensor vs per-channel scales, symmetric vs asymmetric quantizers, and fixed vs learned codebooks. Per-channel learned scales usually reduce quantization error (they tailor the dynamic range to each channel) and are common in high-accuracy low-bit post-training quant methods. But they come with storage/engineering costs and complicate fast kernels. The paper opts for the minimal route that still achieves strong results when training the architecture end-to-end with the quantized constraint baked in, rather than doing expensive post-training calibration.

What the paper does not claim and what you might try next
The paper does not present ablations comparing ternary vs other small alphabets or learned thresholds. If you want to evaluate this yourself, reasonable ablations are: (1) per-channel absmean vs per-matrix absmean; (2) learned multiplicative scales per channel (trained jointly) with the same RoundClip thresholds; and (3) different 2-bit codebooks (symmetric $\{-2,-1,+1,+2\}$, asymmetric sets, or learned codebooks). Key metrics to track are validation perplexity, sparsity (fraction of zeros), hardware-relevant costs (extra scalar storage and per-inference ops), and stability of training. My expectation guided by the paper’s design is that per-channel scales will reduce quantization error but give modest accuracy gains at the cost of system complexity, and that more complex alphabets may not justify the hardware/implementation trade-offs given the strong results already achieved with the simple ternary scheme.

Summary
The paper prioritizes a simple, symmetric ternary alphabet with an inexpensive absmean scale because it preserves hardware efficiency, yields useful sparsity (0 as a filter), and is sufficient to match FP16 at scale when training from scratch. Per-channel learned thresholds or richer 2-bit alphabets could further reduce quantization error in theory, but they introduce extra parameters, runtime complexity, and hardware friction; the paper does not report those comparisons and instead presents an empirical recipe optimized for the practical goals of efficiency and ease of integration.
