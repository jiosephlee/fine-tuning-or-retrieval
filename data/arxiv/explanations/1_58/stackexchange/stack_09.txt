### How is quantization applied around SwiGLU and RMSNorm, and are those parameters/activations also quantized?
Question:
The architecture uses RMSNorm and SwiGLU. Are the RMSNorm weights (scales) and SwiGLU projection weights ternary as well? Are the activations feeding into and coming out of the SwiGLU nonlinearity quantized to 8-bit with symmetric per-token scales? How is numerical stability ensured for the RMS normalization and the GELU-like nonlinearity under 8-bit activation quantization?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — in BitNet b1.58 all trainable parameters (including the RMSNorm scale parameters and the SwiGLU linear projections) are constrained to the ternary set $\{-1,0,1\}$, and activations are quantized to 8 bits with a symmetric per-token scale. The paper does not give low-level kernel details for the internal arithmetic of RMSNorm or the nonlinearity, but it explains the quantization design choices that make these components numerically stable in practice.

What the paper explicitly states
The model is trained from scratch with "$1.58$-bit weights and $8$-bit activations." The paper replaces every nn.Linear with BitLinear and uses an "absmean" quantizer that scales a weight matrix by its average absolute value $\gamma$ and then $\mathrm{RoundClip}(\dots,-1,1)$ to produce $\{-1,0,1\}$ weights:
$$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
$$
Because RMSNorm scale parameters and every linear projection in the Transformer are trainable parameters, they are part of this global "every parameter" quantization: they are ternary. The paper also states activations are quantized "to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token" (symmetric per-token scale) to avoid zero-point complications.

Why this design helps numerical stability (intuitive + technical)
1) Symmetric per-token activation scaling preserves signed activation dynamics. Many normalization layers and nonlinearities (RMSNorm and SwiGLU/Swish-like activations) rely on the sign and relative magnitude of activations. By scaling activations per token to a symmetric range around zero, quantization keeps positive/negative balance and roughly preserves the shape of the activation distribution presented to RMSNorm and to the SwiGLU nonlinearity.

2) The absmean weight scaling normalizes each weight matrix by its average absolute magnitude before rounding. That prevents systematic under- or over-scaling of matrix outputs that would otherwise push activations into saturation when followed by nonlinearities. The explicit small $\epsilon$ in the denominator also avoids division-by-zero issues.

3) RMSNorm itself is inherently simpler than LayerNorm: it computes root-mean-square over the hidden dimension and applies a learned scale (here ternary). RMS uses an RMS denominator $\sqrt{\operatorname{mean}(x^2)+\delta}$ which, combined with the per-token activation scale and a small stability constant $\delta$ (standard practice / hinted by $\epsilon$ elsewhere in the paper), keeps the denominator away from zero and stabilizes the division even when inputs are low-bit.

What the paper does not (explicitly) promise and how it is typically handled in practice
The paper does not spell out whether the RMS computation (the mean of squared activations, the sqrt, and the final division) is performed in 8-bit integer arithmetic end-to-end, nor does it give the exact accumulator precision used in kernels. In practice, high-quality quantized implementations typically do one of the following for stability and accuracy: (a) accumulate sums (means of squares) in a higher precision accumulator (FP16 or FP32) even when inputs are 8-bit, (b) perform the division/sqrt in FP16/FP32 and then requantize, or (c) dequantize a small slice to floating point for the normalization step and re-quantize the normalized activations. Any of these choices preserve numerical stability while keeping most heavy matmuls in low precision.

Because the paper targets system-level speedups, the symmetric per-token quantization and the removal of zero-points simplify integer kernels and make it straightforward to do higher-precision accumulation only where needed (normalization and sqrt). The combination of per-token scaling (reduces dynamic-range mismatch), absmean weight scaling (keeps linear outputs in reasonable range), and a small stability constant for normalization is the mechanism the authors rely on to ensure stability for RMSNorm and SwiGLU under 8-bit activations.

If you need implementation guidance
If you implement this yourself, follow the paper’s quantization rules for weights and per-token symmetric activations, remove biases (as the paper does), and ensure RMSNorm’s mean-of-squares and sqrt use at least FP16 accumulation (or a fused op that internally uses FP16/FP32) before rescaling back to 8-bit. That mirrors the practical compromise implied by the paper: most compute in 8-bit for efficiency, but do the small number of numerically sensitive ops in higher precision to keep the model stable.

Summary
RMSNorm scales and SwiGLU projection weights are ternary in BitNet b1.58, activations are 8-bit with symmetric per-token scales, and numerical stability is achieved by (a) per-token symmetric scaling of activations, (b) absmean normalization of weight matrices with $\epsilon$, and (c) the usual practical choice to perform the few numerically sensitive operations (mean-of-squares, sqrt, division) with higher-precision accumulation or fused kernels — a detail the paper does not fully specify but is standard and consistent with the design choices they describe.
