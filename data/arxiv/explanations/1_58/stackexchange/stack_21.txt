\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits}

### How were latency numbers measured: prefill vs decode, sequence lengths, batch sizes, and sampling overhead?
Question:
The paper reports 'time per output token' using FasterTransformer. Was this measured in the decode phase with KV cache, or averaged across prefill+decode? What sequence length and batch size were used, and were tokens generated with greedy or with sampling (which can add overhead)? Are the reported speedups consistent across a range of batch sizes and context lengths, or do they vary significantly?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper gives some measurement facts (FasterTransformer backend; “time per output token”; throughput measured with sequence length $512$; energy/ end-to-end energy reported for $512$ tokens; batch size for throughput found by increasing until OOM) but it does not state explicitly whether the reported “time per output token” is measured only during autoregressive decoding with KV cache (decode-only) or averaged over prefill+decode, nor does it state the decoding sampling strategy (greedy vs sampling) or the exact batch/sequence settings used for every latency number. Because those details are not fully specified in the paper, you must treat some specifics as missing or inferred.

What is explicitly stated in the paper
The authors report latency and memory using the FasterTransformer codebase and say they “reported the time per output token, as it is the major cost for inference.” They also state that latency and memory numbers were measured with a $2$-bit kernel. For throughput experiments with the $70\mathrm{B}$ models on two $80\ \mathrm{GB}$ A100 GPUs they used pipeline parallelism and a sequence length of $512$, and they “increased the batch size until the GPU memory limit was reached” to report maximum batch size / throughput. They also report end-to-end energy for models with $512$ tokens.

What is not stated (and why it matters)
The paper does not explicitly say whether the “time per output token” is:
- measured only during the autoregressive decode phase with an existing KV cache (i.e., per-token decoding time, which excludes the initial prefill cost), or
- an average over the whole request including prefill (the cost to process a full context) plus the subsequent decode tokens.

This matters because prefill (attention over the full context to fill KV caches) is $O(L^2)$ in compute and can dominate latency for long contexts, while per-token decode with KV caching is typically much cheaper (roughly $O(1)$ per token for attention cost aside from the incremental KV updates). If you care about interactive latency, decode-with-KV is the relevant metric; if you care about a single long-batch inference (one-shot evaluation), prefill+decode matters.

The paper also omits the decoding sampling strategy (greedy vs. sampling with top-k/top-p). Sampling adds overhead: decoding code paths that call random sampling and possible CPU/GPU transfers can increase per-token latency relative to pure greedy argmax. It likewise does not list the exact batch sizes and context lengths used for each of the reported “time per token” numbers (except for the throughput experiment and the $512$-token energy/end-to-end number).

Reasonable inference from the paper
Given the language “time per output token, as it is the major cost for inference” and their use of FasterTransformer (which is commonly used to benchmark autoregressive decode with KV cache), it is plausible that the reported per-token times are measured during the decode phase with KV caching enabled (i.e., per-token generation after prefill). This is consistent with the typical LLM benchmarking practice where people quote “time per output token” for autoregressive generation. However, this is an inference — the paper does not say it explicitly.

What they do show about scaling and variation
The paper shows that speedups increase with model size (e.g., BitNet $b1.58$ is $2.71\times$ faster at $3\mathrm{B}$ and up to $4.1\times$ at $70\mathrm{B}$). That implies the relative benefit is larger when matrix-multiply dominated layers occupy more compute — so speedups will generally depend on model size. They also claim BitNet reduces activation size ($16$-bit $\to$ $8$-bit) and KV cache memory, enabling longer effective contexts for the same memory; that suggests latency and memory advantages for longer contexts too, but the paper does not provide a sweep of latency vs. context length or of per-token speedup across batch sizes. For throughput they explicitly varied batch size until OOM, using sequence length $512$, and show large batch-size advantages ($70\mathrm{B}$ BitNet supports up to $11\times$ the batch size of FP16 $70\mathrm{B}$ in that test).

Practical implications and recommended follow-ups
If you need definitive answers for experimental comparison or replication, request these specific measurements from the authors or reproduce them yourself with FasterTransformer. At minimum ask or measure: (1) prefill time (process full prompt/context) and decode-only per-token time with KV cache, reported separately; (2) the context length(s) used for each latency number (e.g., $1,\,128,\,512,\,2048$ tokens); (3) batch sizes used for latency (usually batch=$1$ is relevant for interactive latency) and for throughput; (4) decoding strategy (greedy vs sampling with top-k/top-p) and the sampling parameters; and (5) whether measurements include host/GPU synchronization and token-level CPU-side operations (sampling, logits postprocessing), since these add overhead. If you re-run tests, compare both prefill+decode and decode-only, and sweep batch sizes and context lengths to reveal where speedups vary.

One-line summary
The paper reports per-token time using FasterTransformer and gives throughput/energy details for sequence length $512$, but it does not explicitly state whether the per-token latency is decode-only with KV cache or averaged with prefill, nor which sampling mode was used; speedups are shown to increase with model size and are likely context/batch-dependent, so for precise comparisons you should ask the authors or re-run controlled experiments that separate prefill and decode, sweep context lengths, and fix decoding mode (greedy vs sampling).
