\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits}

### Does the 'new scaling law' have a formal functional form, or is it an empirical equivalence table?
Question:
The paper claims 'BitNet b1.58 is enabling a new scaling law' and lists equivalences like '13B b1.58 is more efficient than 3B FP16.' Is there a quantitative law relating model size, bit-precision, and inference cost/performance (e.g., \(\mathrm{cost} \propto N^\alpha / b^\beta\))? Or are the provided equivalences purely empirical on the tested hardware/software stack? If there is a law, what are the fitted exponents and assumptions?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper does not present a closed-form, validated “new scaling law” like a fitted power-law equation; the statements such as “$13\mathrm{B}$ $\text{b}1.58$ is more efficient than $3\mathrm{B}$ FP16” are empirical equivalences derived from the measurements in their figures and tables on a particular hardware/software stack (FasterTransformer + 2-bit kernel + the quoted A100 runs and energy model). The paper reports trends (latency, memory, energy vs model size and precision) and gives discrete equivalences, but it does not publish an explicit functional law with fitted exponents and error bars.

Why this is an empirical claim rather than a formal analytic law
- The paper’s comparisons are based on measured latency, memory usage and an energy estimate (using an external energy model) for specific model sizes ($3\mathrm{B}, 7\mathrm{B}, 13\mathrm{B}, 30\mathrm{B}, 70\mathrm{B}$) and implementation kernels. Those figures support the equivalences listed, but they are not accompanied by a statistical fit of a parametric law such as $\text{cost} \propto N^\alpha / b^\beta$.  
- The cost components that matter (matrix-multiply time, memory bandwidth for weights and KV cache, activation precision, embedding overhead, kernel efficiency, pipeline/parallelism overhead) scale differently with model size and precision. Because of these mixed components and implementation-dependent constants, a single simple power law with universal exponents is unlikely to capture all behavior across sizes, precisions and hardware without many caveats.

Why a single simple law is hard to justify from the paper’s data
- Different terms dominate at different scales: for large $N$ the cost of nn.Linear (matmuls) dominates and benefits of lower-weight precision grow; for small $N$ the embedding and other fixed-costs are proportionally larger. This produces non-uniform scaling (piecewise or additive contributions) rather than a pure monomial in $N$ and $b$.  
- “Bits” change not only the arithmetic cost per MAC but also memory-transfer bandwidth, kernel utilization, availability of specialized low-bit kernels, and whether multiplications are avoided entirely (BitNet’s integer-add paradigm). Those effects are hardware- and kernel-dependent and introduce different multiplicative constants and break simple power-law universality.  
- The paper’s energy claims use an external arithmetic energy model for 7nm; that model gives a multiplicative factor (energy per FP16 multiply vs INT8 add, etc.), but mapping that into a clean exponent in a single $N/b$ law requires assuming the fraction of work that is matmul vs other ops is a simple function of $N$, which the paper does not formalize.

If you want a fitted functional form (how you could proceed)
The paper’s reported datapoints (latency, memory, estimated energy for a few $N$ at FP16 vs $\text{b}1.58$) are sufficient to attempt an empirical fit. A plausible minimal model to try is additive in cost components, for example
$\mathrm{Cost}(N,b) \approx a_{\text{lin}}(b)\cdot N + a_{\text{fix}}(b)$
or if you insist on a multiplicative power-law form
$\mathrm{Cost}(N,b) \approx C\cdot N^\alpha\cdot b^\beta$,
you can fit $\alpha$ and $\beta$ via log-linear regression on the measured Cost values (taking $\log\,$Cost vs $\log\,N$ and $\log\,b$). But be aware of assumptions: you must pick what “$b$” means (bits per weight, or effective arithmetic complexity), and the fit will be specific to the hardware, kernel implementations, and the range of $N$ used. The paper does not report such fitted exponents or confidence intervals.

Practical takeaway
Treat the paper’s “new scaling law” as an empirical observation that $1.58$-bit BitNet implementations measured on their stack give a Pareto improvement: for the particular hardware/tools they used, a larger-bitnet model often matches or beats a smaller FP16 model in latency/memory/energy while matching perplexity. It is not a universal analytic law with provided exponents in the manuscript; any universal-looking formula would require further modeling (separating matmul vs fixed costs, kernel efficiencies, and hardware energy constants) and a statistical fit across more datapoints and hardware.
