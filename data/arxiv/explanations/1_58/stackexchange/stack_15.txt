### Are KV caches quantized to 8-bit during decoding, and how does this affect long-context stability?
Question:
The paper suggests 8-bit activations and mentions potential for further lossless compression for long contexts. In the reported latency/memory results, are the key/value caches stored in int8 during generation, or kept in FP16/BF16 for compatibility? If quantized, what is the scaling scheme for KV caches across timesteps (per-head, per-token, per-channel)? Any observed degradation in attention quality for long sequence lengths due to cache quantization?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper does not explicitly state that KV caches are kept in $FP16$/$BF16$ or quantized to $int8$ during autoregressive generation; however, the text makes clear that activations are $8$-bit in BitNet b1.58 and that those activations are scaled "per token" to the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$. From that description the most faithful reading is that KV cache entries (they are just stored attention activations) are represented in the same $8$-bit activation format with per-token scaling, not left in $FP16$/$BF16$ for compatibility. The paper also does not report any observed degradation of attention quality at long context lengths caused by KV quantization.

Why this is the reasonable interpretation: the paper emphasizes that BitNet b1.58 uses $8$-bit activations everywhere (section on Quantization Function and activations) and explicitly calls out reduced KV-cache memory as one of the main benefits for long contexts (“reduces the activations from $16$ bits to $8$ bits, allowing the context length to be doubled given the same resources”). The activations quantization they describe is done per-token (they write “the activations are all scaled to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token to get rid of the zero-point quantization”), which implies the following practical scheme for caches: when a new key/value vector is produced for token t, it is scaled and quantized using that token’s activation scale and then stored as an $8$-bit entry in the cache. The paper does not describe using per-head or per-channel scales across timesteps, nor a global FP scaling retained for caches.

What the paper does not claim (and thus you should treat as unknown): they do not provide a detailed specification of the exact scaling granularity used for KV cache storage beyond “per token” activation scaling, and they do not say whether any implementations (e.g., FasterTransformer integration or Ladder $2$-bit kernel used in experiments) kept KV caches in higher precision for compatibility. They also do not report numerical experiments that isolate quantized-KV effects on attention quality as sequence length increases; instead they report end-to-end perplexity and downstream task parity, and memory/latency wins, which indirectly imply no large degradation in practice for the settings they tested.

Practical implications and intuition: quantizing KV caches to $8$-bit with a per-token scale is a natural and low-cost design because keys/values are produced per token and may have token-specific dynamic ranges. Per-token scaling reduces quantization bias accumulation across timesteps compared with using a single global scale for all tokens. Still, quantizing KV caches can in principle hurt attention fidelity for very long contexts because small relative differences in dot products (especially when softmax is sensitive to differences among many keys) can be masked by quantization noise. The paper claims that BitNet b1.58 matches $FP16$ baselines (perplexity and downstream tasks) starting at $3\text{B}$ and highlights the ability to double context for the same resources; that empirical claim suggests they did not observe meaningful long-context failure modes in their evaluations.

If you need a definitive implementation detail (for replication or engineering), check the code or the FasterTransformer/Ladder kernels used in their benchmarks: many inference stacks still store KV caches in $FP16$ by default for compatibility and only quantize on-the-fly for GEMM kernels. The paper notes that latency/memory benchmarks “were measured with a $2$-bit kernel” for the linear ops but does not state whether the caches were physically stored as $int8$ in the measured runs, so the safest next step is to inspect the authors’ release or the exact inference implementation used in measurement.

Suggested experiment to verify effects: run generation with identical models but two cache-storage modes—$FP16$ vs per-token $int8$ (with the paper’s per-token scaling) —and compare (a) logit differences over timesteps, (b) perplexity on long contexts, and (c) downstream accuracy for long-context tasks. That will directly reveal any accumulated degradation due to cache quantization.

Summary: the paper implies KV caches are part of the $8$-bit activation regime and likely stored quantized with per-token scaling, but it does not explicitly document head- or channel-level scaling or supply targeted long-context degradation measurements. For exact implementation/measurement details, consult the code or the FasterTransformer integration used for their benchmarks.
