\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits}

### How exactly is the absmean quantization with RoundClip applied and updated during training?
Question:
The quantization is defined as \(\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon), -1, 1)\) with \(\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|\). Do they compute \(\gamma\) once per forward pass per weight matrix, or is it updated less frequently (e.g., per step or per epoch)? Is \(\gamma\) computed per-tensor, per-row, or per-column? How sensitive is performance to the choice of per-tensor vs per-channel scaling?
Answer:
Short answer: in this paper $\gamma$ is a per-weight-matrix (per-tensor) scale computed from the current full-precision weights $W$ and used to quantize that matrix in the forward pass; $\gamma$ therefore changes as the underlying weights change (i.e. it is recomputed regularly as training proceeds — effectively each forward that uses that matrix). The authors present the quantizer as "scale by the average absolute value of the weight matrix" ($\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|$), which strongly implies per-matrix scaling rather than per-row or per-column, and the experiments and description use that per-matrix (per-tensor) choice.

Why this is the natural interpretation and how it behaves during training. The formula for $\gamma$ is defined directly from the entries of $W$, so whenever $W$ changes (by optimizer updates) $\gamma$ changes as well; in practice this is computed from the current $W$ at quantization time (i.e. each forward pass that needs the quantized weights). Computing $\gamma$ per-forward is cheap (a single elementwise absolute + mean over the matrix) and keeps the quantizer matched to the current weight distribution, which stabilizes training because the ternary thresholds remain adaptive to the scale of the weights. If you instead froze $\gamma$ for many steps or computed it only per epoch you would be applying a stale scale to a changing $W$ and that can degrade training dynamics unless you compensate elsewhere.

Why the paper uses per-tensor (intuitive reasoning). Using the mean absolute value over the whole weight matrix is simple, parameter-efficient and enforces a single scale for all elements of that linear layer, which fits the BitNet design goal of extreme quantization simplicity (ternary weights across the matrix). Per-tensor scaling also integrates naturally with system-level kernels and the claimed runtime/memory advantages.

Per-row / per-column (per-channel) scaling — tradeoffs and sensitivity. The paper does not report experiments comparing per-tensor vs per-channel $\gamma$ for weight quantization, so there is no empirical number in the text about sensitivity. Generally, per-channel scaling can improve representational fidelity when different output channels (rows/columns) have systematically different magnitudes (this is common in convolutional layers and sometimes beneficial in linear layers). The trade-off is extra metadata (one $\gamma$ per channel) and more bookkeeping in kernels. Given the paper’s claim and design choice, they found the per-matrix absmean sufficient to reach parity with $FP16$ at scale (starting around $3\text{B}$), which indicates that for their architecture and training regime performance is not critically sensitive to not using per-channel scaling.

Practical recommendation (consistent with the paper). Implement $\gamma$ as a per-weight-matrix scalar computed from the current FP weights and recompute it whenever you quantize that matrix (i.e. each forward that needs the quantized weights). Use the per-tensor absmean by default (as in the paper); if you suspect large per-channel magnitude variation in your model (or you want to squeeze slightly better accuracy at the cost of extra complexity), try a per-channel version in an ablation to see if it yields meaningful gains for your architecture and dataset.

If you need to experiment: compare (a) per-tensor $\gamma$ recomputed every forward (paper default), (b) per-channel $\gamma$ recomputed every forward, and (c) per-tensor $\gamma$ updated less frequently (e.g. once per step or epoch). The paper’s formulation and results support option (a) as the intended and effective choice.
