### What gradient estimator is used to backpropagate through RoundClip and the ternary quantization?
Question:
The paper quantizes weights via a non-differentiable RoundClip. During training from scratch with 1.58-bit weights, what estimator is used for gradients? Is it a straight-through estimator (STE), and if so, what exact form (e.g., identity through the rounding and clipping range and zero outside)? Do they keep a latent real-valued weight copy for updates, or are updates applied directly in the ternary space?
Answer:
The paper does not explicitly state the gradient estimator used for backpropagating through the RoundClip quantizer. They give the forward quantization precisely:

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}, -1, 1\right),\qquad
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x)))
\]

but there is no accompanying paragraph that describes the backward / gradient path or an implementation snippet in the text.

Given that omission, the only safe statements are: (1) the paper does not specify the estimator, and (2) the usual, well-established practice for training networks with non-differentiable quantizers (and what BitNet-style papers normally use) is to employ a straight-through estimator (STE) and to keep a latent real-valued copy of the weights that is updated by SGD/Adam. I therefore summarize the likely (and practical) design they almost certainly used, explain why, and point out how you can confirm it.

What is almost certainly done (practical/standard choice)
- A latent full-precision weight tensor $W$ (the one in the equations) is maintained and updated by the optimizer. The quantized weights $\widetilde{W}$ are obtained from $W$ by the RoundClip transform and used in the forward pass (and in inference).
- For the backward pass a Straight-Through Estimator is used: the gradient is propagated from the loss through $\widetilde{W}$ back to $W$ as if the quantization were the identity. Concretely, a common implementation makes the backward derivative $d\widetilde{W}/dW$ equal to $1$ (or $1$ inside the clipping interval and $0$ where a hard clip saturates), i.e. the gradient w.r.t. $\widetilde{W}$ is passed to $W$ directly. This lets the optimizer nudge the real-valued $W$ so the rounded values change over training.

Why this is the practical choice
- Without a latent real-valued copy and STE, it is essentially impossible to train: pure discrete updates in ternary space make gradient-based optimization intractable or noisy. Maintaining $W$ and using STE preserves gradient flow and is the standard method in quantized-net literature.
- The STE is simple and empirically effective for rounding-based quantizers; BitNet and other low-bit quantization training papers follow the same pattern (round forward, identity-ish backward).

Edge detail about the clipping behavior
- There are two common variants seen in code: (a) pure STE for round+clip that simply treats $\mathrm{RoundClip}(x)$ as $x$ in the backward pass (derivative $1$ everywhere), and (b) an STE that respects clip saturation so the backward pass zeroes gradients outside the clipping bounds. The paper does not say which variant they used. Both variants are used in the literature; variant (a) is simpler and more typical for rounding operations, while (b) can be used to discourage parameters from moving into saturated regions.

What to do to confirm
- Check the project code / repo or FasterTransformer / Ladder kernels they integrated — the authors say they use BitNet and Ladder kernels and these codebases often reveal the exact backward behavior. If no code is available, ask the authors or look for a supplementary implementation. Empirically you can also probe training behavior: if a latent full-precision copy is used you will see smooth changes in pre-quantized $W$ and discrete jumps in $\widetilde{W}$; pure discrete updates would make optimization very unstable.

In short: the paper omits the explicit backward rule, but the standard, almost-certain implementation is STE with a latent full-precision weight copy that is updated by the optimizer. If you need absolute certainty for reproducibility, check their code or ask the authors which STE variant (identity everywhere vs. zero outside clip) they used.
