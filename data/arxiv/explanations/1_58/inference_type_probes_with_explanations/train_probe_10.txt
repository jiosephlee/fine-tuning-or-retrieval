According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', if, contrary to the paper's observation, non-linear components grew to dominate computation at large scale, BitNet b1.58's relative energy advantage would decrease. The paper’s reported gains stem from replacing FP16-heavy matrix multiplications in nn.Linear with ternary/INT8 additions, which drastically cut arithmetic energy and memory bandwidth for weights; moreover, the authors explicitly note that the fraction of compute in nn.Linear grows with model size, so BitNet’s advantage expands as models scale. Formally, if we write total energy as E_total = E_linear + E_other and the speedup/energy reduction factor as R = (E_linear^{FP16} + E_other)/(E_linear^{b1.58} + E_other), then with the paper’s regime where E_linear dominates and E_linear^{FP16} ≫ E_linear^{b1.58}, R is large; but if the opposite regime held—e.g., normalization, rotary embeddings, softmax/attention control flow, full-precision embeddings/KV-cache handling, or other non-linear/uncore costs began to dominate so that E_other ≫ E_linear—then the denominator and numerator would both be governed by E_other that BitNet does not significantly reduce, driving R toward 1 and shrinking BitNet’s relative advantage. This aligns with the paper’s causal claim that the advantage grows because “the percentage of nn.Linear grows with the model size,” implying that if this percentage instead decreased in favor of non-linear or non-matmul costs, the benefit from ternary-weight matmuls and reduced DRAM traffic would contribute less to overall energy, thereby diminishing the relative gains.
