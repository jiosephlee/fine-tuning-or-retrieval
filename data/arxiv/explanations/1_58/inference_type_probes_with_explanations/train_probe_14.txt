In the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', adding $\\epsilon$ to the denominator in the quantization formula $\\widetilde{W} = \\mathrm{RoundClip}\\left(\\frac{W}{\\gamma + \\epsilon}, -1, 1\\right)$ serves a practical purpose: it prevents division by zero. This follows because the scale $\\gamma$ is defined as the average absolute weight magnitude, $\\gamma = \\frac{1}{nm}\\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$, which can be exactly $0$ when a weight tensor (or block) is all zeros (e.g., after pruning, strong regularization, or transient optimizer states), or can be so small in finite-precision arithmetic that dividing by it would produce numerical overflow; without the $\\epsilon$ term, $\\frac{W}{\\gamma}$ would be undefined (if $\\gamma=0$) or would blow up (if $\\gamma\\approx 0$), pushing most entries to $\\pm 1$ after $\\mathrm{RoundClip}$ and potentially yielding NaNs that would corrupt training through the straight-through estimator. By adding a small $\\epsilon>0$, the normalization remains well-defined and stable in all cases: if $W=0$ then $\\gamma=0$ and $\\frac{W}{\\gamma+\\epsilon}=0$ so $\\widetilde{W}=0$, aligning with the ternary design that near-zero weights quantize to $0$; if $\\gamma$ is merely tiny, $\\epsilon$ limits the effective gain so that small-magnitude weights map near $0$ rather than saturating to $\\pm 1$, preserving the intended feature-filtering behavior and ensuring robust, bounded quantization throughout training and inference.
