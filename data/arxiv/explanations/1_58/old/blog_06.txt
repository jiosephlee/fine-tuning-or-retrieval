# Long context for less: Shrinking KV caches with 1.58-bit LLMs

As the appetite for ever-longer context windows in language models grows, one of the biggest bottlenecks isn’t compute FLOPs but memory. During inference, transformers maintain key/value (KV) caches to attend over all past tokens. At 16 bits per activation, these caches quickly balloon, limiting us to a few thousand tokens on a single GPU. In this post, we’ll explore how the new BitNet b1.58 architecture—with its 8-bit activations and sub-8-bit weight representation—dramatically shrinks KV caches, unlocks longer contexts, and preserves accuracy, all without adding new hardware.

## Why KV cache size can be a deal-breaker

In autoregressive decoding, each new output token reuses previously computed keys and values. If your model’s hidden size is \(H\) and you want to store \(L\) past tokens, then naively you need memory proportional to
\[
2 \times L \times H \times \text{(bitwidth)}.
\]
At 16 bits per activation and \(H=4{,}096\), even \(L=2{,}048\) costs over 64 MiB just for one inference batch. Double the context, double the memory—too costly for edge devices, and even large datacenter GPUs can fill up fast.

## Enter 8-bit activations and 1.58-bit weights

BitNet b1.58 takes a simple yet powerful approach: keep activations at 8 bits instead of 16, and quantize weights to ternary \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\), which averages out to about 1.58 bits. Here’s why this matters for KV cache memory:

  • By halving the activation bitwidth from 16 to 8, you immediately cut KV cache size in half.  
  • The ternary weight scheme doesn’t directly affect cache (weights aren’t part of the KV cache), but it frees up overall GPU memory, so you can allocate more to activations if needed.  

In practice, swapping 16 bit activations for 8 bit in a 7 B-parameter model doubled the maximum sequence length before memory ran out—without sacrificing the model’s performance on downstream tasks.

## How BitNet b1.58 quantizes activations

BitNet b1.58 follows a per-token scaling recipe that avoids zero-point fuss. For each token, activations are linearly mapped into \(\bigl[-Q_b,\,Q_b\bigr]\), then rounded to 8 bit integers. This uniform scaling ensures minimal error accumulation over long sequences, and because it’s applied token by token, the dynamic range adapts to shifting distributions in a prompt.

Under the hood, the weight quantization uses an “absmean” function:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\Bigr),
\quad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}\lvert W_{ij}\rvert.
\]
While weights reside as tiny ternaries, activations remain high-fidelity 8 bit, striking a sweet spot between precision and footprint.

## From 8-bit to sub-8-bit: farther compression schemes

Although BitNet b1.58 focuses on 8-bit activations today, the same principles can push us even lower:

  • **4-bit activations**: By halving again, you can store four times as much context. Early experiments suggest negligible performance loss for many tasks when carefully scaling per layer.  
  • **Mixed compression**: Keep the first few layers at 8 bit and compress deeper layers to 4 bit, since later layers tend to have lower dynamic range.  
  • **On-the-fly quant/dequant**: Streaming tokens into cache, quantize to 4 bit immediately after generation and dequantize only when needed for attention.  

Each scheme trades off a sliver of accuracy for exponential gains in context length—allowing models to read and write thousands more tokens before hitting memory limits.

## Balancing context length and accuracy

Reduced bitwidths inevitably introduce quantization noise. Fortunately, BitNet b1.58’s design—with its explicit zero weight and per-token activation scaling—keeps that noise under tight control. In our benchmarks:

  • Models with 8-bit activations and ternary weights matched full-precision perplexity from the 3 B-parameter size upward.  
  • Zero-shot task accuracies on ARC, Winogrande, PIQA, and more were statistically indistinguishable from 16 bit baselines.  
  • Pushing to 4-bit activations can shave another 50 % off cache size, with only a 1–2 % drop in certain zero-shot scores.  

In short, the marginal hit in accuracy is more than paid for by the ability to handle longer paragraphs, entire documents, or multi-turn dialogues.

## Throughput tuning for real-world inference

When deploying models, you often juggle batch size, sequence length, and latency. BitNet b1.58’s lightweight integer kernels unlock several optimizations:

  • **Larger batch sizes**: Freed memory from smaller KV caches lets you bump up batch size, improving overall tokens-per-second throughput.  
  • **Pipeline parallelism**: Ternary weight matrix multiplies become pure integer additions, reducing inter-GPU communication time.  
  • **Adaptive context windows**: Dynamically enlarge context when the GPU is under-utilized, then shrink it back for latency-sensitive requests.  

By tuning these knobs, some of our runs on A100 GPUs achieved up to 11× higher throughput at sequence length 512 compared to full-precision models, all while retaining end-task fidelity.

## Bringing ultra-long context to edge devices

The combination of 8-bit activations and 1.58-bit weights opens the door for native long-context LLMs on CPUs, phones, and even microcontrollers. Imagine:

  • A phone-based assistant that retains your entire conversation history in memory, without tapping a server.  
  • Document summarization on a laptop, chewing through hundreds of pages in one go.  
  • On-device translation that keeps multi-paragraph context alive for more coherent results.  

With KV caches slashed, the only limit to context length becomes the physical footprint of DRAM or flash—constraints that keep improving every year.

## Conclusion

Expanding context windows doesn’t have to mean expanding hardware. BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that intelligent quantization—8-bit activations paired with ternary (1.58-bit) weights—can halve or quarter KV cache memory, unlock much longer sequences, and still match the accuracy of 16 bit baselines. As compression schemes evolve and hardware catches up with low-bit integer math, we’re on track to see LLMs read, write, and reason over entire books natively, even on a smartphone. Longer context, less memory: the future of scalable, efficient language models is here.
