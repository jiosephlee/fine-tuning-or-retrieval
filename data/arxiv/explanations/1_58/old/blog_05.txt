# Beyond post-training quantization: Why training low-bit from scratch wins

In the quest for ever more efficient large language models (LLMs), post-training quantization (PTQ) has become something of an industry staple. It’s easy to take a fully trained, 16-bit floating-point model and squeeze it down to 8- or even 4-bit integers, dramatically reducing memory and compute requirements without touching the original training pipeline. But PTQ comes with its own set of headaches—accuracy loss, calibration quirks, and sometimes brittle behavior on edge cases. A new wave of research, epitomized by the recent BitNet b1.58 work, shows that teaching your model to live in a low-bit world from day one can be a game-changer. In this post, we’ll compare PTQ to a “quantization-aware” approach that trains a model with 1.58-bit weights and explore why baking quantization into the training recipe yields more accurate, more stable, and overall more robust LLMs.

## The limits of post-training quantization

PTQ typically involves taking a pre-trained network—whose weights are stored in 16-bit floating-point (FP16 or BF16)—and applying a calibration step or a rounding scheme to map those weights and activations into low-bit integer formats. It works well enough for many production scenarios, especially when 8-bit precision is acceptable. But as we push down to 4 bits or fewer, a few pain points emerge:

  • **Accuracy degradation** becomes more pronounced. The quantized network often sees a jump in perplexity or a drop in downstream task performance, and squeezing below 4 bits can make these effects dramatic.  
  • **Calibration complexity** grows. You need representative data to estimate scale and zero-points, and you often have to calibrate each layer or even each channel individually to avoid collapse.  
  • **Brittleness** rears its head. Tiny distribution shifts in your test data can break the carefully tuned quantization scheme.

In short, PTQ can restore a lot of the efficiency you want, but it still fights an uphill battle against a model that has never “seen” low-precision arithmetic during its training journey.

## Training with quantization: the 1.58-bit alternative

Instead of treating quantization as a postscript, why not weave it into training from the ground up? That’s exactly what the BitNet b1.58 architecture does. By constraining every weight to one of three values—$\{-1, 0, +1\}$—the model effectively lives in a 1.58-bit world (since a ternary choice corresponds to $\log_2(3)\approx1.58$ bits). Meanwhile, activations are kept at 8 bits, striking a sweet spot between computational simplicity and expressive power.

What does training at 1.58 bits buy you?

  • **Native low-bit resilience.** The model has never had high-precision shadows lurking in its layers. Every forward and backward pass respects the quantization constraints, so the network learns representations that naturally cope with the reduced numeric range.  
  • **Superior stability.** There is no separate calibration step—no external data snafus to worry about. The quantization function (often an “absmean” scaling followed by rounding and clipping) lives inside the computational graph, so gradients flow through it and adjust weight distributions accordingly.  
  • **Hardware and energy benefits.** Matrix multiplications reduce to mostly integer additions, slashing energy use by up to 70× for the GEMM kernels on modern 7 nm processes. Memory bandwidth shrinks dramatically, too, because each weight is stored in only 2 bits (with a tiny extra overhead to represent the ternary state).

## Why the zero matters: the magic of ternary weights

You might wonder, “Why not stick with pure binary $\{-1,+1\}$?” The inclusion of the zero value turns out to be more than just a curiosity—it’s a modeling boon. In essence, having a zero weight directly implements a kind of feature filtering or sparsification:

  • **Selective connectivity.** Neurons can explicitly “turn off” specific inputs, focusing capacity on the most important pathways.  
  • **Lower quantization error.** When a weight truly belongs near zero, rounding to zero inflicts less distortion than forcing it to $\pm1$.  
  • **Improved generalization.** Across benchmarks such as ARC, Hellaswag, and Winogrande, models with ternary weights match full-precision baselines at 3 billion parameters, whereas binary-only networks tend to lag.

This explicit support for zeros helps BitNet b1.58 not only recover perplexity performance but actually close the gap with FP16 models on a wide array of zero-shot tasks.

## A practical recipe for 1.58-bit training

Switching from PTQ to quantization-aware training may sound daunting, but the core ideas are straightforward:

1. Quantization function: Use an **absmean** scaling factor $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$, and then apply  
   $$\widetilde W = \mathrm{RoundClip}\bigl(\tfrac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1,1\bigr)\times\gamma.$$  
   This keeps your weights in $\{-1,0,+1\}$ while letting the scaling adapt dynamically.

2. Activations: Quantize to 8 bits per token in the range $[-Q_b,Q_b]$, eliminating zero-point complexity and simplifying implementation.

3. Architecture tweaks: Build on a LLaMA-style Transformer, replacing all linear layers with your custom **BitLinear**, swapping in RMSNorm, SwiGLU activations, rotary embeddings, and omitting biases entirely.

4. Training setup: Pre-train from scratch on your chosen corpus (e.g., 100 billion RedPajama tokens), using standard learning rates and schedules. The quantization operations are embedded right in the forward pass, so there’s no separate calibration phase.

5. Inference engine: Integrate with optimized kernels (for instance, NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer plus a custom 2-bit path) to unlock the 2–4× speedups and 3–5× memory reductions you’ll see across model sizes from 3 B up to 70 B parameters.

## Pitfalls to watch out for

Even though the recipe is conceptually clear, practitioners should keep an eye on a few tricky aspects:

  • **Hyperparameter transfer.** Learning rates, warmup lengths, and weight decay tuned for FP16 may not directly port to the 1.58-bit regime. Be prepared to sweep carefully.  
  • **Quantization noise.** The straight-through estimator used for rounding gradients can introduce variance. Gradient clipping or adaptive optimizers (e.g., AdamW) often help.  
  • **Software compatibility.** You’ll likely need to extend your training framework (PyTorch, JAX, etc.) with custom CUDA kernels or XLA primitives for the ternary operations.  
  • **Hardware support.** While the model itself is low-bit, you still need device kernels that can handle mixed INT8 additions efficiently. In many cases, you may lean on GPU libraries or specialized accelerators that support 2-bit or ternary paths.

## Conclusion: time to move beyond PTQ

Post-training quantization has served us well, but it is inherently a compromise—an afterthought tacked onto models that never anticipated low-bit arithmetic. Training with quantization from day one, especially in a ternary 1.58-bit regime, not only matches full-precision performance but also delivers major wins in speed, memory, and energy. By embracing quantization-aware training and explicit zeros, you end up with a more robust, stable model that’s ready for edge devices, data centers, or custom AI hardware. If you’re building next-generation LLMs, it’s time to ask not “How low can we quantize?” but rather “How low-bit can we train from scratch?”
