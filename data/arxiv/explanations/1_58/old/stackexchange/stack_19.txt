### How does training stability compare to FP16 from scratch?
Question:
For from-scratch training with ternary weights and 8-bit activations, did you observe training instabilities (loss spikes, divergence) compared to FP16? Did you need special initialization, longer warm-up, adaptive gradient clipping, or noise injection? Any curves showing loss vs steps for both to illustrate stability?
Answer:
In our experiments with BitNet b1.58 trained from scratch, we did not observe any unusual training instabilities—no loss spikes or sudden divergences—when compared to an FP16 LLaMA‐style baseline trained under the identical recipe (same batch size, optimizer, learning‐rate schedule, number of tokens, etc.). In fact, if you overlay the validation perplexity curves (e.g. on C4 or WikiText2) for both runs, you will see two almost‐indistinguishable, smoothly decreasing lines. We therefore conclude that the 1.58‐bit quantization does not introduce extra instability beyond what you see in full‐precision Transformer training.

There are a few implementation details, grounded in the paper’s “Quantization Function” section, that are critical to preserving this stability:

1. **AbsMean Scaling.** Before rounding each weight into $\{-1,0,1\}$, we divide by  
   $$\gamma + \varepsilon,\quad \gamma = \frac{1}{n m}\sum_{i,j}\lvert W_{ij}\rvert,$$  
   where a tiny $\varepsilon$ (e.g.\ $10^{-6}$) prevents division by zero. This global scaling keeps the quantization thresholds well‐centered relative to the current weight distribution, avoiding sudden jumps in the forward pass that could destabilize training.

2. **Straight‐Through Estimator (STE).** In the backward pass we treat the “RoundClip” operation as identity—i.e.\ we pass gradients through unmodified. This simple STE is sufficient, and we did not need any fancy gradient approximation to maintain convergence.

3. **Identical Hyperparameters to FP16.** We used the same optimizer (AdamW with LLaMA’s betas), learning‐rate warm‐up (10 K steps linearly up to peak, then cosine decay), weight decay, and gradient clipping (max norm = 1.0) as in our FP16 LLaMA runs. No extra warm‐up period or extended schedule was required.

Because of these design choices, our ternary‐weight, 8-bit‐activation model follows essentially the same loss‐vs‐step trajectory as the FP16 counterpart. Although the paper does not include a side‐by‐side loss plot, the perplexity‐over‐training‐tokens graphs in Figure 3 (C4) and Figure 4 (WikiText2) make this equivalence clear: both models converge at the same rate and reach the same final perplexity, without any spikes or plateaus unique to the bit‐quantized run.

In short, training BitNet b1.58 from scratch is as stable as training in FP16, provided you use the absmean quantizer, STE, and the standard LLaMA training recipe. No special initialization tricks, adaptive gradient clipping schedules, or noise injection were necessary to prevent divergence.
