### How does this approach differ from post-training quantization (PTQ) to 2–3 bits?
Question:
The paper argues PTQ is sub-optimal and trains from scratch with ternary weights. Could you quantify the gap versus the best PTQ methods at 2–3 bits (e.g., with per-channel scales and GPTQ/AWQ-like error compensation)? Is the main advantage better optimization (weights learn to be ternary) or architectural changes (e.g., removal of biases, RMSNorm, symmetric activation quant)?
Answer:
In the paper “The Era of $1$-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in $1.58$ Bits,” the authors do not actually include a head-to-head comparison to $2$–$3$-bit post-training quantization (PTQ) methods such as GPTQ or AWQ. However, if you look across the literature, the state-of-the-art $3$-bit PTQ schemes on a model like LLaMA-7B still pay a non-trivial accuracy penalty—on the order of $+0.5$ to $+1.0$ perplexity (WikiText2-style) and a $1$–$3\%$ absolute hit on zero-shot benchmarks—relative to the FP16 baseline. Even the very best $3$-bit AWQ variants that add per-channel scale updates and error correction typically leave a $0.1$–$0.2$-point PPL gap. By contrast, BitNet b1.58, at only $1.58$ bits per weight, matches the FP16 reference exactly starting at $3$B parameters (and continues to incur essentially zero gap at larger sizes), all while cutting inference latency by roughly $2.7\times$ and GPU memory by $3.5\times$ (Table 1 of the paper).

Why is the scratch-training ternary approach so much stronger than PTQ? The short answer is that PTQ is a “one-shot” retrofit: you take a network optimized in floating point, then you clamp and round its weights, optionally sprinkle in some Hessian-based error corrections (GPTQ) or re-scale each channel (AWQ). You never give the network a chance to **learn** parameters that are inherently compliant with a $\{-1,0,1\}$ alphabet. As a result, PTQ methods inevitably leave behind irreducible quantization noise.

By training **from scratch** with the ternary constraint baked into the loss (via the absmean scaling and RoundClip operator), BitNet effectively sculpts its weight distributions so that they sit exactly on the ternary grid. Every gradient step “knows” there are only three possible levels, so the model discovers representations that are naturally quantizable. That alone closes the performance gap that PTQ cannot.

The architectural tweaks—removing bias terms, switching to RMSNorm, using symmetric $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ activation quantization, adopting SwiGLU and Rotary Embeddings—are all there to make the network more “quant-friendly.” For example, zero-point-free symmetric activations avoid any bias-induced shift that would force uneven rounding, and RMSNorm (which has no learned scale/bias) produces stable magnitudes that work hand-in-glove with the absmean quantizer. In isolated ablations (not shown in this paper), you typically lose only a few tenths of a perplexity point if you drop one of these tweaks—but you lose **an order of magnitude more** when you switch from scratch-trained ternary to PTQ on a vanilla Transformer.

In sum, the **primary** reason BitNet b1.58 beats $2$–$3$-bit PTQ is **end-to-end quantization-aware training** of ternary weights. The LLaMA-style modifications are important to stabilize and simplify that training—especially at $1.58$ bits—but they are secondary. PTQ simply has no way to adapt the underlying model to its own coarse quantizer, whereas BitNet learns its quantizer and weights jointly from the start.
