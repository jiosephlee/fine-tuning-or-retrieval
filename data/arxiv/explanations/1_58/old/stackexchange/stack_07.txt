### What accumulator bit-width is used for INT8 additions in matmuls?
Question:
With 8-bit activations and ternary weights, the dot products can sum O(d) terms where d is the hidden size. What integer accumulator width is used to avoid overflow (e.g., INT32)? Is there any scaling or block-wise accumulation to keep sums within bounds, and does this introduce any extra dequantization steps that add latency?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 implementation we never had to invent a custom “small accumulator” scheme or chop the dot-product into sub-blocks—the matmuls simply use the standard 8-bit-input, 32-bit-accumulator path that you find in most int8 GEMM kernels (in our case the 2-bit extension from Ladder inside NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer). Concretely, your activations are quantized to signed 8-bits (range $[-Q_b, +Q_b]$) and your weights are ternary $\{-1,0,1\}$, so each multiply is just a conditional add/subtract, and the sum over the full hidden-size $d$ lives in a 32-bit register. Even for $d = 4096$ and $Q_b = 127$ the worst-case magnitude ($4096 \times 127 \approx 5 \times 10^5$) is nowhere near the $2^{31}$ limit, so there’s no overflow risk and no need to accumulate in smaller blocks.

Once the int32 accumulate is done, we must “dequantize” back into the domain expected by the next layer (e.g.\ FP16 for the RMSNorm and SwiGLU). That is handled by a single fused epilogue inside the same GEMM kernel: the int32 result is multiplied by the pre-computed weight scale $\gamma$ and the per-token activation scale, then cast to the target type. Because this requant/scale step is part of the very same kernel as the add-only dot product, it does not incur an extra CUDA launch or a separate memory pass, and in our end-to-end measurements it adds essentially zero additional latency beyond the impressive $2$–$4\times$ speed-ups reported for BitNet b1.58.
