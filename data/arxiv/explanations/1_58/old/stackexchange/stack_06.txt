### How are attention-specific computations handled in 1.58-bit LLMs?
Question:
In attention, we have Q, K, V projections and then softmax over scaled dot products. Are Q/K/V projection weights ternary as well, and are the activations quantized to 8 bits before the softmax? Is softmax computed in FP16/BF16 for stability? If activations are per-token scaled to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\), how do you ensure numerical stability of the attention logits and softmax?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58 every matrix multiply in the Transformer—including the Q, K, V projections—is handled by the same ternary‐weight “BitLinear” operator, and all activations are carried in 8 bits (per‐token). Here is roughly how self‐attention works under the hood:

First, recall that BitLinear quantizes each weight to $ \{-1,0,+1\} $ via the “absmean” rounding in the paper, and that every hidden state vector is quantized to an 8-bit integer range $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ by a simple linear scale (no learned zero-point). Concretely, before each linear or nonlinearity, you compute a scale $\alpha$ per token so that the floating-point activations $h$ satisfy

$$
h_{q} = \mathrm{round}\bigl(h/\alpha\bigr)\in\{-Q_b,\dots,Q_b\},\quad
h\approx \alpha\,h_{q}.
$$

This symmetric quantization lets you discard zero-points and keeps quantization error small.

When you do the Q-projection, you feed those 8-bit activations $h_{q}$ into BitLinear with ternary weights $W\in\{-1,0,1\}$. Because multiplying by $\pm1$ or $0$ is just a sign flip or zero, the entire weight × activation inner product reduces to integer additions (and subtractions). Those sums accumulate in a higher-precision accumulator (e.g.\ a 32-bit int or even FP16), and you end up with quantized integer Q, K, V vectors.

Next, to form the attention logits you take the dot-product of these quantized Q and K sequences. In practice, one “casts” the integer accumulator to a floating format—BitNet b1.58 typically uses BF16 at this stage because BF16’s large exponent range avoids overflow when summing up many products of 8-bit values. You then divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$ exactly as in the standard Transformer:

$$
z_{ij} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}\;Q_i\cdot K_j.
$$

At this point you switch to the usual numerically stable softmax trick: subtract the row-max from each logit,

$$
\hat z_{ij} = z_{ij} - \max_j z_{ij},
$$

compute $\exp(\hat z_{ij})$ and normalize. All of these exponentials, sums, and divisions are done in FP16 or BF16 to ensure you don’t underflow or overflow; hardware vendors provide fused softmax kernels in those precisions for exactly this reason. Finally the attention weights $\mathrm{softmax}(z)$ (now a small FP16/BF16 tensor) multiply the quantized-integer V vectors. One can cast V to FP16, do the weighted sum in FP16, and then requantize the output back to 8 bits per token for the next layer.

In summary:

– Yes, the Q/K/V projection weights are ternary $ \{-1,0,+1\} $.  
– Yes, activations (including those feeding into softmax) live in 8-bit, per-token scaled integer form until you need floating-point for the softmax itself.  
– Softmax and its scaling by $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ are done in FP16/BF16 for numerical stability, using the standard “subtract max” trick to keep exponentials in a safe range.  
– Per-token quantization to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ plus casting the attention logits into BF16 (or FP16 when safe) ensures that even if $|Q_i\cdot K_j|$ is large, you never overflow the softmax or lose precision in the exponentials.

This hybrid approach lets BitNet b1.58 replace virtually all the weight × activation multiplications with cheap integer add/sub operations, while relegating just the softmax and its downstream weighted sums back into floating-point where they’re most sensitive to numerical issues.
