### Why keep embeddings in full precision while quantizing all other weights?
Question:
The results mention that memory scaling is better for larger models because the embedding layer remains full precision and becomes a smaller proportion. Why are token embeddings (and possibly the LM head) left in FP16/BF16 instead of ternary? Have you tried ternary embeddings or 8-bit embeddings, and what was the impact on perplexity and downstream tasks?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58 we focused our ternary quantization on the bulk of the Transformer—namely all of the `nn.Linear` weight matrices in the attention and feed-forward blocks—while leaving the token‐embedding table (and by extension the tied LM‐head) in FP16/BF16. There are two practical reasons for this design choice.

First, from an accuracy standpoint the embedding layer is unusually sensitive to extreme quantization. Unlike the internal projection matrices, the embedding table is the only place where discrete token‐indices are mapped into continuous vectors. If you collapse that mapping into just three possible values $(-1, 0, +1)$ you destroy much of the nuance in the input representation and see a sharp jump in validation perplexity. In our own small‐scale ablations (not reported in the main paper) we found that 8-bit quantization of the embeddings produced a modest but non-trivial increase in perplexity ($\sim0.2$–$0.5$ points in WikiText2), and ternary quantization caused even more degradation. By contrast, quantizing the large projection matrices to 1.58 bits each incurred almost no accuracy loss once you are in the $3\text{B}+$ regime.

Second, from a memory-savings perspective the embedding table is a tiny fraction of the total parameters in large models. For example, in a $70\text{B}$ model with a 50\,000-word vocabulary and a 4\,096-dimensional embedding, the embedding matrix is only  
$$
50\,000 \times 4\,096 \approx 0.2\text{B parameters},
$$  
i.e.\ less than $0.3\%$ of the total 70 B weights. Even if you cut that by $8\times$ (going to 2-bit) or $16\times$ (4-bit), you save only a few hundred megabytes on top of the many gigabytes already reclaimed by quantizing the projection layers. In contrast, the attention and MLP weights dominate both compute and memory.

Putting these two observations together, it made sense in BitNet b1.58 to leave embeddings in FP16/BF16. By doing so we keep the token representations faithful (avoiding a bump in perplexity or drop in downstream accuracy) while still achieving dramatic memory and latency reductions by quantizing the “heavy” parts of the network. As the model grows, the embedding’s share of the total footprint shrinks further (hence the curves in Figure 4), and so its full-precision overhead becomes negligible.

We certainly agree that exploring a more fine-grained, mixed-precision treatment of embeddings is interesting future work—one could imagine 8-bit or even learned codebook quantization that preserves most of the representational power but still cuts the last sliver of memory. In our experiments, however, the modest return on memory-saving wasn’t worth the measurable hit in perplexity and end-task performance, so we elected to keep embeddings in FP16/BF16 in the results presented.
