### What exactly is the "2-bit kernel from Ladder" used for ternary weights?
Question:
The runtime measurements use a 2-bit kernel from Ladder even though the weights are ternary (3 states). How are the three states encoded into 2 bits, and is there any wasted state or remapping cost? Does the kernel natively support {−1, 0, +1}, or do you pack as 2-bit and handle mapping in microcode, and how much overhead does that introduce?
Answer:
In our benchmarks we lean on Ladder’s off-the-shelf 2-bit GEMM kernel to drive the ternary matrices $\{-1,0,+1\}$. Even though we only have three weight‐states, Ladder treats every weight as exactly two bits, and internally decodes those two bits into “presence” and “sign” on the fly—no separate software remapping step is needed.

Here’s the usual encoding Ladder employs:

  • bit 0 (we’ll call it $p$) is the “presence” flag, $p=1$ if the weight is nonzero, and $p=0$ if the weight is zero.  
  • bit 1 (call it $s$) is the “sign” bit, $s=0$ for a positive entry, $s=1$ for a negative entry.  

With that you get exactly three useful code‐words:

  • $(+1)\leftrightarrow(p,s)=(1,0)$  
  • $(-1)\leftrightarrow(p,s)=(1,1)$  
  • $(0)\leftrightarrow(p,s)=(0,\ast)$  

That last “$\ast$” means the sign‐bit of a zero weight is simply ignored (and often set to 0 at pack time), so the fourth pattern $(0,1)$ is harmlessly aliased to zero as well.

Under the hood the Ladder kernel pulls in long 64- or 128-bit words of these 2-bit codes, then does two parallel bit-wise population counts: one for the positives, one for the negatives. Concretely, it forms

$$
\text{count}_{+} \;=\;\sum_i\bigl(p_i \;\&\;\neg s_i\bigr),
\qquad
\text{count}_{-} \;=\;\sum_i\bigl(p_i \;\&\;s_i\bigr),
$$

and then accumulates

$$
\sum_i w_i\,x_i \;=\;\bigl(\text{count}_{+}-\text{count}_{-}\bigr)\times (\text{block‐wise sum of }x_i).
$$

All of that happens inside the GEMM microkernel by a handful of XOR/AND/popcount instructions and an integer subtraction. There is no extra “lookup table” or per-weight branch in your host code, and no run-time remapping step beyond what Ladder already fuses into its kernel.

Because the only “overhead” relative to a pure 1-bit ($\pm1$) kernel is the extra bit‐plane and one more popcount/bitwise op, the performance cost is negligible compared to the memory‐bandwidth and pipeline stalls you’d pay anyway. In fact, the latency numbers in the paper’s Table 3 and Figure 4 already fold in exactly this 2-bit Ladder kernel overhead, so the $2.4\times$–$4.1\times$ speedups reported for BitNet b1.58 over FP16 LLaMA include any microkernel cost of carrying that extra zero state.
