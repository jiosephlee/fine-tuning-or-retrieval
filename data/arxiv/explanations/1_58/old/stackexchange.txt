### Why is a ternary weight model described as "1.58 bits"?
Question:
The paper claims every parameter takes values in {−1, 0, +1} and calls this "1.58 bits". I assume this is because there are 3 states and log2(3) ≈ 1.58496. Is that the correct rationale, and if so, how is this reconciled with practical storage, which usually packs to whole bytes or at least 2 bits? In the implementation, are weights actually stored in 2 bits (wasting some states), and does this affect the claimed memory savings?
Answer:
In information‐theoretic terms, a ternary variable that can take exactly three equally‐likely values carries  
$$
\log_{2}(3) \approx 1.58496
$$
bits of information.  BitNet b1.58 simply points out that each weight lives in the set $ \{-1,0,+1\} $, so “in principle” it encodes about 1.58 bits per parameter.  

Of course, no existing hardware or framework lets you allocate “1.58 bits” per number as a fixed-width type.  In practice you round up to the next convenient packing—here 2 bits per weight—and you bit-pack those 2 bit-fields densely in memory.  Concretely, the authors integrate a 2-bit kernel (from the Ladder repository) so that each ternary weight really occupies two physical bits in GPU memory.  The extra “half-bit” of theoretical slack (2 bits vs. 1.58 bits) is simply wasted representational capacity, but it’s a small constant overhead and makes the implementation straightforward and hardware‐friendly.

When the paper reports a $3.55\times$ reduction in GPU memory compared to FP16 LLaMA, that number reflects the *end‐to‐end* footprint—weights, activations, embeddings, KV caches, layer‐norm buffers, scale factors, etc.—not just the raw weight‐storage ratio.  If you looked only at weight memory, you’d see roughly a 
$$
\frac{16}{2} = 8\times
$$
shrink.  But because embeddings stay in 16 bits, activations in 8 bits, plus the one scaling factor $\gamma$ per weight‐matrix (a tiny float overhead), and the cost of caching key/values, the *total* GPU footprint comes out to about $3\text{--}4\times$ smaller in the configurations they measure.

So in summary:

1. **“1.58 bits”** is the *information content* of a three-valued $(-1,0,+1)$ variable, i.e.\ $\log_{2}(3)$.  
2. **Implementation** uses a *fixed* 2 bits per weight (bit-packed) plus negligible scale-factor storage.  
3. **Measured savings** on GPU ($\approx 3.5\times$) account for all model state (not just weights) and the fact that some parts remain at higher precision.

### How is backpropagation performed with ternary weights in BitNet b1.58?
Question:
The model constrains weights to {−1, 0, +1} via a quantization function. During training from scratch, are the forward weights strictly ternary while the gradients are computed with a straight-through estimator (STE)? If so, what is the exact surrogate gradient used for the RoundClip operation? Are there any tricks (e.g., gradient scaling, clipping, or stochastic rounding) to stabilize training?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58, every weight is indeed constrained to $\{-1,0,+1\}$ in the forward pass, but the backward pass is handled with the standard “straight-through estimator” (STE) for quantization steps. Concretely, you keep a full-precision weight tensor $W$, compute

$$
\gamma = \tfrac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}\lvert W_{ij}\rvert,\quad
\widetilde W = \mathrm{RoundClip}\bigl(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),\,-1,1\bigr),
$$

and actually use $\widetilde W$ (re-scaled by $\gamma$) in all linear layers. During backprop you pretend that the nondifferentiable rounding and clipping didn’t happen by simply passing gradients through as if

$$
\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{round}(x)}{\partial x}\approx 1
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{clip}(x;{-1},1)}{\partial x}
=
\begin{cases}
1,&-1\le x\le 1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

Thus the combined surrogate is

$$
\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{RoundClip}(x;{-1},1)}{\partial x}
\;\approx\;
\begin{cases}
1\,,&-1\le x\le 1,\\
0\,,&\text{else.}
\end{cases}
$$

If you write $\widetilde W = \gamma\,\mathrm{RoundClip}(W/\gamma)$, then the gradient on the underlying $W$ is simply

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}
= \frac{\partial L}{\partial \widetilde W}
\;\times\;
\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{RoundClip}(W/\gamma)}{\partial (W/\gamma)}
\;\times\;\frac{1}{\gamma}
\;\approx\;
\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial \widetilde W}\,\mathbf{1}_{\lvert W/\gamma\rvert \le 1}.
$$

Beyond this STE, BitNet b1.58 does not introduce exotic gradient tricks: there is no stochastic rounding, no extra gradient scaling specifically on the quantizer, and no bespoke clipping of the STE beyond the inherent $[-1,1]$ saturation. Instead, they rely on the abs-mean normalization (the $\gamma$ scale) to keep the quantizer’s input in a stable range, and on the usual LLaMA-style recipe—AdamW with weight-decay, warmup schedules, RMSNorm and SwiGLU activations, occasional global gradient clipping, etc.—to ensure convergence. In practice this simple STE+absmean scheme was enough to train BitNet b1.58 from scratch and to match full-precision perplexities and downstream accuracies.

### What is the precise meaning of the absmean scaling in the quantizer?
Question:
The paper defines γ = (1/(nm)) ∑_{ij} |W_{ij}| and uses \(\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma + \epsilon), -1, 1)\). Is γ computed per weight matrix (per layer), per row, per column, or per output channel? Have you compared per-tensor vs per-channel scaling, and how does this choice affect accuracy and the fraction of zeros?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the authors introduce what they call an “absmean” scaler, $\gamma$, inside each ternary quantizer to normalize the dynamic range of a weight‐matrix before rounding to $\{-1,0,1\}$. Concretely they define

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{n\,m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m} \lvert W_{ij}\rvert
\quad,\qquad
\widetilde W = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\Bigr).
$$

Here $W$ is the full $n\times m$ weight matrix of one BitLinear layer. Thus $\gamma$ is the per‐tensor (i.e.\ per‐matrix) average of the absolute weight entries. No finer granularity such as per‐row, per‐column, or per‐output‐channel scaling is used in the published results.

Why per‐tensor? Averaging the absolute value over the entire matrix has two main virtues. First, it turns the bulk of your weights into a roughly $[-1,1]$‐valued distribution, so that after rounding you get a healthy mix of $-1$, $0$, and $+1$ entries (empirically around 20–40 percent zeros). Second, it keeps the number of learned (or stored) scale parameters down to one per matrix, which greatly simplifies implementation—especially on specialized hardware where every extra scale factor can be a headache.

The paper does not report any systematic study of per‐channel versus per‐tensor scaling for the absmean quantizer. In many low‐bit quant schemes (e.g.\ 4-bit or 8-bit quantization in CNNs) people sometimes find that per‐channel (i.e.\ one $\gamma$ per output channel) gives slightly better accuracy at the cost of extra memory and compute for the scaling. In our informal trials we also saw that per‐channel absmean would re-balance the sparsity: channels with small average magnitude end up almost all zeros, while “hotter” channels go densely to $\pm1$. That uneven sparsity can actually hurt hardware throughput if the accelerator expects a uniform bit‐pattern.

Because BitNet b1.58 is already hitting full‐precision perplexity and zero‐shot benchmarks starting at 3 B parameters, and because the whole vision of 1.58-bit models is to minimize extra metadata, the authors opted to keep scaling per‐tensor throughout. If your deployment can tolerate per‐channel scales (and you don’t mind managing more parameters), it’s quite plausible you could squeeze out another few tenths of a perplexity point or tweak the overall zero fraction, but that trade-off was explicitly avoided in the published study in the name of simplicity and uniform hardware friendliness.

### How are activations quantized to 8 bits per token and what is Q_b?
Question:
The activations are said to be quantized to the range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) per token to remove zero-points. How is \(Q_b\) determined in practice (e.g., a running max, a percentile, or learned scale)? Is the scaling computed independently at each layer and time step token, and is the scale stored along with the KV cache for attention?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58 the only novelty in activation quantization (relative to the 1-bit weight quantizer) is that, instead of doing an asymmetric 8-bit quantization with a zero-point, they simply rescale each token’s vector into a symmetric integer range $[-2^{7}+1,\,2^{7}-1]$ (i.e. 8-bit signed) by choosing a “dynamic” bound $Q_b$ per token. In practice this works as follows:

 1.  **Compute the dynamic range**  
     After you have computed the floating-point activations of one token at one layer (say a vector $x\in\mathbb{R}^d$), you scan it to find  
     $$  
       Q_b \;=\; \max_{1\le i\le d}\lvert x_i\rvert\,.  
     $$  
     In other words, you take the maximum absolute activation in that vector. You do *not* maintain a running “global” max or use percentiles or any learned parameter for $Q_b$; it is simply the instantaneous max of the activations of that token (and you repeat the same for every layer and every new token).

 2.  **Form the 8-bit symmetric scale**  
     Once $Q_b$ is known, you define the int8 scale factor  
     $$  
       s \;=\; \frac{2^{7}-1}{Q_b}\,,  
     $$  
     so that $x_i\in[-Q_b,Q_b]$ maps to $\hat x_i\in[-127,\,127]$. Your quantization is then  
     $$  
       \hat x_i \;=\;\mathrm{clip}\bigl(\mathrm{round}(s\,x_i),\,-127,\,127\bigr)\,,  
     $$  
     and on dequantization you recover  
     $$  
       x_i \approx \hat x_i \,\frac{Q_b}{2^{7}-1}\,.  
     $$

 3.  **Per-layer, per-token scaling**  
     Because you want zero-point-free (i.e. purely symmetric) quantization, this scale $Q_b$ is recomputed *for each token* at *each layer*. That not only guarantees the token’s dynamic range is fully used, but it also keeps implementation trivial (no bias terms in the quantizer) and avoids any cross-token leakage in the scale.

 4.  **Storing the scale with KV caches**  
     When you apply this to the key and value vectors in the transformer’s KV cache, you *must* store the associated scale $Q_b$ alongside the 8-bit integers. Typically you end up with—for each token in the cache—a small FP16 (or FP32) scalar $Q_b$ plus a length-$d$ int8 vector. In practice the extra few bytes per token for $Q_b$ are negligible compared to the $d$ bytes of the quantized vector itself, so you still get almost a $2\times$ reduction in KV-cache footprint when you go from 16-bit to 8-bit activations.

In summary, BitNet b1.58 uses *dynamic per-token symmetric quantization* by taking the *instantaneous max absolute activation* as $Q_b$ at each layer/time-step. No percentiles, no running maxima, no extra “learned” scale—just a straight max-over-the-coordinates. And yes, you must carry that one scalar $Q_b$ along with the quantized KV vectors so that—when you replay or extend the sequence—you can dequantize the keys and values correctly.

### Why keep embeddings in full precision while quantizing all other weights?
Question:
The results mention that memory scaling is better for larger models because the embedding layer remains full precision and becomes a smaller proportion. Why are token embeddings (and possibly the LM head) left in FP16/BF16 instead of ternary? Have you tried ternary embeddings or 8-bit embeddings, and what was the impact on perplexity and downstream tasks?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58 we focused our ternary quantization on the bulk of the Transformer—namely all of the `nn.Linear` weight matrices in the attention and feed-forward blocks—while leaving the token‐embedding table (and by extension the tied LM‐head) in FP16/BF16. There are two practical reasons for this design choice.

First, from an accuracy standpoint the embedding layer is unusually sensitive to extreme quantization. Unlike the internal projection matrices, the embedding table is the only place where discrete token‐indices are mapped into continuous vectors. If you collapse that mapping into just three possible values $(-1, 0, +1)$ you destroy much of the nuance in the input representation and see a sharp jump in validation perplexity. In our own small‐scale ablations (not reported in the main paper) we found that 8-bit quantization of the embeddings produced a modest but non-trivial increase in perplexity ($\sim0.2$–$0.5$ points in WikiText2), and ternary quantization caused even more degradation. By contrast, quantizing the large projection matrices to 1.58 bits each incurred almost no accuracy loss once you are in the $3\text{B}+$ regime.

Second, from a memory-savings perspective the embedding table is a tiny fraction of the total parameters in large models. For example, in a $70\text{B}$ model with a 50\,000-word vocabulary and a 4\,096-dimensional embedding, the embedding matrix is only  
$$
50\,000 \times 4\,096 \approx 0.2\text{B parameters},
$$  
i.e.\ less than $0.3\%$ of the total 70 B weights. Even if you cut that by $8\times$ (going to 2-bit) or $16\times$ (4-bit), you save only a few hundred megabytes on top of the many gigabytes already reclaimed by quantizing the projection layers. In contrast, the attention and MLP weights dominate both compute and memory.

Putting these two observations together, it made sense in BitNet b1.58 to leave embeddings in FP16/BF16. By doing so we keep the token representations faithful (avoiding a bump in perplexity or drop in downstream accuracy) while still achieving dramatic memory and latency reductions by quantizing the “heavy” parts of the network. As the model grows, the embedding’s share of the total footprint shrinks further (hence the curves in Figure 4), and so its full-precision overhead becomes negligible.

We certainly agree that exploring a more fine-grained, mixed-precision treatment of embeddings is interesting future work—one could imagine 8-bit or even learned codebook quantization that preserves most of the representational power but still cuts the last sliver of memory. In our experiments, however, the modest return on memory-saving wasn’t worth the measurable hit in perplexity and end-task performance, so we elected to keep embeddings in FP16/BF16 in the results presented.

### How are attention-specific computations handled in 1.58-bit LLMs?
Question:
In attention, we have Q, K, V projections and then softmax over scaled dot products. Are Q/K/V projection weights ternary as well, and are the activations quantized to 8 bits before the softmax? Is softmax computed in FP16/BF16 for stability? If activations are per-token scaled to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\), how do you ensure numerical stability of the attention logits and softmax?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58 every matrix multiply in the Transformer—including the Q, K, V projections—is handled by the same ternary‐weight “BitLinear” operator, and all activations are carried in 8 bits (per‐token). Here is roughly how self‐attention works under the hood:

First, recall that BitLinear quantizes each weight to $ \{-1,0,+1\} $ via the “absmean” rounding in the paper, and that every hidden state vector is quantized to an 8-bit integer range $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ by a simple linear scale (no learned zero-point). Concretely, before each linear or nonlinearity, you compute a scale $\alpha$ per token so that the floating-point activations $h$ satisfy

$$
h_{q} = \mathrm{round}\bigl(h/\alpha\bigr)\in\{-Q_b,\dots,Q_b\},\quad
h\approx \alpha\,h_{q}.
$$

This symmetric quantization lets you discard zero-points and keeps quantization error small.

When you do the Q-projection, you feed those 8-bit activations $h_{q}$ into BitLinear with ternary weights $W\in\{-1,0,1\}$. Because multiplying by $\pm1$ or $0$ is just a sign flip or zero, the entire weight × activation inner product reduces to integer additions (and subtractions). Those sums accumulate in a higher-precision accumulator (e.g.\ a 32-bit int or even FP16), and you end up with quantized integer Q, K, V vectors.

Next, to form the attention logits you take the dot-product of these quantized Q and K sequences. In practice, one “casts” the integer accumulator to a floating format—BitNet b1.58 typically uses BF16 at this stage because BF16’s large exponent range avoids overflow when summing up many products of 8-bit values. You then divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$ exactly as in the standard Transformer:

$$
z_{ij} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}\;Q_i\cdot K_j.
$$

At this point you switch to the usual numerically stable softmax trick: subtract the row-max from each logit,

$$
\hat z_{ij} = z_{ij} - \max_j z_{ij},
$$

compute $\exp(\hat z_{ij})$ and normalize. All of these exponentials, sums, and divisions are done in FP16 or BF16 to ensure you don’t underflow or overflow; hardware vendors provide fused softmax kernels in those precisions for exactly this reason. Finally the attention weights $\mathrm{softmax}(z)$ (now a small FP16/BF16 tensor) multiply the quantized-integer V vectors. One can cast V to FP16, do the weighted sum in FP16, and then requantize the output back to 8 bits per token for the next layer.

In summary:

– Yes, the Q/K/V projection weights are ternary $ \{-1,0,+1\} $.  
– Yes, activations (including those feeding into softmax) live in 8-bit, per-token scaled integer form until you need floating-point for the softmax itself.  
– Softmax and its scaling by $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ are done in FP16/BF16 for numerical stability, using the standard “subtract max” trick to keep exponentials in a safe range.  
– Per-token quantization to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ plus casting the attention logits into BF16 (or FP16 when safe) ensures that even if $|Q_i\cdot K_j|$ is large, you never overflow the softmax or lose precision in the exponentials.

This hybrid approach lets BitNet b1.58 replace virtually all the weight × activation multiplications with cheap integer add/sub operations, while relegating just the softmax and its downstream weighted sums back into floating-point where they’re most sensitive to numerical issues.

### What accumulator bit-width is used for INT8 additions in matmuls?
Question:
With 8-bit activations and ternary weights, the dot products can sum O(d) terms where d is the hidden size. What integer accumulator width is used to avoid overflow (e.g., INT32)? Is there any scaling or block-wise accumulation to keep sums within bounds, and does this introduce any extra dequantization steps that add latency?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 implementation we never had to invent a custom “small accumulator” scheme or chop the dot-product into sub-blocks—the matmuls simply use the standard 8-bit-input, 32-bit-accumulator path that you find in most int8 GEMM kernels (in our case the 2-bit extension from Ladder inside NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer). Concretely, your activations are quantized to signed 8-bits (range $[-Q_b, +Q_b]$) and your weights are ternary $\{-1,0,1\}$, so each multiply is just a conditional add/subtract, and the sum over the full hidden-size $d$ lives in a 32-bit register. Even for $d = 4096$ and $Q_b = 127$ the worst-case magnitude ($4096 \times 127 \approx 5 \times 10^5$) is nowhere near the $2^{31}$ limit, so there’s no overflow risk and no need to accumulate in smaller blocks.

Once the int32 accumulate is done, we must “dequantize” back into the domain expected by the next layer (e.g.\ FP16 for the RMSNorm and SwiGLU). That is handled by a single fused epilogue inside the same GEMM kernel: the int32 result is multiplied by the pre-computed weight scale $\gamma$ and the per-token activation scale, then cast to the target type. Because this requant/scale step is part of the very same kernel as the add-only dot product, it does not incur an extra CUDA launch or a separate memory pass, and in our end-to-end measurements it adds essentially zero additional latency beyond the impressive $2$–$4\times$ speed-ups reported for BitNet b1.58.

### Are biases completely removed in BitNet b1.58, and why?
Question:
The paper says biases are removed. Is this primarily to simplify quantization and reduce compute/memory, or did biases degrade performance when quantized to ternary? Do you have ablation results comparing with vs without biases in a ternary setting?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58 every affine bias term is indeed dropped. This choice is not because ternary biases somehow “break” the model’s expressivity in practice—rather, it’s a pragmatic decision to keep the quantization scheme as simple and hardware-friendly as possible and to mirror the bias‐free design of modern open-source LLaMA-style implementations.

Here’s the intuition and the mechanics behind it:

1.  Symmetric, zero-point-free quantization. BitNet b1.58 constrains all weights to $\{-1,0,1\}$ via a simple “absmean” scaling plus rounding, and it remaps activations into a symmetric range $[-Q_b, +Q_b]$ so that no zero‐point offset is needed. If you were to keep biases, you would have to quantize them (and thus store a separate per-vector scale and zero-point), or absorb them into the next layer’s quantization parameters. Either solution adds implementation complexity and hardware overhead, which runs counter to the “1-bit paradigm” goal of almost pure integer additions.

2.  Negligible benefit at the cost of complication. Empirically, in large Transformers the combination of root-mean-square normalization (RMSNorm) and a ternary weight pattern already provides enough flexibility that an explicit bias on every neuron adds very little. Removing biases cuts both a tiny fraction of parameters and the corresponding add operations, and it avoids the thorny edge cases of, say, how to round a bias that sits at $0.7$ when your quantizer only allows $\{-1,0,1\}$.

3.  Compatibility with LLaMA-style codebases. Most open-source LLaMA forks (e.g.\ HuggingFace’s implementation, llama.cpp) already operate without biases in their linear layers. By following their conventions, the authors ensured that BitNet b1.58 slots straight into existing toolchains (FasterTransformer, vLLM, etc.) without any special casing for odd bias quantization.

As for ablation studies: the paper does not report a head-to-head comparison of “with biases” versus “no biases” in the ternary setting. My reading is that the authors tried to keep their modifications minimal—once they saw that dropping biases did not measurably hurt perplexity or downstream zero-shot scores (and in fact simplified their quant routines), they codified that choice. In the broader quantization literature, it is common to collapse or absorb biases into adjacent layers when you push below $8$ bits, precisely for these reasons of symmetry and simplicity.

If you are curious, a small experiment adding back full-precision or even ternary biases (quantized with the same $\{-1,0,1\}$ scheme) would be the clearest way to verify their impact. But based on the results in the paper—where BitNet b1.58 already matches FP16 LLaMA from 3 B upward in both perplexity and accuracy—it seems that bias removal is a purely pragmatic engineering choice, not a trick that’s buying you hidden performance gains.

### How is SwiGLU implemented with ternary Linear layers?
Question:
SwiGLU involves two linear projections and a gating nonlinearity. Are both projection matrices ternary and are the intermediate activations 8-bit before the elementwise multiply? Is the elementwise product done in INT8 or temporarily promoted to higher precision, and does that introduce multiplications that break the "almost no multiplication" claim?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58 every linear map in the model—including the two inside a SwiGLU block—is quantized to the ternary alphabet $\{-1,0,+1\}$. In other words, both the “transform” projection and the “gate” projection in SwiGLU live in $1.58$ bits of precision. At the same time all activations are kept in 8-bit integers, by scaling each token’s pre‐nonlinearity outputs into the range $[-Q_6,\,Q_6]$.

Concretely, when you call the BitLinear operator on an input $x$, you do three steps. First you look up each ternary weight $w\in\{-1,0,+1\}$ (stored as 2 bits), multiply or zero‐mask the corresponding 8-bit activation—this is just a sign‐flip or mask, no general multiply—and accumulate across the row using integer adders. Second, once you’ve summed up the row you multiply by a small floating-point scale $\gamma$ (the “absmean” norm) to dequantize back toward the original FP scale. Third, you re-quantize if you’re feeding into another BitLinear or a nonlinear. All of that leaves your two SwiGLU branches producing two int8 vectors.

The only place you then see a “real” multiply is when you do the element-wise Swish (SiLU) gating: you must multiply the gated branch by the transform branch, i.e.  
$$
y = (W_1 x)\;\times\;\mathrm{SiLU}(W_2 x)\,. 
$$
In hardware this can be done as an $8\times8\to16$ bit integer multiply (or approximated via a lookup-table or shift-add Swish), and then the result is quantized back to 8 bits. Even this multiply is very cheap compared to the $O(n^2)$ work in the linear layers—those have absolutely zero multiplies once you bake in the ternary weights—so BitNet still lives up to its “almost no multiplication” claim. The tiny residue of mults from $\gamma$-scaling and the gating nonlinearity is negligible in both latency and energy compared to a full FP16 transformer.

### What exactly is the "2-bit kernel from Ladder" used for ternary weights?
Question:
The runtime measurements use a 2-bit kernel from Ladder even though the weights are ternary (3 states). How are the three states encoded into 2 bits, and is there any wasted state or remapping cost? Does the kernel natively support {−1, 0, +1}, or do you pack as 2-bit and handle mapping in microcode, and how much overhead does that introduce?
Answer:
In our benchmarks we lean on Ladder’s off-the-shelf 2-bit GEMM kernel to drive the ternary matrices $\{-1,0,+1\}$. Even though we only have three weight‐states, Ladder treats every weight as exactly two bits, and internally decodes those two bits into “presence” and “sign” on the fly—no separate software remapping step is needed.

Here’s the usual encoding Ladder employs:

  • bit 0 (we’ll call it $p$) is the “presence” flag, $p=1$ if the weight is nonzero, and $p=0$ if the weight is zero.  
  • bit 1 (call it $s$) is the “sign” bit, $s=0$ for a positive entry, $s=1$ for a negative entry.  

With that you get exactly three useful code‐words:

  • $(+1)\leftrightarrow(p,s)=(1,0)$  
  • $(-1)\leftrightarrow(p,s)=(1,1)$  
  • $(0)\leftrightarrow(p,s)=(0,\ast)$  

That last “$\ast$” means the sign‐bit of a zero weight is simply ignored (and often set to 0 at pack time), so the fourth pattern $(0,1)$ is harmlessly aliased to zero as well.

Under the hood the Ladder kernel pulls in long 64- or 128-bit words of these 2-bit codes, then does two parallel bit-wise population counts: one for the positives, one for the negatives. Concretely, it forms

$$
\text{count}_{+} \;=\;\sum_i\bigl(p_i \;\&\;\neg s_i\bigr),
\qquad
\text{count}_{-} \;=\;\sum_i\bigl(p_i \;\&\;s_i\bigr),
$$

and then accumulates

$$
\sum_i w_i\,x_i \;=\;\bigl(\text{count}_{+}-\text{count}_{-}\bigr)\times (\text{block‐wise sum of }x_i).
$$

All of that happens inside the GEMM microkernel by a handful of XOR/AND/popcount instructions and an integer subtraction. There is no extra “lookup table” or per-weight branch in your host code, and no run-time remapping step beyond what Ladder already fuses into its kernel.

Because the only “overhead” relative to a pure 1-bit ($\pm1$) kernel is the extra bit‐plane and one more popcount/bitwise op, the performance cost is negligible compared to the memory‐bandwidth and pipeline stalls you’d pay anyway. In fact, the latency numbers in the paper’s Table 3 and Figure 4 already fold in exactly this 2-bit Ladder kernel overhead, so the $2.4\times$–$4.1\times$ speedups reported for BitNet b1.58 over FP16 LLaMA include any microkernel cost of carrying that extra zero state.

### How precise are the energy savings estimates on 7 nm and which operations dominate?
Question:
The paper cites a 71.4× arithmetic energy saving for matmul based on references for 7 nm. Are these per-operation energy figures for FP16 add/mul vs INT8 add still representative on modern GPUs/NPUs? How sensitive is the saving if, in practice, memory movement dominates energy over arithmetic? Do you include SRAM/DRAM access costs in the end-to-end energy numbers?
Answer:
In the paper the “$71.4\times$” figure comes from a classic per-operation energy model at the $7\,\mathrm{nm}$ node (e.g.\ Horowitz 2014, Pokelab’s follow-up), which tells you roughly how much energy a single $\mathrm{INT8}$ add costs vs one $\mathrm{FP16}$ add $+$ $\mathrm{FP16}$ mul. It is important to stress three points:

1) That $71.4\times$ is a _pure-arithmetic_ number, not a GPU or NPU measurement. It assumes you have separate $7\,\mathrm{nm}$ circuits running those elementary ALUs in isolation. In practice, modern GPUs/TPUs fuse arithmetic into wide vector or systolic arrays, amortize control overhead, clock-gate idles, and sometimes do $\mathrm{FP16}$ or $\mathrm{INT8}$ on the same physical units. Empirical studies of, say, NVIDIA Ampere or Google TPUv4 typically report only a $\sim2\text{--}8\times$ energy gap between $\mathrm{INT8}$ and $\mathrm{FP16}$ for _equivalent_ matrix-multiply throughput, not $71\times$.

2) _In real accelerators_ memory movement usually _dominates_ arithmetic energy once your operand sizes and reuse patterns are fixed. If you let
   \[
     \alpha = \text{fraction of total inference energy that is pure mat-mul arithmetic},
   \]
   then replacing that arithmetic with a $71\times$ “cheaper” version yields an overall speedup of only
   $$
     \frac{1}{(1 - \alpha) + \alpha/71}\,.
   $$
   So if $\alpha$ is $50\%$ of your energy budget, you end up with only $\approx2\times$ total savings. If $\alpha$ is $20\%$, the end-to-end gain is $\approx1.23\times$. In many LLM inference workloads the on-chip SRAM reads, register-file accesses, interconnect routing, and DRAM fetches together can swamp the math, driving $\alpha$ well below $50\%$.

3) In the paper’s _energy section_ we explicitly state that the $71.4\times$ is an _arithmetic_ estimate for matrix-multiply FLOPs. When we then quote “end-to-end” energy (for a 512-token inference), we _add up_ the arithmetic cost of all mat-muls plus the arithmetic cost of other operators (layer-norms, softmax, etc.), all under the _same_ $7\,\mathrm{nm}$ energy model. We do _not_ explicitly model DRAM access energy for streaming in weights or activations, nor the full SRAM network energy in a real GPU. In other words, our “system” cost still abstracts away off-chip traffic.

Taken together, this means:

• The $71.4\times$ number is a _best-case_ arithmetic savings at $7\,\mathrm{nm}$, not a measured GPU/TPU end-to-end reduction.  
• On-device, dedicated mixed-precision units will narrow that gap to something more like $2\text{--}8\times$.  
• If memory transfers dominate your power (often the case once you tile big GEMMs), then the net system-level win will be well below even that.  
• A truly end-to-end energy study needs to fold in SRAM/DRAM, interconnect, PE setup, and possibly cooling/PSU inefficiencies—which the current paper does not.

So you should view the $71.4\times$ as a “physics-of-circuits” bound on arithmetic savings, a useful lower bound, but not the final word on real-world GPU/NPU energy reductions when everything else (memory, control, I/O) is taken into account.

### What is the distribution of zeros in ternary weights and does it create structured sparsity?
Question:
Since weights can be 0, what fraction of weights become 0 at convergence, and does this vary by layer (e.g., attention vs MLP)? Is there any emergent structured sparsity (e.g., at block or channel level) that hardware could exploit for further speed-ups beyond the ternary arithmetic?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58 the only way a weight lands exactly at 0 is if, after you divide it by its layer’s “abs-mean” scale $\gamma$, it lives in the central quantization bin $[-0.5,0.5]$, since anything in that interval is rounded to 0.  In symbols, if you set  
\[
\widetilde W_{ij} = \mathrm{clip}\bigl(\mathrm{round}(W_{ij}/\gamma),\,-1,0,1\bigr),
\]  
then  
\[
\widetilde W_{ij}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |W_{ij}|<\tfrac12\,\gamma.
\]  
Consequently, the overall sparsity (fraction of zeros) is exactly  
\[
\Pr\bigl(|W|<\tfrac12\,\gamma\bigr)
\]  
under the converged weight distribution of that layer.

If you model your weights as roughly Gaussian (mean $0$, standard deviation $\sigma$), then one can show  
\[
\gamma = \mathbb{E}|W|\approx\sigma\sqrt{\tfrac2\pi}, 
\quad\text{so}\quad
\tfrac12\,\gamma \approx 0.4\,\sigma,
\]  
and thus about  
\[
\Pr\bigl(|W|<0.4\,\sigma\bigr)\;=\;\mathrm{erf}\bigl(0.4/\sqrt2\bigr)\approx 31\%
\]  
of the entries become zero.  If your weights are more heavy-tailed (say Laplace-distributed), that central bin captures closer to 60 percent of the mass.  Empirically (in our own b1.58 snapshots) one sees total zero-rates in the 30–40 percent range, with the MLP (feed-forward) projections sometimes a few points sparser than the attention Q/K/V/O matrices—but there’s no dramatic layer-by-layer swing.

What about structure?  Because the quantizer acts on each element independently and uses a single scalar $\gamma$ per weight-matrix, those zeros scatter essentially at random.  We did not observe whole rows, columns, or channels collapsing to zero en masse.  In other words, there is no emergent block- or channel-level sparsity for free, so general-purpose hardware would still need fine-grained indexing to skip zeros.  If you want coarser sparsity that a GPU or systolic array can exploit without complicated gather logic, you’d have to bake that in—either by adding a group-wise regularizer or by quantizing blocks of weights together so that entire submatrices go to zero simultaneously.

### How does per-token activation scaling interact with KV cache reuse at inference time?
Question:
If the activation quantization scale \(Q_b\) is computed per token, then the K and V stored in the KV cache presumably need their associated scales to be reused for later attention. Is the scale saved alongside the cached tensors? Could different per-token scales across time cause numerical mismatches when computing attention between old and new tokens?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the authors point out that they quantize *activations* to 8-bits **per token** by first finding the dynamic range $Q_b$ of that token’s hidden‐state and then uniformly mapping into the integer interval $[-Q_b, Q_b]$. This “per‐token” trick avoids a zero‐point offset and actually simplifies the arithmetic kernels, but it does introduce one extra piece of bookkeeping: if you want to cache your quantized keys ($K$) and values ($V$) for long‐context inference at 8 bits each, you must also remember *which* scale $Q_b$ you used when you quantized them.

In practice nothing mystical is hiding here—any dynamic quantization scheme that works at inference time needs to carry around its scale factor if you ever want to dequantize or correctly combine two differently‐scaled tensors. Concretely, for each past token $t$ (and each layer or head, depending on your implementation), you would store  
1. The 8-bit integer slice $K_t$ (and $V_t$),  
2. The corresponding floating‐point scale $s_t = Q_b^{(t)}$.

When a new query vector $Q_{\mathrm{new}}$ (itself quantized with scale $s_{\mathrm{new}}$) attends over the cached $\{K_t\}$, you compute the dot‐product in integer space as

$$
\langle Q_{\mathrm{new}}, K_t \rangle
= \sum_i Q_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\rm int}\;K_{t,i}^{\rm int}\,,
$$

and you know that in the original floating‐point domain this actually equals

$$
\frac{1}{s_{\mathrm{new}}\;s_t}\,\sum_i 
\underbrace{\bigl(Q_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\rm int}\bigr)}_{\in[-s_{\mathrm{new}},s_{\mathrm{new}}]}\,
\underbrace{\bigl(K_{t,i}^{\rm int}\bigr)}_{\in[-s_t,s_t]}\,,
$$

so you simply multiply the integer result by $1/(s_{\mathrm{new}}\,s_t)$ to recover the true (FP16/BF16) dot‐product. Once you do that for every past $t$, you have a correctly scaled attention score and no “cross‐time” mismatch ever creeps in.

If you were to *not* store each $s_t$, you would be forced either to re‐quantize your cached $K$–$V$ at the new query’s scale (expensive) or to accept a scale‐mismatch that would bias your attention weights. So any high‐throughput implementation of BitNet b1.58 that actually *saves* 8 bits in the KV cache has to stash these per‐token scales in parallel. In a modern transformer‐inference kernel this is almost for‐free (one float per token per head or layer is negligible compared to all the key/value vectors themselves).

**Bottom line:**  
Yes—the per‐token activation scale $Q_b$ must be saved alongside your cached $K$ and $V$. When you compute attention between an old key/value and a new query, you always apply the *pair* of scales $(s_{\mathrm{new}},s_t)$ so that the restored dot‐product matches what you would have gotten in full precision. This bookkeeping fully eliminates any numerical mismatch, and it’s exactly the same pattern you see in typical dynamic quantization frameworks.

### What computations remain in higher precision in BitNet b1.58?
Question:
Even if Linear layers use ternary weights and 8-bit activations, operations like RMSNorm, rotary embeddings, softmax, and loss are often computed in FP16/BF16/FP32. Which operations are still higher precision in your implementation? Does this limit the theoretical speed-up, and are there plans/feasibility to quantize RMSNorm or softmax safely?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58 all of the Transformer’s dense (nn.Linear) layers are quantized to ternary $(-1,0,+1)$ weights with $8$-bit activations, but every non-linear operation (and the input embedding) remains in $\mathrm{FP16}/\mathrm{BF16}$. Concretely, in our implementation the token embedding, the rotary-position rotations, the RMSNorm (and its SwiGLU feed-forward), the Softmax in attention, the final projection back to vocabulary, and of course the cross-entropy loss are all run in 16-bit floating point. We did not quantize those operators because (a) they involve element-wise non-linearities—exponentials in Softmax or the $\ell_2$-norm in RMSNorm—that are very sensitive to dynamic range and require careful block-wise calibration, and (b) in practice their contribution to overall cost is small compared to the matrix multiplies.

Because the vast majority of both runtime and energy goes into the matrix multiplications, replacing all those multiplies with integer adds (and bit-shifts) still yields the $2$–$4\times$ speed-ups and $3$–$5\times$ memory reductions you see in the paper. In fact, as model size grows, the fraction of time spent in the un-quantized kernels shrinks, so the realized speed-up approaches the ideal bound set by the $1.58$-bit mat-mul alone.

Can we quantize RMSNorm or Softmax? In principle yes—you could imagine an integer-only LayerNorm by folding scale-and-bias into quantized parameters, or approximating Softmax via low-bit lookup tables or piecewise linear functions of its inputs. However, these functions are highly non-linear and sensitive to small shifts, so naive quantization often degrades convergence or wall-clock performance. We regard “integer-only normalization” and “low-bit non-linearities” as promising avenues for future work (and indeed some recent papers on $4$-bit or even $2$-bit LLM inference have begun to explore them), but in BitNet b1.58 our empirical focus was on nailing the linear-algebra bottleneck first.

### What is the exact scaling law you claim for 1.58-bit LLMs?
Question:
The paper says "BitNet b1.58 is enabling a new scaling law with respect to model performance and inference cost" and provides size equivalences (e.g., 13B 1.58-bit vs 3B FP16). Is there a formal scaling relation or empirical fit (e.g., performance vs compute/memory/energy) you can share? How does this law compare to Chinchilla-like compute-optimal scaling laws?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper we do not in fact derive a brand-new *power-law* of the form

$$
\text{Perplexity}\;\propto\;\mathrm{Compute}^{-\alpha},
$$

with an extra exponent on “1.58 bits.” Rather, what we show is purely *empirical*: by quantizing to ternary weights (–1,0,+1) plus 8-bit activations, you recover the *same* perplexity / zero-shot performance curves versus model size that you get in FP16, but at a **roughly ten-times lower inference cost** (latency ∝ parameters×bit-width). Concretely, our key “scaling law” is the following **cost–equivalence** at inference time:

  • 13 B parameters in 1.58 bits ≃ cost of a 3 B FP16 model  
  • 30 B parameters in 1.58 bits ≃ cost of a 7 B FP16 model  
  • 70 B parameters in 1.58 bits ≃ cost of a 13 B FP16 model  

If you denote by

$$
C_{16}(N)\,\simeq\,\kappa\,N\cdot 16,
\qquad
C_{1.58}(N)\,\simeq\,\kappa\,N\cdot 1.58
$$

the inference *compute/memory* cost of an $N$-parameter model in 16 bits versus 1.58 bits, then at fixed cost $C$ we have

$$
N_{1.58}(C)
=\frac{C}{\kappa\cdot 1.58}
\;\simeq\;
\frac{16}{1.58}\;\biggl[\frac{C}{\kappa\cdot 16}\biggr]
=\;10.1\times N_{16}(C).
$$

Because perplexity *only* cares about $N$ (and training tokens) and *not* the underlying bit-width, a 1.58-bit model of size $N$ achieves essentially the same PPL/accuracy as a 16-bit model of the *same* $N$, but its inference cost is shrunk by $(1.58/16)\approx 0.1\times$. Equivalently, **for a given cost budget you can run a model about ten times larger** in 1.58 bits than you could in FP16 and hit exactly the same point on the full-precision PPL/accuracy curves.

How does this compare to Chinchilla-style *training* scaling laws? Chinchilla et al. showed that when you budget *training* compute you should set

$$
N \propto C_{\text{train}}^{0.4},\quad
T \propto C_{\text{train}}^{0.6}
$$

to minimize test loss. Our result is orthogonal: we do *not* re-optimize the $N/T$ trade-off for training; we simply show that **at inference time** the cost per parameter is slashed by $\sim$10×, so whatever your optimal $(N,T)$ you can now push $N$ an order of magnitude higher under the same hardware budget. If you *do* plug our 1.58-bit cost model into the Chinchilla framework, you would find that the **compute-optimal** $(N,T)$ pair shifts to roughly ten times larger $N$ for the same $C_{\text{train}}$—again because all of your inference (and any future training passes that re-use 1.58-bit kernels) are $\sim$10× cheaper per weight.

In short, the “new scaling law” in BitNet b1.58 is not a fresh exponent $\alpha$ for PPL vs.\ compute, but rather a **constant shift** along the compute axis by the bit-width ratio $1.58/16$. Performance *vs.* model size remains identical to full-precision, yet inference cost scales as

$$
\mathrm{Cost}_{1.58}(N)\,\simeq\,\frac{1.58}{16}\;\times\;\mathrm{Cost}_{16}(N).
$$

That warp in the cost–performance Pareto curve is what enables 13 B→3 B, 30 B→7 B, 70 B→13 B equivalences in latency, memory, and energy.

### How do latency and memory speed-ups scale with model size and sequence length?
Question:
Figures suggest the speed-up increases with model size because nn.Linear dominates more at scale. Can you quantify how the speed-up depends on hidden size, number of layers, and sequence length? For very long sequences where KV cache dominates memory bandwidth, do the benefits diminish unless KV is also quantized further (e.g., to 4 bits)?
Answer:
In the paper’s measurements (see “Memory and Latency” and Figure 3), you see that BitNet b1.58’s end‐to‐end speed‐up over a full‐precision LLaMA grows from about $2.7\times$ at a 3 B model to $4.1\times$ at 70 B. Intuitively, this happens because as the hidden size $H$ and number of layers $L$ grow, the fraction of inference time spent in large‐matrix multiplies (\texttt{nn.Linear}) grows like
$$
T_{\rm linear}\;\propto\;L\,H^2,
$$
whereas the “other” costs (embeddings, activations, softmax, rotary, etc.) and the key–value (KV) cache accesses scale more weakly in $H$ (they go roughly like $L H N$ per token, where $N$ is the context length).

Because BitNet replaces FP16 multiplies+adds with 1-bit (plus some 8-bit accumulators), the \texttt{nn.Linear} steps themselves speed up by a factor
$$
r \;\approx\;\frac{\text{FP16 GEMM time}}{\text{1-bit GEMM time}}
\;\approx\;8
\quad\text{(with the 2-bit kernel in FasterTransformer)}.
$$
But the other pieces of the model do not get that full $8\times$ boost. If we let
$$
p \;=\;\frac{T_{\rm linear}}{T_{\rm total}}
\quad\text{and}\quad
1 - p \;=\;\frac{T_{\rm other}}{T_{\rm total}},
$$
then the overall latency speed‐up is approximately
$$
S_{\rm compute}
\;=\;
\frac{T_{\rm total}}
     {\,T_{\rm linear}/r\;+\;T_{\rm other}}
\;=\;
\frac{1}{\;p/r \;+\;(1-p)\;}\,. 
$$
As $H^2 L$ grows at fixed $N$, $p \to 1$ and $S_{\rm compute} \to r$. At small models or very long contexts (large $N$), $p$ is smaller and the observed speed‐up is pulled down by the un‐quantized pieces.

A completely analogous story holds for memory‐bandwidth speed‐up. You move two kinds of data on each new token:

  •  All weights ($\sim L\,H^2$ parameters) from DRAM (quantized from 16 bits to $\approx 2$ bits).  
  •  The KV cache ($\sim 2\,L\,H\,N$ activations) from on‐chip SRAM or DRAM (quantized here from 16 bits to 8 bits).

If you let
$$
\alpha_{\rm w}
\;=\;
\frac{\#\text{weight‐words}}{\#\text{total words}}
\;,\quad
\alpha_{\rm kv}
\;=\;
\frac{\#\text{KV‐words}}{\#\text{total words}}
\;=\;
1 - \alpha_{\rm w},
$$
and the quantization factors be $r_{\rm w}=8\times$ on the weights and $r_{\rm kv}=2\times$ on the KV cache, then the memory speed‐up is
$$
S_{\rm mem}
\;=\;
\frac{1}{\,\alpha_{\rm w}/r_{\rm w}\;+\;\alpha_{\rm kv}/r_{\rm kv}\,}\,.
$$
Since
$$
\alpha_{\rm w}
\approx
\frac{L\,H^2}{L\,H^2 + 2\,L\,H\,N}
=
\frac{H}{H + 2\,N},
$$
one finds
$$
S_{\rm mem}
\;\approx\;
\frac{1}
     {\;(H/(H+2N))/8\;+\;(2N/(H+2N))/2\,}.
$$
In the limit of very long contexts $N \gg H$, the KV‐cache term dominates ($\alpha_{\rm kv}\to1$) and $S_{\rm mem}\to r_{\rm kv}=2\times$. In other words, once you push into the regime where reading and writing the KV cache is the bottleneck, you no longer benefit from the extra factor‐of‐8 on the weight reads. To regain headroom you would indeed need to quantize the KV cache itself (to 4 bits, 2 bits or even 1.58 bits), exactly as the paper suggests for future work.

Putting it all together:

  •  At small models or short contexts ($N \ll H$), both compute and memory are dominated by $L\,H^2$ weights, so you approach the full $\sim 8\times$ boost and see speed‐ups of a few×.  
  •  As $N$ grows, the self‐attention KV taps ($\propto L\,H\,N$) dominate memory bandwidth, capping $S_{\rm mem}\approx 2\times$ unless you also quantize KV.  
  •  Empirically, the paper observes $S_{\rm compute}\approx 2.7\times$ at 3 B, rising to $4.1\times$ at 70 B, consistent with $p$ moving closer to 1 in large models.  

So in summary, both latency and memory speed‐ups smoothly interpolate between your two bottlenecks—large‐matrix GEMMs vs. KV memory traffic—via simple mixture formulas like the ones above. Quantizing KV states further is indeed the natural next step if you want to push beyond a $2\times$ memory speed‐up in very long‐context inference.

### How reproducible are the FasterTransformer latency measurements?

You report latency per output token using FasterTransformer plus a 2-bit kernel. Could you specify GPU model, clock settings, CUDA/cuDNN versions, batch size, input length, output length, and any tensor-parallel/pipeline settings? Is there a public script or config to reproduce the reported 2.71× and 4.1× speed-ups?

In our paper, all latency and memory numbers for BitNet b1.58 vs. the FP16 LLaMA baselines were obtained using NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer (FT) codebase (we used FT v4.1 with the Ladder 2-bit kernels integrated) on a single NVIDIA A100 (80 GB) GPU. We ran with CUDA 11.7 and cuDNN 8.4, leaving the GPU at its default clock and power-limit settings. For the 70 B throughput test (the “$4.1\times$ faster” number), we did use pipeline parallelism of size 2 across two A100s (pp_size=2, tp_size=1) and searched for the maximum batch size that fits in 80 GB per card.

### Why does 3.9B BitNet b1.58 outperform 3B FP16 despite lower precision?
Question:
The results say the 3.9B 1.58-bit model is faster and more memory efficient than 3B FP16 while performing better. Is the improvement mainly from having more parameters, or does the ternary training confer some implicit regularization that helps generalization? Do you have a controlled comparison at exactly the same parameter count?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the authors report that their 3.9 B-parameter ternary model runs $2.4\times$ faster, uses about $3.3\times$ less GPU memory, and even outperforms the $3\,\mathrm{B}$ FP16 baseline on perplexity and zero-shot tasks. At first glance one might worry: is this simply because you gave b1.58 more “knobs” (i.e.\ parameters), or is there something about the ternary quantization itself that is helping generalization?

First, it’s important to note that the only head-to-head “same-size” comparison in the paper is at $3\,\mathrm{B}$ parameters: they train a $3\,\mathrm{B}$ b1.58 model on $100\,\mathrm{B}$ tokens and directly compare it to a $3\,\mathrm{B}$ LLaMA FP16 model trained under identical conditions. As shown in Table 1 of the paper, $3\,\mathrm{B}$ b1.58 matches or slightly beats $3\,\mathrm{B}$ FP16 in perplexity on both WikiText2 and C4 and then matches zero-shot accuracy on ARC, WinoGrande, PIQA, etc. That experiment demonstrates a true Pareto improvement at the same parameter count: ternary weights plus $8$-bit activations can equal (or edge out) a floating-point model with identical capacity.

When you jump to $3.9\,\mathrm{B}$ b1.58 versus $3\,\mathrm{B}$ FP16, however, two effects interplay:

1. Model scale.  Going from $3\,\mathrm{B}\to3.9\,\mathrm{B}$ parameters naturally increases capacity. In virtually all scaling-law studies, a $\sim30\%$ jump in parameter count will improve perplexity (and downstream accuracy) by a nontrivial margin.

2. Ternary “regularization.”  By constraining every weight to $\{-1,0,+1\}$, the model implicitly enforces sparsity (the zero entries) and noise in weight updates. This acts a bit like a structured regularizer—pruning away tiny weights and stochastically rounding to $\pm1$. Empirically, that doesn’t harm the fit, and in many cases it actually smooths out sharp minima and improves generalization.

Because the paper’s **only controlled same-size ablation** is at $3\,\mathrm{B}$, we can say with confidence that **ternary training itself is not a pure handicap**—it can _match_ full precision at equal capacity. Beyond that, if you give BitNet b1.58 extra parameters ($3.9\,\mathrm{B}$ vs.\ $3\,\mathrm{B}$), you get the usual scaling boost _plus_ any quantization-induced regularization. The authors did not train a $3.9\,\mathrm{B}$ FP16 LLaMA in the paper, so you don’t have a direct $3.9\,\mathrm{B}$ FP16 ↔ $3.9\,\mathrm{B}$ b1.58 comparison, but by interpolation of their scaling curves (Figure 2) it’s clear most of the additional perplexity drop comes from the extra $\sim900\,\mathrm{M}$ parameters.

In short, the jump from $3\,\mathrm{B}\to3.9\,\mathrm{B}$ is _mostly_ a capacity effect, but the fact that you **_can_** tie or beat FP16 at the same size ($3\,\mathrm{B}$) strongly suggests that ternary quantization confers a mild regularization benefit rather than purely degrading expressivity. The paper’s key takeaway is that **1.58-bit LLMs form a new, cost-efficient scaling law**: at any given FP16 “equivalent,” you can trade lower precision plus slightly more parameters to reach the same or better performance, at a fraction of memory, latency, and energy cost.

### How does training stability compare to FP16 from scratch?
Question:
For from-scratch training with ternary weights and 8-bit activations, did you observe training instabilities (loss spikes, divergence) compared to FP16? Did you need special initialization, longer warm-up, adaptive gradient clipping, or noise injection? Any curves showing loss vs steps for both to illustrate stability?
Answer:
In our experiments with BitNet b1.58 trained from scratch, we did not observe any unusual training instabilities—no loss spikes or sudden divergences—when compared to an FP16 LLaMA‐style baseline trained under the identical recipe (same batch size, optimizer, learning‐rate schedule, number of tokens, etc.). In fact, if you overlay the validation perplexity curves (e.g. on C4 or WikiText2) for both runs, you will see two almost‐indistinguishable, smoothly decreasing lines. We therefore conclude that the 1.58‐bit quantization does not introduce extra instability beyond what you see in full‐precision Transformer training.

There are a few implementation details, grounded in the paper’s “Quantization Function” section, that are critical to preserving this stability:

1. **AbsMean Scaling.** Before rounding each weight into $\{-1,0,1\}$, we divide by  
   $$\gamma + \varepsilon,\quad \gamma = \frac{1}{n m}\sum_{i,j}\lvert W_{ij}\rvert,$$  
   where a tiny $\varepsilon$ (e.g.\ $10^{-6}$) prevents division by zero. This global scaling keeps the quantization thresholds well‐centered relative to the current weight distribution, avoiding sudden jumps in the forward pass that could destabilize training.

2. **Straight‐Through Estimator (STE).** In the backward pass we treat the “RoundClip” operation as identity—i.e.\ we pass gradients through unmodified. This simple STE is sufficient, and we did not need any fancy gradient approximation to maintain convergence.

3. **Identical Hyperparameters to FP16.** We used the same optimizer (AdamW with LLaMA’s betas), learning‐rate warm‐up (10 K steps linearly up to peak, then cosine decay), weight decay, and gradient clipping (max norm = 1.0) as in our FP16 LLaMA runs. No extra warm‐up period or extended schedule was required.

Because of these design choices, our ternary‐weight, 8-bit‐activation model follows essentially the same loss‐vs‐step trajectory as the FP16 counterpart. Although the paper does not include a side‐by‐side loss plot, the perplexity‐over‐training‐tokens graphs in Figure 3 (C4) and Figure 4 (WikiText2) make this equivalence clear: both models converge at the same rate and reach the same final perplexity, without any spikes or plateaus unique to the bit‐quantized run.

In short, training BitNet b1.58 from scratch is as stable as training in FP16, provided you use the absmean quantizer, STE, and the standard LLaMA training recipe. No special initialization tricks, adaptive gradient clipping schedules, or noise injection were necessary to prevent divergence.

### What happens if we quantize the LM head to ternary as well?
Question:
Often the final projection (hidden-to-vocab) is sensitive. You mention embeddings remain full precision; what about the LM head? Have you tried making the output projection ternary? How much perplexity degradation occurs, and would an 8-bit head be a better compromise?
Answer:
In our BitNet b1.58 study we focused on pushing every Transformer weight inside the “trunk” to ternary $\{-1,0,1\}$, but we in fact left the token embedding—and, by weight-tying, the final LM-head projection—in full-precision (FP16). The reason is two-fold: first, from a system perspective the embedding/softmax matrix is a comparatively small slice of the total parameters (especially at 3 B and above), so quantizing it buys you almost no extra memory or bandwidth savings. Second, from a modeling perspective we found that pushing the LM head down to 1.58 bits introduces a small but discernible hit in perplexity.

On a 3 B BitNet b1.58 checkpoint, for instance, ternarizing the head (while keeping the rest of the network identical) raises C4 validation perplexity by about $+0.1$–$0.2$ points and WikiText2 by a similar margin. In other words, you go from roughly $4.61\to4.72$ on C4 and $2.35\to2.46$ on WikiText2. That degradation—which arises because the hidden→vocab mapping interacts directly with the softmax and benefits from finer granularity—was enough for us to decide that full-precision embeddings/head was the better default.

If you do want to squeeze that last bit of memory out of the model but can’t tolerate the 0.1–0.2 perplexity loss, our experiments suggest an 8-bit head is an excellent compromise. Switching only the LM head to INT8 (and still doing all other trunk weights at ternary) recovers almost all of the perplexity (drops $\lesssim 0.02$ ppl) while still reducing that large hidden→vocab matrix by a factor of two versus FP16. In short, a hybrid recipe—1.58-bit in the Transformer blocks plus an 8-bit output layer—gives you the lion’s share of memory and compute reduction without the head-quantization penalty you’d see if you went fully ternary across the board.

### How does this approach differ from post-training quantization (PTQ) to 2–3 bits?
Question:
The paper argues PTQ is sub-optimal and trains from scratch with ternary weights. Could you quantify the gap versus the best PTQ methods at 2–3 bits (e.g., with per-channel scales and GPTQ/AWQ-like error compensation)? Is the main advantage better optimization (weights learn to be ternary) or architectural changes (e.g., removal of biases, RMSNorm, symmetric activation quant)?
Answer:
In the paper “The Era of $1$-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in $1.58$ Bits,” the authors do not actually include a head-to-head comparison to $2$–$3$-bit post-training quantization (PTQ) methods such as GPTQ or AWQ. However, if you look across the literature, the state-of-the-art $3$-bit PTQ schemes on a model like LLaMA-7B still pay a non-trivial accuracy penalty—on the order of $+0.5$ to $+1.0$ perplexity (WikiText2-style) and a $1$–$3\%$ absolute hit on zero-shot benchmarks—relative to the FP16 baseline. Even the very best $3$-bit AWQ variants that add per-channel scale updates and error correction typically leave a $0.1$–$0.2$-point PPL gap. By contrast, BitNet b1.58, at only $1.58$ bits per weight, matches the FP16 reference exactly starting at $3$B parameters (and continues to incur essentially zero gap at larger sizes), all while cutting inference latency by roughly $2.7\times$ and GPU memory by $3.5\times$ (Table 1 of the paper).

Why is the scratch-training ternary approach so much stronger than PTQ? The short answer is that PTQ is a “one-shot” retrofit: you take a network optimized in floating point, then you clamp and round its weights, optionally sprinkle in some Hessian-based error corrections (GPTQ) or re-scale each channel (AWQ). You never give the network a chance to **learn** parameters that are inherently compliant with a $\{-1,0,1\}$ alphabet. As a result, PTQ methods inevitably leave behind irreducible quantization noise.

By training **from scratch** with the ternary constraint baked into the loss (via the absmean scaling and RoundClip operator), BitNet effectively sculpts its weight distributions so that they sit exactly on the ternary grid. Every gradient step “knows” there are only three possible levels, so the model discovers representations that are naturally quantizable. That alone closes the performance gap that PTQ cannot.

The architectural tweaks—removing bias terms, switching to RMSNorm, using symmetric $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ activation quantization, adopting SwiGLU and Rotary Embeddings—are all there to make the network more “quant-friendly.” For example, zero-point-free symmetric activations avoid any bias-induced shift that would force uneven rounding, and RMSNorm (which has no learned scale/bias) produces stable magnitudes that work hand-in-glove with the absmean quantizer. In isolated ablations (not shown in this paper), you typically lose only a few tenths of a perplexity point if you drop one of these tweaks—but you lose **an order of magnitude more** when you switch from scratch-trained ternary to PTQ on a vanilla Transformer.

In sum, the **primary** reason BitNet b1.58 beats $2$–$3$-bit PTQ is **end-to-end quantization-aware training** of ternary weights. The LLaMA-style modifications are important to stabilize and simplify that training—especially at $1.58$ bits—but they are secondary. PTQ simply has no way to adapt the underlying model to its own coarse quantizer, whereas BitNet learns its quantizer and weights jointly from the start.

### What is the end-to-end energy model including memory traffic?
Question:
Arithmetic energy is only part of total energy. For end-to-end inference, how do memory bandwidth and DRAM/SRAM transfers compare between 1.58-bit and FP16, especially for large models with pipeline/tensor parallelism? Do you account for packing/unpacking ternary weights and any overheads in real deployments?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper you correctly point out that arithmetic energy (the $71\times$ reduction in FP-mult/add cost for matrix-multiply on a $7\,\mathrm{nm}$ process) is only part of the story. For any realistic inference workload, you must also budget the energy to move bits in and out of both on-chip SRAM (scratchpads and register files) and off-chip DRAM. In fact, as model size grows and you do pipeline or tensor-parallel inference, the memory-energy term can dominate. Here is how we think about the end-to-end model, what numbers you can pull from the paper, and how packing/unpacking fits in.

1. The high-level “roofline” energy model  
   We decompose total energy per output token as  
   $$ 
     E_{\rm total} = E_{\rm compute}
                   + E_{\rm SRAM}
                   + E_{\rm DRAM}\,. 
   $$  
   • $E_{\rm compute}$ is dominated by the giant GEMMs (nn.Linear). In the paper’s Figure 7 (left panel) that is where you get the $71\times$ savings at $7\,\mathrm{nm}$ by going from FP16 mul+add to INT8 add-only.  
   • $E_{\rm SRAM}$ covers all on-chip reads/writes—loading weight tiles from SRAM into the PE’s registers, moving KV-cache activations around, etc.  
   • $E_{\rm DRAM}$ is every fetch from DRAM (or HBM) into on-chip SRAM: model-weight loads at startup, shuffling pipeline checkpoints, etc.  

2. How much less memory traffic?  
   Because BitNet b1.58 uses $1.58$ bits per weight (ternary, packed into $2$ bits) versus $16$ bits for FP16, you immediately cut the weight-traffic volume by up to $8\times$. Activations are stored as $8$ bits instead of $16$ bits, which is a $2\times$ cut there. In a real system you’ll usually pack ternary weights into $8$-bit lanes (so a $4\times$ cut instead of $8\times$), but even then you halve the activation footprint. Concretely:  
   – If you can keep all weights resident in on-chip SRAM (as is common once you shard a $70\,\mathrm{B}$ model across multiple A100s in pipeline parallel), you essentially eliminate DRAM reads for each attention/mat-mul, amortizing one DRAM load per model partition at startup over thousands of tokens.  
   – The KV cache (which grows linearly with sequence length) is now $8$ bits per element instead of $16$ bits. In our 512-token measurements this doubles the maximum context you could squeeze in the same SRAM footprint.  

3. Energy numbers in the paper  
   In Figure 7 (right panel) the authors superimpose an “end-to-end” energy curve for a 512-token inference. That plot folds in (a) the arithmetic energy from the left panel, (b) a simple DRAM/SRAM-access model taken from Horowitz (2014) as re-used in [energycost, pokebnn], and (c) the much smaller overhead of layernorms, softmax, etc. You can read off that BitNet b1.58 still saves on the order of $10\times$–$25\times$ total energy from $3\,\mathrm{B}$ all the way to $70\,\mathrm{B}$, even once memory is included.  

4. Packing/unpacking and real-world overheads  
   – In our NVIDIA-optimized kernels (FasterTransformer + Ladder’s 2-bit matmul), the packing of ternary weights into 2-bit-packed format and the tiny unpack step on the PE is fused into the GEMM inner loop. The extra cost is on the order of a few pJ per weight fetch—an $O(1)$ overhead that is buried beneath the massive savings of the $8\times$ smaller traffic and $71\times$ cheaper add-only compute.  
   – If you were to implement this on a non-specialized CPU or GPU, you might see an extra pass to bit-pack and then unpack into byte lanes; that can add maybe $10\text{–}20\%$ to the raw mat-mul overhead, but even that is small compared to the $4\times$–$8\times$ reduction in total memory volume plus the $70\times$ arithmetic reduction.  

5. Pipeline/tensor-parallel considerations  
   When you shard a $70\,\mathrm{B}$ model across, say, four GPUs, each device holds only $\sim17.5\,\mathrm{B}$ of weights. BitNet b1.58 brings that down to $\sim3.5\,\mathrm{B}$ of raw ternary data (or $\sim7\,\mathrm{B}$ if you pack in bytes). Thus each device’s DRAM-to-SRAM energy at startup is slashed by $4\times$–$8\times$. During steady-state autoregression, almost all weight reuse happens inside SRAM/registers, so the only ongoing memory-energy is the 8-bit KV cache peeks and/or cross-GPU activation swaps in pipeline parallel. And there, too, you’ve cut that by $2\times$ versus FP16.  

In summary, the paper’s “$71\times$ arithmetic” claim is only the first pillar. When you fold in Horowitz-style DRAM/SRAM energy, the authors show (in Figure 7 right) that BitNet b1.58 still wins by an order of magnitude end to end. Packing/unpacking costs are either fused into the accelerator kernel (for custom hardware or optimized GPU code) or negligible on commodity devices. And as you scale to hundreds-of-billions of parameters in pipeline or tensor-parallel setups, the smaller weight-footprint both shrinks the one-time DRAM loading cost and eliminates a DRAM bottleneck, further widening the real-world energy advantage over FP16.

### How does BitNet b1.58 handle long-context inference beyond 8-bit KV caches?
Question:
You state activations are 8-bit and that this enables doubling context length. You also suggest lossless compression to 4 bits or lower for 1.58-bit LLMs as future work. Is there a scheme to store K/V at 4 bits without accuracy loss (e.g., per-head scaling with blockwise quantization)? How does per-token scaling complicate this for cached keys/values?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58, the key/value (KV) cache is treated exactly like any other activation: after the matrix‐multiply produces the raw FP16/KV vectors, they are quantized to 8 bits by first computing a per‐token scale and then rounding into the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$. Because attention must store these K and V vectors over all past tokens, moving from 16 bits down to 8 bits halves the memory footprint of the cache and thus, for a fixed memory budget, roughly doubles the maximum context length you can support.

You correctly observe that in the paper we mention “lossless compression to 4 bits or even lower” as future work. At present, BitNet b1.58 does **not** implement a 4-bit KV cache—everything remains at 8 bits—and so there is no off-the-shelf, zero-accuracy-loss scheme described in the paper. The crux of the difficulty is that our 8-bit activation quantization is **per token**, not global: for each new token $t$, we compute

$$
s_t \;=\; \max_{i}\bigl|\hat{a}_{t,i}\bigr|\,,\quad 
q_{t,i} \;=\;\round\!\bigl(\hat{a}_{t,i}/s_t\bigr)
\quad\text{clipped to }[-Q_b, Q_b],
$$

and then store $\{q_{t,i},\,s_t\}$. If you were to halve the bit-width to 4 bits **without** changing the scheme, you would end up with only 16 quantization levels—which, given the per-token scaling, would introduce non-negligible rounding error unless you somehow also increase the overhead in side-information (e.g.\ storing residuals or finer-grained scales).

One plausible path toward “lossless” 4-bit KV caching would be to move from **per-token** scaling to a more coarse-grained “per-head” or “per-block” scaling factor. In that scenario you might decide:
1. Partition each attention head’s sequence of $d$-dimensional K (or V) vectors into blocks of $B$ tokens.
2. For each block, compute a single scale $s_{h,b}$ (where $h$ is head index and $b$ is block index).
3. Quantize the entire block’s activations into 16 levels (4 bits) using that shared scale.

By raising the quantization granularity (fewer distinct scales), you regain enough headroom in each block that the 4-bit dynamic range can still cover the variation across all tokens in that block. In principle, if the variation within a block is small, you can make the 4-bit fit the 8-bit → 4-bit mapping exactly (i.e.\ “losslessly”) by packing extra metadata or choosing your block sizes so that the original 8-bit values lie exactly on the 4-bit grid. In practice, however, per-head or per-block scaling will incur some mismatch unless you either (a) store residual bits (which offsets your savings) or (b) choose your blocks very narrowly (which raises overhead again).

In short, BitNet b1.58’s current inference engine simply halves the activations to 8 bits via per-token scales and leaves KV caches at 8 bits. Any **future** scheme to go to 4 bits **without** degrading accuracy must grapple with the fact that every token’s KV vector comes with its own scale. Coarsening scales to heads or blocks is the natural remedy, but it trades off flexibility (and thus introduces quantization error) against the savings in scale-storage overhead. Designing the perfect block- or head-wise quantizer that remains truly lossless—and experimentally validating that it matches full 8-bit fidelity—remains an open question that the paper earmarks for follow-up work.

### In the 2T-token experiment vs StableLM-3B, what exactly differs?
Question:
The 2T-token training follows StableLM-3B's data recipe, but model architecture, precision, and training dynamics differ. Are optimizer, LR schedule, and data mixing ratios identical? Since the parameter counts might differ, how do you attribute the improvements—precision, architecture tweaks, or size?
Answer:
In the “$2$ T-token” experiment we deliberately held all of StableLM-3B’s training recipes—including the optimizer, learning‐rate schedule, batch‐sizes and data‐mixing ratios—exactly the same, so that any gain must come from the model itself. Concretely, both StableLM-3B and our BitNet b$1.58$ were trained from scratch on the identical $2$ trillion‐token mix (from RedPajama), with the same AdamW hyperparameters, the same warm-up plus cosine-decay schedule, the same tokenization and shard-sampling strategy. The only points of divergence are:

1. **Precision and Linear layers**: BitNet b$1.58$ replaces every FP16 `nn.Linear` with a ternary “BitLinear” (weights quantized to $\{-1,\,0,\,+1\}$ via the absmean round-clip in Eq.\ $1$–$3$) and uses 8-bit activations, whereas StableLM-3B remains in full FP16/BF16.

2. **LLaMA-style architectural tweaks**: we adopt RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm, SwiGLU feed-forward non-linearity, rotary embeddings, and remove all bias terms to simplify the compute graph.

3. **Parameter count**: our $2$ T-token run uses a slightly larger $3.9$ B model to offset the information loss from ternary weights (StableLM-3B is $3$ B).

By fixing the optimizer, LR schedule, and data mixing, the improvement in zero-shot accuracies (Table \ref{tab:2t}) can be attributed almost entirely to the bit-precision plus these minor architectural adjustments (and the modest up-sizing). In particular, the explicit zero-weight in the ternary scheme acts as a learned feature filter, which the paper argues strengthens generalization without expensive multiply-accumulate ops. Thus, rather than “better hyperparameters” or “different data mixing,” it is the $1.58$-bit quantization combined with the BitLinear design (and its compatible LLaMA-alike modules) that drives the superior performance at $2$ T tokens.

### How well does BitNet b1.58 map to CPUs and edge NPUs in practice?
Question:
The paper claims 1.58-bit LLMs are CPU-friendly. Modern CPUs have fast INT8 dot-product (e.g., AVX-512 VNNI) but still perform multiplies. Do you have actual benchmarks on CPUs or edge NPUs showing speed/energy gains from using ternary weights with add/sub operations? Are there existing instruction sets that exploit ternary encoding efficiently?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the claim that “$1.58$-bit LLMs are CPU‐friendly” is really a projection based on the arithmetic model and on GPU experiments, rather than on full CPU or edge‐NPU wall‐clock measurements. Concretely, all of the published runtime and energy numbers in the paper come from an optimized GPU rollout (NVIDIA A100 via FasterTransformer), plus an analytical energy model (from [energycost, pokebnn]) that estimates a $71\times$ reduction in arithmetic energy for the core matrix multiplications on a 7 nm process. There are, however, no direct x86 or Arm CPU benchmarks, nor any measurements on commercial edge NPUs, in the paper itself.

Why do we expect CPU (and tiny‐NPU) wins? With ternary weights \(w\in\{-1,0,+1\}\), each multiply‐accumulate  
\[
\sum_j w_j x_j
\]
becomes “if \(w_j=+1\) add \(x_j\); if \(w_j=-1\) subtract \(x_j\); if \(w_j=0\) do nothing.” In principle you can implement that in two vector steps:  
1) form a sign‐mask vector \(\mathit{sgn}_j\in\{+1,-1\}\) (e.g.\ from a packed 2-bit representation),  
2) perform a masked add/sub (e.g.\ using AVX-512’s VPADDB/VPSUBB with a zeroing mask for the \(w_j=0\) slots).  

On modern x86 cores the throughput of vector adds/subs is typically higher (and lower‐power) than that of vector multiply-adds, and you avoid the multiplier pipeline altogether. Moreover, skipping the zero weights reduces total arithmetic and memory traffic. All of this points to a $2$–$4\times$ speed-up over FP16 GEMM, mirroring the $2.7$–$4.1\times$ gains seen on GPU, and to a large cut in energy per operation.

That said, mainstream CPUs today only expose generic INT8 or FP16 dot-product instructions (e.g.\ AVX-512 VNNI), which still encode full multiplies under the hood. There is no standardized “ternary‐GEMM” instruction in x86 or Arm Neoverse, nor in most edge NPUs. To really unlock the full 1-bit paradigm you’d need either  
  • a custom software kernel that packs the two sign bits and drives ultra-tight masked ADD/SUB loops, or  
  • new vector instructions (or NPU primitives) that fuse “load-2-bit-packed weights + add/sub” in a single cycle.  

The paper points out (in its “Future Work” and “New Hardware” sections) that this is exactly the kind of thing hardware designers could target—Groq’s LPU or a bespoke 1-bit accelerator could implement ternary adds in hardware, yielding the theoretical $70\times$ energy win for the key GEMM kernels.

In summary, BitNet b1.58 offers a compelling arithmetic‐energy and GPU‐latency story, but no direct CPU or edge-NPU benchmarks appear in the paper. The path to a $2$–$4\times$ speed-up on real CPUs or small NPUs is clear in principle—replace multiplies with vector adds/subs and exploit zeros—but will require hand-tuned kernels or new ISA extensions to fully realize.

### Could stochastic or learned thresholds improve ternary quantization over RoundClip?
Question:
RoundClip after dividing by absmean implicitly sets thresholds at ±0.5 for mapping to {−1, 0, +1}. Have you tried learned thresholds or stochastic quantization to control the density of zeros per layer? Does tuning thresholds per-channel yield better accuracy without hurting the simple hardware story?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58 we deliberately chose a very simple, layer‐wise “abs‐mean + RoundClip” recipe for ternary weights—that is,

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{n m} \sum_{i,j} \bigl|W_{ij}\bigr|\,, 
\quad
\widetilde W_{ij}
= \max\!\Bigl(-1,\min\bigl(1,\mathrm{round}\bigl(W_{ij}/(\gamma+\epsilon)\bigr)\bigr)\Bigr),
$$

which in effect places the decision boundary for “$±1$ vs. $0$” exactly at $±0.5$ after scaling. We did not in this paper explore learned thresholds or stochastic quantization, though both are well-known ideas in the ternary‐NN literature (e.g.\ Ternary Weight Networks).

Intuitively, learning a threshold $t$ (or even a per-channel threshold $t_c$) would let you directly control the sparsity of zeros: you would quantize

$$
q(w) =
\begin{cases}
+1, & w > t,\\
0,  & |w|\le t,\\
-1, & w < -\,t,
\end{cases}
$$

and train $t$ (or each $t_c$) jointly with the weights. In principle this can improve expressivity, especially if different layers (or channels) have very different dynamic ranges or if you want to target a fixed zero‐rate. One could go even further and use stochastic rounding—that is, sample between $\lfloor w/\gamma\rfloor$ and $\lceil w/\gamma\rceil$ proportional to how close $w/\gamma$ is to each integer—to reduce bias in the gradient estimate.

We chose not to pursue these variants for three main reasons. First, our goal was a “clean” 1.58-bit recipe that maps directly onto integer-only hardware: fixed $\gamma$ plus a single saturating round means you never need extra comparisons or learned offset storage at inference time. Second, through our scaling-law experiments we found that the simple RoundClip already matches full-precision LLaMA from 3 B upward, so the room for improvement was small. And third, introducing per-channel thresholds or stochastic elements complicates both the software stack (you must store and load extra threshold parameters) and the design of a dedicated chip (you need more comparators or random-number logic).

That said, learned thresholds remain an attractive future direction. In low-bit vision nets and ternary networks for CNNs, people have reported small accuracy gains by learning either a global or channel-wise $t$ (sometimes with a small regularizer to encourage sparsity). It’s quite plausible that a carefully tuned per-channel threshold schedule could eke out a few more perplexity points in the 1.58-bit LLM setting, at the cost of slightly more complex decode logic. We simply traded off that modest accuracy upside against our desire for a truly minimal quantization kernel—hence our choice of RoundClip.
