# From FP16 to Ternary: How BitNet b1.58 Matches Full-Precision LLMs

Large language models have driven huge gains in NLP, but their growing compute and memory costs are becoming a hard constraint. BitNet b1.58 demonstrates a different tradeoff: train and run a Transformer with ternary weights \{-1, 0, +1\} (about 1.58 bits per weight) and 8-bit activations, and you can match full-precision (FP16/BF16) LLaMA-like models in both perplexity and zero-shot task accuracy — while substantially cutting latency, memory, throughput and energy cost.

Below I walk through the key ideas and head-to-head results reported for BitNet b1.58 versus FP16 LLaMA-style baselines, and explain why the ternary variant is such an attractive engineering point.

## What is BitNet b1.58 (quick summary)

BitNet b1.58 is a BitNet variant in which every weight is constrained to be ternary: -1, 0, or +1. Important design details:

- Weights: ternary \{-1, 0, +1\}, which yields an effective 1.58 bits per weight in binary representation.
- Activations: 8-bit quantized.
- Architecture: a LLaMA-like Transformer (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings), with nn.Linear replaced by BitLinear; biases are removed to match common open-source backbones.
- Training: trained from scratch (no post-training quantization) on the RedPajama corpus for 100B tokens for the main experiments; also trained a model on 2T tokens for a token-scaling check.

The ternary representation gives a middle value 0 for explicit feature filtering (sparser expressive capacity than pure sign-only weights), while keeping the compute primitive dominated by integer additions rather than FP multiplies.

## How ternary weights are produced (absmean quantization)

BitNet b1.58 uses a simple per-matrix scaling and rounding procedure referred to as "absmean" quantization. For a weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\), compute the average absolute scale

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|,
\]

then scale and round with clipping:

\[
\widetilde{W} = \text{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\qquad
\text{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a, \min(b, \text{round}(x))).
\]

Activations are scaled to a symmetric range per token (to avoid zero-point quantization): scaled to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) rather than \([0,Q_b]\).

## Head-to-head empirical results

The paper compares BitNet b1.58 against a reproduced FP16 LLaMA-like baseline of the same model sizes. Models were pre-trained for 100B tokens (same data recipe). Evaluation includes validation perplexity (WikiText2, C4) and zero-shot accuracy on standard tasks (ARC, HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ), and system-level metrics (GPU memory, latency, throughput, and an estimate of arithmetic energy).

Key takeaways:

- Matching performance starts at 3B: BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA baselines in both perplexity and zero-shot end-task accuracy beginning at the 3B model size.
- Efficiency at 3B: BitNet b1.58 is 2.71× faster at inference and uses 3.55× less GPU memory than the FP16 3B LLaMA baseline (measured with an optimized inference stack including FasterTransformer and a 2-bit kernel).
- Pareto improvements with larger sizes: a 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is 2.4× faster and uses 3.32× less memory than FP16 3B, while outperforming FP16 3B on accuracy and perplexity — meaning you can increase bit-squeezing and model size and still improve quality at lower cost.
- Scaling benefits grow with size: speed-up and memory advantages increase as model size grows. For example, the 70B BitNet b1.58 model is reported to be 4.1× faster than the 70B FP16 LLaMA baseline.
- Throughput at large scale: on two 80 GB A100 GPUs with pipeline parallelism, BitNet b1.58 70B supported up to 11× the batch size of the FP16 baseline and achieved 8.9× higher throughput for sequence length 512.
- Energy for matrix multiply: based on an energy model for arithmetic operations on 7nm chips, BitNet b1.58 saves 71.4× in arithmetic energy for the matrix-multiplication component versus FP16 (INT8 additions vs FP16 adds and multiplies). As nn.Linear dominates cost more at larger sizes, overall energy efficiency improves with scale.

The authors summarize several practical equivalences enabled by this efficiency:

- 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than 3B FP16.
- 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16.
- 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16.

These equivalences illustrate a new cost-performance scaling law: with ternary weights and 8-bit activations you can afford larger models at lower inference cost than much smaller FP16 models.

## Token scaling and generalization

The authors also trained a BitNet b1.58 variant with 2T tokens (following a StableLM-3B-like recipe) and evaluated on a small benchmark (Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, ARC-easy). BitNet b1.58 at 2T tokens outperformed the StableLM 3B baseline at 2T, indicating the ternary models can scale in token count and maintain or improve generalization.

## Why this works in practice

There are a few pragmatic reasons BitNet b1.58 can match FP16 performance while delivering system-level wins:

- Ternary weights with a central zero let the model discard/attenuate irrelevant features explicitly, which is a stronger representation than sign-only binaries.
- Constraining weights during training (vs. post-training quantization) produces models that learn to operate under low-bit constraints directly, reducing the accuracy penalty.
- The compute primitive shifts from FP multiplies to mostly integer additions (and bitwise-friendly ops), which are both cheaper energy-wise and faster on many implementations/hardware.
- Reducing weight and activation precision shrinks memory bandwidth needs and on-chip footprint, so inference benefits from less DRAM-to-SRAM pressure and larger effective caches, improving latency and throughput.

## Practical implications and next steps

BitNet b1.58 suggests several immediate opportunities:

- More cost-effective inference: you can deploy larger models for the same latency/memory envelope, or reduce latency and cost for the same model quality.
- Edge and mobile: the lower memory and integer-friendly compute make it more feasible to run competitive LLMs on CPU-dominant or memory-constrained platforms.
- MoE and long-context scaling: lower memory use (and cheaper activation storage) directly helps MoE routing and KV cache scaling for longer context windows.
- New hardware: the computational pattern here motivates hardware that accelerates ternary/low-bit integer additions and compact on-chip storage.

## Limitations and caveats

The reported results are tied to the experimental setup in the paper: training recipes, datasets, and inference kernels (FasterTransformer + a 2-bit kernel). Some caveats to keep in mind:

- Embeddings were kept in full precision, so memory advantages grow as the relative share of linear layers increases with model size.
- The energy savings quoted focus on matrix-multiplication arithmetic; real system-level energy depends on many factors (memory transfers, control logic, I/O), though reducing bits helps these too.
- Gains depend on good kernel support; the paper uses optimized kernels but further engineering could change absolute numbers.

## Bottom line

BitNet b1.58 shows that you can train Transformers with ternary weights and 8-bit activations from scratch and reach FP16-like language modeling and zero-shot task performance starting at 3B parameters — while significantly reducing inference latency, GPU memory usage, throughput limits, and arithmetic energy. This opens a practical path: instead of only pushing quantization as a post-training trick, design models and training recipes around low-bit native representations to get both quality and system-level efficiency.
