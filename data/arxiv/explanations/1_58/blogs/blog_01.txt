\title{Blogs about the Paper: The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits}

# What Is a 1.58-bit LLM? The Ternary Twist Behind BitNet b1.58

Large language models have been drifting toward lower numeric precision to save memory, bandwidth, latency, and energy. BitNet b1.58 takes that idea to an extreme: every model weight is constrained to one of three values, {-1, 0, +1}. That ternary design is what the paper calls a "1.58-bit LLM." Here I'll unpack what that means, why ternary weights are useful, how the authors train a Transformer with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations without sacrificing accuracy, and what practical gains this enables.

## Why three values = 1.58 bits

Bits measure information. If a variable can take N equally likely states, its information content is log2(N) bits. Ternary weights have three possible values, so the per-weight information is

$ \log_2(3) \approx 1.585\ \text{bits}. $

That is the origin of "1.58-bit." It’s not a literal representation of a weight with fractional bits in memory; it’s shorthand for "ternary weights" with the information content of about 1.58 bits each.

Why ternary instead of binary? Binary weights (two states) correspond to 1 bit, but adding a third state 0 brings two practical advantages: (1) you retain most of the extreme-sparsity and fast-integer-compute benefits of 1-bit representations, and (2) the explicit zero enables feature filtering—weights can explicitly turn off contributions—improving expressivity compared to pure sign-only weights.

## How BitNet b1.58 constrains weights to {-1, 0, +1}

BitNet b1.58 trains from scratch and replaces standard linear layers with a BitLinear primitive that enforces ternary weights. The core quantization pipeline used in the paper is an "absmean" scaling followed by rounding and clipping. For a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ they compute a per-matrix scale

$ \gamma = \dfrac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|, $

then scale and quantize:

$ \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Big(\dfrac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\ -1,\ 1\Big), $

where

$ \mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))). $

This maps each scaled weight to -1, 0, or +1. In practice the model stores these ternary weights (and the small scaling factors) and uses integer arithmetic for the bulk of the computation.

Activations are quantized in an 8-bit scheme similar to BitNet's earlier work. A notable implementation choice is to scale activations per token into the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, avoiding zero-point shifts and simplifying inference-system integration.

## Training a Transformer with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations

BitNet b1.58 is a Transformer-based model that otherwise follows modern LLM design choices (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, bias removal) so it integrates easily with open-source toolchains. The authors train models from scratch with ternary weights and 8-bit activations on large corpora (RedPajama, 100B tokens for their comparisons) and observe that:

- Starting at about 3B parameters, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 (or BF16) LLaMA-style baselines on perplexity and a variety of zero-shot downstream tasks.
- A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model outperforms a 3B FP16 LLaMA baseline while using less memory and exhibiting lower latency.
- As model size grows, performance gaps shrink and the efficiency advantages grow — e.g., the 70B ternary model is substantially faster and lower-memory than the FP16 counterpart.

Two practical points make this possible. First, the absmean scaling promotes stable quantization across matrices by adapting to the typical magnitude of weights. Second, activations are kept at 8-bit, preserving representational richness where it helps most; weights are ternary but activations still carry fine-grained information.

## What changes in compute, memory, latency and energy

Ternary weights change the dominant compute pattern. Instead of floating-point multiply-accumulate (FP MAC) heavy matrix multiplications, BitNet b1.58 can rely primarily on integer additions and simple sign operations. The paper reports large efficiency gains:

- Measured inference latency is lower: for example, a 3B-scale comparison shows BitNet b1.58 being about 2.7× faster and using 3.55× less GPU memory than the FP16 baseline in the experiments.
- Energy for matrix multiply arithmetic can be dramatically lower. Using an energy model cited in the paper, BitNet b1.58 saves about 71.4× in arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication on 7 nm chips (because INT-adds cost far less than FP multiplies and additions).
- Throughput improves because ternary weights reduce memory bandwidth and on-device memory pressure. In one reported experiment the 70B ternary model ran with up to 11× larger batch sizes (and ~8.9× higher throughput) on two 80GB A100s compared to the FP16 baseline arranged with pipeline parallelism.

Because the memory footprint per weight is much smaller, KV cache memory and activation storage also shrink. That directly helps long-context inference and on-device deployment.

## Modeling implications: why adding zero matters

Ternary weights allow explicit deactivation (weight = 0). This is not just a storage trick — zero weights can act as learned feature filters, letting the network suppress unwanted contributions cleanly. According to the paper, this improves modeling power over sign-only binary models while preserving the computational advantages of low-bit integer arithmetic.

Empirically, the authors show that this modeling boost, combined with careful training recipes and 8-bit activations, is enough to match or exceed FP16 baselines at practical model scales.

## Scaling and practical equivalences

The authors argue a new kind of scaling law: because ternary models are so much cheaper at inference, larger ternary models can be a Pareto improvement over smaller FP16 models. As rough equivalences reported:

- 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than 3B FP16.
- 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16.
- 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16.

So you can get higher effective model capacity for the same or lower operational cost by switching to ternary weights and 8-bit activations.

## Where this helps next

The paper highlights several practical directions where 1.58-bit LLMs are especially promising:

- Mixture-of-Experts (MoE): lower memory and communication costs make large MoE deployments more feasible.
- Long-context models: smaller activation and KV cache sizes make native long-sequence inference cheaper.
- Edge and mobile: ternary weights plus 8-bit activations fit better into constrained devices and are friendlier to CPUs.
- Novel hardware: 1-bit / ternary compute suggests opportunities for hardware optimized around sign/adder primitives rather than FP ALUs.

## Bottom line

BitNet b1.58 shows that constraining Transformer weights to three states {-1, 0, +1} — roughly 1.585 bits of information per weight — is a practical, trainable design when paired with 8-bit activations and careful scaling/quantization. The ternary representation preserves modeling power (starting at about 3B parameters it matches FP16 baselines in the reported experiments) while delivering substantial wins in memory, latency, throughput, and arithmetic energy. For practitioners and system designers, ternary LLMs present a compelling trade-off: slightly different numeric primitives in exchange for much lower deployment cost and a new set of hardware-software optimization opportunities.
