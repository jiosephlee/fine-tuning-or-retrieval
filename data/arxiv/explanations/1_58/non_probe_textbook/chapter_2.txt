How Attention-Based Learners Spend Compute: A Cost and Hardware Primer

# Attention-based anatomy: attention, feed-forward blocks, and where arithmetic concentrates

Attention-based sequence learners are built from repeating computation blocks, each of which contains two distinct computational subunits. The first subunit computes pairwise interactions among positions of an input sequence: each position is translated into several linear projections that play the roles of query, key, and value. These projections are then combined so that each position gathers information from other positions according to similarity scores. The second subunit is a dense, position-wise transform that expands and contracts the hidden dimensionality and applies a non-linear gating step in between. Intuitively, the first subunit handles context mixing across positions, and the second subunit remixes features at every position independently.

When you count arithmetic work, the vast majority arises from the dense matrix-by-matrix and matrix-by-vector products that implement both subunits. Forming the query, key, and value matrices from the input requires multiplying an input matrix by three learned matrices; computing pairwise similarity is a big product between the query set and the transposed key set; and combining values back into output representations is another product. The feed-forward subunit is implemented with two dense transforms separated by a non-linear gating, and each of those transforms is another heavy matrix product. Because these products involve large dimensions and are applied across every position in a sequence, they swamp the cost of the comparatively small pointwise non-linearities and of bookkeeping work like position encodings and simple vector scaling.

A compact way to see where arithmetic goes is to write the shapes. Let $L$ be sequence length and $D$ the main hidden dimension. A projection from input to queries has cost proportional to $L \times D \times D_q$ in terms of pairwise products and summations, where $D_q$ is the per-head projection dimension. The pairwise similarity step creates an $L \times L$ attention matrix at the per-head level, which makes that subunit scale quadratically with $L$ when viewed purely as operations on positions. The feed-forward subunit typically uses an intermediate size that is larger than $D$, so its dense transforms cost roughly $L \times D \times D_{ff}$ each. Summed across subunits and across all repeated blocks, those dense products dominate arithmetic counts.

# Matrix product as the dominant compute hotspot in large language learners

Dense matrix products are the kernel that determines both time and energy in attention-based systems. If $A$ is size $L\times D$ and $B$ is $D\times M$, the work to produce their product is proportional to the product of those three symbolic dimensions, which one can write as a dependence on $L$, $D$, and $M$. When $L$ or $D$ or $M$ grows, the arithmetic grows multiplicatively. That multiplicative scaling explains why modest increases in hidden size or sequence length quickly produce very large increases in arithmetic work.

Because the same dense product pattern appears throughout the design—projections, the pairwise similarity formation, the gathering step, and the two transforms in the feed-forward block—any change that touches matrix product cost has outsized effects on end-to-end runtime and on energy use. This is the fundamental reason why efforts to accelerate attention-based learners often target faster implementations of dense products or seek algorithmic ways to reduce the sizes of those products.

The paper that motivates this chapter takes that observation to heart and demonstrates a practical route: by constraining the learned coefficient matrices to a very small discrete set of values, the per-element arithmetic becomes simpler and the amount of data that must be fetched from memory shrinks. Because dense products are the dominant hotspot, simplifying their per-element work cascades into large reductions in runtime and energy.

# Latency, sustained rate, memory requirement, and transfer capacity

When evaluating a deployment, four resource-oriented quantities matter and they interact.

Latency is the elapsed time to produce a single requested output from the moment the system receives its input. This depends on both the raw arithmetic throughput available on the processor and on the memory transfers and sequential dependencies that must be satisfied before the output can be returned.

Sustained rate is the steady-state number of outputs the system can produce per unit time when it is fed a continuous stream. Sustained rate benefits from parallelism: running many inputs in flight amortizes memory transfers and allows hardware pipelines to be filled.

Memory requirement is the amount of storage one must hold on the hardware to run the model: that includes the stored learned matrices, temporary buffers used during the dense products, and the per-request caches that keep intermediate results across time.

Transfer capacity is the rate at which data can be moved between slower off-chip storage and the processor’s fast on-chip storage. In many systems, transfer capacity is a bottleneck because the dense product kernels are hungry for coefficient data and for per-position intermediate values.

These four concepts interact through trade-offs. Increasing the amount of on-chip storage that holds coefficient tables reduces repeated off-chip transfers, which lowers latency and raises sustained rate. Increasing concurrency (feeding more inputs at once) can boost sustained rate but may increase end-to-end latency for a single request, because the shared resources are time-sliced. Critically, because dense products are so dominant, any technique that shrinks the data that must be moved or reduces per-element arithmetic tends to simultaneously lower latency, increase sustained rate, and reduce memory requirement.

# Off-chip memory versus on-chip fast storage: capacities, transfer limits, and movement costs

Modern processors pair a large, relatively slow, off-chip memory with a much smaller, much faster on-chip store. The off-chip bank can hold large learned matrices and long-lived data, but fetching a block from off-chip into the processor incurs a substantial time and energy overhead compared to reading the same bytes from on-chip storage.

On-chip store offers high parallel access, low latency, and energy-efficient reads and writes, but its capacity is limited. That means, in practice, learned coefficient tables are streamed from off-chip memory into on-chip buffers just in time for the dense products. The cost of moving those bytes can dominate when the arithmetic per byte is modest; in other words, when arithmetic intensity is low, data motion is what kills performance.

Reducing the size of the stored tables changes the arithmetic/communication balance. If each stored number requires fewer bytes, the same on-chip store can hold more of the learned tables, which reduces the need to stream from off-chip and allows higher reuse of data already resident on chip. The paper illustrates this effect by constraining stored coefficients to a tiny discrete set; that reduces the bytes that must be transported and stored, so the apparent pressure on off-chip transfer falls and on-chip buffers can be used more effectively.

Because movement from off-chip storage is expensive in both time and energy, the practical effect of a reduced stored table size is two-fold: first, fewer off-chip transfers lower latency and increase sustained rate; second, less energy is spent moving data, permitting a more power-efficient implementation overall.

# Power as a fundamental limit; why energy savings translate to speed

Modern silicon is power-limited: chips have a maximum thermal and electrical envelope they can operate within. That envelope constrains the product of frequency, parallelism, and per-operation energy. When per-element arithmetic and data movement are expensive, the chip cannot run more units in parallel or at higher frequency without exceeding its power limit.

Lowering the energy use per arithmetic operation and per byte moved buys back headroom in the power envelope. That headroom can be converted into more parallel functional units active at once, higher operating frequency, or simply longer sustained operation at peak rates. Concretely, if a change to the numeric format or to the arithmetic kernel reduces per-element energy substantially, the implementation can either run faster or handle more parallel inputs for the same power limit.

The paper uses this principle: by replacing complex floating arithmetic with much simpler integer-like operations and by slashing the amount of data transferred for learned coefficient tables, the per-matrix-product energy drops sharply. Because the chip is not allowed to exceed its power budget, that energy drop allows a larger fraction of the chip’s silicon to be devoted to useful work at a given clock, which manifests as higher effective speed and higher sustained rate during deployment.

# Precision’s impact on arithmetic intensity and memory traffic

Precision of stored numerical values governs two crucial quantities: the number of bytes that must be transferred for each learned coefficient, and the complexity of the per-element arithmetic. Arithmetic intensity is the ratio of arithmetic work to bytes moved; higher arithmetic intensity is attractive because it means more computation is performed per byte of transfer, making the implementation more compute-bound and less limited by transfer capacity.

If the bytes per stored coefficient shrink, the denominator of arithmetic intensity shrinks, which raises the arithmetic intensity. Higher arithmetic intensity makes it easier to keep on-chip compute units busy and reduces the fraction of time spent waiting on data transfers. Moreover, if the per-element arithmetic is simplified (for example, replacing multiply-and-accumulate sequences with operations that require only selection, sign flips, or low-precision accumulation), the energy per arithmetic operation drops, and the overall energy-per-output declines.

The paper demonstrates this by enforcing a ternary constraint on learned coefficients so that each stored entry takes one of three possible values. That constraint both reduces the storage footprint of the learned tables and simplifies the per-element processing when computing matrix products: instead of general high-precision multiplications and wide accumulators, the inner workings can use streamlined integer arithmetic and accumulation. The net effect on arithmetic intensity and memory traffic is large because dense products dominate the total work.

An analytic way to present the effect uses symbolic quantities. Let $F$ denote the number of arithmetic operations required for a dense product over a given input, and let $B$ denote the number of bytes that must be moved from slow storage for those operations. Arithmetic intensity can be written as $I = F / B$. If a format change reduces per-coefficient bytes by a factor $s$ (with $s$ expressed as a fraction), then the new intensity is $I' = F / (s B) = I / s$, which increases as $s$ decreases. Higher $I'$ improves the ability to hide data transfer behind computation and thus improves effective hardware utilization, latency, and sustained rate.

# Takeaways and practical implications

The dominant lesson is simple and robust: because dense matrix products account for the bulk of arithmetic and data movement in attention-based sequence learners, efforts that reduce the size of stored coefficient tables or that simplify the per-element arithmetic in those products have outsized benefits. Reducing storage per coefficient shrinks off-chip transfer needs and raises arithmetic intensity; simplifying arithmetic reduces energy per operation and frees power headroom that can be converted into extra parallelism or higher effective speed.

Architectural choices that narrow the set of per-coefficient values, and corresponding system support that streams those compact representations efficiently into on-chip buffers, transform the cost profile of the entire system. The paper that motivates this primer shows a practical instance of this idea: by restricting stored coefficients to a tiny discrete set and using low-precision representations for intermediate responses, the authors move the dominant kernel from costly, high-precision products to much lighter integer-like operations and reduced traffic. Because matrix products are the hot spots, that single change cascades into large improvements in response latency, sustained rate, memory requirements, and energy per output on realistic hardware.

For engineers and researchers, the practical implication is to focus optimization effort where it yields the largest leverage: the dense product kernels and the memory subsystem that feeds them. Changes that reduce per-coefficient storage, increase reuse of on-chip buffers, or simplify inner-loop arithmetic will pay dividends across latency, sustained rate, and energy metrics.
