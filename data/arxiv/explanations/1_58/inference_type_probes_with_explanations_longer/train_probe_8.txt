In the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', the mathematical expression that explains why three possible weight values correspond to that many bits per parameter is $\log_2(3)$. This follows directly from information theory: if each weight can take one of three discrete values (here, $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ as specified by the absmean quantization), then the number of distinct states per parameter is $M=3$, and the minimum number of binary digits needed to uniquely encode those states is the base-2 logarithm of $M$, i.e., $\log_2 M = \log_2(3) \approx 1.585$. The paper explicitly constructs a ternary weight space by rounding scaled real-valued weights to the nearest element of $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, thereby defining a ternary “alphabet” for parameters; labeling the model “b1.58” reflects that the optimal binary representation of a ternary symbol takes about $1.58$ bits per parameter, not the full $2$ bits one might use naively.

This interpretation is also sensible in the context of the paper’s system-level claims about memory and bandwidth. While hardware often stores integers in whole bytes or multiples thereof, the theoretical per-parameter information content for a ternary weight is $\log_2(3)$ bits; with suitable packing (e.g., grouping many weights and coding them jointly), an implementation can approach this limit on average. The inclusion of $0$ as a third value (compared to the original 1-bit $\{-1, +1\}$ design) both increases modeling capacity via explicit feature filtering and moves the symbol alphabet from $M=2$ to $M=3$, hence the shift from $1$ bit to $1.58$ bits per parameter. This aligns with the paper’s motivation: by constraining weights to three levels, BitNet b1.58 retains the computation advantages of integer additions while achieving stronger expressivity, and its storage footprint scales with the entropy of the ternary alphabet, quantified as $\log_2(3)$.
