According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', if, contrary to the paper's observation, non-linear components grew to dominate computation at large scale, BitNet b1.58's relative energy advantage would decrease. The core energy savings reported for BitNet b1.58 come from replacing FP16 matrix multiplications in the Transformer’s linear layers with integer additions, yielding a large reduction in arithmetic energy (about 71.4× on 7nm for the matmul component). The paper explicitly notes that the proportion of time and energy spent in the linear layers grows with model size, which is why the relative speed and energy advantages increase as models scale. If instead the compute shifted toward components that do not benefit as much from 1.58-bit weights—such as softmax, normalization, activation functions (e.g., SwiGLU), KV-cache operations, and embedding lookups—then the share of work that enjoys the “71.4×” kind of reduction would shrink, mechanically reducing the overall advantage.

We can make this quantitative. Let the FP16 baseline energy be decomposed as
\(E_{\mathrm{FP16}} = E_{\mathrm{lin}} + E_{\mathrm{non}}\),
and the BitNet b1.58 energy as
\(E_{\mathrm{b1.58}} \approx \frac{E_{\mathrm{lin}}}{\kappa} + \beta\,E_{\mathrm{non}}\),
where \(\kappa \approx 71.4\) captures the arithmetic savings in matrix multiplication and \(\beta \leq 1\) reflects smaller or negligible savings on non-linear parts (often much closer to 1 than to \(\kappa^{-1}\)). Define the linear fraction \(\alpha = E_{\mathrm{lin}} / E_{\mathrm{FP16}}\). The relative energy advantage is then
\[
R \;=\; \frac{E_{\mathrm{FP16}}}{E_{\mathrm{b1.58}}}
\;\approx\;
\frac{\alpha + (1-\alpha)}{\alpha/\kappa + \beta(1-\alpha)}
\;=\;
\frac{1}{\alpha/\kappa + \beta(1-\alpha)}.
\]
As observed in the paper, when \(\alpha\) grows with scale, \(R\) increases because more of the workload is in the highly optimized linear layers. Conversely, if non-linear components dominate so that \(\alpha \to 0\), then \(R \to 1/\beta\), which is much smaller than the gains seen when \(\alpha\) is large; in the limiting case where non-linear parts see no improvement (\(\beta=1\)), the advantage collapses to \(R \to 1\). Thus, BitNet b1.58’s relative energy advantage necessarily decreases if non-linear components become the dominant cost.
