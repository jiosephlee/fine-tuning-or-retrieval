Because the normalization uses a global per-matrix scale \(\gamma\), the rounding boundary \(|W_{ij}| < \tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\epsilon)\) adapts to the weight distribution in each layer, preferentially zeroing out low-energy coefficients. This makes the ternary mapping both noise-robust and efficient: values near \(0\) that would otherwise introduce negligible or noisy signal are pruned to \(0\), while only weights with \(|W_{ij}| \gtrsim \tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\epsilon)\) survive as \(\pm 1\), yielding the intended sparse, sign-preserving representation.

In the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', the added $\epsilon$ in $\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}$ guards against division by zero. The quantization scheme sets $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$, i.e., the average absolute value of the weight tensor. If the weight block under consideration becomes all zeros (which can happen at initialization, after updates that drive a block toward zero, or under sparsity-inducing dynamics encouraged by ternary quantization), then $\gamma = 0$. Without the $\epsilon$, the rescaling step $\frac{W}{\gamma}$ would be undefined; with $\gamma + \epsilon > 0$, the scaling is always well-defined, ensuring the quantizer can apply $\mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\cdot,-1,1\right)$ safely across all training iterations and parameter states.

In addition, even when $\gamma$ is very small but not exactly zero, the $\epsilon$ term prevents excessively large scaling factors that would blow up $\frac{W}{\gamma}$ and cause most entries to saturate to $\pm 1$ under $\mathrm{round}(\cdot)$, degrading gradient signals and destabilizing training. By keeping the denominator bounded away from zero, $\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}$ yields numerically stable forward passes and well-behaved gradients through the straight-through estimator, preserving the intended ternary behavior $W \mapsto \{-1,0,1\}$ while avoiding pathological cases tied to vanishing $\gamma$.

In the paper “The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits”, interpreting ternary weights in $\{-1,0,1\}$ as actions on inputs, BitNet b$1.58$ matrix multiplications effectively reduce to add, subtract, or skip. Concretely, for a linear layer with output $y = W x$ and $W_{ij} \in \{-1,0,1\}$, each output component is
$y_i = \sum_{j} W_{ij}\, x_j$, so the contribution of each input $x_j$ to $y_i$ is either $+x_j$ (add if $W_{ij}=1$), $-x_j$ (subtract if $W_{ij}=-1$), or $0$ (skip if $W_{ij}=0$). Because activations are quantized to integer grids (8-bit) and weights are ternary integers, the inner product can be implemented with integer accumulators and sign-controlled addition rather than general multiplication. The paper’s quantization function,
$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$ with $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$, ensures the stored weights are exactly $\{-1,0,1\}$, so at inference the arithmetic for the dominant matrix multiplications is driven by integer adds with optional sign flips and zero contributions, aligning with the claim that multiplication is replaced by add/subtract/skip.

Moreover, BitNet b1.58 eliminates zero-point quantization for activations by scaling them per token into $[-Q_b,Q_b]$, which simplifies the datapath: there is no need to add offsets before accumulation, further reinforcing an addition-centric kernel. The empirical and energy analyses in the paper corroborate this computational model: the “majority of BitNet b1.58 is INT8 addition calculation,” while FP16 baselines incur both additions and multiplications, yielding a $71.4\times$ reduction in arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips. In summary, with $\widetilde{W}_{ij}\in\{-1,0,1\}$ and integer-quantized $x_j$, the core linear algebra in BitNet b1.58 operationally becomes sign-directed accumulation, i.e., add, subtract, or skip, rather than general multiply-accumulate.

According to the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', if the average absolute weight $\gamma$ becomes larger, the proportion of weights quantized to zero after $\mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$ increases. This follows directly from the absmean quantization rule they adopt: each weight is first normalized by $\gamma+\epsilon$, then rounded to the nearest integer in $\{-1,0,1\}$. In a nearest-integer scheme, rounding to zero happens whenever the normalized magnitude falls below the half-integer threshold, i.e., when $\left|\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma+\epsilon}\right|<\tfrac{1}{2}$, which is equivalent to the absolute-value condition $\lvert W_{ij}\rvert < \tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\epsilon)$. As $\gamma$ increases, the right-hand side threshold $\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\epsilon)$ increases, so a larger set of entries satisfy the inequality and are rounded to zero. Geometrically, the “zero bin” in the original weight space widens with larger $\gamma$, enlarging the portion of weights mapped to $0$ by the ternary quantizer.

Moreover, because $\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}\lvert W_{ij}\rvert$ is a global scale computed per matrix, it is sensitive to heavy tails. If a subset of weights grows large, $\gamma$ rises, which expands the zero-quantization region for the many moderate and small weights, pushing more of them into the interval $\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\epsilon),\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma+\epsilon)\right)$ and thus to $0$. This behavior is consistent with the paper’s emphasis on explicit feature filtering enabled by the inclusion of $0$: the absmean scaling creates a data-adaptive sparsification mechanism where a higher global scale concentrates representational capacity on the few large-magnitude directions (quantized to $\pm 1$) while more aggressively suppressing weaker features (quantized to $0$). Hence, a larger $\gamma$ naturally increases the proportion of zeros under the stated quantization, improving the filtering effect the authors highlight.
