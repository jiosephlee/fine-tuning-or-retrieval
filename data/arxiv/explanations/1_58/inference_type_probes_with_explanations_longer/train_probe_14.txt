In the paper 'The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits', adding $\epsilon$ to the denominator in the quantization formula $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$ serves a practical purpose: it prevents division by zero. The scaling factor $\gamma$ is defined as the average absolute value of the weights, $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$. If an entire weight tensor (or block) becomes zero—for example, at initialization, after pruning, or transiently during training—then $\gamma = 0$. Without $\epsilon$, the normalization $\frac{W}{\gamma}$ would be undefined, breaking the quantization step and potentially propagating NaNs through the forward pass. By adding a small constant $\epsilon > 0$, the denominator is lower-bounded, guaranteeing that $\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}$ remains finite even when $\gamma = 0$, and thus ensuring the ternary mapping via $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ is always well-defined.

Beyond the pathological $\gamma=0$ case, $\epsilon$ also stabilizes computation when $\gamma$ is very small but nonzero. Since the normalization scales by $\frac{1}{\gamma}$, a tiny $\gamma$ would amplify $W$ excessively, causing most entries to saturate to $\pm 1$ in $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ and making the quantizer highly sensitive to minute perturbations. With $\epsilon$, the effective scale satisfies $\left\lvert \frac{1}{\gamma + \epsilon} \right\rvert \le \frac{1}{\epsilon}$, which bounds the amplification and limits abrupt saturation. This improves numerical stability during training with straight-through estimators, as gradients passing through the scale factor are less likely to explode when $\gamma \approx 0$, and it reduces the risk of underflow/denormal issues in low-precision arithmetic. In short, adding $\epsilon$ is a standard numerical safeguard that makes the absmean-based ternary quantization robust across all regimes of $W$, including sparse or zero blocks, while preserving the intended scaling behavior when $\gamma$ is well away from zero.
