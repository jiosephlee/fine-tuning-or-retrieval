\title{A Textbook about the Paper: 1_58}

Why 1-bit LLMs: Motivation, Challenges, and the Promise of BitNet b1.58

# The compute and energy footprint of modern LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) today achieve their capabilities by scaling up model size and training data. That improvement, however, comes at a steep cost in computation, memory, and energy. The dominant work in Transformer-based LLMs is dense matrix multiplication (the many occurrences of \texttt{nn.Linear} in attention and feed-forward layers). In full-precision training and inference (FP16 or BF16), each matrix multiply requires floating-point multiplications and additions. Floating-point operations, especially multiplications, are expensive in energy and latency relative to integer arithmetic. Consequently, as model size increases, the fraction of total runtime and energy consumed by matrix multiplies grows rapidly, and so does the total power draw of the system.

Another important source of cost is memory: model parameters must be stored in DRAM and streamed into on-chip SRAM or accelerator memory during inference. Moving large FP16 parameter arrays repeatedly from DRAM is costly both in time (bandwidth limits) and energy. Increasing on-chip SRAM to avoid frequent DRAM traffic is possible, but expensive in silicon area and dollars. Thus, the LLM scaling trend runs into two practical bottlenecks: arithmetic energy of floating-point operations and the memory-bandwidth and capacity cost of moving and storing large FP16 parameter sets. These constraints become dominant as we scale to billions or trillions of parameters.

# Limits of conventional scaling: memory bandwidth and power

When we say "limits of scaling" we are pointing to where cost, not algorithmic performance, becomes the principal obstacle. Two concrete effects illustrate this: first, the arithmetic cost per inference token grows with model size because the number of multiply–accumulate operations in fully connected layers and attention increases with parameter count. Second, memory traffic—the repeated transfer of weights and activation caches (e.g., KV caches for attention)—grows and can dominate latency because DRAM bandwidth and on-chip memory sizes are finite. Together, these effects limit how large models can be used practically for inference, especially in latency-sensitive settings.

Another consequence is energy and environmental impact. Because power is a fundamental limiting factor in chip throughput (thermal and energy budgets constrain how fast hardware can run), improvements in raw arithmetic throughput often yield only limited practical speedups unless they are accompanied by more efficient arithmetic primitives and reduced memory transfer. The paper highlights that reducing the precision and type of arithmetic can shift the dominant bottleneck: if matrix multiplies can be implemented with far cheaper integer additions (or bitwise operations) instead of FP16 multiplies and adds, then both energy and latency can be substantially reduced.

# Post-training quantization vs. architectural rethinking

A widely used approach to lowering inference cost is post-training quantization (PTQ): take an already-trained FP16 model and quantize weights and/or activations to fewer bits for inference. PTQ is attractive because it is convenient and compatible with existing models, and industry adoption has been strong for 4-bit and other low-bit variants. However, PTQ is, by construction, a retrofit: it treats the original FP16 architecture as fixed and tries to squeeze it down in precision after the fact. The paper argues—and demonstrates empirically—that this is sub-optimal. Some components of the model do not quantize gracefully, and simply lowering precision without adapting the architecture or the training recipe can lead to accuracy loss.

Architectural rethinking takes a different stance: design the model from scratch to operate natively with low-bit weights and efficient arithmetic. BitNet and its descendant BitNet b1.58 are examples of that strategy. Rather than post-processing a full-precision model, BitNet b1.58 replaces the conventional dense linear layers with a low-bit primitive (BitLinear) and trains the model from scratch with ternary weights and low-precision activations. This lets the model and training dynamics naturally adapt to the constraints imposed by ultra-low-bit arithmetic, avoiding many of the pitfalls PTQ faces and enabling a new computation paradigm where matrix multiplication can be implemented almost without multiplications.

# Contributions of BitNet b1.58: ternary weights, 8-bit activations, FP16-level quality

BitNet b1.58 is an intentionally minimal and practical design for an LLM that uses ternary weights and small activations while matching full-precision baselines in quality. Each weight is constrained to the set \{-1, 0, +1\}. This ternary choice adds a neutral "zero" state compared to strict bipolar \{-1,+1\}, which provides explicit support for feature filtering (the ability to zero out connections), strengthening the modeling capacity relative to a purely bipolar 1-bit representation. Because there are three possible states per parameter, the information content per parameter is about $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits, giving the model the paper's name "b1.58".

Concretely, BitNet b1.58 replaces the usual \texttt{nn.Linear} modules in a Transformer with \texttt{BitLinear}, trains the whole model from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations, and preserves a LLaMA-like backbone in other respects. The architecture uses LLaMA-alike components—RMSNorm, SwiGLU activation in the feed-forward, rotary embeddings, and no biases—so that BitNet b1.58 integrates easily into popular open-source ecosystems. Embeddings are kept in full precision (FP16/BF16), which is an important practical detail: they occupy a shrinking fraction of memory as models grow, making the low-bit gains for the bulk of parameters more impactful at larger scales.

The paper specifies the quantization function used to map a full-precision weight matrix $W$ to its ternary counterpart $\widetilde{W}$. First, the matrix is scaled by its mean absolute value $\gamma$ and then rounded and clipped to the ternary alphabet:

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\bigg(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\bigg),
\]

\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))),
\]

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]

This "absmean" scaling anchors the dynamic range so that round-to-nearest maps weights sensibly into \{-1,0,+1\} per-layer. Activation quantization follows BitNet's approach but with a practical simplification: activations are scaled per-token to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ rather than introducing zero-point adjustments, which simplifies implementation and system-level optimization while incurring negligible performance loss in experiments.

Importantly, arithmetic for the dense matrix multiplies in BitNet b1.58 is dominated by integer additions (or very cheap integer operations) rather than FP16 multiplies and accumulates. This is because with ternary weights the multiply reduces to selecting and adding or subtracting activation terms (or skipping when weight is zero), a much cheaper primitive in energy and latency.

# Overview of key results: speed, memory, energy, and throughput gains

BitNet b1.58 is evaluated against a reproduced FP16 LLaMA LLM baseline. For a fair comparison the models were trained on the same corpus (RedPajama) for 100 billion tokens, and evaluations include validation perplexity on WikiText2 and C4 and zero-shot performance on standard language and reasoning benchmarks (ARC, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ, etc.). Runtime costs were measured using the FasterTransformer inference framework with integration of a 2-bit kernel from Ladder for BitNet b1.58.

The empirical highlights are:

- Perplexity and zero-shot quality: BitNet b1.58 begins to match the FP16 LLaMA baseline starting at 3B model size when trained with the same configuration (model scale, tokens). As model size increases the performance gap narrows further; in some comparisons a slightly larger BitNet b1.58 (e.g., 3.9B) can outperform a 3B FP16 baseline while still having lower latency and memory use.

- Latency and memory: For the 3B comparison, BitNet b1.58 is reported to be about $2.71\times$ faster and use $3.55\times$ less GPU memory than the FP16 LLaMA of the same size. A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 yields a $2.4\times$ speedup and $3.32\times$ memory reduction compared to LLaMA 3B, while outperforming it in accuracy. As model size increases these gains grow: BitNet b1.58 70B is reported to be $4.1\times$ faster than the 70B FP16 baseline.

- Energy: Focusing on the dominating cost of matrix multiplication and using an energy model appropriate for modern process nodes, BitNet b1.58 saves a large factor of arithmetic energy. The paper reports that for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips, BitNet b1.58 reduces arithmetic operation energy by about $71.4\times$ compared to the FP16 baseline. When measuring end-to-end energy on 512-token inputs, the relative efficiency of BitNet b1.58 improves with model size because the fraction of runtime spent on \texttt{nn.Linear} increases with scale.

- Throughput and batching: On two 80GB A100 GPUs, using pipeline parallelism so the 70B FP16 model can be run, BitNet b1.58 70B supports up to $11\times$ the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA baseline and delivers $8.9\times$ higher throughput under the maximum fitting batch size. This is primarily a consequence of the much smaller memory footprint and lower per-token compute cost.

- Training with more tokens: The paper also reports larger training runs: using a 2-trillion-token recipe (following StableLM-3B) for a 3B model, BitNet b1.58 achieves superior zero-shot accuracy across evaluated tasks compared to the StableLM 3B baseline at 2T tokens, indicating that the low-bit representation generalizes well when given more data and demonstrating strong scaling behavior with training tokens.

# A new scaling law linking performance to cost for 1.58-bit LLMs

One of the most consequential claims is that 1.58-bit LLMs change the relationship between model size, inference cost, and performance. Because BitNet b1.58 substantially reduces per-parameter memory and arithmetic cost while preserving model quality, one can trade off model size and precision to achieve better Pareto points (models that are strictly better in both cost metrics and performance).

The paper presents equivalence examples derived from measured latency, memory, and energy curves: a 13B BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient (in latency, memory, and energy) than a 3B FP16 LLM; similarly, a 30B BitNet b1.58 beats a 7B FP16 LLM, and a 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM. These equivalences mean that by switching to ternary weights and 8-bit activations, one can operate models that are substantially larger (with correspondingly better modeling capacity) while consuming less resource than much smaller FP16 models.

Intuitively, this is a new scaling law because the cost curve across model sizes shifts downward for the low-bit regime. As model size grows, the dominant component of cost—dense matrix multiplication—becomes relatively cheaper in BitNet b1.58 than in FP16. The embedding memory, which remains FP16 in the current design, becomes a smaller fraction of the total footprint for larger models, so the benefit of ternary-weight compression compounds with scale. The result is that increases in parameter count become affordable in both memory and energy terms, enabling larger models for the same or lower deployment cost.

This new cost–performance frontier has practical implications. For deployments constrained by latency, memory, or energy, teams can choose BitNet b1.58 models that are larger (and typically better) than their FP16 counterparts while still meeting hardware limits. Conversely, for a fixed target quality, the low-bit design can substantially reduce the needed hardware resources.

# Synthesis: why the ternary design matters and what to watch next

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that carefully designed ultra-low-bit architectures can preserve FP16-level modeling power while delivering large wins in latency, memory, energy, and throughput. The ternary alphabet \{-1,0,+1\} provides two concrete benefits: it enables near-multiplication-free matrix multiplies (transforming multiplies into sign operations and adds or skips), and it introduces an explicit zero state that supports feature filtering and sparse-like behavior inside a compact representation. The absmean scaling and RoundClip quantizer are simple but effective mechanics to make ternary constraints compatible with gradient-based training from scratch.

Practically, BitNet b1.58's gains are amplified at larger scales because the proportion of parameters represented in ternary form grows and embeddings (kept in FP16) become fractionally less significant. The reported empirical numbers (speedups up to $4.1\times$, memory reductions above $3\times$, matrix-multiplication arithmetic energy reductions around $71.4\times$ on 7nm models, and throughput increases up to $8.9\times$ at 70B) show that this is not just a theoretical idea but one with system-level consequences.

The paper also points toward areas where 1.58-bit models can unlock capabilities currently constrained by hardware: Mixture-of-Experts models could be deployed with less inter-chip communication overhead, KV caches for long-context inference could be doubled in length (because caches and activations move from 16-bit to 8-bit) and potentially further compressed, and on-edge or mobile LLM inference could become feasible due to lower memory and energy demands. Finally, the work motivates custom hardware optimized for the 1-bit/ternary computation paradigm; because the dominant arithmetic changes from FP multiplies to integer additions and selection/skip operations, new accelerator designs could exploit those primitives to reach even larger gains.

In summary, BitNet b1.58 illustrates how architectural rethinking—training Transformers from scratch with ternary weights and low-precision activations—can alter the practical scaling laws of LLMs. It provides a concrete pathway to match FP16 quality while substantially reducing the cost of running large language models, enabling new trade-offs and deployments previously limited by memory, bandwidth, and energy.

Background: Transformers, Inference Cost, and Quantization Basics

# Transformer internals: attention, MLPs, and the dominance of linear layers

Modern large language models (LLMs) are built on the Transformer architecture. At a conceptual level, a Transformer block is composed of two kinds of computation: a self-attention sub-layer and a feed-forward (MLP) sub-layer. Each of these sub-layers is implemented largely with linear algebra: the attention mechanism relies on linear projections to form queries, keys, and values and then computes weighted sums of value vectors; the MLP is implemented as one or more linear layers sandwiched around elementwise nonlinearities (for example, SwiGLU in the LLaMA family). Concretely, those projections and the internal MLP matrix multiplications are instances of dense matrix multiplication (the typical nn.Linear in popular deep-learning frameworks).

Because both attention and the MLP are realized as dense linear transforms, the bulk of Transformer computation is matrix multiplication. In large models this is the dominant cost in both training and inference. The research behind BitNet and BitNet b1.58 highlights this point directly: the “bulk of any LLMs is matrix multiplication,” and replacing the standard floating-point linear operators (e.g., FP16 nn.Linear) is the most direct route to reducing model cost. Practically, this is why BitNet replaces nn.Linear with a specialized BitLinear operator: by changing how the dense linear transforms are represented and computed, one achieves large gains in compute, memory, and energy.

# Inference bottlenecks: FLOPs, memory access, latency, and bandwidth

When we assess the runtime cost of a Transformer model, multiple factors contribute: raw arithmetic operations (FLOPs), the cost of moving data (memory access), and system-level effects such as latency and bandwidth constraints. The BitNet b1.58 paper emphasizes two aspects of this stack.

First, matrix multiplications dominate arithmetic operations. In conventional FP16 LLMs, each matrix multiply involves FP16 multiplications and additions. These floating-point operations are relatively expensive in energy and time compared to integer additions. Because the proportion of time spent in nn.Linear grows as models get larger, any improvement to the implementation of matrix multiplication scales strongly with model size.

Second, memory movement is a major bottleneck in inference. Model parameters are typically stored off-chip in DRAM and must be transferred into on-chip accelerator memory (for example, SRAM) for computation. The paper points out that transferring parameters from DRAM into on-chip memory during inference is expensive; enlarging on-chip SRAM to avoid this transfer increases hardware cost substantially compared to DRAM. Lower-bit models reduce both memory capacity needs and bandwidth; by shrinking the representation of weights and activations, 1.58-bit models reduce the cost and time of loading weights from DRAM and thus reduce end-to-end latency.

Latency and throughput are therefore influenced by a combination of raw compute (what arithmetic is performed) and the cost of moving model parameters and activations through the memory hierarchy. Because matrix multiply time and memory footprint both increase with model size, optimizations that reduce per-parameter storage (for example, moving from 16-bit floating point to ternary representations) can produce disproportionately large improvements in latency, memory usage, and throughput as models scale.

# Quantization primer: weights vs. activations, symmetric vs. asymmetric, zero-point

Quantization means representing values (weights or activations) with fewer bits than full precision floating point. There are two axes to consider: whether you quantize weights, activations, or both; and how you map real-valued ranges into discrete integer levels.

Weights vs. activations. Weights are the persistent parameters stored in memory; reducing weight precision primarily reduces model storage and the bandwidth needed to move weights at inference time. Activations are transient runtime values, such as the outputs of linear layers or cached key/value tensors; quantizing activations reduces working memory (for example, KV caches) and can allow larger context windows or higher batch sizes for a fixed device memory budget. BitNet b1.58 trains with ternary weights (values in \{-1, 0, +1\}) and uses 8-bit activations, illustrating a mixed strategy: extreme compression of the persistent weight tensors together with modestly reduced-precision activations.

Symmetric vs. asymmetric quantization and zero-point. A common quantization choice is whether to center the integer representation around zero (symmetric) or to allow an offset (asymmetric), which uses a zero-point. The presence of a zero-point complicates implementation and system-level optimization because it requires additional arithmetic to translate between integer ranges and true zero. BitNet b1.58 explicitly avoids zero-point quantization for activations: activations are scaled per token into the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ rather than shifted into $[0, Q_b]$. This removes the zero-point, simplifying implementation and system-level optimization with negligible performance impact in their experiments.

A concrete quantizer used for the weights in BitNet b1.58 is the “absmean” ternary quantization. Given a full-precision weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$, the method first computes a scale based on the mean absolute value:
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|.
\]
The scaled weights are then rounded and clipped to the ternary set $\{-1,0,1\}$:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))).
\]
This construction is symmetric around zero (no zero-point) and explicitly provides a zero value in the ternary alphabet, which—unlike a pure \{-1,+1\} binarization—permits feature filtering and sparse-like behavior.

# Post-training quantization landscape: SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ, QuIP and trade-offs

A widely used approach in industry for getting lower-precision models for inference is post-training quantization (PTQ): take a trained full-precision model and quantize its weights and/or activations without performing a new training run. The paper notes that PTQ methods such as SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ, QuIP (and variants like QuIP_sharp) are extensively used to move from 16-bit representations down to 4-bit and lower for inference. The advantage of PTQ is operational simplicity: you can quantize an existing model and immediately reduce memory and computational needs.

However, PTQ is not without trade-offs. The paper emphasizes that PTQ is “sub-optimal” relative to training models that are designed from the start to operate in very-low-bit regimes. Quantizing in a post hoc fashion can break delicate numerical structures and degrade model performance; as a result, PTQ often involves sophisticated calibration, per-channel scales, or other engineering to reduce accuracy loss. By contrast, BitNet b1.58 takes a different path: design and train-from-scratch a model whose weights and operators are already constrained to the low-bit format, which avoids some of the compromises inherent to PTQ.

In summary, PTQ techniques provide pragmatic routes to deploy lower-bit models quickly, but there is a performance vs. simplicity trade-off: PTQ can be easier to deploy, but may not attain the same accuracy–efficiency Pareto frontier as architectures and training recipes explicitly built for 1–2 bit operation.

# Quantization-aware training vs. train-from-scratch low-bit models

Two broad approaches exist to obtain low-bit models that maintain accuracy: (1) apply quantization-aware techniques during training (quantization-aware training, QAT) or (2) design the architecture and train-from-scratch so that weights and activations are constrained to the low-bit formats throughout training. The BitNet line—and BitNet b1.58 specifically—adopts the latter philosophy: the Transformer replaces standard linear operators with BitLinear and is trained from scratch with ternary weights and 8-bit activations.

The paper contrasts this with PTQ approaches: whereas PTQ modifies a trained full-precision model afterward, BitNet b1.58 starts the optimization process within the constrained quantized parameter space. This has several implications. From the algorithmic perspective, training from scratch lets the optimizer and the model’s parameterization adapt to the limited expressivity of the low-bit alphabet (for example, exploiting the explicit zero in ternary weights to enable feature filtering). From a systems perspective, train-from-scratch low-bit models permit simpler inference kernels: when the model parameters are known to be ternary or low-bit during training, inference kernels and hardware can be optimized around those operations (for example, replacing expensive FP16 multiply–add sequences with integer additions).

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that, when trained from scratch with an appropriate architecture and quantizer, 1.58-bit models can match full-precision FP16 baselines in perplexity and downstream zero-shot tasks starting at 3B parameter scale, while obtaining substantial resource advantages.

# Why 1–2 bit arithmetic promises orders-of-magnitude energy savings

Energy consumption in arithmetic is determined by both the type of operation (e.g., floating-point multiply versus integer addition) and the volume of such operations. The BitNet b1.58 paper makes two central claims explaining why moving to 1–2 bit regimes is so powerful.

First, replacing FP16 multiply–add operations with lower-precision integer-only operations reduces the per-operation energy cost. In the BitNet paradigm, matrix multiplications are implemented with integer additions (and simple integer logic) rather than FP16 multiplications and additions. Because many modern chips are power-constrained, reducing the energy per arithmetic operation can directly translate into faster computation: power limits often bound how many floating-point units can be simultaneously active, so a less power-hungry integer path allows more parallelism or higher clocking for the same power envelope.

Second, low-bit representations shrink memory footprint and bandwidth requirements, reducing data-movement energy. The paper emphasizes that the cost of moving weights from DRAM into on-chip memory is high, and smaller parameter encodings reduce both the energy and the latency of these transfers. For activations—especially KV caches in long-context inference—reducing bitwidth increases the feasible context size for the same memory cost.

The paper quantifies these effects in one concrete metric: using an energy model cited in their references, BitNet b1.58 saves 71.4× in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7 nm process chips compared to FP16 LLaMA-style baselines. Practically, this large per-matrix-multiply energy advantage compounds because matrix multiplications are the dominant operation and their fraction grows with model scale. Consequently, as model size increases, the relative gains in latency, memory usage, and energy of 1.58-bit models increase as well: reported speedups and memory reductions grow from the 3B scale up to 70B in the paper’s experiments.

Taken together, these two mechanisms—cheaper arithmetic and reduced data movement—explain why 1–2 bit arithmetic promises orders-of-magnitude energy and latency improvements. The BitNet b1.58 results demonstrate that, with a design that includes an explicit ternary weight alphabet and per-token symmetric activation scaling, it is possible to capture these system-level advantages without sacrificing model quality at sufficient model scales.

From 16-bit to Ternary: The Evolution from BitNet to BitNet b1.58

# Introduction: Why move away from 16-bit floating point?

Large Language Models (LLMs) have historically used floating-point representations such as FP16 or BF16 for weights and activations because they simplify optimization and retain numerical precision. However, most of an LLM’s runtime cost and energy consumption is concentrated in the dense matrix multiplications that implement the linear layers. The BitNet line of work takes a different approach: instead of optimizing floating-point arithmetic, it redesigns the representation of weights so that the dominant multiply-accumulate (MAC) work in linear layers can be implemented with much cheaper integer operations. BitNet b1.58 is one such step: every weight is constrained to the ternary set \{-1, 0, +1\} and activations are quantized to 8 bits. This change has two immediate consequences. First, the arithmetic in matrix multiplication is dramatically simplified (multiplications become conditional additions/subtractions and zeroing). Second, the memory footprint and bandwidth requirements shrink, which reduces DRAM transfers and overall energy and latency. Empirically, BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision (FP16/BF16) Transformer LLMs in perplexity and downstream task performance from a certain scale onward, while delivering substantial improvements in latency, memory, throughput and energy efficiency.

The rest of this chapter explains the core ideas behind BitNet and BitNet b1.58, why moving from binary to ternary weights matters, how ternary weights encode information (the origin of the “1.58 bits” name), how zero weights enable feature filtering and sparsity effects, and how modeling capacity compares between binary and ternary representations under comparable training.

# BitNet concept: replacing floating-point multiplications with integer additions

The classical dense linear layer computes an output matrix or vector via sums of products: for weights \(W\) and input activations \(X\), the product \(XW\) requires arithmetic multiplications and floating-point additions. When weights are restricted to a small discrete set, the cost structure of this computation changes.

In BitNet and BitNet b1.58 the linear layer (implemented as BitLinear in the paper) constrains weights to a tiny set of discrete values. For the original 1-bit BitNet that set was \{-1, +1\}; for BitNet b1.58 it is \{-1, 0, +1\}. When activations are represented as low-precision integers (BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit activations), multiplication by a ternary weight no longer needs a general-purpose floating-point multiplier. Multiplying an 8-bit activation \(a\) by a ternary weight \(w \in \{-1,0,1\}\) reduces to one of three simple operations: return \(a\) (for \(w=1\)), return \(-a\) (for \(w=-1\)), or return \(0\) (for \(w=0\)). Those are integer passes, sign-flips, and zeroing operations that can be implemented by conditional addition/subtraction and routing rather than full multiply units.

Because dense matrix multiplication is a linear combination of these element-wise multiplications, the bulk of the MAC work can be implemented using integer additions and subtractions (with occasional sign handling), and the hardware datapath no longer needs the power-hungry FP multipliers. The paper highlights that matrix multiplication in BitNet b1.58 consists primarily of INT8 additions, whereas FP16 models perform FP16 multiplications and additions. Given that matrix multiplication is the dominant cost in LLM inference and training, this shift has outsized effects on latency, throughput, memory transfers, and energy consumption.

# Binary vs. ternary weights: why introducing zero matters

Binary weights (\{-1, +1\}) and ternary weights (\{-1, 0, +1\}) both dramatically simplify the arithmetic. But adding the zero value does more than provide another codepoint: it introduces an explicit ability to deactivate connections and perform feature filtering—an important modeling primitive.

With binary weights, every input feature contributes either positively or negatively to every output via a full-strength sign. There is no native way to say “this input feature should not contribute at all to this output.” In contrast, with ternary weights the model can choose to set a weight to zero, thereby eliminating that input’s contribution to the corresponding output dimension. This enables a direct, learnable form of sparsity and selective gating at the weight level.

The paper emphasizes two practical advantages of including zero. First, zero weights explicitly support feature filtering: the model can suppress irrelevant features instead of forcing them to appear with a fixed sign. This better aligns with how dense floating-point weights naturally become small or near-zero when they are not useful. Second, adding zero increases the modeling flexibility: the network no longer must encode both the magnitude (sign) and the presence of a connection with a single binary decision. The net effect reported in the paper is that BitNet b1.58 (ternary) shows stronger modeling capability than the original 1-bit BitNet (binary), allowing BitNet b1.58 to match FP16 LLaMA baselines starting from model sizes of around 3B when trained under the same schedule and data.

The inclusion of zero also interacts well with the quantization and scaling design used in the model, which we next make explicit.

# Quantization recipe and the information content: why log2(3) ≈ 1.58 bits

A compact way to quantify how many bits are represented by a discrete alphabet is to take the base-2 logarithm of the alphabet size. For ternary weights with three possible symbols, each weight carries at most
\[
\log_2(3) \approx 1.58496\ \text{bits},
\]
which the paper rounds and refers to as “1.58 bits.” This is the information-theoretic maximum per weight under a uniform prior over symbols; practical effective bits can be lower depending on symbol distribution, but the \(\log_2(3)\) figure gives a straightforward and useful label for the representation density.

BitNet b1.58 uses an “absmean” quantization function to map continuous floating-point weights to the ternary set while retaining a useful scale. The quantization consists of scaling the full-precision weight matrix \(W\) by the average absolute value across the matrix and then rounding and clipping to \(\{-1,0,1\}\). Concretely, if \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\) then
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|,
\]
and the ternary quantized weight matrix \(\widetilde{W}\) is
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\; -1,\; 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max\big(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))\big),
\]
and \(\epsilon\) preserves numerical stability. The scalar \(\gamma\) acts as a learnable or tracked scale (depending on implementation) so that the ternary weight matrix \(\widetilde{W}\) represents the directionality and sparsity pattern while the scale encodes magnitude information. This two-part representation—discrete ternary signs/zeros plus a scale—gives the model greater expressive capacity than simply storing a uniform ternary code without scaling.

For activations, BitNet b1.58 follows the original BitNet implementation with one notable change: activations are scaled per-token into the symmetric integer range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) rather than \([0, Q_b]\) with a zero-point. This eliminates the need for zero-point handling and simplifies system-level implementation while having only negligible empirical impact on performance.

# Feature filtering via zero weights and sparsity effects

Zero weights create a native mechanism for the network to perform feature selection and create sparse effective connectivity patterns. Conceptually, this has several important consequences.

First, when a weight is zero, that input dimension does not need to be read, multiplied, or even routed into the accumulator for a particular output unit. On hardware and in optimized kernels, this can be exploited to reduce memory bandwidth or arithmetic operations if sparse representations or specialized kernels are used. Even without explicit sparse kernels, zero-valued weights reduce the number of non-trivial multiply-add operations because a multiplication by zero is trivially bypassed.

Second, zero weights approximate the role of small-magnitude floating-point weights but with a stronger and more interpretable effect: complete deactivation. In many trained floating-point networks, many weights are near zero and their contributions are negligible but still incur memory and computational cost. Ternary quantization forces a cleaner division between “contribute” and “do not contribute,” enabling the model to compress unimportant connections into the zero symbol.

Third, including zeros can improve robustness of the quantized network. When binary weights must flip between \(-1\) and \(+1\), small weight perturbations can change sign and dramatically affect output. With ternary weights, small fluctuations in the underlying floating-point pre-quantized weights can either remain in the same active sign or cross a threshold to zero; the presence of the zero state can make the quantization boundaries more semantically meaningful and reduce harmful sign flips in some cases. The paper reports that empirically this additional expressivity leads to stronger modeling capability and better parity with FP16 baselines.

Finally, the absmean scaling mentioned earlier lets the model preserve global magnitude information even while individual weights are discretized. The combination of a global scale \(\gamma\) and a sparse ternary pattern produces a parsimonious representation: a small set of active connections combined with a scale approximates a richer set of real-valued weights at substantially lower bit cost.

# Comparing modeling capacity: binary vs. ternary under similar training

Quantitatively, the paper evaluates BitNet b1.58 against FP16 LLaMA-style models trained from scratch on the same data (RedPajama, 100B tokens) and using the same model configurations and training tokens. Several empirical trends are worth noting and relate directly to modeling capacity:

- Starting at model sizes of around 3B parameters, BitNet b1.58 matches the perplexity and zero-shot end-task performance of full-precision FP16 LLaMA models trained in the same conditions. This suggests that at these scales the representational and optimization limitations imposed by ternary weights are effectively compensated by model size and training, making ternary networks a practical alternative.

- Performance differences between ternary and FP16 narrow as model size increases. The empirical results show that as models scale to 7B, 13B and 70B, BitNet b1.58’s gap to FP16 performance shrinks and, when measured against cost metrics (latency, memory, energy), BitNet b1.58 becomes strictly more efficient. For example, BitNet b1.58 yields a 2.71× speedup and 3.55× lower GPU memory consumption relative to FP16 at the 3B scale, and up to a 4.1× speedup at 70B.

- Comparing ternary to binary specifically, the paper states that BitNet b1.58’s ternary representation produces stronger modeling capability than the original 1-bit BitNet. While the paper does not report a head-to-head numerical table of binary vs. ternary for every model size, it highlights that adding the zero symbol—plus the absmean scaling, 8-bit activations, and LLaMA-compatible architectural choices (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and bias removal)—enables matching FP16 performance from 3B onward. In other words, ternary weights alleviate a key capacity and optimization bottleneck that pure binary weights faced.

- BitNet b1.58 also scales well with training tokens. A model trained with 2T tokens (following the StableLM-3B data recipe) outperforms the StableLM 3B baseline trained with the same tokens on a composite benchmark (including Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA and ARC-easy), indicating that ternary models maintain strong generalization behavior under long-data training regimes.

From an optimization perspective, ternary weights may be easier to train to high performance than binary weights because the zero state gives the optimizer an intermediate target when shifting weights away from one sign or the other. Combined with the global scaling factor, training can better preserve the relative magnitudes and directional effects of pre-quantized weights while imposing the severe bit constraints needed for hardware efficiency.

# Practical consequences: hardware, scaling laws and deployment

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates several practical advantages that stem directly from the above modeling and computational principles. Because the dominant computation becomes INT8 additions and conditional operations rather than FP16 multiplies, energy consumption for matrix multiplication is dramatically reduced: using an energy model referenced in the paper, BitNet b1.58 saves roughly 71.4× in arithmetic-operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips compared to FP16 LLaMA. Latency and memory results also improve: BitNet b1.58 attains multi-fold speedups and reduced memory usage across model scales, and throughput experiments show that a 70B BitNet b1.58 can support up to 11× the batch size and 8.9× the throughput on two 80GB A100 GPUs compared to the FP16 baseline.

The paper also frames a practical scaling-law-like equivalence in cost-efficiency: for example, 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 3B FP16 LLM in latency, memory, and energy; similarly, 30B b1.58 beats 7B FP16, and 70B b1.58 beats 13B FP16. Those equivalences illustrate how switching representation formats changes the Pareto frontier of cost versus performance.

Beyond GPU datacenter inference, ternary models open deployment opportunities for edge and mobile devices because they impose far lower memory and power requirements and are friendlier to CPU execution. They are also promising for specialized hardware: because operations reduce to additions, subtractions and zero-masking, custom accelerators could be optimized around these primitives to further amplify the energy and throughput gains.

# Conclusion: the role of zero and the path forward

Moving from FP16 to 1-bit and ternary-weight architectures changes the fundamental arithmetic, memory and communication patterns of LLM inference and training. BitNet b1.58 shows that by combining ternary weights \{-1,0,1\}, global scaling via absmean, 8-bit activations, and modern Transformer design choices, we can preserve modeling performance comparable to FP16 LLaMA models at practical scales while reaping large wins in latency, memory, throughput and energy. Crucially, adding the zero symbol unlocks feature filtering, sparsity-like effects, and smoother optimization targets that increase modeling capacity over pure binary schemes, and the information-theoretic cost per weight is compactly expressed as \(\log_2(3)\approx 1.58\) bits. These advances suggest a new computation paradigm and motivate further work on kernels, system optimizations, and hardware that are natively tuned for extremely low-bit LLMs.

The BitNet b1.58 Architecture

# BitLinear in place of nn.Linear: what changes and why

The most fundamental architectural change in BitNet b1.58 is the replacement of the standard dense linear layer (typically implemented as PyTorch's nn.Linear) with a specialized BitLinear layer. At a high level the difference is simple to state: nn.Linear performs matrix multiplications with full-precision floating-point weights (e.g., FP16/BF16) and floating-point activations, whereas BitLinear operates with ternary weights constrained to \{-1, 0, +1\} and low-bit integer-friendly activations. But this swap has a cascade of consequences for computation, memory, and training.

From a compute perspective, BitLinear exploits the structure of ternary weights to avoid the usual floating-point multiply-accumulate operations that dominate nn.Linear. When a weight can take only the values \{-1, 0, +1\}, each output element of a dense multiply can be computed as a sum (and sign flips) of selected input activations, eliminating most multiplications and replacing them with integer additions and sign operations. In practice this drastically reduces the arithmetic complexity and, more importantly, the energy cost and latency of the kernel. The paper demonstrates that this is not merely an implementation curiosity: matrix multiplication in BitNet b1.58 becomes predominantly integer-addition work, which is far cheaper on modern silicon than FP16 multiply–add.

From a systems and memory viewpoint, BitLinear reduces model footprint: storing ternary weights takes only 1.58 bits per weight on average (the title's shorthand for $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits), as opposed to 16 bits for FP16. Lower parameter bandwidth reduces the cost to stream weights from DRAM to on-chip SRAM during inference, and the smaller memory footprint enables higher batch sizes and larger effective context in constrained hardware. Finally, BitLinear enforces a different training regimen: because the weights are constrained to a ternary set, the model must be trained from scratch with the quantization behavior baked into the optimization (see the training-from-scratch section), rather than being converted after conventional full-precision training.

# Weights at 1.58 bits (ternary), activations at 8 bits

BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights that can take values in \{-1, 0, +1\}, and 8-bit activations. The ternary choice yields approximately $ \log_2 3 \approx 1.585 $ bits of information per weight, which the paper abbreviates as "1.58 bits." Constraining weights to this discrete set is enforced via an explicit quantization function during both forward and backward passes, so that the network learns to represent its functions using the ternary basis.

The paper uses an "absmean" quantization scheme to produce the ternary weights. The scheme first scales the floating-point weight matrix $W$ by its mean absolute value $\gamma$, then rounds and clips to the ternary set:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
with
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))),
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
This scaling by $\gamma$ normalizes the weight matrix before discretization so that the ternary values preserve relative magnitudes across different weight matrices; the small constant $\epsilon$ prevents division by zero. The presence of the zero value in the ternary set is deliberate: it enables explicit feature filtering (sparsity) in a way that a strict \{-1,+1\} binarization cannot. The paper highlights that allowing zeros strengthens modeling capability by permitting the network to entirely turn off unnecessary connections.

Activations are quantized to 8 bits, but BitNet b1.58 departs slightly from the original BitNet activation treatment: instead of scaling activations into $[0, Q_b]$ before non-linearities and using a zero-point, BitNet b1.58 scales activations per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, removing the need for zero-point quantization. This choice simplifies implementation and system-level optimization while yielding negligible performance differences in the experiments reported. The 8-bit activations plus ternary weights produce a computation profile dominated by low-bit integer additions, which the authors exploit for energy and latency savings.

# RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary positional embeddings, and bias removal

BitNet b1.58 deliberately adopts a set of architectural components that are "LLaMA-alike": RMSNorm, SwiGLU activation in the feedforward, rotary positional embeddings, and removal of all biases. The paper motivates this design choice primarily by compatibility: adopting the same components as LLaMA makes BitNet b1.58 straightforward to integrate into existing open-source toolchains and inference stacks. Beyond compatibility, these components also align well with the low-bit paradigm.

RMSNorm is a normalization layer that computes root-mean-square statistics and normalizes activations accordingly. Using RMSNorm (rather than, say, layer normalization with learnable biases) reduces the number of per-layer parameters and simplifies the numerical behavior, which can be advantageous when weights are heavily quantized. SwiGLU is the gated SiLU-based feedforward non-linearity used in recent Transformer variants; maintaining SwiGLU keeps the feedforward's expressivity while retaining architectural parity with LLaMA-like models. Rotary embeddings provide position information via a multiplicative frequency-based rotation of query/key vectors rather than adding a learned positional vector, which is more convenient when lowering precision and avoids introducing extra full-precision positional parameters that could complicate quantization. Finally, removing biases entirely simplifies quantization and inference kernels (no need to account for bias accumulation in low-bit arithmetic) and further improves compatibility with existing LLaMA-based implementations.

The paper emphasizes that these choices were made to ensure that BitNet b1.58 can slot into popular open-source software (Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp) "with minimal efforts," so the architecture intentionally mirrors the backbone used by modern open-source LLMs.

# Keeping embeddings in full precision and why that matters

Although weights inside the linear layers are ternary, BitNet b1.58 keeps embeddings in full precision (FP16/BF16). The paper notes that embedding memory occupies a relatively smaller proportion of total memory for larger models, but nonetheless explicitly retains embeddings at full precision. There are a few practical reasons for this choice reported or implied in the work.

First, token embeddings and final projection layers interact directly with the vocabulary and input token distributions; quantizing them aggressively can introduce perceptible degradation to language modeling and downstream metrics. Keeping embeddings in full precision preserves fine-grained representational capacity where it matters most for token-level modeling. Second, embeddings are lookup tables rather than dense matrix multiplies for which the ternary arithmetic trick applies; the advantages of quantizing embeddings into ternary form are smaller relative to the potential accuracy cost. Third, because embeddings are a smaller share of memory for large models, the overall cost-saving benefit of quantizing them is modest compared to quantizing the dense layers, so the architecture chooses to prioritize correctness and compatibility by keeping embeddings full precision.

The practical upshot is a hybrid memory profile: most of the model's dense weights are highly compressed to 1.58 bits each, delivering major bandwidth and storage savings, while embeddings remain in higher precision, ensuring stable token representations and simplifying integration with existing tokenizers and embedding pipelines.

# Compatibility with open-source stacks: Hugging Face, vLLM, llama.cpp

A central engineering goal in BitNet b1.58's design is interoperability with popular open-source LLM ecosystems. By aligning component choices (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, biasless layers) with LLaMA-like implementations, BitNet b1.58 minimizes the friction for adoption in Hugging Face transformers, inference engines such as vLLM, and lightweight C inference runtimes like llama.cpp. This compatibility is not merely cosmetic: it allows practitioners to reuse tokenization, data pipelines, evaluation harnesses, and deployment infrastructure without extensive rewrites.

From an implementation perspective, the differences that do exist—primarily BitLinear and the quantization-aware weight/activation pipelines—can be modularized as replacement kernels or operators. The paper reports integrating BitNet b1.58 with the FasterTransformer inference stack and using a 2-bit kernel from Ladder for inference, demonstrating that existing inference frameworks can host the low-bit kernels required. The result is practical: BitNet b1.58 can be plugged into the same ecosystem used for LLaMA-style models while delivering the arithmetic, memory, and energy benefits of the ternary-weight paradigm.

# Training from scratch paradigm for low-bit LLMs

A key methodological point in the paper is that BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with its low-bit constraints, rather than obtained by post-training quantization (PTQ) of a full-precision model. The paper contrasts this training-from-scratch approach with PTQ workflows, noting that PTQ—while convenient for industry rollouts—tends to be sub-optimal compared to training that internalizes the quantization behavior.

Training from scratch with the ternary constraint forces the model to discover representations and internal scaling that are compatible with the discrete weight set and lower-precision activations. The absmean quantizer and per-layer normalization of weights become part of the optimization dynamics; the optimizer learns to use the available ternary degrees of freedom (including zeros) to shape computation. Empirically, the paper shows that models trained this way can match FP16 baselines starting from the 3B scale in both perplexity and end-task accuracy, and that they scale well to larger sizes and token budgets (including a 2T-token training run reported in the results).

Put differently, the training-from-scratch paradigm is essential to realizing the benefits of BitNet b1.58: it enables the model to fit the function class permitted by ternary weights and 8-bit activations, rather than trying to approximate a full-precision function after the fact. This approach is what allows the architecture to simultaneously deliver competitive performance and the substantial memory/compute advantages of the 1.58-bit regime.

Quantization Mechanics in BitNet b1.58

# Absmean scaling: $\gamma = \text{mean}(|W|)$ and its intuition

BitNet b1.58 constrains each weight to the ternary set $\{-1,0,+1\}$. To make that ternary mapping meaningful across a wide range of weight magnitudes, the paper first rescales the floating-point weight matrix by a single scalar derived from the matrix itself. Concretely, the scale is
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
\]
where $W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ is a weight matrix and $\gamma$ is the average absolute value of its entries. The quantized matrix is then produced by dividing by $\gamma$ (with a tiny $\epsilon$ for numerical stability) and rounding-clipping to $\{-1,0,1\}$.

Intuitively, using the mean absolute value as the scale normalizes the typical magnitude of elements in the matrix so that the three discrete levels correspond to a meaningful partition of the weight distribution. If weights are much smaller than $\gamma$, they will quantize to $0$; if they are moderately large in magnitude they will quantize to $\pm 1$. This single, global scale per matrix makes the ternary mapping robust to overall layer-wise magnitude differences that arise during training, and it keeps the mapping symmetric with respect to sign because $\gamma$ is based on absolute values. The choice of the absolute mean (rather than variance, max, or another statistic) favors simplicity and a direct link to the typical magnitude of weights, which is sufficient for mapping into three symmetric discrete levels.

# RoundClip operator to map to $\{-1,0,+1\}$

BitNet b1.58 implements the mapping to ternary values via a two-step operation: rounding followed by clipping. The paper defines this compactly as
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\biggl(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\biggr),
\]
with
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max\bigl(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))\bigr).
\]
Operationally, each scaled element $x_{ij} = W_{ij}/(\gamma+\epsilon)$ is first rounded to the nearest integer (so values in $[-0.5,0.5)$ round to $0$, values above $0.5$ round to $1$, etc.). After rounding, any results outside the target interval $[-1,1]$ are clipped back into that interval. Because the rounded integers are constrained to $-1,0,1$ by the clipping, the net effect is a straightforward mapping from continuous weights to the ternary set.

This construction is simple and deterministic: it does not require special thresholding logic beyond the rounding step, and the clipping step guarantees numerical safety. The use of rounding (as opposed to, say, sign-thresholding alone) yields an explicit middle bin for small-magnitude weights, enabling the model to zero out weak components rather than forcing them to $\pm 1$.

# Symmetric activation quantization to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ per token (no zero-point)

For activations BitNet b1.58 departs from a zero-to-positive scheme and instead scales activations per token into a symmetric integer range. The paper follows BitNet’s activation quantization mechanics but removes the earlier per-layer positive-only scaling; specifically, activations are scaled to the symmetric interval $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ for each token. The goal of this choice is to remove any zero-point offset in quantization (i.e., there is no need to represent a nonzero integer value as the quantized representation of real zero), so the integer representation of activations is symmetric around zero.

Practically, the per-token symmetric scaling means that for each token (i.e., each position in the sequence) the activation values are rescaled so the extremes map to $\pm Q_b$. That mapping is applied before any non-linearities that follow in the layer pipeline. The symmetric integer range is then used downstream by the BitLinear layers and other integer-friendly kernels. BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit activations overall, so $Q_b$ is chosen consistently with that bit width.

# Practical effects of symmetric activations on implementation and accuracy

The paper highlights two practical consequences of adopting symmetric, per-token activation scaling. First, from an implementation and system perspective, symmetric quantization eliminates the need for a zero-point in the integer encoding. A zero-point requires extra arithmetic to adjust integer values when performing accumulations and can complicate fused kernels; by mapping activations directly to a signed integer range $[-Q_b,Q_b]$, arithmetic can be implemented as plain signed integer operations, which simplifies kernel code and system-level optimizations. The paper explicitly states that this choice is "more convenient and simple for both implementation and system-level optimization."

Second, empirically this symmetric per-token scheme has "negligible effects to the performance" in the authors’ experiments. That is, altering the activation scaling to be symmetric per token did not materially degrade the model’s perplexity or downstream task performance compared to the prior activation scaling scheme used in BitNet, while it provided the implementation benefits described above. Thus the symmetric choice is presented as a practical engineering tradeoff that favors system simplicity without meaningful accuracy cost, at least within the experimental setups reported.

# Per-matrix vs. per-channel scaling considerations

The weight quantization in BitNet b1.58 uses the per-matrix absmean scale $\gamma$ defined over the whole $n\times m$ matrix. That is, a single scalar scale is computed for each weight matrix and applied uniformly to every element in that matrix. The paper presents this per-matrix approach explicitly through the definition of $\gamma$.

A per-matrix scale is compact (one scalar per matrix), easy to compute, and straightforward to store and load during inference. It preserves the symmetry and relative ordering of weights inside the matrix while enforcing a uniform mapping threshold for rounding. The paper does not present per-channel (per-row or per-column) scaling experiments or report results using finer-grained scales, so it focuses on the per-matrix design as the practical choice in their implementation.

Because the paper emphasizes implementation simplicity and integration into common toolchains, the per-matrix scaling choice aligns with those goals: fewer scaling parameters must be tracked, the quantization operation is simpler, and the resulting integer kernels can be designed assuming a single known scale per matrix. The paper does not claim that per-matrix scaling is universally optimal for all possible layer types or data distributions, but it demonstrates that with per-matrix absmean scaling the BitNet b1.58 models can be trained from scratch at scale and achieve comparable accuracy to full-precision baselines for sufficiently large model sizes.

# Training considerations: gradient-through-quantization and stability

The paper states that BitNet b1.58 models are trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations, and it reports successful large-scale training (for example, models trained on 100B tokens and a 2T token run). However, the text provided does not specify the exact technique used to pass gradients through the non-differentiable quantization steps (for instance, whether a straight-through estimator or some other surrogate gradient method was applied) nor does it provide a detailed recipe for gradient scaling, learning-rate adjustments, or other optimization tricks specific to quantized weights.

What the paper does make clear empirically is that stable end-to-end training at scale is possible with the combination of the presented quantization choices (absmean scaling, RoundClip to $\{-1,0,1\}$, and symmetric per-token activation scaling) and the broader architectural decisions (e.g., using LLaMA-like components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and removal of biases). The reported results — matching FP16 perplexity and downstream task performance from about the 3B model size up — indicate that whatever gradient-passing mechanism was used in training was sufficient for convergence and stability in the authors’ experiments.

Because explicit gradient-through-quantization details are not provided in the paper text, practitioners aiming to reproduce or extend BitNet b1.58 should consult the implementation used by the authors (the paper references integration points like FasterTransformer and Ladder kernels) or accompanying code releases for the exact optimizer and surrogate-gradient choices. The high-level takeaway in the paper is that the quantization scheme, when combined with the model and training setup described, yields stable large-scale training and strong performance, but the paper leaves lower-level optimization and surrogate-gradient specifics unspecified in the text.

A New Computation Paradigm: Integer Additions Replace Floating-Point Multiplications

# Matrix multiply with ternary weights: coding multiplies as adds and sign operations

At the heart of BitNet b1.58 is a very simple but powerful observation: if each weight is constrained to the ternary set \{-1, 0, +1\}, a large portion of the work ordinarily done by floating‑point multipliers can be replaced with much cheaper integer additions, subtractions, and simple sign operations. To see this concretely, consider a single fully connected layer or linear projection that computes the product between an activation matrix $A$ and a weight matrix $W$. In a conventional floating‑point implementation we compute
\[ Y = A W^\top, \]
with each output element formed by many multiply–accumulate (MAC) operations.

BitNet b1.58 quantizes the full‑precision weight matrix $W$ into a scaled ternary matrix $\widetilde W\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n\times m}$ using the absmean rule described in the paper:
\[
\widetilde W = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|.
\]
Since $W \approx \gamma \widetilde W$, the layer's output can be written as
\[
Y \approx \gamma \left(A \widetilde W^\top\right).
\]
Now exploit the ternary structure. Decompose $\widetilde W$ into two binary indicator matrices $P$ and $N$:
\[
P_{ij} = \begin{cases}1 & \widetilde W_{ij}=+1\\0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases},
\qquad
N_{ij} = \begin{cases}1 & \widetilde W_{ij}=-1\\0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases},
\]
so $\widetilde W = P - N$. Then
\[
A\widetilde W^\top = A P^\top - A N^\top.
\]
Each column of $A P^\top$ and $A N^\top$ is simply a sum of a subset of columns of $A$ (where the subset is determined by the positions of +1 or −1 in the corresponding weight column). In other words, instead of performing many independent multiplications by arbitrary FP numbers, we perform a set of masked sums (integer additions) and a final subtraction between two integer accumulators. Entries corresponding to weight 0 are skipped entirely. Finally, the scalar $\gamma$ scales the integer result back toward the original magnitude.

This rewriting eliminates the general multiply operation at the per‑weight level: multiplying by +1 is a no‑op (copy / add), multiplying by −1 is a sign flip (or subtract), and multiplying by 0 is skipping the term. When activations are quantized to integer ranges (BitNet uses 8‑bit activations), the inner accumulation can be implemented with integer add/subtract on small accumulators, followed by a cheap scaling by $\gamma$ (a single FP multiply per output element or folded into later FP operations). This change is what enables the dramatic simplification of the arithmetic logic used during matrix multiplication.

# Arithmetic intensity vs. memory movement for low-bit kernels

When evaluating performance of kernels we often look at arithmetic intensity, which is the ratio of arithmetic operations (e.g., MACs) to data movement (bytes transferred). Two competing effects arise when moving from FP16 weights/activations to ternary weights and low-bit activations:

1. Reduced data movement per parameter: storing weights in 1.58 bits and activations in 8 bits drastically reduces the number of bytes that must be read from off‑chip DRAM or from on‑chip caches. For the same matrix shape, the byte volume of weight traffic drops by an order of magnitude compared to 16‑bit floats. Because the denominator in the arithmetic intensity (bytes transferred) falls, arithmetic intensity tends to increase, which is favorable when the kernel is memory‑bound.

2. Simpler arithmetic per weight: the primitive arithmetic changes from an FP multiply–add to integer adds/subtracts and masked accumulation. The cost per arithmetic operation is lower, and in many hardware platforms the peak integer throughput and packing can be significantly higher. Moreover, skipping zeros (weights equal to 0) reduces both arithmetic and memory traffic further.

Which limit dominates (compute vs. memory) depends on the model size, kernel implementation, and hardware. For large Transformer layers the cost of reading weights from DRAM and streaming them through the accelerator often dominates computation. Lowering the bits-per-weight and bits-per-activation therefore reduces the memory traffic and can move the kernel from memory‑bound toward compute‑bound (or at least improves the compute/memory balance), enabling higher overall throughput and lower latency. BitNet b1.58 leverages both effects: fewer bits per weight and cheap integer arithmetic so that the effective end‑to‑end cost per output token is much lower.

# Energy model intuition: FP16 add/mul vs. INT8 add

Energy per operation is an essential factor for real systems. Multipliers, especially floating‑point multipliers, are complex circuits: they require handling exponent alignment, mantissa multiplication, normalization, and rounding. These operations are expensive in area and energy compared to integer adders. Intuitively:

- A floating‑point multiplication consumes significantly more energy than a floating‑point addition.
- Integer additions (especially on 8‑bit or 16‑bit data) are far cheaper than FP multiplies and much cheaper than FP multiplies plus FP adds.

BitNet b1.58’s matrix multiplies are dominated by integer additions and subtraction plus occasional small integer accumulations, while a conventional FP16 Transformer spends most of the time performing FP16 multiplications and additions in MAC units. Using the energy model referenced in the paper (see [energycost, pokebnn] cited there), the authors estimate that for matrix multiplication on a 7‑nm process node, BitNet b1.58 saves about 71.4× in arithmetic operation energy compared to FP16 LLaMA for the matrix‑multiplication workload. The high‑level reason is that the expensive FP16 multiply units are largely avoided: the per‑weight operation becomes either “add this activation” or “subtract this activation” (for +1 or −1), or “skip” (for 0).

Putting it together: lower energy per operation (integer add vs. FP multiply) times fewer bits moved (lower weight and activation size) yields a multi‑order reduction in energy consumption for the dominant layer operations. That reduction translates directly into lower power draw and/or the ability to run more operations within the same power budget.

# Memory hierarchy: DRAM to SRAM transfers dominate at scale

In modern accelerators and GPUs the memory hierarchy is layered: large off‑chip DRAM holds the full model parameters, while smaller on‑chip SRAM or caches (and register files) hold working sets for lower‑latency access. Moving data from DRAM into SRAM and into the compute units is expensive in both time (latency) and energy. Two facts make this especially critical for large LLMs:

1. The matrix multiplication layers comprise most of the parameter mass and therefore dominate DRAM bandwidth requirements. Each inference token requires streaming many weight tiles through the compute units unless the on‑chip SRAM can hold the relevant working set.

2. Increasing on‑chip SRAM capacity to avoid DRAM transfers is costly in chip area and dollar cost; practical accelerators must balance capacity and cost.

BitNet b1.58 reduces the quantity of data that must be transferred for weights by storing them in roughly 1.58 bits each. For a fixed DRAM bandwidth and fixed SRAM capacity, this yields two major system benefits. First, weight tiles can be loaded from DRAM more quickly, reducing the latency of serving an output token. Second, more of the model (or larger working tiles) can fit in on‑chip SRAM, further reducing DRAM traffic and the need to re‑stream weights across tiles. In the paper this effect is directly visible in the measured memory usage (e.g., BitNet b1.58 uses 3.55× less GPU memory at 3B size and 3.32× less at 3.9B) and in higher throughput and lower latency when measured with FasterTransformer.

Another practical point made in the paper is that activations are quantized to 8 bits per token and scaled to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token to avoid zero‑point complications; this makes kernel implementation and system‑level optimization simpler and reduces the memory occupied by KV caches during long‑context inference, enabling longer effective context length for the same memory budget.

# Why power limits dominate chip performance and how 1.58-bit helps

Modern accelerator designs are often power‑ or energy‑limited rather than transistor‑limited. There are two related constraints that make power and energy central to system performance:

- Thermal and power delivery limits constrain how many arithmetic units can be turned on and at what frequency. Even if a chip could fit many more multipliers on die, it cannot necessarily run all of them at full speed because of the power and cooling budget.
- The energy cost per operation determines how many operations can be executed per unit time under a given power cap. If each operation is cheaper in energy, more operations can be sustained in the same power envelope.

Because BitNet b1.58 replaces energy‑expensive FP multiplications with low‑energy integer additions, the average energy per useful arithmetic operation falls dramatically. The practical implications are:

- For a fixed power budget, the system can perform more useful work per second (higher throughput) because each operation consumes less energy.
- Latency per token can be reduced because less time is lost waiting for DRAM transfers or because more compute engines can be kept active within the same thermal envelope.
- For a given performance target, fewer chips (or less expensive chips) may be required, cutting deployment costs and energy consumption.

The paper provides empirical evidence that these theoretical advantages matter in practice: BitNet b1.58 achieves multiple× speedups (2.71× faster at 3B, 4.1× faster at 70B) and large reductions in memory and estimated arithmetic energy (71.4× for matrix multiplication on 7 nm) compared to FP16 LLaMA, demonstrating that reducing per‑op energy directly translates to system‑level improvements.

# Kernel support: Ladder 2-bit kernels and integration in FasterTransformer

Achieving the gains described above depends on concrete kernel implementations that can exploit ternary/low‑bit formats efficiently. In the experiments reported, the authors integrated a 2‑bit kernel from the Ladder project into NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer library to run optimized inference measurements. Ladder provides kernels specialized for low‑bit operations, packing multiple low‑bit weights into machine words and exposing efficient vectorized accumulation paths that accumulate integer results into higher‑precision accumulators. Integrating such kernels into a high‑performance inference runtime (FasterTransformer) is crucial to measure realistic per‑token latencies and GPU memory usage.

A few practical implementation points highlighted by the paper:

- Activations are kept at 8 bits and scaled per token to a symmetric interval $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ instead of using a per‑tensor zero point. This simplification makes it easier to implement kernels without zero‑point biases and streamlines system‑level optimization.
- The ternary weights are derived with a per‑matrix scale $\gamma$, so the integer core of the kernel performs the masked integer accumulations, and a final scaling by $\gamma$ (which is a single scalar per weight matrix) restores the magnitude. This design means kernels can focus on very fast integer addition/subtraction and rely on a small number of FP multiplies for scaling or final conversions.
- The use of Ladder’s 2‑bit kernel inside FasterTransformer allowed the authors to report time‑per‑output‑token metrics and end‑to‑end latency/throughput numbers that account for realistic memory and compute behavior on GPUs. The reported numbers show large practical gains (e.g., 8.9× throughput at 70B with two 80GB A100s) and indicate that existing inference frameworks can be adapted to support 1.58‑bit models with relatively modest engineering effort.

The paper also notes that the 2‑bit kernels used for the measurements are not the final word — there is room for further kernel and hardware co‑design to extract additional gains. The authors encourage the development of specialized hardware (LPUs and similar) and further kernel optimizations that take even better advantage of ternary weight structure and integer accumulation pipelines.

---

Summary

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates how a small change in per‑weight discretization — moving to ternary weights \{-1,0,1\} with a scalar scale factor — yields a new computation paradigm: replace most FP multiplies with integer additions and masked accumulation. That change reduces bits moved through the memory hierarchy, lowers the energy per arithmetic operation dramatically (the paper estimates a 71.4× arithmetic energy saving for matrix multiplies on 7‑nm), and shifts real workloads into a regime where latency, memory footprint, and power are all improved. Practical kernel support (e.g., Ladder 2‑bit kernels integrated into FasterTransformer) is sufficient to realize large end‑to‑end speedups and memory savings on current GPUs, and the design is compatible with common Transformer components used in the LLaMA family, easing deployment. Together these effects explain why ternary‑weight matrix multiplication can be both dramatically cheaper and, at sufficient scale, competitive in accuracy with full‑precision models.

Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol

# Pretraining data: RedPajama 100B tokens

A cornerstone of any fair comparison between model families is using the same pretraining data and the same amount of exposure to that data. In this work every model — both the 1.58-bit BitNet variant and the FP16 LLaMA-like baselines — was pretrained from scratch on the RedPajama corpus for 100 billion tokens. Using identical corpora and token budgets ensures differences in final performance stem from architecture, quantization, and implementation choices rather than discrepancies in data coverage or token count.

When reproducing or extending these experiments, it is important to apply the same text preprocessing and tokenization pipeline to both model variants so that token boundaries and vocabulary statistics are identical. The paper explicitly compares models trained with the same model sizes and the same 100B-token pretraining budget; keeping these constants is the simplest and most reliable way to support a fair apples-to-apples comparison.

# Model scales: 3B, 3.9B, 7B, 13B, 70B

To examine both scaling behavior and practical trade-offs across sizes, the paper evaluates a range of model parameter counts: 3B, 3.9B, 7B, 13B, and 70B. Each scale is trained from scratch under the same RedPajama 100B-token schedule. The 1.58-bit variant (BitNet b1.58) uses ternary weights in \{-1,0,1\} (i.e., an average storage cost of approximately 1.58 bits per weight) and 8-bit activations; the FP16 LLaMA-like baselines use standard 16-bit floating-point weights and activations.

Evaluating multiple sizes is necessary to reveal (1) at which scale the low-bit model reaches parity with the FP16 baseline in perplexity and end-task accuracy, and (2) how runtime and memory advantages evolve as parameter counts grow. In the paper, BitNet b1.58 is observed to start matching FP16 performance at the 3B scale and shows increasing inference and memory advantages at larger scales.

# Perplexity benchmarks: WikiText2, C4

Perplexity is a fundamental intrinsic measure of a language model’s next-token prediction quality. The paper reports validation perplexity on two standard corpora: WikiText2 and C4. These datasets are widely used for measuring LM perplexity because they are of different flavors (WikiText2 is smaller and Wikipedia-like; C4 is large and web-derived), providing complementary views of token-level modeling ability.

Perplexity is computed in the usual way from token-level log-probabilities reported by the models on held-out validation sets. Ensuring comparable tokenization and the same evaluation text split across models is essential. Because BitNet b1.58 uses quantized weights and 8-bit activations, small implementation differences (for example in activation scaling) were normalized: activations are scaled per token to the range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) to eliminate zero-point complications, which simplifies implementation while producing negligible performance differences. This keeps the perplexity calculation comparable across the quantized and full-precision models.

# Zero-shot end tasks: ARC (Easy/Challenge), HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ

Beyond perplexity, downstream zero-shot task performance measures practical capability. The paper evaluates zero-shot accuracy on a suite of common benchmarks: ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, and BoolQ. These tasks probe reasoning, commonsense, and question-answering capabilities, and they are used in many LLM evaluations to provide a standard comparison.

All zero-shot experiments follow the evaluation pipeline of lm-evaluation-harness, which standardizes prompt templates and evaluation mechanics. For each task the model is given the same prompt templates and the output is scored against the canonical references in the harness. For tasks reported with multiple metrics (e.g., accuracy and “normalized accuracy”) the paper averages the two as described in the StableLM comparison. Using a single, well-known evaluation harness helps avoid discrepancies introduced by ad-hoc prompt engineering or different scoring implementations.

# Measurement methodology: time per output token, memory footprint

The paper focuses on two practical measurement axes for inference: latency as time per output token, and GPU memory footprint during inference.

Time per output token: Conditional generation cost is dominated by the token-by-token autoregressive decode phase. To measure latency robustly the paper reports time per output token (wall-clock time to produce one next token) using a heavily optimized inference stack (see Implementation Stack). This metric captures the real user-facing latency during typical generation workloads and abstracts away sequence-length dependent setup costs. All timing measurements integrate the specialized low-bit kernels and are performed under the same hardware conditions for fair comparison.

Memory footprint: For inference the relevant memory budget is GPU device memory used for model weights, activations (including KV cache), and any implementation buffers. The paper reports peak GPU memory usage during inference runs. Importantly the embedding parameters are kept full precision (FP16) in both the BitNet b1.58 and LLaMA baselines; because the embedding size is a smaller fraction of total memory in larger models, the relative memory savings of BitNet b1.58 grow with model size. When measuring memory, the protocol increments batch sizes until hitting the device’s memory limit (see Throughput below) to determine capacity and to compare the maximum achievable batch sizes between model variants.

Throughput measurement: For throughput experiments (how many tokens or sequences per second can be processed), the paper scales batch size upward until the GPU memory limit is reached (with a fixed sequence length of 512) and reports the maximal throughput achieved. For the 70B experiments, two 80GB NVIDIA A100 cards are used with pipeline parallelism so that FP16 LLaMA 70B can run; the same hardware and parallelism setup is used for BitNet b1.58 70B to ensure parity. This strategy ensures throughput comparisons are constrained only by available device memory and the same parallelism strategy.

# Implementation stack: FasterTransformer with 2-bit kernels, GPU environment

To measure inference latency and memory accurately the paper uses a high-performance inference stack. FasterTransformer is used as the base framework; it is a production-grade, highly-optimized codebase for transformer inference on NVIDIA GPUs. For the quantized linear operations in BitNet b1.58 the authors integrate a 2-bit kernel from Ladder to accelerate matrix multiplication; this 2-bit kernel supports the low-bit arithmetic patterns required by the ternary weight representations and low-bit activations.

Key implementation and deployment details to replicate or compare against the paper’s measurements:

- Use FasterTransformer for inference-time measurements so that both FP16 and low-bit kernels can be compared under the same optimized runtime.
- Integrate the Ladder 2-bit kernel (or the equivalent low-bit kernels used in the work) for BitNet b1.58 to realize the reported speedups; the experiments measure latency with these kernels enabled.
- Run on the same class of GPUs for reported comparisons. The throughput experiment for 70B models uses two 80GB A100 cards with pipeline parallelism; latency/memory trends were also measured across model sizes on comparable GPU devices.
- For evaluation harnesses and compatibility with existing tooling, the BitNet design adopts LLaMA-alike components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings) and removes biases. This design choice reduces integration friction with popular open-source tooling (e.g., Hugging Face stacks, vLLM, and llama.cpp) and avoids discrepancies caused by differing core block implementations.

# Model internals and quantization specifics relevant to fairness

To make a fair comparison, the paper ensures both models share the same high-level architecture except for the quantization of linear layers and activation precision. BitNet b1.58 preserves LLaMA-like components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, bias removal) so architectural differences are minimized. The quantized weights and activations follow carefully specified transformations:

Weights are constrained to \{-1,0,+1\} by an absmean scaling followed by rounding and clipping. The paper gives the quantization functions exactly as:

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]

\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))),
\]

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
\]

where \(\gamma\) is the mean absolute value of the weight matrix and \(\epsilon\) is a small constant to avoid division by zero. This absmean scaling places the real-valued weights on a scale where the nearest ternary values reasonably approximate the original weights.

For activations, the implementation follows the BitNet activation quantization but scales activations per token to the symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) to eliminate zero-point complications. The activations are represented in 8 bits in BitNet b1.58 for the experiments reported. This per-token symmetric scaling simplifies implementation and system-level optimizations while having negligible impact on measured performance.

# Energy and arithmetic-cost estimation

Aside from latency and memory, the paper estimates arithmetic energy consumption focusing on matrix multiplication cost (the dominant computational kernel in transformers). Using an energy model from prior work, the paper reports that, on 7 nm process chips, BitNet b1.58 saves an estimated factor of 71.4× in arithmetic operations energy for matrix multiplications compared to FP16 LLaMA. This dramatic arithmetic-energy reduction arises because BitNet b1.58 replaces many FP16 multiplies and adds with much cheaper integer additions (and bitwise-friendly operations) in the quantized kernels. Note that this energy estimate concentrates on arithmetic cost and does not by itself account for all system-level power sinks (DRAM transfers, on-chip memory movement, and fixed overheads); nevertheless, it highlights the potential for large gains when arithmetic dominates.

# Protocols to ensure fair comparisons

The following checklist summarizes the protocol used to ensure fairness and reproducibility between BitNet b1.58 and FP16 LLaMA-like baselines as reported in the paper:

1. Identical pretraining corpus and token budget: both models trained from scratch on RedPajama for 100B tokens (and, in one experiment, BitNet trained to 2T tokens following the StableLM-3B recipe for token-scaling tests).
2. Matched architectural components: both models adopt the same LLaMA-like components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings) and bias removal so that the only systematic differences are quantization and activation precision.
3. Explicit quantization recipe: absmean scaling and ternary rounding for weights; per-token symmetric activation scaling to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) with 8-bit activations.
4. Use the same evaluation harness: zero-shot tasks evaluated through lm-evaluation-harness to ensure consistent prompt templates and scoring.
5. Same optimized inference runtime: FasterTransformer with Ladder 2-bit kernels for low-bit operations, same class of GPUs (A100 80GB for throughput experiments), and identical parallelism strategy (pipeline parallelism for 70B).
6. Measurement definitions: latency reported as time per output token; memory as peak GPU memory during inference; throughput measured by increasing batch size until the device memory limit is reached with a fixed sequence length (512).
7. Keep embeddings in the same precision for both models: embeddings remained full precision (FP16) to avoid introducing differences that would change how embedding layers are represented and evaluated.

# Additional experimental notes (token scaling and end-task reporting)

To probe generalization with larger token budgets, the paper also trains a BitNet b1.58 model under a 2T-token schedule following the StableLM-3B data recipe and reports zero-shot performance on a grouped benchmark (Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, ARC-easy). These comparisons are presented alongside the StableLM 3B numbers at 2T tokens. For the task-level reporting the paper averages accuracy and normalized-accuracy when both are provided, matching the StableLM reporting convention.

# Practical advice for reproduction

- Use the same tokenization and preprocessing pipeline for both models.
- Ensure the quantization code implements the exact absmean scaling and RoundClip functions; small deviations in scaling can produce significant differences in quantized approximations.
- Run inference timing on a warmed-up model (several initial iterations to stabilize caches and memory allocations) and report steady-state time per token.
- When measuring throughput, fix sequence length (the paper uses 512) and increase batch size until out-of-memory to determine maximal achievable throughput; report both peak batch size and tokens-per-second.
- Use a standardized evaluation harness (lm-evaluation-harness) to avoid prompt template and scoring variants.
- When reporting energy or arithmetic cost, specify whether the estimate is arithmetic-only (as in the paper’s 71.4× matrix-multiply energy reduction on 7 nm chips) or end-to-end system energy (which requires additional measurement instrumentation).

By following these experimental design choices and measuring consistently along the axes described above (perplexity, zero-shot accuracy, time per output token, GPU memory footprint, throughput, and arithmetic-energy estimates), experimental results for BitNet b1.58 and FP16 LLaMA-like baselines can be fairly and reproducibly compared.

Results: Accuracy, Perplexity, and Pareto Improvements

# Perplexity parity with FP16 from 3B scale

Perplexity is the canonical measure of how well a language model predicts held-out text: lower perplexity indicates better predictive modeling of token sequences. In the experiments described in the paper, BitNet b1.58 models were pre-trained from scratch on the RedPajama corpus for 100 billion tokens and evaluated on standard validation corpora such as WikiText2 and C4. The key empirical finding is that BitNet b1.58 reaches parity with a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline in perplexity beginning at the 3B parameter scale. In other words, when both models are trained under the same data and model-size configurations, the ternary-weight BitNet b1.58 (weights in \{-1,0,1\}, 8-bit activations) matches the perplexity of the corresponding FP16 model once the model size reaches 3 billion parameters.

This parity is significant because it demonstrates that reducing weight precision to effectively 1.58 bits per parameter does not necessarily incur a modeling capacity penalty at moderate-to-large scales, provided the architecture and training recipe are aligned (the paper uses LLaMA-like components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings and removes biases). The observation suggests a scale-dependent tradeoff: at smaller sizes the quantization may hurt, but from 3B and up the quantized architecture can represent and optimize the same distribution with equivalent token-wise predictive performance. Formally, if we denote perplexity by $P(\text{model})$, the empirical result is

$P(\text{BitNet b1.58 at 3B}) \approx P(\text{FP16 LLaMA at 3B}).$

This establishes a baseline equivalence in modeling quality that the rest of the results build upon.

# Zero-shot task performance narrowing gap with scale

Beyond perplexity, the paper evaluates zero-shot performance on a set of downstream language tasks (ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ). These tasks test reasoning, commonsense, and understanding abilities that depend on representations learned during pretraining. The experiments show that the performance gap between BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA baselines systematically narrows as model size increases. Put differently, larger BitNet b1.58 models close the gap in zero-shot accuracy that may be present at smaller sizes.

This trend mirrors the perplexity finding: as model capacity grows, the ternary-weight representation becomes sufficient to capture the useful features and reasoning patterns needed by downstream tasks. The paper reports that starting at the 3B scale, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 performance not only in perplexity but also in zero-shot task accuracy. Denote accuracy on a task set by $A(\cdot)$; then the empirical pattern is

$\lim_{s \uparrow} \bigl[A(\text{BitNet b1.58 at size } s) - A(\text{FP16 at size } s)\bigr] \to 0,$

with practical parity observed at $s=3\text{B}$. The practical upshot is that under the same pretraining regimen, moving to 1.58-bit weights does not force a loss in downstream zero-shot capability once the model is large enough.

# 3.9B BitNet b1.58 surpassing FP16 3B at lower cost

A particularly informative comparison in the paper contrasts BitNet b1.58 at 3.9B parameters against FP16 LLaMA at 3B parameters. The empirical result is two-fold: (1) the 3.9B b1.58 model attains higher end-task performance than the FP16 3B model, and (2) it does so with substantially lower inference cost. The cost reductions are quantified: BitNet b1.58 at 3.9B is reported to be 2.4 times faster in latency and to use 3.32 times less GPU memory than the FP16 LLaMA 3B model, while simultaneously achieving better accuracy.

These measurements arise because the dominant computational kernel in Transformers—matrix multiplications implemented by \texttt{nn.Linear} layers—becomes far cheaper in the BitNet paradigm. Matrix multiplication in BitNet is implemented using integer additions (and very limited multiplication), reducing arithmetic and data-movement cost. Practically, if $T_{\text{fp16}}$ is the per-token latency of FP16 LLaMA 3B and $T_{\text{b1.58}}$ is that of BitNet b1.58 3.9B, the measured speedup is

$S = \frac{T_{\text{fp16}}}{T_{\text{b1.58}}} \approx 2.4.$

Likewise, for memory usage $M$,

$\frac{M_{\text{fp16}}}{M_{\text{b1.58}}} \approx 3.32.$

Thus, for deployment budgets constrained by latency or memory, it can be strictly better to run a larger BitNet b1.58 model than a smaller FP16 model: the larger b1.58 model provides higher task accuracy and yet consumes less memory and inference time.

# Pareto frontier shift: accuracy vs. latency/memory

The combination of matched or better accuracy with significantly reduced latency and memory pushes the system-level Pareto frontier for model selection. In conventional settings, model designers trade accuracy against inference cost (latency, memory, energy). The paper demonstrates that BitNet b1.58 shifts that frontier outward: for many points in the cost–accuracy plane, there exists a BitNet b1.58 model that dominates the FP16 alternatives (i.e., better accuracy at lower cost or equal accuracy at much lower cost).

The authors summarize several concrete equivalences that make this shift explicit. Based on measured latency, memory, and energy trends, the paper reports:

- 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than 3B FP16 LLM.
- 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16 LLM.
- 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16 LLM.

Each of these equivalences indicates a cost-aware mapping from FP16 model sizes to larger b1.58 sizes that deliver equal or better task performance at lower inference cost. If we consider the feasible set F of (cost, accuracy) achievable by FP16 models and the set B achievable by BitNet b1.58 models, the empirical evidence indicates B strictly expands the reachable region toward both lower cost and higher accuracy for many practical points. Put another way, for a given cost budget $C$, there often exists a BitNet b1.58 model with accuracy $A_{\text{b}}(C)$ such that

$A_{\text{b}}(C) \ge A_{\text{fp16}}(C)$

and in many cases

$A_{\text{b}}(C) > A_{\text{fp16}}(C).$

This Pareto improvement is driven by the structural cost reductions of 1.58-bit weights (reduced arithmetic energy, reduced DRAM bandwidth and memory footprint, cheaper kernels) combined with sufficient model capacity at larger parameter counts to preserve or improve predictive performance.

# Implications for model selection at deployment time

The empirical results have immediate, practical implications for deployment decisions. When choosing a model for inference under constraints of latency, memory footprint, energy, or throughput, practitioners should treat model size and numeric precision as joint levers rather than independent axes. The paper’s results argue that in many realistic production settings, selecting a larger BitNet b1.58 model will yield strictly better end-task performance for the same or lower resource usage compared to choosing a smaller FP16 model.

Concretely, suppose a deployment has a tight memory budget $M_{\max}$ or latency target $T_{\max}$. The paper’s measurements show cases in which the designer can select a BitNet b1.58 model of size $s_{\text{b}} > s_{\text{fp16}}$ such that

$M(\text{b1.58 at } s_{\text{b}}) \le M_{\max},\quad T(\text{b1.58 at } s_{\text{b}}) \le T_{\max},$

while achieving accuracy

$A(\text{b1.58 at } s_{\text{b}}) \ge A(\text{FP16 at } s_{\text{fp16}}).$

In throughput-bound settings the advantages become even clearer: on two 80GB A100 cards with pipeline parallelism, BitNet b1.58 70B supports up to 11 times the batch size of FP16 LLaMA 70B and achieves 8.9 times higher throughput. Thus, for services that process many concurrent requests, BitNet b1.58 allows higher request concurrency or smaller hardware footprints for a given throughput target.

Energy and long-context scenarios also favor BitNet b1.58. The paper estimates a 71.4x reduction in arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips, and notes that reducing activations from 16-bit to 8-bit (as used in BitNet b1.58) allows doubling of context length for the same KV cache cost. These properties make BitNet b1.58 attractive for energy-constrained deployments and for applications requiring long context windows.

Finally, the paper’s experiment training a BitNet b1.58 model with 2T tokens (following StableLM-3B’s data recipe) showed superior zero-shot performance on a composite benchmark (Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, ARC-easy) compared to StableLM 3B at 2T tokens, indicating that BitNet b1.58 generalizes well under large-token regimes as well. This reinforces the deployment recommendation: when inference cost matters, prefer larger BitNet b1.58 models because they often beat smaller FP16 models in both accuracy and system metrics.

In summary, the results show that BitNet b1.58 advances the cost–accuracy frontier. For practitioners, the correct operational decision is often to trade a modest increase in parameter count for lower-precision ternary weights in order to obtain strictly better accuracy at lower latency, memory, energy, and higher throughput. These takeaways should guide model selection for production systems where both performance and operational cost matter.

Systems Gains: Latency, Memory, Energy, and Throughput at Scale

# Latency trends: up to 4.1x faster at 70B vs. FP16 baseline

Latency for transformer inference is dominated by a combination of two factors: the raw compute required for linear layers (matrix multiplications) and the cost of moving weights/activations between memory hierarchies. BitNet b1.58 attacks both. It replaces most FP16 multiply–accumulate work in the bulk of the model with integer additions and sparse/ternary arithmetic (weights in \{-1,0,+1\} with 8-bit activations), and it compresses the weights so there is less data to move into on-chip SRAM. These two effects compound as model size grows because the fraction of runtime spent in \emph{nn.Linear} (matrix multiplies) increases with model size.

Empirically, this design yields large speedups. With the same training recipe and tokens, BitNet b1.58 achieves a measured latency reduction relative to a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline of 2.71× at 3B parameters and up to 4.1× at 70B parameters. The trend—larger models gain larger speedups—follows from the fact that as model parameter count increases, the absolute cost of linear layers grows faster than costs that do not scale as strongly (embedding lookup overhead, small elementwise operations, etc.). In other words, if the time cost of matrix multiplies grows with parameter count while other costs grow more slowly, any per-op acceleration in matrix multiplies yields an increasing fraction of total latency savings as you scale.

To reason about latency more formally, decompose the per-token latency T into compute and memory components:
\[
T = T_{\text{compute}} + T_{\text{mem}}.
\]
For large transformers, \(T_{\text{compute}}\) is dominated by the cost of the dense linear layers, which scales roughly with the number of parameters and sequence length. BitNet b1.58 reduces the per-operation cost of those linear layers (lower complexity integer operations and fewer effective multiplications), so \(T_{\text{compute}}\) is reduced by a factor that increases in impact as \(T_{\text{compute}}\) becomes the dominant term. In practice this manifests as the observed 2.4–4.1× speedups across the reported model sizes.

# Memory trends: growing benefits as embeddings’ proportion shrinks

When comparing raw bit budgets, converting a model from FP16 (≈16 bits per parameter) to 1.58 bits per parameter suggests a theoretical reduction in weight storage of about
\[
\frac{16}{1.58}\approx 10.13\times.
\]
However, a real transformer’s memory footprint is not only weights: embeddings, optimizer state (during training), activations and a small amount of supporting metadata are often stored in higher precision or outside the quantized representation. As a result, practical reductions are smaller than the theoretical ceiling but still substantial and improve with scale.

Empirical results reflect this. At 3B parameters BitNet b1.58 uses 3.55× less GPU memory than the FP16 LLaMA baseline, and a 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model uses 3.32× less memory while outperforming an FP16 3B model. The reason the observed factor is below the theoretical 10× is twofold: (1) some components, notably token embeddings and certain small buffers, remain in full precision and thus form a larger fraction of the total memory footprint for smaller models; (2) there is engineering overhead and memory fragmentation on GPUs that prevents perfect packing.

As model size grows, the proportion of memory consumed by embeddings and other non-linear-layer structures decreases. If we denote total memory as
\[
M = M_{\text{weights}} + M_{\text{embeddings}} + M_{\text{other}},
\]
and the fraction occupied by weights increases with model size, then reducing \(M_{\text{weights}}\) by the large factor afforded by 1.58-bit weights leads to a larger overall drop in \(M\) for larger models. This explains the observed trend: the effective memory win of BitNet b1.58 becomes more pronounced at 13B, 30B and 70B than at smaller scales.

# Energy estimates: 71.4x arithmetic energy savings for matmul on 7nm

Energy consumption for inference can be split into arithmetic energy (the energy cost of the computational operations) and memory/system energy (moving data between DRAM, SRAM and compute units). Matrix multiplication dominates arithmetic energy in transformers. Using the energy model cited in the paper, BitNet b1.58 replaces energy-expensive FP16 multiply–add operations with primarily INT8 additions and sparse ternary operations. On a 7nm process the arithmetic energy for matrix multiply is estimated to be reduced by a factor of 71.4× when comparing BitNet b1.58’s approach to the FP16 baseline for the matmul component.

A compact way to reason about end-to-end energy is to express total FP16 energy per inference (per token or per sequence) as
\[
E_{\text{FP}} = E_{\text{matmul,FP}} + E_{\text{other,FP}},
\]
and the BitNet total as
\[
E_{\text{B}} = \frac{E_{\text{matmul,FP}}}{71.4} + E_{\text{other,B}}.
\]
If we assume \(E_{\text{other,B}}\approx E_{\text{other,FP}}\) for non-matmul components (conservative approximation), the relative energy ratio \(R\) is
\[
R = \frac{E_{\text{B}}}{E_{\text{FP}}}
  = \frac{\frac{E_{\text{matmul,FP}}}{71.4} + E_{\text{other,FP}}}{E_{\text{matmul,FP}} + E_{\text{other,FP}}}
  = 1 - f\left(1 - \frac{1}{71.4}\right),
\]
where
\[
f = \frac{E_{\text{matmul,FP}}}{E_{\text{FP}}}
\]
is the fraction of total FP16 energy spent on matrix multiplies. This expression clearly shows that as \(f\) approaches 1 (matmul-dominated models, typical of very large transformers), \(R\) approaches \(1/71.4\), i.e., near the full 71.4× arithmetic savings. For smaller models or configurations where \(f\) is smaller, the end-to-end gain is reduced accordingly.

Thus the 71.4× figure applies directly to matmul arithmetic; the end-to-end energy advantage grows with model size because matmul becomes a larger share of the total energy budget. This is consistent with the reported observation that BitNet b1.58 becomes increasingly more energy efficient relative to the FP16 baseline as the model scales to 13B, 30B and 70B.

# End-to-end energy with longer sequences and larger models

Sequence length directly affects two important contributors to energy and memory: (1) the compute needed each token (self-attention scales with sequence length when caching is not used efficiently), and (2) the KV cache size during generation. For autoregressive generation, KV caching is a major memory consumer: the cache stores keys and values for all past tokens. The cache memory scales approximately as
\[
M_{\text{KV}} \propto L \cdot H \cdot b_{\text{act}},
\]
where \(L\) is the context length, \(H\) is hidden dimension size, and \(b_{\text{act}}\) is the bits per activation stored. By reducing activation precision from 16 bits to 8 bits, BitNet b1.58 can—under the same memory budget—support roughly twice the context length \(L\) (ignoring other overheads), i.e., a qualitative doubling of native context in practice.

Energy per token for longer sequences also increases because more data is moved and more accumulation is performed. Using the same decomposition as in the energy section with KV cost included in \(E_{\text{other}}\) or \(E_{\text{mem}}\), reducing the activation bitwidth reduces both memory transfer energy and storage energy in the cache. Therefore, as sequence length increases, the relative energy advantage of a model that uses lower-precision activations grows, since the memory-transfer component (which favors narrower representations) becomes more significant. The paper notes that activations in BitNet b1.58 are 8-bit and suggests the possibility of compressing them further (future work) to achieve additional context-length or energy gains.

In short, for long-sequence use-cases the combination of low-weight precision and lower-precision activations compounds to give larger effective energy and memory wins compared with short context scenarios. This is another reason BitNet b1.58 is especially beneficial at large scale and for long-context applications.

# Throughput on two A100 80GB with pipeline parallelism: 11x batch, 8.9x tokens/sec

Throughput (tokens/sec) in practice is constrained by memory capacity (how large a batch and how much KV cache you can fit) as well as per-token latency. The reported experimental setup places both BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA 70B model on two 80GB A100 GPUs using pipeline parallelism. For a sequence length of 512, the batch size was increased until GPU memory was exhausted. Under this realistic evaluation: BitNet b1.58 70B supports up to 11× the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA 70B before hitting memory limits, and this increased batch capacity yields an observed 8.9× higher throughput (tokens/sec).

Why does a smaller memory footprint translate into such large throughput gains? There are three linked effects. First, holding more examples (larger batch) lets the hardware amortize per-batch overheads and better utilize compute pipelines. Second, because BitNet b1.58 stores most weights and activations more compactly, more model parallelism or larger local caches can be used on-device, reducing inter-device communication overhead in pipeline setups. Third, smaller per-example memory allows more in-flight queries, increasing parallelism and overall utilization. The net result is a significant practical throughput advantage, not merely an asymptotic improvement.

# Kernel and compiler opportunities beyond current 2-bit kernels

The results discussed above used an integrated 2-bit kernel (from Ladder) for parts of the implementation. While this is an important engineering baseline, the BitNet b1.58 design opens additional, orthogonal opportunities for systems and compiler-level optimizations that can push latency, memory, and energy even further down.

At the kernel level, there is room to exploit the ternary nature of weights (\{-1,0,+1\}) and 8-bit activations. A purpose-built kernel can avoid general integer multiplication instructions entirely for many operations: a ternary weight multiply reduces to sign-based selection and addition/subtraction (or masked accumulation), and zero weights imply skipped work (sparsity-aware execution). Efficient bit-packing for the ternary weight arrays and streaming decompression can reduce memory bandwidth demands. Kernels could also use integer accumulators at higher bitwidths to avoid conversion penalties while keeping on-chip register pressure low.

From the compiler and scheduling perspective, a suite of optimizations can be applied. Operator fusion (fusing quantization/dequantization, activation, and matrix multiply into a single kernel) reduces memory traffic and kernel launch overhead. Tensorization—mapping frequently occurring sub-blocks to highly-optimized microkernels—can be tuned to the exact bit patterns of ternary weights. Autotuning can discover optimal block sizes and layouts for a particular GPU or accelerator. Finally, lowering passes that are aware of ternary and low-bit primitives can produce code that exploits hardware vector instructions and avoids unnecessary widening conversions back to FP16.

All of these directions are consistent with the paper's observation that the current 2-bit kernels are a baseline and "there is still room for optimization." Realizing these opportunities likely yields additional speedups, lower energy-to-solution, and even higher throughput beyond the 2.4×–4.1× latency and 8.9× throughput wins already reported.

# Summary: compounding gains as scale increases

Putting the pieces together, BitNet b1.58 produces system-level wins that compound with model scale and sequence length. Three mechanisms drive this compounding behavior:

1. Compute substitution: replacing FP16 multiply–accumulates with ternary and integer accumulation reduces arithmetic energy dramatically (71.4× for matmul on 7nm) and reduces per-op latency.

2. Memory compression: reducing weight precision to 1.58 bits (and activations to 8 bits) shrinks memory footprint and bandwidth needs, which yields larger practical memory multipliers (batch and KV cache) as the relative size of embeddings and other fixed-cost components shrinks with model size.

3. Workload characteristics: larger models and longer contexts are proportionally more matmul- and memory-bound, so any improvement in those domains magnifies end-to-end gains.

These mechanisms explain why measured results show BitNet b1.58: (a) matching FP16 perplexity and end-task performance starting around 3B, (b) delivering 2.4–4.1× latency improvements as model size scales to 70B, (c) reducing GPU memory by 3.3–3.6× in practical runs while still leaving headroom for even larger effective batch/context sizes, (d) cutting arithmetic energy for matrix multiplies by about 71.4× on 7nm processes, and (e) yielding large throughput improvements (11× batchable queries and 8.9× tokens/sec on two A100 80GBs). Finally, the paper highlights that there remains additional room for kernel and compiler optimizations to further amplify these system-level advantages.

A New Scaling Law: Re-thinking Model Size vs. Cost with 1.58-bit LLMs

# Why low-bit changes the performance–cost frontier

The standard view of model scaling treats model size (number of parameters) and training compute as the dominant determinants of performance, while hardware execution cost (latency, memory footprint, energy) is taken as a monotone increasing function of model size. The BitNet b1.58 work forces us to revisit that view: by changing the per-parameter representation from 16-bit floating point (FP16) to ternary weights \{-1, 0, +1\} (effectively 1.58 bits per weight) and using 8-bit activations, the mapping from model size to hardware cost shifts dramatically. This shift creates a new performance–cost frontier in which larger models in 1.58-bit representation can match or beat smaller FP16 models in both application-level performance (perplexity, zero-shot task accuracy) and all major deployment cost axes (latency, memory, energy).

Intuitively, the frontier changes for three reasons that are tightly coupled to the representation change. First, the arithmetic in matrix multiply — the dominant operation in Transformer LLMs — moves from FP16 multiply-adds to largely integer additions for BitNet b1.58. Integer additions cost far less energy per operation than FP16 multiply-adds; the paper reports an arithmetic energy reduction of 71.4 times for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips when measured by the adopted energy model. Second, the memory footprint per parameter shrinks dramatically: ternary weights and 8-bit activations reduce both capacity requirements and bandwidth for weight transfers from DRAM, which often dominate real-world latency because moving data is so costly. Third, because many costs are proportional to the proportion of parameters involved in dense linear layers, the relative benefit grows with model size: as embedding and other non-quantized components become a smaller fraction of total parameters, the advantage from quantizing the bulk of weights compounds.

Taken together, these effects mean that the Pareto-optimal set of design points (model size, precision) changes: some larger 1.58-bit models dominate smaller FP16 models along latency, memory, energy, and performance simultaneously. This is the sense in which BitNet b1.58 “enables a new scaling law” — not a single analytic formula derived from first principles, but an empirical re-mapping of which (size, precision) pairs are cost-effective.

# Empirical equivalences: 13B (1.58-bit) vs. 3B (FP16), 30B vs. 7B, 70B vs. 13B

The paper reports concrete equivalences that operationalize the new scaling law. These equivalences are empirical statements of the form: “A BitNet b1.58 model of size S_b is more efficient (in latency, memory, and energy) than an FP16 model of size S_fp.” The reported equivalences are:

- 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than 3B FP16.
- 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16.
- 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16.

These equivalences should be read as device- and implementation-specific empirical observations (they were measured using FasterTransformer and a 2-bit kernel for inference), but they give a practical capacity-planning rule of thumb: a 1.58-bit model can be scaled up by roughly an order-of-magnitude factor in parameter count while still achieving lower deployment cost than a much smaller FP16 baseline. The precise size ratios vary with the base and target sizes (for the reported points, 13/3 ≈ 4.3, 30/7 ≈ 4.3, 70/13 ≈ 5.4), but the consistent pattern is that larger ternary models cross into dominance over substantially smaller FP16 models.

From an application perspective the equivalences translate into concrete choices. For example, if an FP16 pipeline was budgeted for a 3B model to meet an accuracy target, swapping to a 13B BitNet b1.58 model can yield equal or better accuracy while reducing latency, memory consumption, and energy use — a Pareto improvement.

# Interpreting speed, memory, and energy as co-equal design axes

Deployment decisions are multi-dimensional. Latency (time per output token), memory (device DRAM/SRAM usage and KV cache footprint), and energy (arithmetic plus data movement energy) are all critical. A useful way to think about the new scaling law is to treat these axes as co-equal components of a cost vector C:

C = (L, M, E),

where L is latency, M is memory, and E is energy for a given inference workload. The model designer’s goal is to minimize C while achieving a desired application performance metric P (e.g., perplexity or task accuracy). BitNet b1.58 shifts the feasible region in the (L, M, E) space by moving many previously infeasible or expensive points (large S at low cost) into the feasible region.

Why do all three axes improve together for BitNet b1.58? The reasons are tied to where cost is concentrated in Transformer LLMs:

- Latency L is dominated by the time to execute large dense linear layers and the time to stream weights into compute. The ternary representation enables kernels that avoid FP16 multiplies and reduce memory traffic, yielding measured speed-ups that increase with model size: the paper reports 2.71× faster for 3B, 2.4× faster for a 3.9B model (compared to a 3B FP16 baseline), and up to 4.1× faster for 70B models.
- Memory M drops because weights occupy far fewer bits and activation representations are reduced (8-bit activations), so the required device memory and bandwidth are lower. For the 3B case the measured GPU memory reduction is 3.55×; for 3.9B it is 3.32×.
- Energy E reduces because arithmetic shifts from FP16 multiply-adds to primarily integer additions and cheaper operations. The arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication is estimated to be 71.4× lower on a 7nm process, and because matrix multiplies represent a growing share of overall compute as models scale, the end-to-end energy advantage grows with model size.

Because these axes all improve, BitNet b1.58 yields design points that are strictly preferable in many practical settings. Crucially, these improvements are synergistic: lower memory and bandwidth reduce data-movement energy, and simpler arithmetic reduces both energy and latency.

# Budgeting for deployment: batch size, sequence length, and KV cache

When planning deployment, three concrete resources commonly drive cost: batch size, sequence length (context), and KV cache size. BitNet b1.58 changes how to budget for each.

Batch size. The reduced memory footprint of weights and activations allows larger batch sizes before hitting device memory limits. The paper exemplifies this with a 70B model running on two 80GB A100 GPUs using pipeline parallelism: BitNet b1.58 supported up to 11× the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA 70B baseline, resulting in an 8.9× higher throughput. Practically, this means that if your deployment needs throughput (tokens/sec) for many simultaneous requests, a larger 1.58-bit model can serve much higher aggregate demand on the same hardware.

Sequence length and KV cache. The KV cache for autoregressive decoding stores past key/value activations and therefore scales with sequence length. By reducing activations to 8 bits (from 16 bits), BitNet b1.58 effectively halves the memory per cached token, allowing the same hardware to hold roughly twice the context length before memory saturation. The paper also notes that activations might be further compressed (e.g., to 4 bits) in future work, enabling still longer contexts. Therefore, if your application requires long-context inference (long documents, logs, or dialogues), 1.58-bit models can be a direct enabler of native long-sequence support without exotic memory or sharding strategies.

Overall resource budgeting using BitNet b1.58 follows a different trade-off curve: you may pick a larger model to gain application-level capabilities while keeping batch size, sequence length, or throughput requirements within hardware limits that would be impossible or much more expensive with FP16.

# When to choose 1.58-bit models over smaller FP16 baselines

The paper provides several practical selection guidelines grounded in empirical observations:

1. Match or exceed FP16 performance starting at modest scales. BitNet b1.58 models begin to match full-precision FP16 LLaMA baselines in perplexity and zero-shot task performance starting from a 3B size (when trained with comparable tokens and recipes). Therefore, for any use case where the target FP16 model size is 3B or larger, a BitNet b1.58 alternative is likely to be at least competitive in application quality.

2. Prioritize deployment cost savings at scale. As model size grows, the proportion of compute and memory cost due to dense linear layers grows, amplifying the benefits of ternary weights. If your deployment is constrained by latency, memory capacity, or energy — especially for larger models like 13B, 30B, or 70B — then BitNet b1.58 offers increasingly large multiplicative savings (e.g., 4.1× speed-up at 70B).

3. Need for throughput and concurrency. If your service must handle many concurrent requests (large batch sizes) or you wish to increase throughput on existing hardware, choosing a BitNet b1.58 model can be a straightforward software-level scaling tactic: the 70B b1.58 example supported substantially larger batch sizes and higher throughput than its FP16 counterpart on identical hardware.

4. Long-context applications or edge/mobile deployment. For tasks that require long contexts, BitNet b1.58’s reduction of activation bitwidth directly translates into larger KV caches for the same memory budget. For edge or mobile devices where memory and energy are scarce, the ternary-weight models open the possibility of running larger models locally due to their much lower memory and arithmetic energy footprints.

5. When MoE or inter-chip communication is a bottleneck. For architectures that rely on distributed memory or Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), the reduced weight and activation sizes cut inter-chip communication costs and can reduce the number of devices necessary to deploy a given large model.

Finally, a conservative practical rule is to benchmark. The paper shows that the reported benefits are measured with a particular software stack (FasterTransformer) and a 2-bit kernel for inference; there remains implementation- and hardware-level variance. But the repeated empirical finding is robust: when you are choosing between a smaller FP16 model and a larger BitNet b1.58 model, the 1.58-bit choice often dominates on the combined axes of accuracy, latency, memory, and energy — especially once you reach a few billion parameters.

Concluding remark. The BitNet b1.58 work reframes a familiar trade-off: instead of being forced to choose between smaller-but-cheaper and larger-but-costly models, quantization to ternary weights with 8-bit activations creates a new set of options where larger models can be simultaneously better and cheaper. For practitioners this is a practical scaling law: consider a 1.58-bit model as a viable — and often preferable — alternative to scaling up FP16 capacity, and use the empirical equivalences (13B ≈ 3B FP16, 30B ≈ 7B FP16, 70B ≈ 13B FP16 in deployment efficiency) as concrete starting points for capacity planning.

Training at Token Scale and Generalization

# 2T-token training following StableLM-3B data recipe

A central experiment in the paper is a long-horizon pretraining run of BitNet b1.58 using the same data recipe as StableLM-3B, trained up to $2\ \text{T}$ tokens. The purpose of this experiment is explicit: to test whether a ternary-weight, 1.58-bit LLM trained from scratch can continue to improve and generalize when exposed to a very large token budget, and to compare its downstream zero-shot performance to a state-of-the-art 3B model trained with the same token regime.

Crucially, this training run preserves the key architectural and quantization choices of BitNet b1.58 used elsewhere in the paper: every weight is constrained to \{-1,0,1\} through the absmean rounding-and-clipping procedure, activations are represented in 8-bit, and the model uses the LLaMA-alike components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and bias removal). By matching the StableLM-3B data recipe, the comparison isolates how the ternary-weight BitNet b1.58 behaves under a large token budget relative to a full-precision 3B baseline that used the same data regimen.

# Tasks: Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, ARC-easy

The 2T-token BitNet b1.58 was evaluated in a zero-shot setting on a small suite of established benchmarks: Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC-easy. These tasks were chosen to sample a range of language capabilities (commonsense, physical reasoning, scientific QA, long-context word prediction, and elementary multi-hop question answering), providing a cross-section of downstream behaviours that reflect generalization beyond perplexity on pretraining corpora.

For reporting, tasks that include both accuracy and a normalized accuracy measure were combined by averaging the two metrics, following the procedure in the paper. The reference values for the StableLM 3B baseline under the same $2\ \text{T}$ token regime were taken from the StableLM technical report, enabling a direct apples-to-apples comparison in the paper between an established 3B FP baseline and the 1.58-bit BitNet variant trained to the same token scale.

# Empirical gains over StableLM-3B across tasks

The paper reports that BitNet b1.58, after training to $2\ \text{T}$ tokens, attains superior zero-shot performance over StableLM 3B on every task in the evaluated suite. Although specific numeric scores are reported in the paper’s Table~\ref{tab:2t}, the key empirical takeaway is qualitative and robust: ternary-weight models trained at large token scale can not only close the gap to full-precision models but surpass them on standard zero-shot evaluations when trained under the same data recipe and token budget.

This outcome demonstrates two important points emphasized in the work. First, the 1.58-bit constraint does not prevent the model from absorbing and distilling large amounts of data into useful generalizable knowledge. Second, the inclusion of a zero weight value (the ternary aspect) is effective in practice: it yields a modeling capacity that benefits from extended training on many tokens and transfers to improved performance on diverse downstream tasks.

# Token scaling effects for ternary-weight models

The number of training tokens is a fundamental axis along which LLM performance scales. In this paper, the $2\ \text{T}$ token experiment is presented as a direct test of token-scale generalization for a low-bit, ternary-weight model family. The empirical finding is that BitNet b1.58 continues to improve with large additional token exposure and that those improvements translate into stronger zero-shot behaviour compared to a full-precision baseline trained to the same token count.

Intuitively, several properties of the BitNet b1.58 design make it amenable to token scaling. The ternary weight set \{-1,0,1\} includes an explicit "feature filtering" capability through zeros, allowing the network to represent sparse, selective connections even with severely constrained weight resolution. When training tokens grow large, the optimization process can refine which features should be emphasized, inverted, or suppressed, and the zero state gives a direct mechanism for suppressing spurious or noisy features. In addition, training from scratch under the absmean quantization scheme lets the scale of weight matrices be adjusted jointly with discrete rounding so that coarse weights still capture the dominant signal directions present in large corpora.

The practical consequence reported in the paper is that token scaling remains an effective lever for improving performance even for 1.58-bit models: more tokens yield better generalization, and those gains are realized at the level of downstream task accuracy.

# Stability and optimization considerations at long training horizons

Training for very long token budgets raises potential stability and optimization challenges, especially for aggressive quantization schemes. The paper documents several architectural and quantization design choices intended to preserve stable training dynamics over long horizons for BitNet b1.58. First, the absmean-based quantization constrains weights relative to their average absolute magnitude before rounding and clipping; this per-matrix scaling keeps the discretized weight magnitudes well conditioned as training proceeds. Second, activations are scaled to a symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token (rather than introducing asymmetric zero-points), which simplifies numerical behavior and implementation while avoiding biases introduced by asymmetric quantization. Third, adopting LLaMA-style components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and no biases) is consistent with architectures known to train reliably at large scale; this choice avoids introducing extra sources of instability that might be exacerbated by quantization.

Empirically, the paper shows that these combined modeling and quantization choices are sufficient to maintain stable improvement across the long $2\ \text{T}$ token training run: BitNet b1.58 not only converges but achieves better downstream zero-shot performance than the StableLM 3B baseline trained on the same data recipe and token budget. This observation suggests that carefully designed quantization-aware architectures and scaling rules can mitigate the usual optimization pitfalls of low-bit models over long training horizons.

# Implications and summary

The $2\ \text{T}$ token experiment in the paper provides concrete evidence that 1.58-bit LLMs can scale in the token dimension and achieve superior generalization relative to a same-sized, full-precision baseline trained on the same data recipe and token budget. The ternary weights’ explicit zero state (feature filtering), the absmean quantization, symmetric per-token activation scaling, and the LLaMA-alike architectural choices together enable stable optimization across long training runs. Practically, this means that low-bit models like BitNet b1.58 can be trained to large token budgets to achieve or exceed the generalization of well-known FP baselines, while retaining the efficiency benefits (memory, latency, energy) emphasized throughout the paper.

These findings support the broader thesis of the work: that 1.58-bit LLMs are not merely an inference-time compression trick, but a viable modeling family that scales with tokens and generalizes at least as well as competing FP models when trained responsibly at scale. The successful $2\ \text{T}$ token run therefore strengthens the case for continued research into low-bit training recipes, scaling laws for discrete-weight models, and system/hardware efforts that exploit these efficiency gains.

Implications, Applications, and Future Directions

# MoE with 1.58-bit: reducing memory and inter-chip communication

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures route different tokens to different expert subnetworks so that an overall very large model can be executed with lower per-token FLOPs. This property makes MoE attractive because it reduces the compute FLOPs required per token. In practice, however, MoE suffers from two important system-level costs: large memory footprint (because many experts must be stored) and substantial inter-chip communication (because activations routed among experts on different devices must be transmitted). The BitNet b1.58 design directly addresses both of these bottlenecks by shrinking the size of the model parameters and the activation footprint.

Because every weight in BitNet b1.58 is ternary \{-1,0,+1\}, the information content per parameter is \(\log_2(3)\approx 1.585\) bits rather than 16 bits (FP16) or even 4 bits. This reduction in parameter size translates to lower DRAM capacity needs and lower bandwidth for streaming weights into on-chip SRAM. For MoE, where the number of experts multiplies memory needs, this compression can reduce the number of physical devices required to host all experts. Fewer devices means fewer network hops and less inter-chip communication for routed activations.

The paper emphasizes that the reduced memory footprint also cuts activation transfer overheads. If an MoE deployment previously required multiple chips simply to store experts, the same MoE with 1.58-bit weights may fit within a single chip (or far fewer chips), at which point the inter-chip transfer of activations becomes negligible. In short, the BitNet b1.58 recipe preserves MoE’s computational efficiency while materially reducing two of its major deployment costs — memory capacity and activation communication — thereby making MoE more practical in real-world systems.

# Long-context inference: 8-bit activations and potential 4-bit KV caches

One of the largest practical obstacles to long-context LLM inference is the memory cost of the key/value (KV) cache: as sequence length grows, the KV cache grows linearly and can quickly exhaust on-chip memory. The paper points out a straightforward systems-level win for BitNet b1.58: activations are stored in 8-bit form rather than 16-bit, immediately halving the per-element memory needed for KV storage relative to FP16-based models.

Halving the bit-width of activations has a direct, quantifiable effect on the maximum context length supported for a fixed memory budget. If the KV cache dominated memory consumption, moving from 16-bit activations to 8-bit activations can approximately double the possible context length on the same hardware. The paper also suggests a promising future direction: lossless or further compression of the KV cache to 4 bits or lower for 1.58-bit LLMs. While this is left as future work, the important point is that the BitNet b1.58 design already reduces activation precision in a way that materially improves support for long sequences.

Operationally, BitNet b1.58 also adjusts activation quantization so that activations are scaled to the symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) per token; this eliminates zero-point complications and simplifies system-level optimizations for storing and transmitting KV tensors. In short, the combination of ternary weights and lower-precision activations directly addresses the dominant memory bottleneck for long-context inference and opens a clear path to even longer sequences with additional activation-compression work.

# Edge and mobile deployment: CPU-friendliness and on-device LLMs

A major motivation for BitNet b1.58 is to reduce the cost of running LLMs in constrained environments such as edge devices and mobile phones. Two properties of BitNet b1.58 make it particularly favorable for such deployments. First, the ternary-weight design eliminates most floating-point multiplications during matrix multiplication, replacing them with integer additions. Integer arithmetic is typically less resource-hungry and often more efficient on commodity CPUs than floating-point heavy operations. Second, the large reduction in model memory — both in terms of parameter capacity and activation bandwidth — substantially lowers the memory and energy requirements that are the principal barriers to on-device LLMs.

The paper highlights that BitNet b1.58 is "more friendly to CPU devices," meaning that typical edge processors, which may lack high-throughput FP16 matrix units, can execute these models more effectively because the dominant operations are low-precision integer adds. Together, these factors imply that BitNet b1.58 can enable new classes of on-device applications that were previously impractical due to memory, thermal, or power constraints. The paper stops short of full on-device end-to-end evaluations, but the empirical reductions in memory, latency, and estimated energy suggest a viable path from cloud-only LLMs toward local, private, and low-latency deployments on edge hardware.

# Specialized hardware opportunities (e.g., LPUs) for 1–2 bit arithmetic

BitNet b1.58 is not only a software recipe; it also defines a new computation paradigm that invites hardware innovation. The fundamental observation is that the core multiply–accumulate (MAC) operations in a Transformer can be reinterpreted when weights are ternary and activations are low-precision. In BitNet b1.58 the dominant work becomes integer addition (and small-integer accumulation) rather than FP16 multiply–add. That shift changes which hardware structures are bottlenecks: area and energy that used to be devoted to FP multipliers and wide FP adders can instead be repurposed for massive banks of very cheap integer adders, narrower accumulators, and specialized memory hierarchies optimized for low-bitwidth streaming.

The paper references industry examples such as Groq and makes an explicit call for new chips optimized for the 1-bit (and 1–2 bit) arithmetic regime. Such chips could incorporate lightweight Processing Elements (PEs), reduced-precision SRAM segments, and communication fabrics tailored to the smaller data widths and simpler arithmetic. The energy model quoted in the paper estimates that BitNet b1.58 saves 71.4× arithmetic operation energy for matrix multiplication on 7 nm chips relative to FP16 LLaMA; this dramatic projected reduction highlights the potential returns to hardware specialization.

In practice, specialized hardware need not be monolithic. Even on general-purpose accelerators, there is opportunity to design micro-kernels or microcode that exploit ternary weights and 8-bit activations to reduce DRAM bandwidth and arithmetic energy. At the same time, purpose-built devices could push these gains further by re-architecting memory hierarchies, widening integer datapaths, and removing unused floating-point machinery.

# Software ecosystem: serving stacks, compilers, and kernel libraries

Realizing the system-level potential of BitNet b1.58 requires a software stack that knows how to exploit ternary weights and low-precision activations. The paper demonstrates that BitNet b1.58 can integrate with popular open-source software such as Hugging Face-style tooling, vLLM, and llama.cpp by adopting LLaMA-alike components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and bias removal). This choice of compatible building blocks lowers friction for adoption.

Beyond model-level compatibility, high-performance inference depends on optimized kernels and runtime systems. In the experiments the authors used FasterTransformer and integrated a 2-bit kernel from Ladder to measure latency and memory, showing substantial speedups and lower memory use. These integrations are examples of how kernel libraries and inference runtimes must evolve: new quantized matrix–vector and matrix–matrix kernels, streaming-friendly KV cache layouts, and per-token symmetric activation scaling are all necessary pieces.

Compilers and deployment stacks also have a role: they must lower models into these new kernels while preserving numerical behavior, manage mixed-precision regions (for example, embeddings remaining in full precision), and orchestrate memory transfers to exploit smaller weight and activation footprints. Finally, serving layers should expose trade-offs (e.g., memory vs. throughput) and select kernel variants adaptively. In short, the software ecosystem — including compilers, optimized kernel libraries, and serving stacks — is a key enabling layer that translates the BitNet b1.58 model-level benefits into real-world throughput, latency, and energy wins.

# Open challenges: quantizer design, activation precision, robustness, and evaluation

While BitNet b1.58 demonstrates promising results, the paper also highlights several open research and engineering challenges that future work must address.

Quantizer design. The paper uses an "absmean" quantization function that rescales a weight matrix by its average absolute value \(\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|\) and then applies a clipped rounding:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\bigl(\tfrac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\bigr),\quad
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))).
\]
This simple scheme works well empirically for BitNet b1.58, but it leaves open many questions. How should \(\gamma\) be estimated and updated during training? When and where is per-channel versus per-tensor scaling better? How do alternative rounding strategies (stochastic rounding, learned thresholds, or soft relaxations during backpropagation) affect convergence and final performance? Designing quantizers that trade off ease of implementation, training stability, and final accuracy remains an active area.

Activation precision and KV compression. The paper shows that moving activations to 8 bits already yields large system gains and proposes that KV caches could be compressed to 4 bits or lower as future work. But the interactions between activation quantization, transformer dynamics, and long-context behavior are complex. Determining when activations can be compressed losslessly (or with acceptable loss) and how to do so across network layers, attention heads, and tokens will require careful empirical and theoretical study.

Robustness and evaluation. The experiments in the paper demonstrate parity with FP16 baselines on standard perplexity and a set of zero-shot tasks starting at the 3B scale, but robustness across distribution shifts, adversarial inputs, or downstream fine-tuning settings remains to be mapped out. Ternary weight models may react differently to pruning, fine-tuning, or adversarial perturbations; evaluating those behaviors is important for safe deployment.

System-level validation. The paper reports energy estimates (e.g., 71.4× lower arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication) and shows real latency and memory measurements using optimized kernels. Still, full end-to-end system validation across a wider range of hardware, workloads (e.g., chat-style multi-turn interactions with KV cache growth), and deployment patterns is a necessary next step. The interactions between embedding precision (which remains full precision in these experiments), system pipelines, and mixed-precision regions need detailed profiling and benchmarking.

Generalization across scales and recipes. The authors found that BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA baselines starting at 3B when trained on the same token budget; they also show strong performance when training to 2T tokens following a StableLM recipe. Nevertheless, faithfully reproducing these results across different datasets, tokenization schemes, or training hyperparameters requires further confirmation. Research is needed to determine the best practices (recipes) for training new BitNet models at different scales and for different application domains.

In summary, while BitNet b1.58 opens many promising directions — from better MoE deployments and longer context windows to on-device inference and hardware co-design — realizing the full potential requires work on quantizers, activation compression, robustness testing, system benchmarks, and software tooling. The paper lays out both the immediate system-level wins and the research agenda that follows, inviting a broad community effort across machine learning, systems, and hardware design.
