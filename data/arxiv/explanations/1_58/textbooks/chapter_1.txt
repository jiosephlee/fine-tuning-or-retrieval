\title{A Textbook about the Paper: The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits}

Chapter 1: The Era of 1-bit LLMs: Motivation and Overview

# Why LLMs are expensive: compute, memory, latency, and energy bottlenecks

Large Language Models (LLMs) have grown dramatically in parameter count and training data, and with that growth has come large costs across multiple system axes. The dominant computational primitive in transformer-style LLMs is dense matrix multiplication (the various linear layers that implement projections in attention and feed‑forward blocks). In common practice these operations are performed in floating point formats such as FP16 or BF16. Floating point matrix multiplication requires both multiplication and addition, and the energy and time cost of these arithmetic operations — together with moving large parameter matrices and activation tensors between memory hierarchies — becomes the dominant contributor to latency, power draw, and monetary cost at scale.

Memory is another tight constraint. Model parameters are typically stored in DRAM and fetched into on‑chip SRAM or accelerator memory (SRAM, caches) for execution. For very large models the weight footprint forces frequent DRAM transfers; DRAM bandwidth and access latency become bottlenecks for throughput. Increasing on‑chip SRAM to avoid DRAM transfers quickly becomes prohibitively expensive, so reducing parameter precision or size is a practical lever to reduce both the capacity and the bandwidth required.

Latency and throughput are tightly linked to both computation and memory behavior. For inference the primary cost is producing output tokens: every additional cycle of matrix multiplication or every stall caused by memory transfers increases per‑token latency and reduces throughput. Finally, energy consumption is critical — not only from a cost and sustainability standpoint, but because many hardware platforms (especially edge and mobile) are power constrained. When the arithmetic units and memory accesses consume the bulk of the energy, reducing the precision or altering the arithmetic to cheaper operations can translate directly into reduced energy per token and higher attainable speeds.

# From FP16/BF16 to low-bit inference: 8-bit, 4-bit, and beyond

A natural response to the costs above has been reducing numerical precision. The industry and research community moved from 32‑bit floating point to 16‑bit formats (FP16/BF16), and then explored lower‑precision inference formats such as 8‑bit integers or mixed formats. Reducing precision saves both storage and bandwidth for weights and activations and can make arithmetic cheaper on hardware that supports efficient low‑precision operations.

Work on 4‑bit inference and other very low‑bit formats has advanced rapidly. These approaches target inference cost reduction by shrinking the per‑parameter storage and replacing a portion of the expensive floating‑point math with cheaper integer operations. The basic idea is that for many tasks the model's functional behavior can be preserved even when weights and activations are represented with fewer bits, provided the quantization is done carefully.

However, going to very low precision is not free: naively quantizing a trained FP16 model to 4 bits or fewer often introduces substantial degradation in perplexity or downstream task performance. This has motivated improved quantization schemes, and sometimes re‑training or fine‑tuning in the low‑precision regime. The trend has therefore progressed from simple post‑training quantization toward methods that adapt the model or its training to operate robustly at low bit widths.

# Limits of post-training quantization (e.g., SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ, QuIP/QuIP-Sharp)

Post‑training quantization (PTQ) is attractive because it can be applied to an existing pretrained model without full re‑training. PTQ packages such as SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ, QuIP and QuIP‑Sharp focus on minimizing the accuracy loss incurred when mapping FP16/BF16 models to lower precision representations. These methods commonly perform per‑tensor or per‑channel scaling, block quantization, and compute-aware calibration on representative data batches to choose quantization parameters that preserve model behavior.

Despite their practical value and wide industry adoption, post‑training quantization approaches are inherently sub‑optimal in the sense that they try to retrofit a high‑precision model into a low‑precision format after the fact. The original weights and internal dynamic ranges were optimized for floating‑point training objectives, not for operation under extreme discretization. As a result PTQ often leaves performance on the table compared with solutions that train directly in low precision or redesign computations for cheap arithmetic. In other words, PTQ is a cost‑effective bridge but not necessarily the best final destination for extreme quantization regimes.

# BitNet lineage and the shift to integer-addition–centric computation

BitNet represents a more radical approach: design the model and training process for a discrete, low‑bit numerical format from scratch rather than compress a floating‑point model after training. The original BitNet proposed replacing standard dense linear layers (implemented with floating‑point multiply–accumulate operations) with versions that use highly quantized weights. In that formulation matrix multiplication could be implemented with integer additions rather than floating‑point multiplications, delivering large savings in arithmetic complexity and energy.

BitNet b1.58 follows this lineage but introduces a practical and powerful extension: ternary weights that take values in \{-1, 0, +1\}. Operationally, these ternary weights allow matrix multiplication to be implemented primarily as integer additions and subtractions (and counts of non‑zeros), rather than FP16 multiply–adds. The reduction from floating‑point multiplications to integer additions dramatically reduces the arithmetic energy cost per multiply–accumulate, and often yields faster kernels on devices that can exploit simple integer arithmetic paths. The BitNet family therefore reframes the computation model of the standard Transformer: instead of optimizing floating‑point matrix multiplication, designers can optimize integer‑addition centric kernels and memory formats tailored to small integers.

# What 1.58 bits means: ternary \{-1, 0, +1\} and \log_2(3) \approx 1.585

Naming a model "1.58‑bit" requires some unpacking: the long‑standing unit "bits per parameter" measures the information capacity of each weight. A weight that can take three distinct values (for example \{-1,0,+1\}) carries \log_2(3) bits of information. In LaTeX notation:

$${\rm bits\ per\ ternary\ weight} \;=\; \log_2(3) \;\approx\; 1.585\ldots$$

Rounded to two decimal places this is approximately $1.58$ bits, which gives the name BitNet b1.58. Conceptually, shifting from a binary \{\-1,+1\} representation (which would be exactly 1 bit) to a ternary representation adds a special neutral or "sparsifying" state 0. The presence of 0 has two important consequences: first, storage per weight remains extremely small compared to FP16; second, the 0 value provides explicit support for feature filtering — the model can effectively zero out some contributions rather than scaling them to a small nonzero value. That sparsifying capability often improves representational flexibility compared with strictly bipolar binary weights, while keeping arithmetic and memory costs very low.

# Quantization mechanics in BitNet b1.58: the absmean rule and activation scaling

BitNet b1.58 constrains weights to the ternary set using a simple and effective quantization rule called the "absmean" quantization function. The function first computes a global scale term $\gamma$ as the mean absolute value of the weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$:

$$
\gamma \;=\; \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|.
$$

The matrix is then scaled by $\gamma + \epsilon$ and each scaled element is rounded and clipped to the discrete set $\{-1,0,1\}$:

$$
\widetilde{W} \;=\; \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Big(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\Big),
$$

where

$$
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) \;=\; \max\big(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))\big).
$$

This per‑matrix scaling keeps the relative pattern of magnitudes within the weight matrix but maps them to a small discrete alphabet anchored by the mean absolute magnitude. Intuitively, $\gamma$ captures a typical magnitude in the matrix; dividing by it normalizes the distribution so that large coefficients map to \(\pm 1\), small ones map to 0, and intermediate ones are rounded to the nearest ternary symbol.

Activations in BitNet b1.58 are quantized separately. Following BitNet's general plan, activations are represented at 8 bits in inference. The paper modifies BitNet's original activation scaling by choosing per‑token scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ rather than shifting into $[0,Q_b]$ and handling zero points. This per‑token symmetric scaling simplifies implementation and system‑level optimization and has negligible perfomance impact in the experiments reported.

# Architectural and software design: LLaMA‑alike components and BitLinear

To ease integration with open‑source LLM ecosystems and to be a practical drop‑in for current tooling, BitNet b1.58 adopts a LLaMA‑like Transformer backbone and a small set of component choices that have become standard in recent models. Specifically, it uses RMSNorm for normalization, SwiGLU activations in feed‑forward blocks, rotary positional embeddings, and removes all bias terms. The major architectural change is that standard dense linear layers (implemented by typical implementations of nn.Linear) are replaced with BitLinear, which produces ternary weights and uses the integer‑addition centric computation model.

This design choice enables BitNet b1.58 to be used with popular software such as Hugging Face tooling, vLLM, or llama.cpp with minimal modification. Embedding layers remain full precision in the reported experiments, but the bulk of linear operations inside the Transformer blocks are captured by BitLinear with ternary weights and 8‑bit activations.

# Contributions of BitNet b1.58: matching FP16 accuracy at lower cost; a new scaling law; hardware implications

BitNet b1.58 makes several concrete contributions that address the cost–performance trade‑off for LLMs:

1. Match FP16 performance from moderate model sizes. When trained from scratch on the same data and with the same recipe (100B tokens on RedPajama), BitNet b1.58 matches the full precision (FP16/BF16) LLaMA baselines in both validation perplexity and zero‑shot downstream tasks, starting from model sizes around 3B parameters. In the experiments reported, BitNet b1.58 at 3B matches FP16 perplexity and demonstrates comparable zero‑shot accuracies; a 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model even outperforms a 3B FP16 LLaMA model while being cheaper to run.

2. Substantial runtime, memory and energy savings. The ternary representation and integer‑addition based computation yield real system benefits. For example, BitNet b1.58 at 3B is reported as 2.71× faster and consumes 3.55× less GPU memory than the equivalent FP16 LLaMA baseline in the FasterTransformer inference measurements cited. At larger scales the advantage grows: a 70B BitNet b1.58 model is reported to be 4.1× faster than the 70B FP16 LLaMA baseline. Estimated arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication favors BitNet heavily: using the energy model referenced in the paper, BitNet b1.58 saves 71.4× arithmetic operation energy for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips compared to FP16.

3. A new cost‑aware scaling law mapping low‑bit and full‑precision model sizes. The empirical results suggest practical equivalences between BitNet b1.58 and FP16 models in terms of inference cost (latency, memory, energy). The reported examples include: 13B BitNet b1.58 being more efficient than 3B FP16, 30B b1.58 being more efficient than 7B FP16, and 70B b1.58 being more efficient than 13B FP16. These correspondences imply that deploying a larger ternary model can be strictly better in both performance and cost than deploying a smaller full‑precision model.

4. Practical throughput improvements for multi‑GPU setups. Throughput experiments with a 70B model on two 80GB A100 GPUs using pipeline parallelism show that BitNet b1.58 supports up to 11× the batch size of the FP16 baseline under the same memory budget, yielding an overall 8.9× higher throughput when increasing batch sizes to GPU limits. This is a concrete gain for serving scenarios that depend on batching to maximize device utilization.

5. Training and generalization at scale. The paper reports that a BitNet b1.58 model trained for 2T tokens following a strong data recipe outperforms the StableLM 3B baseline trained with the same token budget on a suite of zero‑shot tasks. This indicates that ternary models trained from scratch can generalize strongly at large training budgets.

6. System and hardware implications. Beyond immediate inference gains, BitNet b1.58 opens up a new computation paradigm where the primary arithmetic is integer additions and subtractions rather than floating‑point multiply–adds. This suggests novel opportunities for hardware and system co‑design: specialized low‑precision units (LPUs), memory systems optimized for packing ternary weights, and accelerators that exploit the sparsifying 0 state to reduce both arithmetic and memory traffic. The paper references examples like Groq as evidence that hardware specialized for LLMs is feasible and argues for further exploration of hardware tailored to 1‑bit/1.58‑bit models.

# Practical implications: memory, KV cache, MoE and edge deployment

The small per‑weight footprint and lower precision activations have multiple downstream system effects. First, reduced model memory allows more of a model (or more experts in a Mixture‑of‑Experts architecture) to fit on fewer devices, which reduces inter‑device communication and can make MoE architectures more practical to deploy. Second, KV caches used for autoregressive decoding are a major consumer of activation memory. BitNet b1.58 stores activations at 8 bits in the reported setup; halving activation precision effectively doubles the feasible context length for the same memory budget, and the paper suggests that activations could be further compressed (to 4 bits or less) in future work. Finally, the lower power and memory costs make these models attractive for edge and mobile devices where CPU friendliness and low energy are crucial.

# Summary and takeaways

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates a path beyond post‑training quantization: design and train LLMs to operate natively with ternary weights \{-1,0,+1\} (about $\log_2(3)\approx 1.585$ bits per weight) and 8‑bit activations. The ternary representation enables sparsifying zeros in weights, improving modeling flexibility over bipolar binary schemes, while enabling integer‑addition centric matrix multiplication that drastically reduces arithmetic energy and delivers sizable throughput and memory advantages in inference. Empirically, BitNet b1.58 matches or exceeds FP16 baselines from moderate model sizes onward, and the cost scaling implied by the experiments yields practical equivalences (for latency, memory, and energy) between larger ternary models and much smaller full‑precision ones. The approach invites new hardware and system designs that natively exploit the 1‑bit/1.58‑bit computation paradigm, and it suggests that the future of cost‑efficient, high‑performing LLMs may lie in architectures and training recipes that are quantization‑native rather than retrofit.
