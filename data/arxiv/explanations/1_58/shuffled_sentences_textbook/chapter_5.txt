The paper states that BitNet b1.58 is "trained from scratch, with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations," but it does not enumerate every training-time representation choice such as the precision used for optimizer state (momentum, Adam moments), gradient accumulation buffers, or embedding tables. This choice simplifies hardware implementation (no zero-point handling) and aligns well with the ternary-weight design where symmetric positive/negative representations are natural. The empirical result reported in the paper is that — when trained from scratch on the same data budget and with matched model size — BitNet b1.58 can match FP16 baselines in perplexity and downstream tasks starting at roughly 3B parameters, while offering large efficiency benefits in memory, latency, and energy. Chapter 5: Training BitNet b1.58 From Scratch   One of the central design choices for BitNet b1.58 is to train the model from scratch at low precision rather than to take a pretrained full-precision (FP16/BF16) model and post-hoc quantize it. The authors attribute “stronger modeling capability” to this explicit feature filtering capability, and they find in experiments that ternary weights help close the performance gap relative to FP16 baselines. \] This scale $\gamma$ puts the weight elements onto a common relative scale so that rounding to \{-1,0,1\} produces meaningful preserved structure (and the added $\epsilon$ guards against division by zero). The paper explicitly removes biases across the model. For weights, the paper adopts an "absmean" quantization strategy: before quantizing to \{-1,0,+1\} the raw weight matrix $W$ is normalized by its mean absolute value. To enable end-to-end optimization, training schemes must adopt a strategy that provides meaningful gradient-like signals through those operations. BitNet b1.58 therefore adopts the low-bit regime as a first-class design constraint and trains directly with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. Quantization operations such as rounding and clipping are piecewise-constant or non-differentiable, which breaks the usual backpropagation chain. Maintaining parity in model size and token budget is essential: when claiming that a low-bit architecture matches a full-precision baseline, the only dimension permitted to change for an efficiency claim should be the representation and resulting system/resource cost. Instead, the activations are scaled per token to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$. The paper follows this principle and reports perplexity and zero-shot downstream tasks under those matched conditions. The paper frames these as "LLaMA-alike components" and uses them both for behavioral stability and for ease of integration into popular inference stacks. For the longer token study, BitNet b1.58 was trained with 2T tokens following the StableLM-3B data recipe, matching the large-data recipe used by the comparison model. Together, these architectural choices are the stable ingredients the authors chose to pair with the ternary-weight, low-precision design so that training remains robust and the model integrates well with open tooling. For activations, per-token scaling to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ is analogous in spirit: instead of applying a single global scale across an entire batch or sequence, each token's activations are rescaled so the quantizer operates around zero with symmetric bounds. Empirically, the paper reports that BitNet b1.58 3B matches FP16 3B in perplexity and downstream tasks; larger BitNet b1.58 models (e.g., 3.9B, 7B, 13B, 30B, 70B) not only maintain competitive accuracy but also yield larger relative gains in latency, memory usage, and estimated arithmetic energy compared to FP16 baselines. RMSNorm is a scale-based normalization without learned bias terms; it is a lightweight alternative to LayerNorm and fits naturally with the bias-free design. To evaluate whether BitNet b1.58 can match full-precision performance, the authors trained BitNet b1.58 models and reproduced FP16 LLaMA-style models with matched model sizes and the same data budget. The paper argues that per-token symmetric scaling "gets rid of the zero-point quantization," is more convenient for implementation and system-level optimization, and has negligible impact on performance in their experiments. Rotary embeddings give a parameter-free way to incorporate position, avoiding additional learned parameters that would need special quantization treatment. An important methodological point emphasized in the paper is fairness of comparison. Because the paper emphasizes the absmean scaling and per-token symmetric activation scaling, the quantizers themselves are designed to be numerically well-conditioned (scales avoid saturation) and to preserve signed information (symmetry around zero). The paper demonstrates empirically that BitNet b1.58 can be trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations, which implies that some form of gradient propagation through quantizers (or an equivalent training-time surrogate) was employed so that parameter updates remain informative despite hard quantization. BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that it is practical to train LLMs from scratch with ternary weights and 8-bit activations and to achieve parity with FP16 baselines starting from medium-scale model sizes. Thus, the parity appearing from around 3B parameters is an empirical outcome of (a) the additional expressivity introduced by ternary weights with zeros, and (b) the scaling properties of neural networks where higher-capacity models can compensate for per-parameter representational constraints. The paper documents the crucial implementation details that made this possible: an absmean weight quantizer that normalizes by mean absolute weight magnitude, symmetric per-token activation scaling to remove zero-point complications, and the adoption of LLaMA-style architectural choices (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE) together with bias removal. The empirical scaling behavior reported in the paper shows a consistent trend: the performance gap between BitNet b1.58 and FP16 LLaMA narrows as model size increases, and parity in perplexity and downstream zero-shot performance is observed starting at about 3B parameters. SwiGLU (a gated linear unit variant with the SwiSH activation) provides a modern, high-performance MLP nonlinearity that remains compatible with quantized weights and low-bit activations. The paper motivates this choice by noting that post-training quantization is widely used in industry but is sub-optimal: earlier work on low-bit inference (e.g., 4-bit and 2-bit post-training quantization) reduces memory and compute but cannot fully recover the modeling capacity or efficiency possible when the architecture and training procedure are designed for low-bit weights and activations from the beginning. Because the quantization is present during forward and backward passes throughout training, the model can learn to make representational and algorithmic choices that exploit ternary weights (including the extra expressivity provided by zeros) rather than attempting to approximate a dense FP solution after the fact. A key implementation detail for BitNet b1.58 is how activations are quantized. The inclusion of 0 increases modeling flexibility without substantially changing the efficient computation paradigm: zeros permit sparse-like behavior in the weight matrix that can block noise and selectively gate features. The paper does not provide low-level details of the specific gradient estimator or straight-through mechanism it used to propagate learning signals through the non-differentiable rounding and clipping operations. Because the paper reports standard pretraining experiments (e.g., trained on the RedPajama dataset for 100B tokens and on a 2T-token run for a 3B-sized model), it implicitly uses a training pipeline that preserves enough numerical fidelity for long runs, but the text does not list which elements (optimizer states or shadow weights) were kept in higher precision.
