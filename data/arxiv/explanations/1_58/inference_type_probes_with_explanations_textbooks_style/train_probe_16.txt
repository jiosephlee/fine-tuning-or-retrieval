Chapter Title: Stability in Absmean Quantization: Why BitNet b1.58 Divides by $\gamma + \epsilon$

# Overview of BitNet b1.58 and Its Quantization Strategy

BitNet b1.58 is a Transformer-based Large Language Model whose central design choice is to constrain all weights to a ternary set, specifically $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This results in an effective precision of 1.58 bits per parameter. The architecture replaces traditional dense linear layers, denoted as $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$, with specialized $\mathrm{BitLinear}$ modules and trains the model from scratch using 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. In addition to its architectural choices, BitNet b1.58 adopts components commonly used in LLaMA-like models, such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and the removal of biases, allowing seamless integration with widely used tooling.

A key mechanism making ternary weights workable in practice is the quantization function that projects full-precision weights onto the ternary set. This quantization must provide two properties simultaneously: it must be simple and efficient to implement, and it must be stable across the full training process and deployment environments. BitNet b1.58 achieves this through an “absmean” scaling followed by rounding and clipping, applied to each weight matrix. The core transformation is
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where $\gamma$ is the average absolute value of the entries of $W$ and $\epsilon$ is an added constant in the denominator. The $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ operator rounds each entry to the nearest integer and clips it to the interval $[-1, 1]$, yielding values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$:
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\!\big(a, \min\!\big(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\big)\big),
\]
and
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
The role of $\gamma$ is to normalize each matrix by its average scale so that rounding into $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ aligns with the distribution of the weights, while $\epsilon$ is added to the denominator to guard against division by zero. This chapter explains the intuition behind this quantization, the necessity of the $\epsilon$ term, and how these design choices produce both modeling strength and numerical reliability.

# Absmean Scaling: Normalizing Before Ternary Rounding

The decision to first scale a weight matrix $W$ by its average absolute value $\gamma$ before rounding and clipping is crucial to producing meaningful ternary weights. Without such scaling, the raw magnitudes in $W$ could be arbitrarily large or small, making a direct ternary rounding unreliable. By dividing by $\gamma$, BitNet b1.58 normalizes $W$ into a matrix whose entries are typically of order one in magnitude. This makes the subsequent rounding to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ align with the statistical structure of $W$ rather than its absolute scale.

To see why this normalization is effective, consider that $\gamma$ averages $\{|W_{ij}|\}$ across all entries in the matrix. If most entries are small compared to a few large outliers, the average will reflect the typical scale while maintaining sensitivity to bulk behavior. After dividing by $\gamma$, entries larger than the average in magnitude are more likely to be rounded to $\pm 1$, while entries smaller than the average are more likely to be rounded to $0$. This induces a form of feature selection or filtering: the inclusion of the $0$ value enables the model to explicitly suppress unimportant features by zeroing their weights, while passing important features through with $\pm 1$. This property strengthens the modeling capability compared to binary-only choices like $\{-1, +1\}$ because the ternary set can discard weak signals rather than forcing them into a positive or negative contribution.

The subsequent rounding-and-clipping operator, $\mathrm{RoundClip}(\cdot)$, finalizes this projection by snapping normalized values to the nearest integer and ensuring no entry exceeds the bounds $[-1, 1]$. The clipping is particularly important because normalization does not guarantee every entry lies within $[-1, 1]$. When values fall outside this interval, clipping preserves the ternary constraint and prevents out-of-bound representations that would violate the 1.58-bit design.

# The Role of $\gamma$ and the Need for $\epsilon$

The scaling factor $\gamma$ is defined as
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
\]
where $n$ and $m$ are the dimensions of $W$. This definition reveals a simple but critical fact: $\gamma = 0$ if and only if $W$ is the all-zero matrix. In this edge case, dividing by $\gamma$ would involve division by zero, producing undefined behavior. To prevent this, BitNet b1.58 adds $\epsilon$ in the denominator:
\[
\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}.
\]
The addition of $\epsilon$ thus guards against division by zero. When $\gamma > 0$, the presence of $\epsilon$ has negligible effect on the normalized values because the denominator is dominated by $\gamma$. When $\gamma = 0$, however, the denominator becomes $\epsilon$, and the expression $\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}$ reduces to $\frac{W}{\epsilon}$. If $W$ is indeed all zeros, this yields a zero matrix after scaling, and the subsequent rounding-and-clipping produces a matrix of zeros, consistent with the ternary constraint. The operation remains well-defined, and no numerical error is introduced.

This guard is essential for maintaining stability in the quantization pipeline across all possible states of $W$. During training, weight matrices may transiently approach or reach near-zero averages due to optimization dynamics, regularization, or architectural constraints that produce sparse or zero-valued blocks. Without $\epsilon$, such states would trigger undefined divisions, potentially causing failures in the training loop or corrupting model parameters. With $\epsilon$, the quantization function behaves consistently across all cases, ensuring robustness without changing the intended behavior when $\gamma$ is nonzero.

# Intuition for Stability: What Goes Wrong Without $\epsilon$

To understand the importance of $\epsilon$ from a numerical standpoint, consider the behavior of the expression $\frac{W}{\gamma}$ as $\gamma \to 0$. If $\gamma$ were allowed to be exactly zero, the division would be undefined. If $\gamma$ were extremely small but positive, the division would produce extremely large magnitudes in $\frac{W}{\gamma}$ even for modest values of $W$, leading to saturation after rounding and clipping. This would push most entries to $\pm 1$, collapsing the model’s ability to use $0$ for feature filtering and potentially degrading performance.

By adding $\epsilon$, BitNet b1.58 ensures a controlled lower bound on the denominator. In the exact-zero case ($\gamma = 0$), the scaled matrix remains at a finite magnitude, and since $W$ is zero, it remains zero. In near-zero cases ($\gamma$ very small), the denominator does not become arbitrarily tiny, limiting the risk of unintended saturation. The result is more predictable and stable behavior of the quantization function across training and inference, enabling the model to exploit the full ternary range.

In short, $\epsilon$ enforces a safety net: it guarantees the denominator is never zero and never vanishingly small, preserving both numerical soundness and the modeling benefits of ternary quantization.

# How Ternary Quantization Interacts with Activations

BitNet b1.58 also quantizes activations, following the implementation used in BitNet with an important simplification: activations are scaled to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token, and the design explicitly omits zero-point quantization. This choice is motivated by convenience and simplicity for both implementation and system-level optimization, and experiments indicate negligible effects on performance.

The relationship between ternary weights and quantized activations is complementary. Ternary weights are produced by the absmean normalization and rounding described earlier, while activations are constrained to a symmetric range without a separate zero-point. This symmetry aligns well with the ternary weight set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, allowing computations (particularly matrix multiplications) to be realized largely with integer additions. This reduces energy consumption and memory bandwidth requirements compared to full-precision baselines. The simplification of activation scaling further streamlines the pipeline, enabling efficient integration with the specialized kernels that support BitNet b1.58.

Within this design, the $\epsilon$ in the weight quantization step plays a foundational role in ensuring the weight projection is always defined. It thus indirectly supports the overall computation paradigm by maintaining the integrity of the weight normalization stage, which underlies the efficient integer arithmetic used in inference.

# Practical Consequences for Training and Inference

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that ternary weights can match the perplexity and end-task performance of full-precision baselines starting from 3B model sizes, while being significantly more cost-effective in latency, memory, throughput, and energy consumption. These results hinge on a reliable quantization mechanism. The inclusion of $\epsilon$ in the denominator of the absmean scaling is a small but vital detail ensuring that the quantization remains well-defined and numerically stable across model sizes and training regimes.

The practical consequences of this stability are far-reaching:

First, training from scratch with 1.58-bit weights requires quantization to be integrated throughout the optimization loop. Any undefined operation would halt or corrupt training. The $\epsilon$ term avoids such failures by guaranteeing a nonzero denominator, even for matrices that become zero on average.

Second, during inference, weights are repeatedly transferred and recomputed across accelerators, and activations are quantized per token to symmetric ranges. The simple and robust form of the weight quantization ensures that the ternary representation is maintained faithfully. This supports the model’s efficient computation paradigm, in which matrix multiplication involves almost no multiplication and consists primarily of integer addition, greatly reducing energy costs and facilitating faster computation.

Third, the broader gains reported—such as improved throughput and reduced memory footprint compared to FP16 baselines—depend on the reliability of every step in the quantization path. The guard against division by zero is therefore not merely a mathematical nicety; it is a design choice that underpins the reproducibility and robustness of the 1.58-bit workflow.

# Summary

BitNet b1.58 achieves ternary weights via an absmean quantization function, normalizing each weight matrix by its average absolute value $\gamma$ and then applying rounding and clipping to produce entries in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This normalization aligns rounding decisions with the typical magnitude of the weights, enabling explicit feature filtering through the inclusion of $0$ and preserving the model’s expressive power under a highly compressed parameterization.

A crucial element of this quantization is the addition of $\epsilon$ in the denominator, yielding $\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}$. Since $\gamma$ is the average of absolute weights, it equals zero exactly when $W$ is the zero matrix. The added $\epsilon$ guards against division by zero, ensuring the quantization function is always defined. Beyond the exact-zero case, $\epsilon$ also limits excessive magnitudes when $\gamma$ is very small, improving numerical stability and preventing unintended saturation to $\pm 1$.

Together with symmetric per-token activation scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ and the LLaMA-alike architectural components, this quantization scheme enables BitNet b1.58 to deliver performance on par with full-precision baselines while offering significant gains in latency, memory, throughput, and energy efficiency. The inclusion of $\epsilon$ is therefore a core stabilizing feature that helps make the 1.58-bit computation paradigm practical and robust.
