Chapter Title: Understanding Absmean Quantization in BitNet b1.58 and the Role of Average Absolute Weight

# The Context of 1.58-bit Large Language Models

Large Language Models traditionally rely on full-precision floating-point parameters, such as FP16 or BF16, which make inference expensive in terms of latency, memory, and energy consumption. A different path is established by architectures like BitNet, which reframe the core computation from floating-point multiplications to simpler integer additions. This is especially impactful because the fundamental limit to compute performance on many chips is power, and reducing the energy cost of arithmetic operations can translate directly into faster computation.

BitNet b1.58 extends this idea by constraining every parameter to lie in the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This change defines a 1.58-bit representation and introduces a new computation paradigm that removes most multiplications from matrix multiplication, leaning on integer addition instead. The model is trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations, and adopts LLaMA-alike components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and removal of all biases. In addition to efficiency gains in latency, memory, throughput, and energy, BitNet b1.58 improves modeling capability by explicitly supporting feature filtering via the inclusion of $0$ in the weight set.

The ternary nature of the weights is achieved through a specific quantization function that maps full-precision weights to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Understanding this quantization mechanism, particularly the role of the average absolute weight, is essential for interpreting how BitNet b1.58 achieves both efficiency and performance comparable to full-precision LLMs under the same training conditions.

# The Absmean Quantization Function

BitNet b1.58 constrains weights using a quantization function based on the average absolute value of the weight matrix. Let $W$ denote a full-precision weight matrix of size $n \times m$. The quantization proceeds by first computing the scaling factor
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm} \sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
\]
which is the mean of the absolute values of the entries in $W$. The scaled weight matrix is $\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}$, where $\epsilon$ is a small constant to stabilize the division. After scaling, each entry is rounded to the nearest integer and clipped to the interval $[-1, 1]$ via
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\big(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))\big).
\]
In effect, each original weight $W_{ij}$ is mapped to one of $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ by: scaling by $\gamma + \epsilon$, rounding to the nearest integer, and clipping to stay within the ternary range. Because $\gamma$ depends on the entire weight matrix, this scheme normalizes the weights based on their global average magnitude before applying the rounding operation. The inclusion of $0$ as a valid quantized value enables explicit feature filtering, which is particularly meaningful in the context of Transformer models, where selectively suppressing certain features can strengthen modeling power.

This absmean scaling creates a coupling between the distribution of the original weights and the resulting ternary assignments. As the average absolute magnitude increases, the normalized values shrink, which interacts directly with the rounding step that determines whether an entry becomes $-1$, $0$, or $+1$.

# How Average Absolute Weight Controls Zero Assignments

The rounding in $\mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$ is performed after dividing by $\gamma + \epsilon$. This division rescales the entire matrix so that entries are expressed relative to the mean absolute magnitude. The larger $\gamma$ becomes, the smaller the normalized values $\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma + \epsilon}$ become in absolute terms.

Rounding to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ is governed by proximity to the integers $-1$, $0$, and $1$. Intuitively, if $\left|\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma + \epsilon}\right|$ is much less than $1$, then $\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma + \epsilon}\right)$ becomes $0$. More specifically, when the normalized value lies closer to $0$ than to $\pm 1$, it is rounded to $0$. As $\gamma$ increases, the normalized values shrink toward $0$ for all entries, which pushes more of them into the rounding basin of $0$. Because the clipping ensures the results stay in $[-1, 1]$, the only possible outcomes after rounding and clipping are $-1$, $0$, and $1$, and the threshold behavior of rounding is decisive.

To understand this mechanism in a concrete way, consider a fixed distribution of original weights $W_{ij}$ and imagine gradually increasing $\gamma$. Each normalized value $\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma + \epsilon}$ scales down, reducing the proportion of entries whose normalized magnitude is close enough to $\pm 1$ to round to those endpoints. Consequently, a larger fraction of entries fall near $0$ in the normalized space and are rounded to $0$. Therefore, as $\gamma$ grows, the proportion of weights quantized to zero increases.

This effect is intrinsic to the design of the absmean quantizer: the relative scale of the entries is determined by the average absolute magnitude, so growing the average compresses the normalized distribution toward zero. By coupling a global scale to local rounding, the quantization function establishes a self-normalizing mechanism that can aggressively prune small-to-moderate weights into zeros when the average magnitude is high.

# Feature Filtering via Zero Weights

The inclusion of $0$ in the ternary set is not merely a quantization artifact; it serves a modeling purpose. The paper highlights that BitNet b1.58 gains stronger modeling capability through explicit support for feature filtering enabled by the presence of $0$ in the quantized weights. When entries are mapped to zero, the corresponding features in linear transformations are suppressed. This suppression is aligned with the intuition that not all features should contribute equally at all times, and a ternary scheme with zeros enforces sparse participation of features based on the quantizer’s normalization and rounding behavior.

The dependence of zero assignments on $\gamma$ provides a principled control: as the average absolute weight grows, more entries are driven to zero, intensifying feature filtering. This can be viewed as a form of global contextual gating, where the collective magnitude of the weight matrix determines how aggressively the model prunes contributors. Because the quantization is applied to every linear layer replaced by BitLinear in the architecture, this effect percolates through the Transformer and can influence both attention and feedforward pathways.

Importantly, this feature filtering happens within the training-from-scratch regime of BitNet b1.58 with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. The training benefits from the simplicity and stability of scaling and rounding, while the resultant sparsity patterns—more pronounced when $\gamma$ is larger—can contribute to effective representation learning. The end result, supported by the empirical findings in the paper, is that BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision performance starting from the 3B scale, while delivering substantial reductions in latency, memory, throughput constraints, and energy consumption.

# Implications for Efficiency and Scaling

The quantization behavior shaped by $\gamma$ has downstream implications for system-level efficiency. Zero weights lower the effective participation of parameters in integer addition during matrix multiplication, which is the dominant computation in LLMs. While the paper’s energy analysis focuses on the shift from FP16 multiplication and addition to INT8 addition, the existence of zero-valued weights further simplifies the arithmetic path that needs to be exercised during inference.

This interacts synergistically with the broader efficiency gains reported in the paper. BitNet b1.58 reduces memory consumption and latency relative to FP16 baselines, with the speed-up increasing as model size scales. The architecture’s reliance on integer addition allows it to save arithmetic energy dramatically compared to FP16 computation. As model size grows, the proportion of computation attributable to linear layers increases, making the advantages of the 1.58-bit representation even more pronounced. In this context, the absmean quantizer’s tendency to assign more zeros when $\gamma$ is large complements the overarching drive toward efficient inference by simplifying the effective computation in practice.

Moreover, because BitNet b1.58 employs 8-bit activations and removes biases, it fits cleanly into the LLaMA-alike component ecosystem and can be integrated into widely used software stacks. The quantization of activations to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token, eschewing zero-point quantization, contributes to a simpler implementation and system-level optimization. Together, these design choices create an environment where the quantizer’s dynamics—including the dependence of zero assignments on $\gamma$—work in concert with hardware-friendly computation.

# Summary

BitNet b1.58 constrains weights to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ via an absmean quantization function that scales by the average absolute value $\gamma$ and then applies rounding and clipping. Because the normalized values $\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}$ shrink as $\gamma$ grows, more entries fall near $0$ and are rounded to zero. This increases the proportion of zero weights as $\gamma$ becomes larger. The presence of zeros enables explicit feature filtering, which the paper identifies as strengthening modeling capability. In practice, this quantization behavior dovetails with the system-level advantages of BitNet b1.58, contributing to its ability to match full-precision performance at scale while significantly reducing latency, memory, throughput constraints, and energy consumption.
