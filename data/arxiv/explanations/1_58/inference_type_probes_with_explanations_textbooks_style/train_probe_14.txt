Chapter Title: Quantization in BitNet b1.58 and the Role of ε in Stable Ternary Weights

# Introduction to Ternary 1.58-bit LLMs and Quantization

BitNet b1.58 represents a significant step in the development of efficient large language models by constraining every weight to a ternary set of values, namely $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This design yields a model with an effective precision of 1.58 bits per weight. The key motivation behind this approach is to preserve the modeling capability of full-precision Transformers while drastically reducing the computational and memory costs associated with inference and training. In BitNet b1.58, the architecture remains Transformer-based, with standard components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and rotary embeddings, but the dense linear layers are replaced by BitLinear layers that operate with quantized weights and low-bit activations.

At the heart of BitNet b1.58 is a carefully designed quantization function that maps full-precision weight matrices $W$ to ternary matrices $\widetilde{W}$. This mapping makes it possible to replace expensive floating-point arithmetic in matrix multiplications with operations based primarily on integer addition, translating into substantial energy and latency savings. In addition, the reduced memory footprint per parameter greatly reduces bandwidth and on-chip memory requirements, enabling improved throughput, especially as model size scales.

To support strong modeling performance despite the aggressive bit-width reduction, BitNet b1.58 introduces a quantization mechanism that includes an explicit scaling step and a clipping-and-rounding transformation. A central ingredient of this mechanism is the average absolute value of the weight matrix, which governs how full-precision weights are normalized prior to ternarization. A seemingly small but essential detail in this formulation is the inclusion of a small constant $\epsilon$ in the denominator of the scaling step. This term plays a practical role in ensuring the quantization pipeline is well-defined for all weight configurations.

# The Absmean Quantization Function

The quantization function in BitNet b1.58 enforces ternary weights by first scaling and then discretizing the full-precision weights. Concretely, given a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, the quantized matrix $\widetilde{W}$ is defined by
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where $\gamma$ is the average absolute value of the entries of $W$:
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
The operator $\mathrm{RoundClip}(\cdot, a, b)$ performs elementwise rounding to the nearest integer followed by clipping to the interval $[a, b]$:
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\!\big(a, \min\!\big(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\big)\big).
\]

This formulation uses the “absmean” (average absolute value) as a scale estimator for the magnitude of $W$. Dividing by $\gamma$ normalizes the typical weight magnitude to be on the order of $1$, aligning the majority of values near the ternary targets $-1$, $0$, and $1$ prior to rounding. The scaling is crucial: without it, the raw weight magnitudes could be arbitrarily large or small, leading to poor ternary assignments after rounding. By adapting the scale per weight matrix, the quantization keeps the discretization thresholds appropriately calibrated throughout training.

# The Role of the Scaling Factor $\gamma$

The choice $\gamma = \tfrac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$ provides a simple and robust measure of the overall scale of the weights in a given matrix. Unlike using, for example, a maximum absolute value, the average absolute value reflects the typical magnitude across entries rather than being dominated by outliers. This helps ensure that the distribution of the normalized weights $\tfrac{W}{\gamma}$ aligns well with the intended ternary targets, ideally mapping a large portion of values close to $-1$, $0$, or $1$ after rounding.

In practice, this normalization serves two intertwined objectives. First, it harmonizes the quantization behavior across layers by providing a consistent reference for the rounding thresholds. Second, it integrates naturally into training from scratch: as the optimizer updates $W$, the scale $\gamma$ tracks changes in the average magnitude, maintaining a stable mapping into the ternary set. The combination of per-matrix scaling with explicit clipping ensures that the ternary representation remains bounded and interpretable.

A key consideration arises when $\gamma$ is exactly zero. This situation occurs precisely when every element of $W$ is zero, i.e., $W_{ij} = 0$ for all $i, j$. In such a case, the scaling factor must be handled carefully to avoid undefined operations. This is where the constant $\epsilon$ plays a necessary role.

# RoundClip and Ternarization into $\{-1, 0, 1\}$

After scaling by $\gamma + \epsilon$, the entries are passed through $\mathrm{RoundClip}(\cdot, -1, 1)$. The rounding step $\mathrm{round}(\cdot)$ maps each scaled value to its nearest integer, and the clipping step ensures the result remains within the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Thus, any value with magnitude sufficiently close to $0$ maps to $0$, values near $1$ map to $1$, and values near $-1$ map to $-1$.

This design has two notable properties. First, it produces exact zeros in the weight matrices, enabling explicit feature filtering. The presence of $0$ as an allowed weight value provides a mechanism to suppress unhelpful features or connections, improving modeling capacity relative to strictly binary $\{-1, +1\}$ schemes. Second, since the outputs are guaranteed to be in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the downstream matrix multiplications in BitLinear layers can be implemented with highly efficient integer arithmetic that minimizes multiplications, aligning with the energy and latency reductions emphasized in BitNet b1.58.

# The Practical Role of $\epsilon$ in the Denominator

In the scaling step, BitNet b1.58 uses
\[
\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon} \quad \text{with} \quad \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|.
\]
The inclusion of $\epsilon$ in the denominator serves a practical and essential purpose: it prevents division by zero.

To see why this is necessary, consider the case when all entries of $W$ are zero. In that situation, the average absolute value is $\gamma = 0$. If we were to compute $\tfrac{W}{\gamma}$, the denominator would be zero, which is undefined. By adding a small constant $\epsilon$ to the denominator, the expression remains well-defined even when $\gamma = 0$. The scaled matrix becomes $\tfrac{W}{\epsilon}$, which is identically zero, and the subsequent $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ maps it cleanly to the ternary zero matrix. This behavior is both mathematically well-defined and semantically consistent with the intended quantization, since an all-zero full-precision matrix should quantize to an all-zero ternary matrix.

Thus, although the quantization function is primarily driven by the adaptive scaling $\gamma$ and the rounding/clipping operation, the $\epsilon$ term is indispensable for guaranteeing that the quantization procedure is defined for all possible weight matrices, including the degenerate all-zero case.

# Activation Quantization Choices in BitNet b1.58

While the focus of BitNet b1.58 is on 1.58-bit weights, activations are also quantized to low bit-width. Specifically, activations are quantized to 8 bits. In contrast to the original BitNet implementation, BitNet b1.58 scales activations to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token and avoids zero-point quantization. This design choice simplifies implementation and system-level optimization and was found to have negligible impact on performance in experiments.

This symmetric scaling for activations complements the ternary weights by maintaining a straightforward arithmetic pathway in the BitLinear operations. Integer addition dominates the resulting matrix multiplications, consistent with the broader objective of minimizing multiplication-heavy floating-point computation. The combination of ternary weights and 8-bit activations strikes a balance between aggressive quantization and empirical performance, enabling BitNet b1.58 to match full-precision baselines at sufficiently large model sizes while delivering substantial savings in memory, latency, and energy.

# System-Level Implications of Ternary Weights

By constraining weights to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, BitNet b1.58 dramatically reduces computation and data movement costs. Matrix multiplications—the dominant workload in Transformer models—are transformed into operations that rely heavily on integer addition, which is significantly more energy-efficient than floating-point addition and multiplication. This efficiency is especially valuable because power is often the limiting factor for compute performance in modern chips, and lowering energy per operation enables faster computation for a fixed power budget.

The reduction in model precision also lowers the memory footprint and bandwidth requirements. During inference, transferring parameters from DRAM to on-chip memory can be a bottleneck. Ternary weights substantially reduce the amount of data that must be moved, diminishing both the latency and the energy cost of weight loading. The paper reports that these benefits compound as model size increases, with BitNet b1.58 achieving higher speed-ups and larger memory savings at larger scales. For example, at 70B parameters, BitNet b1.58 is reported to be significantly faster and more memory-efficient than a comparable FP16 baseline.

These system-level improvements also affect end-to-end performance. Lower per-token latency directly accelerates generation, higher throughput allows for larger batch sizes within the same memory budget, and lower energy costs make large-scale inference more economically and environmentally viable. Collectively, these gains establish BitNet b1.58 as a cost-effective alternative to full-precision LLMs without sacrificing performance at scale.

# Summary

BitNet b1.58 achieves a compelling balance between efficiency and performance by quantizing weights to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ using an absmean-based scaling and a RoundClip discretization. The quantization function
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right), \quad \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|
\]
is central to this design. The scaling factor $\gamma$ aligns the weight magnitudes with the ternary targets, while the rounding and clipping enforce the discrete values. The inclusion of the small constant $\epsilon$ in the denominator serves a crucial practical role: it prevents division by zero when $\gamma = 0$, ensuring that the quantization procedure is always well-defined, including for the all-zero weight matrix. Together with symmetric 8-bit activation quantization, this approach enables a computation paradigm dominated by integer addition, leading to substantial benefits in latency, memory, throughput, and energy consumption, and allowing BitNet b1.58 to match or exceed full-precision baselines at appropriate scales.
