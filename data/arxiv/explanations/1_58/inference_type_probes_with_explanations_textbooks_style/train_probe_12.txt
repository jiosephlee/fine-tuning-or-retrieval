Chapter Title: Understanding Weight Quantization in BitNet b1.58: The Role of the Scale Factor $\gamma$

# BitNet b1.58 and the 1.58-bit Paradigm

BitNet b1.58 represents a significant step in the development of highly efficient Large Language Models by constraining every parameter to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Unlike conventional full-precision models that rely on FP16 or BF16 floating-point arithmetic, BitNet b1.58 eliminates most multiplications in matrix multiplication and primarily uses integer additions. This shift dramatically reduces energy consumption and improves latency, memory usage, and throughput. The approach retains performance on par with full-precision Transformer LLMs of the same size and training tokens, starting from 3B parameters, and yields a new scaling law that links performance with inference cost in ways favorable to 1.58-bit models.

The architecture is built on the Transformer backbone, replacing standard linear layers with BitLinear. It uses LLaMA-alike components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and removes all biases, enabling integration into popular open-source ecosystems. BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations, and it includes explicit support for feature filtering via the weight value $0$, which strengthens modeling capability compared to pure 1-bit designs.

# Ternary Weight Quantization: How the Formula Works

Central to BitNet b1.58 is the quantization procedure that constrains the weights to $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. The quantization method is described as an absmean quantization function. Given a weight matrix $W$, the algorithm applies the following transformation:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\left(a, \min\left(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\right)\right),
\]
and
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm} \sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
Here, $\gamma$ is the average absolute value of the entries in $W$, and $\epsilon$ is a small constant added for numerical stability. The operator $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ first rounds each entry to the nearest integer and then clips the result to the interval $[-1, 1]$, guaranteeing ternary outputs.

This procedure can be understood in two steps. First, the weight matrix is scaled by a factor derived from its own statistics. Second, the scaled weights are discretized to the nearest ternary value and constrained by clipping. Together, these steps convert a full-precision $W$ into a ternary $\widetilde{W}$ that preserves the representational structure of $W$ while enabling the new 1.58-bit computation paradigm.

# The Conceptual Role of $\gamma$ as a Scale Factor

The quantity $\gamma$ serves as a scale factor that calibrates the magnitudes in $W$ before rounding to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Since $\gamma$ is the average absolute value of the entire weight matrix, dividing by $\gamma$ normalizes the overall scale of $W$ so that typical entries are brought into a range where rounding to $-1$, $0$, or $+1$ is meaningful. In other words, $\gamma$ defines the reference magnitude against which each individual weight is evaluated during quantization.

Conceptually, the ternarization process depends on how entries of $W$ compare to a common baseline. If weights are too large in magnitude, rounding without scaling would push most values to $\pm 1$, erasing the middle state $0$ and losing the feature filtering capability introduced by the ternary design. If weights are too small, rounding would push most values to $0$, collapsing the matrix and severely degrading modeling power. By using $\gamma$ to scale $W$, the quantization aligns the typical weight magnitudes to a regime where all three discrete states are utilized appropriately. Thus, $\gamma$ controls the dynamic balance between $-1$, $0$, and $+1$ in the quantized representation.

This role is further clarified by the fact that $\gamma$ is computed as the average absolute value across the full matrix. That choice makes the scaling sensitive to the distribution of magnitudes in $W$ while remaining simple and efficient to compute. It ensures that the quantization step operates on a normalized matrix in which a typical weight has magnitude near $1$ after scaling. The addition of $\epsilon$ prevents numerical issues when the average magnitude is extremely small. Together, $\gamma$ and $\epsilon$ stabilize the mapping from a full-precision domain to the ternary domain, making the rounding and clipping operations effective across layers and training stages.

# RoundClip, Discrete States, and Feature Filtering

After scaling by $\gamma$, the function $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ applies rounding and constrains outputs to $[-1, 1]$:
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\left(a, \min\left(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\right)\right),
\]
with $a=-1$ and $b=1$. The rounding step maps each scaled value to the nearest integer, and the clipping step enforces the ternary set. The presence of the zero state in the ternary set is a crucial design choice in BitNet b1.58. It explicitly supports feature filtering by allowing weights to suppress certain features via $0$, rather than forcing all contributions to be additive or subtractive. The scaling by $\gamma$ is instrumental in enabling this middle state to be used meaningfully: by calibrating the typical magnitude of weights, values that are small relative to the average tend to round to $0$, while more decisive contributions round to $\pm 1$.

This behavior connects directly to the modeling advantages observed in BitNet b1.58. The zero state allows the model to selectively pass or block signals, improving the expressive power of a low-bit network. If $\gamma$ did not appropriately scale the matrix, the ternary decomposition could become imbalanced, underutilizing either the $0$ state or the $\pm 1$ states. In that sense, $\gamma$ is the mechanism that sets the thresholding behavior implicit in ternary quantization.

# Activation Quantization and Per-Token Scaling

While the focus in BitNet b1.58 is on ternary weights, activations are also quantized. The activation quantization follows the implementation in the original BitNet, with a notable modification: activations are scaled to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token instead of scaling to $[0, Q_b]$ before non-linear functions. This eliminates zero-point quantization, simplifying both implementation and system-level optimization. The paper reports negligible performance impact from this change in experiments.

This per-token scaling for activations parallels the role of $\gamma$ for weights in spirit: both procedures normalize values to a controlled range before discretization or further processing. For weights, $\gamma$ is computed as the average absolute value across a matrix, and serves as the scale factor in the quantization formula. For activations, the per-token scaling brings values into a symmetric interval, facilitating efficient low-bit arithmetic. In both cases, the normalization step is key to making low-bit representations effective while maintaining performance.

# Performance, Efficiency, and the New Scaling Law

BitNet b1.58 achieves a compelling trade-off between performance and cost. In experiments pre-trained on 100 billion tokens and evaluated across a range of tasks, BitNet b1.58 matches full precision baselines starting from 3B parameters. It outperforms the 3B full-precision baseline at 3.9B parameters while consuming substantially less memory and achieving lower latency. As models scale to 7B, 13B, and 70B parameters, the observed speed-ups grow, with the 70B variant being 4.1 times faster than the corresponding FP16 baseline. Memory savings follow a similar trend given that embeddings remain full precision, and their relative contribution decreases at larger scales.

Energy analysis focused on matrix multiplication indicates that the majority of BitNet b1.58 operations are INT8 additions, while full-precision baselines rely on FP16 additions and multiplications. The reported model yields a 71.4 times reduction in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips, and the end-to-end energy cost improves further as model size increases, due to the growing dominance of linear layers. Throughput experiments demonstrate up to 8.9 times higher throughput at 70B parameters by supporting much larger batch sizes within the same memory constraints.

These results support a new scaling law for 1.58-bit models, under which larger ternary-weight models can be more efficient than substantially smaller FP16 models in latency, memory usage, and energy consumption. For example, 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 3B FP16 LLMs, 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16 LLMs, and 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16 LLMs. The quantization design—and specifically the calibration provided by the scale factor $\gamma$—is central to enabling these gains without sacrificing model quality.

# Broader Implications and Future Directions

The 1.58-bit design opens paths for improved deployment in several settings. Mixture-of-Experts models stand to benefit from reduced memory footprints and lower inter-chip communication overheads, potentially enabling deployments on fewer devices or even a single chip. Long sequence support becomes more tractable by halving activation precision from 16 bits to 8 bits, with future work suggesting the possibility of further compression to 4 bits or below. Edge and mobile devices, which are constrained by memory and compute, become practical targets for high-capability LLMs due to the reduced energy and memory costs and the friendliness of integer arithmetic to CPU execution. Finally, the unique computation paradigm invites the design of specific hardware optimized for 1-bit LLMs.

Across all these directions, the quantization framework—anchored by the scale factor $\gamma$—remains a foundational element. By normalizing the weight matrices to a meaningful reference magnitude before rounding and clipping, BitNet b1.58 achieves a ternary representation that preserves performance while enabling substantial efficiency gains. The conceptual role of $\gamma$ as a scale factor is therefore not only a detail in a formula, but a core ingredient that makes the 1.58-bit era viable and effective.
