Chapter Title: Actions of Ternary Weights: Addition, Subtraction, and Skipping in BitNet b1.58

# The Era of 1-bit LLMs and the BitNet b1.58 Paradigm

Large Language Models have achieved strong performance by scaling parameters and data, but the standard reliance on full-precision floating-point arithmetic has made inference costly in terms of latency, memory bandwidth, and energy consumption. Recent work on 1-bit model architectures, particularly BitNet, establishes a new computation paradigm by replacing expensive floating-point multiplications with integer additions in the core matrix multiplications of Transformer-based models. BitNet b1.58 advances this direction by constraining every weight to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, resulting in a 1.58-bit model that retains the advantages of 1-bit computation while improving modeling capability and matching full-precision baselines from the 3B scale.

BitNet b1.58 is a Transformer variant that replaces standard linear layers with BitLinear, trains from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations, and adopts LLaMA-alike components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and bias removal. It is designed for compatibility with popular open-source tooling and for system-level efficiency. Beyond practical integration, its core significance is computational: by making every weight ternary, matrix multiplications devolve to sequences of integer additions where multiplications are either trivial or unnecessary. This shift enables substantial energy savings and reduces inference-time memory traffic, translating into faster computation and higher throughput under the constraints of modern hardware.

# Ternary Weights as Discrete Actions on Inputs

The essence of BitNet b1.58 lies in the algebraic consequences of restricting weights to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Consider a linear transformation with weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ applied to an input vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$. In full precision, the output is given by
\[
y = W x, \quad y_i = \sum_{j=1}^{m} W_{ij} x_j.
\]
BitNet b1.58 realizes $W$ via quantization to a ternary matrix $\widetilde{W} \in \{-1, 0, 1\}^{n \times m}$ using an absmean scaling:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right), \quad \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))).
\]
With $\widetilde{W}_{ij} \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$, each term in the sum defining $y_i$ becomes one of three discrete actions on the input coordinate $x_j$:
\[
\widetilde{W}_{ij} x_j =
\begin{cases}
+ x_j & \text{if } \widetilde{W}_{ij} = +1, \\
0     & \text{if } \widetilde{W}_{ij} = 0, \\
- x_j & \text{if } \widetilde{W}_{ij} = -1.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus the matrix-vector product reduces to accumulating signed copies of input activations and skipping those marked zero:
\[
y_i = \sum_{j=1}^{m} \left( \mathbb{I}[\widetilde{W}_{ij} = +1] - \mathbb{I}[\widetilde{W}_{ij} = -1] \right) x_j,
\]
where entries with $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = 0$ do not contribute. In other words, each weight selects whether to add $x_j$, subtract $x_j$, or skip $x_j$ entirely. This additive, subtractive, or skipping behavior is the operational meaning of ternary weights as actions on inputs.

The inclusion of $0$ in the ternary set is crucial. Compared to pure $\{-1, +1\}$ quantization, the zero allows explicit feature filtering: connections that do not contribute meaningfully can be omitted by assigning zero, reducing unnecessary accumulation and improving modeling expressivity. This capability is directly tied to the performance improvements observed for BitNet b1.58 over the original 1-bit BitNet.

# A Computation Paradigm with Almost No Multiplication

Because each $\widetilde{W}_{ij}$ is in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the multiplication $\widetilde{W}_{ij} x_j$ can be implemented without general-purpose multipliers. At the core of the matrix multiplication, the operations reduce to integer addition and subtraction, with skip operations for zeros. In practice, BitNet’s matrix multiplication only involves integer addition, saving orders of energy cost relative to floating-point operations. This shift is enabled end-to-end by two design choices in BitNet b1.58:

First, weights are quantized to 1.58 bits using the absmean function, which constrains them to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ while preserving scale via $\gamma$ during training. Second, activations are quantized to 8 bits, and are scaled per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, eliminating zero-point quantization. This per-token scaling ensures that activation values used in accumulation are integer-friendly while being simple for implementation and system optimization, with negligible effects on performance in experiments.

Under these conditions, the dominant arithmetic in linear layers becomes accumulation of integer values, specifically INT8 addition. This design exploits the fact that the bulk of LLM computation is matrix multiplication in linear layers, and thus the replacement of floating-point multiplications with integer additions yields broad efficiency gains at inference. The result is a computation paradigm with almost no multiplication, replacing the costliest operation in standard LLMs with cheaper, power-efficient additions.

# From Algebra to Systems: Energy, Latency, and Memory Implications

Interpreting matrix multiplication as add, subtract, or skip has concrete systems implications. First, energy: on 7nm chips, BitNet b1.58 replaces FP16 addition and FP16 multiplication with predominantly INT8 addition. According to the energy model cited, this leads to a $71.4\times$ reduction in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication. As model size increases, the proportion of time and energy spent in $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ grows, amplifying the benefit of this additive paradigm. End-to-end energy measured at 512 tokens demonstrates increasing efficiency advantages as the model scales, since other components contribute relatively less.

Second, latency: inference time per output token is dominated by the cost of linear layers. Since additions are cheaper than floating-point multiplications, BitNet b1.58 shows a consistent speed-up that grows with model size. Empirically, at 3B scale BitNet b1.58 is $2.71\times$ faster, and at 70B it is $4.1\times$ faster than the FP16 baseline. These speed-ups reflect the cumulative effect of replacing multiplications with additions and reducing weight transfer overheads.

Third, memory: the ternary weights drastically reduce memory footprint both in capacity and bandwidth. Transferring parameters from DRAM to on-chip memory (e.g., SRAM) is a major inference cost; smaller, lower-bit weights lessen this burden. In measurements using optimized kernels, BitNet b1.58 uses $3.55\times$ less GPU memory at 3B and maintains a similar trend at larger scale, aided by the fact that embeddings remain full precision but contribute a smaller fraction of overall memory as the model grows. Reduced activation precision to 8 bits further lowers KV cache requirements, improving feasibility for long sequences under fixed resources.

These gains propagate to throughput in distributed settings. On two 80GB A100 GPUs with pipeline parallelism and sequence length 512, BitNet b1.58 70B supports up to $11\times$ the batch size of the FP16 baseline, achieving $8.9\times$ higher throughput. This improvement is aligned with the lower per-token latency and reduced per-example memory footprint made possible by the additive-subtractive-skip computation.

# Feature Filtering and Modeling Capability with Zero Weights

Adding $0$ to the ternary weight set confers explicit support for feature filtering. In the matrix multiplication $y = \widetilde{W}x$, entries where $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = 0$ cause the corresponding input feature $x_j$ to be skipped for output coordinate $i$. This skip operation acts as a learned mechanism to remove irrelevant contributions, reducing interference and sharpening representations.

This feature filtering capability strengthens modeling compared to pure 1-bit designs. In empirical evaluations, BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision baselines in terms of perplexity and end-task performance starting from 3B model size under identical training configurations (same model size and training tokens). Notably, at the 3.9B scale BitNet b1.58 is $2.4\times$ faster, consumes $3.32\times$ less memory, and performs significantly better than the FP16 LLaMA 3B baseline. These results demonstrate that the additive-subtractive-skip mechanism does not merely reduce computation—it enhances representational control through selective skipping, contributing to improved accuracy.

# Scaling Law and Performance–Cost Pareto Improvements

BitNet b1.58 enables a new scaling law that relates model performance and inference cost. As the model size increases, the benefits of replacing multiplications with additions and of reducing memory bandwidth become more pronounced. The empirical equivalences observed are illustrative:

At 13B, BitNet b1.58 is more efficient in latency, memory usage, and energy consumption than a 3B FP16 LLM. At 30B, it is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM. At 70B, it outperforms a 13B FP16 LLM on the same metrics. These relationships reflect the compounding advantage of the additive-subtractive-skip computation as the proportion of $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ cost grows with model size.

Zero-shot accuracy results further show that the performance gap between BitNet b1.58 and FP16 baselines narrows as model size increases, with parity beginning at 3B. Validation perplexity exhibits the same trend. Consequently, BitNet b1.58 represents a Pareto improvement: for a given accuracy, it reduces latency and memory, and for a given resource budget, it delivers higher accuracy at larger ternary models than smaller full-precision ones.

# Training and Evaluation Observations at Scale

BitNet b1.58 models were trained on 100 billion tokens from RedPajama and evaluated on standard zero-shot language tasks, including ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ, alongside perplexity on WikiText2 and C4. Across sizes, results confirm that additive-subtractive-skip computation can sustain accuracy with less cost, with matching performance at 3B and superior results at 3.9B relative to FP16 3B.

To probe token-scale viability, a 3B BitNet b1.58 was trained with 2T tokens using the StableLM-3B data recipe and evaluated on Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC-easy, reporting zero-shot accuracy. BitNet b1.58 achieved superior performance on all end tasks, indicating that the ternary additive paradigm generalizes well with more data. This supports the view that weight actions of add, subtract, and skip can scale in accuracy while retaining system-level efficiencies.

# Future Directions within the Add–Subtract–Skip Paradigm

The consequences of ternary weight actions extend beyond single-expert Transformers. Mixture-of-Experts models suffer from memory and inter-chip communication overheads; adopting 1.58-bit weights reduces the number of devices needed and lowers activation transfer costs, potentially enabling configurations where entire models fit on a single chip. For long-sequence inference, reducing activations from 16 bits to 8 bits effectively doubles context length under fixed resources, and the paper suggests further lossless compression to 4 bits or even lower for 1.58-bit LLMs as future work.

Edge and mobile deployments benefit from the reduced memory and energy requirements of BitNet b1.58, which is friendlier to CPU devices commonly used in these environments. Finally, the additive-subtractive-skip paradigm motivates hardware specialization. With matrix multiplication dominated by integer addition and almost no multiplication, there is a clear path to systems and accelerators tailored to 1-bit LLMs, echoing the promise shown by specialized LLM hardware and calling for designs optimized for this new computational regime.
