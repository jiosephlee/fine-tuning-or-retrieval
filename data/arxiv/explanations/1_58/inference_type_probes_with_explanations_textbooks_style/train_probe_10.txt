Chapter Title: Energy Scaling in BitNet b1.58 and the Role of Non-linear Components

# Foundations: Where BitNet b1.58 Gains Its Energy Advantage

The paper introduces BitNet b1.58, a 1.58-bit Large Language Model in which every parameter is ternary, taking values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Its core computational advantage is grounded in how it executes the dominant operations inside Transformer-based LLMs. In vanilla full-precision LLMs (e.g., FP16 or BF16 models), the bulk of computation is matrix multiplication, which is performed within layers implemented by $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$. These multiplications and additions occur in floating-point arithmetic, and their cumulative energy cost is substantial. The paper emphasizes that in typical LLMs, both perplexity and task performance improvements have historically been achieved by scaling model size, and that the cost overhead from floating-point addition and multiplication has grown accordingly.

BitNet b1.58 adopts a computation paradigm in which the matrix multiplications in its $\mathrm{BitLinear}$ layers avoid almost all multiplication operations and are executed with integer addition. This transformation moves the dominant arithmetic pathway from FP16 addition and FP16 multiplication to INT8 addition, with profound consequences for energy. According to the energy model cited by the paper, the shift to integer addition in matrix multiplication yields a $71.4\times$ reduction in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips. The paper focuses its estimates primarily on matrix multiplication because it “contributes the most to the cost of LLMs,” and it argues that as long as the fundamental limit to compute performance in many chips is power, these energy savings translate into faster computation as well.

A central empirical observation in the paper is that, as model size scales, the percentage of time and energy due to $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ increases. This directly amplifies BitNet b1.58’s relative advantage, because its largest savings lie precisely in the arithmetic pathway that grows most dominant with scale. The figures discussed in the paper report that BitNet b1.58’s speed-up and energy efficiency improve with model size, and the end-to-end energy measurements further confirm that the model becomes increasingly efficient compared to the FP16 LLaMA baseline as size increases. In other words, the gains are tied to where the computation is concentrated, and BitNet b1.58’s design targets that concentration.

# Components and Their Relative Costs in Transformer LLMs

The paper attributes most computation and energy cost to matrix multiplication inside $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ layers. Besides these linear layers, the Transformer backbone includes non-linear and auxiliary components. The BitNet b1.58 architecture aligns with LLaMA-alike components: it uses RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary positional embeddings, and removes biases. The paper also details the quantization of weights and activations. Weights are quantized with an absmean function that scales a weight matrix $W$ by its average absolute value $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$, and then rounds to the nearest integer in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Activations are quantized to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token to eliminate zero-point quantization, using 8-bit activations throughout. While these non-linear and auxiliary components are essential to modeling capability, the paper’s measurements and analysis consistently treat them as a smaller portion of the computational and energy budget compared to the linear layers.

The empirical observations reinforce that with larger models, the proportion of resources spent on $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ grows, while the relative costs from other components become smaller. The memory analysis also parallels this picture: embeddings remain full precision, but their share of memory shrinks as model size increases; the activation footprint is reduced by the 8-bit quantization in BitNet b1.58, which benefits contexts of longer sequence lengths. All of these details support the central claim that, under observed scaling behavior, the pathway where BitNet b1.58 saves energy becomes increasingly dominant.

# Scaling Behavior Observed in the Paper

The paper reports latency and memory measurements across scales up to 70B parameters. BitNet b1.58 becomes faster than the FP16 baseline by increasing factors as the model grows, reaching $4.1\times$ speed-up at 70B. The same trend holds for memory, with the caveat that some components (like embeddings) remain full precision and therefore take a constant per-parameter memory footprint; this constant footprint forms a smaller fraction of total memory as models get larger.

Energy analysis in the paper is framed around the composition of arithmetic operations. For BitNet b1.58, the majority of arithmetic operations are INT8 additions, whereas the FP16 baseline has both FP16 addition and FP16 multiplication. The measured end-to-end energy cost on sequences of length $512$ shows that the larger the model size, the more efficient BitNet b1.58 becomes, aligning with the statement that “the percentage of $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ grows with the model size, while the cost from other components is smaller for larger models.” Hence, the observed scaling law is favorable to BitNet b1.58: the bigger the model, the more of the workload resides in exactly the part of the computation where BitNet b1.58 achieves its savings.

The paper distills this into practical equivalences: a 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 3B FP16 LLM; a 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM; and a 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM. These qualitative equivalences rest on the documented trends in latency, memory, and energy across scales, and they depend crucially on the growing dominance of $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ in the overall budget.

# Hypothetical Reversal: If Non-linear Components Dominated at Large Scale

The paper’s observed efficiency gains and scaling law hinge on the dominance of matrix multiplication in $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ and the fact that this dominance increases with model size. Consider, instead, a hypothetical scenario that runs counter to the paper’s observation: suppose that as models grow, non-linear components became the dominant contributor to computation and energy. In the context of BitNet b1.58, non-linear components include the functions and mechanisms outside of $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$, such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary positional embeddings, and the handling of quantized activations. The paper does not claim large energy savings for these components; rather, its core savings are explicitly tied to the transformation of matrix multiplication into integer addition.

Under this hypothetical reversal, the end-to-end energy composition would shift away from the pathway where BitNet b1.58 is most advantageous. To build intuition, imagine decomposing total energy into two parts,
$E_{\mathrm{total}} = E_{\mathrm{linear}} + E_{\mathrm{other}}$,
where $E_{\mathrm{linear}}$ is the energy attributable to matrix multiplications in $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ and $E_{\mathrm{other}}$ captures the rest. The paper’s measured $71.4\times$ energy savings apply to arithmetic operations in matrix multiplication, i.e., they act directly to reduce $E_{\mathrm{linear}}$ in BitNet b1.58 compared to the FP16 baseline. If, in contradiction to the paper’s observation, $E_{\mathrm{other}}$ grew to dominate $E_{\mathrm{total}}$ at large scale, then the fraction of $E_{\mathrm{total}}$ affected by BitNet b1.58’s savings would diminish.

In such a case, even if $E_{\mathrm{linear}}$ remained much cheaper in BitNet b1.58, its contribution to the overall budget would be smaller, and the end-to-end energy ratio between BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 baseline would move closer to parity. The same reasoning would extend to latency measurements, since the paper ties speed-ups to the time spent in $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$. If non-linear components took the majority of time, the headline speed-ups that “increase as the model size scales” would no longer be amplified, and the relative advantage in latency would decrease. In short, BitNet b1.58’s efficiency is strongest where it replaces FP16 operations with INT8 addition in matrix multiplication; if that region ceases to dominate, the net advantage shrinks.

This hypothetical also sheds light on the paper’s reported scaling law equivalences. Statements like “70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16 LLM” arise from the observed growth in the percentage of $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ with model size. If, instead, non-linear components scaled to dominate, the basis for these equivalences would weaken. The net effect would be a reduction in BitNet b1.58’s relative energy advantage at large scale, because the growing part of the computation would lie outside the pathway where BitNet b1.58 is designed to save energy.

# Practical Intuition: Why Dominance Matters

The paper’s energy estimates are not generic; they focus specifically on the arithmetic operations in matrix multiplication and demonstrate a major reduction by replacing FP16 addition and multiplication with INT8 addition. This specificity explains both the observed and the hypothetical behaviors. When the computation that dominates is the computation that BitNet b1.58 optimizes, end-to-end gains are large and grow with scale. Conversely, if the dominant computation lies elsewhere, then optimizing matrix multiplication—even by large factors—has a smaller effect on the whole.

This perspective contextualizes several reported trends in the paper. The improvement in throughput, especially at 70B with pipeline parallelism, is consistent with the idea that BitNet b1.58 can carry larger effective batch sizes because its dominant operations are lighter. The memory advantages follow from reduced bit-widths for weights and activations, but the paper points out that components left in full precision (e.g., embeddings) become a smaller proportion of total memory as scale increases, which again leans on where the budget lies. Energy advantages for longer sequences are also tied to the cost structure: by reducing activation bit-width (to 8 bits in the paper’s setup), BitNet b1.58 cuts the KV cache footprint, which facilitates longer contexts under the same resource constraints. All of these advantages are maximized when the optimized pathway is the pathway that matters most.

Returning to the hypothetical, if non-linear components—those not transformed into integer addition-based matrix multiplications—grew to dominate at large scale, then these advantages would be attenuated. While BitNet b1.58 would retain its benefits in the parts it optimizes, the proportion of the full computation affected by its design would be smaller, and thus the end-to-end energy advantage would decrease. This conclusion follows directly from the paper’s framing that “the percentage of $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ grows with the model size,” and that this growth is the reason BitNet b1.58 becomes “increasingly more efficient” with scale; reversing the premise reverses the implication.

# Conclusion

BitNet b1.58’s relative energy advantage is rooted in transforming the dominant matrix multiplications inside $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ from FP16 arithmetic to INT8 addition, achieving a $71.4\times$ reduction in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication. The paper shows that, empirically, the fraction of computation spent in $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ increases with model size, which amplifies BitNet b1.58’s end-to-end gains in energy, latency, and memory.

If, contrary to the paper’s observation, non-linear components grew to dominate computation at large scale, the portion of the workload where BitNet b1.58 secures its largest savings would decrease, and its relative end-to-end energy advantage would diminish accordingly. This hypothetical underscores the central insight of the paper: BitNet b1.58’s scaling law and efficiency hinge on the dominance of matrix multiplication within LLM computation.
