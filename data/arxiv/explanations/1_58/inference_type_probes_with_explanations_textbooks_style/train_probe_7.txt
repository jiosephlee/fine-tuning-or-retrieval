Chapter: Why Ternary Weights Matter: Modeling Capability in BitNet b1.58

# The emergence of 1.58-bit LLMs

Large Language Models have advanced rapidly, but their deployment cost and energy footprint have grown alongside their capabilities. A central contributor to this cost is the reliance on full-precision matrix multiplications, which are dominated by floating-point addition and multiplication operations. Recent work on 1-bit model architectures, exemplified by BitNet, shows that a drastically different computation paradigm—replacing floating-point operations with integer addition—can save orders of energy cost while enabling faster computation when power is the limiting factor. Building on this direction, BitNet b1.58 introduces a crucial modification: all weights are ternary, restricted to the set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, yielding what the authors describe as “1.58 bits” per parameter in the binary system.

BitNet b1.58 retains the core advantages of the original 1-bit BitNet. It dramatically reduces memory footprint, transfer bandwidth requirements, and inference latency relative to full-precision baselines, and it keeps the computational structure centered on integer addition for matrix multiplication. In terms of energy consumption, BitNet b1.58 matches the original 1-bit approach because the majority of its arithmetic remains efficient INT8 addition rather than FP16 operations. Beyond these system-level benefits, the key conceptual advance of BitNet b1.58 is its improved modeling capability, derived from allowing weights to take the value $0$. This simple addition enables explicit feature filtering, which the paper identifies as a major driver of the observed performance improvements over strictly binary $\{-1, +1\}$ weights.

# Ternary weights and explicit feature filtering

The inclusion of the value $0$ in the weight set is not just a numerical tweak; it fundamentally changes what the model can represent and how it can behave during training and inference. In a strictly binary regime with weights restricted to $\{-1, +1\}$, every connection participates in the computation with a non-zero contribution. There is no straightforward way to make a weight “silent” without switching signs, and as a result, every feature contributes either positively or negatively at every layer where that feature is connected. BitNet b1.58, by contrast, allows weights to be zero, which introduces explicit feature filtering. When a weight is $0$, a feature can be cleanly excluded from a particular linear transformation, enabling the model to suppress noisy or irrelevant signals without resorting to balancing positive and negative contributions.

The paper emphasizes that this explicit support for feature filtering “significantly improve[s] the performance of 1-bit LLMs.” The intuition is clear. In high-capacity architectures such as Transformers, the ability to gate or block information pathways can be critical for stabilizing learning dynamics and improving the fidelity of learned representations. By supporting $0$ weights, BitNet b1.58 gives the network the flexibility to pass, invert, or suppress signals at the level of individual parameters. This expands the representational repertoire relative to strictly binary weights and makes it easier for the model to implement sparse or selective transformations where desirable.

Importantly, this enhancement in modeling capability does not come at the cost of the system advantages inherent to 1-bit designs. The paper explicitly states that BitNet b1.58 “has the same energy consumption as the original 1-bit BitNet,” while it remains “much more efficient in terms of memory consumption, throughput and latency compared to FP16 LLM baselines.” In other words, the inclusion of $0$ delivers a notable improvement in expressive power without impairing the efficiency gains that motivate low-bit architectures in the first place.

# Quantization that enables ternary weights

BitNet b1.58 enforces ternary weights via an “absmean” quantization function that scales the weight matrix by its average absolute value and then rounds to the nearest integer within the ternary set. Concretely, given a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, the quantized weights $\widetilde{W}$ are computed as
\[
    \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
    \mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))),
\]
and the scaling factor $\gamma$ is the average absolute value of weights:
\[
    \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
The small constant $\epsilon$ stabilizes the division. The effect of this scheme is to normalize the scale of the weight matrix to a predictable range so that rounding to $\{-1, 0, +1\}$ is meaningful and stable. Values near zero after scaling will round to $0$, while larger magnitudes round to $\pm 1$.

This quantization design is aligned with the modeling rationale for ternary weights. By centering the quantization around an average magnitude, the method creates a natural thresholding effect: weak signals in $W$ map to $0$ and are filtered out, while strong signals are retained as $\pm 1$. In practice, this produces selective connectivity patterns within each BitLinear layer. Because the architecture is trained end-to-end from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations—without resorting to post-training quantization—the model can learn to exploit this selective filtering throughout pre-training.

The activations are also quantized, following the approach in BitNet with a key modification: instead of scaling to $[0, Q_b]$ before non-linear functions, activations are scaled per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ and do not use zero-point quantization. This design choice is described as “more convenient and simple for both implementation and system-level optimization,” and in experiments it has “negligible effects to the performance.” While the activation quantization concerns throughput and practical deployment, it complements the weight quantization’s role in establishing an efficient and tractable integer arithmetic pipeline.

# Evidence of improved performance with $0$ weights

The paper provides multiple lines of empirical evidence that BitNet b1.58 achieves strong performance relative to full-precision baselines, and that its advantages are pronounced at scale. Models were trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations on 100 billion tokens from RedPajama, using LLaMA-alike components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and bias-free layers, and evaluated on zero-shot language tasks and validation perplexity. The results show that BitNet b1.58 starts to match full precision performance at the 3B parameter scale in terms of perplexity, while being 2.71 times faster and using 3.55 times less GPU memory than the FP16 baseline. Moreover, the 3.9B BitNet b1.58 variant is 2.4 times faster, consumes 3.32 times less memory, and performs significantly better than the 3B FP16 LLaMA LLM baseline.

On end tasks such as ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ, the performance gap narrows with increasing model size, and BitNet b1.58 matches the full precision baseline starting at 3B parameters. For larger models, efficiency advantages grow: at 70B parameters, BitNet b1.58 is 4.1 times faster than the FP16 baseline under the measured setup. Energy modeling further reinforces the efficiency story. The majority of BitNet b1.58’s computation is INT8 addition rather than FP16 addition or multiplication, which translates to substantial energy savings. According to the cited energy model, BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4$ times arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips, and end-to-end energy advantages increase with model size because the proportion of time spent in $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ grows.

These results support the paper’s claim that BitNet b1.58 is a Pareto improvement: it offers lower latency, lower memory consumption, and lower energy usage while matching or surpassing the full-precision baseline in perplexity and zero-shot performance at realistic scales. Given that the authors attribute a key part of these modeling gains to the explicit support for feature filtering via $0$ weights, reverting to strictly binary $\{-1, +1\}$ weights would remove this capability. The most likely outcome—as suggested by the paper’s discussion—would be worse performance relative to BitNet b1.58, because the network would lose the learned ability to silence connections cleanly and would be forced to approximate suppression through combinations of positive and negative contributions.

# Why removing $0$ would harm modeling capability

Understanding the consequences of removing $0$ requires revisiting the role that ternary weights play during training. With $\{-1, 0, +1\}$ weights, the model can align its learned transformations to three regimes: passing a signal positively, passing a signal negatively, or filtering it out entirely. The absmean quantization function reinforces this partition by mapping small magnitudes to $0$ and larger magnitudes to $\pm 1$. This aligns with the notion that not all features should be active in every transformation, and it provides a mechanism to increase representational selectivity without additional architectural constructs.

If the weights are constrained to $\{-1, +1\}$, the third regime disappears. Every learned connection must contribute either as a positive or negative pass-through, and there is no direct way to encode a decision to omit a feature at a given linear layer. In practice, this limitation reduces the expressive flexibility of each BitLinear transformation and restricts the network’s ability to implement sparse filters. The paper’s statement that explicit feature filtering “can significantly improve the performance of 1-bit LLMs” points to this mechanism as a central modeling advantage. Without $0$, the network’s representational capacity for selective suppression is impaired, and the most likely modeling outcome is degraded performance relative to BitNet b1.58.

Crucially, the paper also makes clear that the addition of $0$ does not worsen the efficiency profile. BitNet b1.58 “retains all the benefits of the original 1-bit BitNet,” keeps “almost no multiplication operations” in matrix multiplication, and “has the same energy consumption as the original 1-bit BitNet.” Therefore, the tradeoff that might normally justify removing $0$—namely, gains in speed or energy at the expense of modeling capability—does not apply here. Instead, removing $0$ sacrifices modeling power while preserving no additional efficiency beyond what BitNet b1.58 already provides.

# Scaling law and broader implications

Beyond immediate performance comparisons, BitNet b1.58 supports a new scaling law relating model performance to inference cost. The paper documents equivalences across model sizes that quantify the efficiency advantages over full-precision baselines. For example, a 13B BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient in latency, memory, and energy consumption than a 3B FP16 LLM; a 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM; and a 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM. These relationships reflect the fact that as models scale, the cost of $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ dominates more strongly, and BitNet b1.58’s integer-add-centric computation becomes even more advantageous.

The paper also reports a training experiment with 2 trillion tokens at the 3B scale, showing that BitNet b1.58 achieves superior performance on all evaluated end tasks relative to a state-of-the-art open-source 3B model trained under a similar data recipe. This indicates strong generalization and scalability in the low-bit regime when the architecture supports explicit feature filtering. Removing $0$ would undermine the very mechanism that supports such selectivity, and thus the most likely outcome would be worse modeling performance compared to the ternary design.

In sum, BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that introducing $0$ as a third weight value yields a tangible modeling benefit—explicit feature filtering—without sacrificing the efficiency hallmarks of 1-bit computation. The empirical results in perplexity, zero-shot accuracy, latency, memory, energy, and throughput collectively show that the ternary design achieves parity or superiority to full-precision baselines at practical scales. Given these observations and the paper’s explicit rationale, reverting to strictly binary $\{-1, +1\}$ weights would most likely degrade modeling capability and performance relative to BitNet b1.58, while offering no compensatory gains in efficiency.
