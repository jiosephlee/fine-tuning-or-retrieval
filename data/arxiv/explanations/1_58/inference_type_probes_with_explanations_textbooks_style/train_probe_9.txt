Chapter Title: Feature Filtering in BitNet b1.58: Why the Zero Matters

# The 1.58-bit Paradigm and Its Motivation

Large Language Models have traditionally relied on full-precision parameters, such as FP16 or BF16, which make their training and inference expensive in memory, latency, throughput, and energy consumption. BitNet b1.58 introduces a highly cost-effective alternative by constraining every parameter to a small ternary set, specifically $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This design preserves a new computation paradigm in which matrix multiplications can be implemented with almost no multiplications, relying primarily on integer additions. It offers the same energy consumption advantages as earlier 1-bit designs, while achieving superior efficiency in memory and latency compared to FP16 baselines. Crucially, BitNet b1.58 also delivers strong modeling capability and can match full-precision LLMs in perplexity and end-task performance from the 3B scale onward under matched training configurations.

Among the advantages introduced by BitNet b1.58, the inclusion of $0$ in the weight space plays a pivotal role. The paper emphasizes that adding $0$ enables explicit support for feature filtering and significantly improves the performance of 1-bit LLMs. Understanding this singular addition is essential to grasp why 1.58-bit models achieve both high performance and strong efficiency, and it is central to understanding what is lost if the weights were restricted back to $\{-1, 1\}$.

# Quantization and the Ternary Weight Set

BitNet b1.58 constrains weights to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ through an absmean quantization function. Given a full-precision weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, it computes the average absolute value
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
\]
and scales $W$ by $(\gamma + \epsilon)$, followed by integer rounding with clipping:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\!\big(a, \min\!\big(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\big)\big).
\]
The result $\widetilde{W}$ contains entries in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

This procedure enables a simple and system-friendly quantization of weights while maintaining the desirable performance characteristics of the model. In parallel, activations are quantized to 8 bits and scaled to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token, eliminating zero-point quantization and simplifying implementation. Unlike post-training quantization, BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with these constraints, aligning the modeling process directly with the low-bit computation paradigm.

# What Feature Filtering Means in BitNet b1.58

Feature filtering refers to the model’s ability to explicitly suppress the contribution of particular features by assigning their associated weights the value $0$. When a weight is $0$, it effectively removes the corresponding feature’s influence in the linear transformation, neither adding nor subtracting from the output. In a Transformer architecture where the bulk of computation is matrix multiplication (as in replacing $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ with $\mathrm{BitLinear}$), this capability is directly expressed in the product $\widetilde{W}x$ for an input vector $x$: any coordinate where $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = 0$ does not participate in the sum that forms the output. Thus, the inclusion of $0$ provides an explicit mechanism for feature selection at the level of individual connections.

This selective suppression matters because it allows the model to avoid propagating features that are uninformative or harmful for a given transformation. In a low-bit regime, where each nonzero contribution is coarse ($-1$ or $+1$ after scaling), the ability to silence features can reduce interference among signals. By filtering features, BitNet b1.58 can better shape the representational flow within the Transformer, modulating which aspects of the input are emphasized, which are inverted, and which are ignored altogether. The paper highlights that this explicit support for feature filtering “can significantly improve the performance of 1-bit LLMs,” underscoring that having a $0$ weight value is not merely a minor convenience but a central modeling capability.

# What Is Lost with Only $\{-1, 1\}$ Weights

If BitNet b1.58 were reverted to only $\{-1, 1\}$ weights, the model would lose its most direct mechanism for feature filtering. Under purely binary weights, every feature mapped through a linear layer is compelled to contribute either positively or negatively. There is no explicit way to make a feature’s contribution exactly zero within the weight parameterization. In other words, the model cannot directly “turn off” a feature at the weight level; it can only add it or subtract it.

This distinction is crucial in understanding modeling expressiveness in a low-bit setting. With ternary weights, a linear transformation can be conceptualized as partitioning features into three groups: those that add ($+1$), those that subtract ($-1$), and those that are suppressed ($0$). Reverting to binary weights collapses this partition into two groups only, eliminating the suppress group. The immediate consequence is the inability to filter features explicitly, removing a form of control that BitNet b1.58 uses to improve performance. The paper directly attributes stronger modeling capability to the inclusion of $0$ and states that feature filtering significantly improves the performance of 1-bit LLMs. Therefore, the most directly lost capability upon removing $0$ is the capacity to perform explicit feature filtering.

# Why Zero Enables Stronger Modeling in Low-Bit Regimes

BitNet b1.58 operates with coarse, low-bit representations, where each nonzero weight is inherently a strong, discrete action: add or subtract. In such a context, the $0$ weight acts as a precise valve, allowing the model to exclude features that would otherwise exert unavoidable influence. This provides control over the representational dynamics without increasing bit width or resorting to compensatory scaling that may complicate the system.

From an architectural standpoint, BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and bias removal. Within this backbone, the linear transformations in attention and feed-forward blocks are dominant determinants of performance and cost. Ternary weights with $0$ allow these transformations to be more discriminative in selecting which signals to carry forward. Because the model is trained end-to-end with the ternary constraint, the learning process can discover where suppression benefits the overall objective, improving perplexity and end-task metrics while preserving the energy and memory advantages of the 1-bit computation paradigm.

# Evidence from Results: Performance with Ternary Weights

The paper reports that BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision baselines from the 3B scale onward under matched configurations, and that adding $0$ confers stronger modeling capability. At the 3.9B size, BitNet b1.58 is faster and more memory efficient than the 3B FP16 baseline while performing significantly better. As model size increases, the latency and memory advantages become more pronounced, and the energy savings are substantial due to replacing FP16 addition and multiplication with primarily INT8 additions. These results align with the intuition that explicit feature filtering in low-bit regimes helps the model allocate its limited representational precision more effectively, thereby narrowing or eliminating performance gaps relative to full precision.

Beyond single-size comparisons, the paper presents a scaling law perspective in which larger BitNet b1.58 models are more efficient than smaller FP16 models across latency, memory, and energy. Although these system-level benefits do not depend on feature filtering alone, the ability to filter features contributes to achieving strong accuracy and perplexity while retaining the efficiency afforded by the computation paradigm.

# Training and Inference Design Choices that Support Feature Filtering

BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. This aligns the learning process with the ternary constraint rather than imposing it post hoc. The absmean-based quantization encourages weights to adopt $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ values in a way that stabilizes training and enables suppression as part of optimization. The per-token activation scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ simplifies system-level considerations by eliminating zero-point quantization, which complements the model’s architectural choices that remove biases and use LLaMA-alike components. Together, these design decisions help the training dynamics discover when to use $0$ to filter features, when to add, and when to subtract, producing a workable balance of expressiveness and efficiency in low-bit form.

On the inference side, the model benefits from kernels optimized for low-bit computation, such as the integrated 2-bit kernel, and system pathways in which the memory footprint is greatly reduced. The lower weight bandwidth requirements improve DRAM-to-SRAM transfers, while the predominance of integer addition in matrix multiplication delivers the reported energy savings. Feature filtering is maintained at inference because it is encoded directly into the learned weights; the network continues to suppress certain connections through $0$ entries, preserving the trained representational structure without additional overhead.

# Broader Implications of Losing Feature Filtering

Removing $0$ from the weight set would make every connection active in the sense of adding or subtracting, erasing a core tool of control. In practice, this means the model would have fewer options to prevent undesirable contributions from specific features within a linear map, potentially increasing interference among signals. The paper’s insight that explicit feature filtering significantly improves performance in 1-bit LLMs suggests that reverting to $\{-1, 1\}$ would directly impair the model’s ability to achieve the reported parity with full precision, especially at model scales where BitNet b1.58 currently matches or exceeds FP16 baselines under comparable settings.

Moreover, BitNet b1.58’s vision extends to future developments such as long sequence support, Mixture-of-Experts architectures, edge and mobile deployment, and new hardware specialized for the 1-bit computation paradigm. While these areas primarily benefit from the overall efficiency of low-bit computation, their viability depends on maintaining strong modeling quality. Feature filtering contributes to such quality without compromising the efficiency profile, making the $0$ weight particularly consequential in practical deployments and scaling scenarios.

# Conclusion

BitNet b1.58’s ternary weights $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ are not merely a minor extension of binary weights; the inclusion of $0$ introduces explicit feature filtering, a central modeling capability that significantly improves the performance of 1-bit LLMs. If the model were restricted to $\{-1, 1\}$, the most directly lost capability would be precisely this feature filtering. In the low-bit computation paradigm, where each nonzero weight is a strong discrete action, the ability to set weights to $0$ enables the model to suppress unhelpful features, reduce interference, and better shape the representational flow through the Transformer. The paper’s results show that with this capability, BitNet b1.58 can match full-precision baselines from the 3B scale and deliver substantial efficiency gains in latency, memory, throughput, and energy.
