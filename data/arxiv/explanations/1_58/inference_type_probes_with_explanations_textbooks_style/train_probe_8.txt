Chapter: Understanding 1.58 Bits per Parameter in BitNet b1.58

# The Emergence of 1-bit and 1.58-bit Large Language Models

Large Language Models have grown rapidly in capability and size, but this growth has also amplified the latency, memory, energy, and deployment challenges inherent to full-precision computation. Traditional Transformer-based LLMs operate with 16-bit floating point parameters, and their dominant cost arises from matrix multiplication, which in turn is dominated by floating-point additions and multiplications. As model sizes increase, these costs become increasingly prohibitive for both training and inference, and they impose practical constraints on deployment, especially in environments with limited compute and memory resources.

A promising paradigm shift is to reduce numerical precision in the model architecture itself, rather than as a post-processing step. Prior trends have moved from 16-bit to lower-bit inference through post-training quantization, but these approaches are sub-optimal. An alternative is to design architectures that are fundamentally low-bit during training and inference. Within this family, BitNet-type models demonstrate that matrix multiplication can be implemented with integer additions, dramatically reducing arithmetic energy. In this setting, reducing parameter precision has a twofold benefit: it lowers the arithmetic energy per operation and reduces the memory footprint and bandwidth requirements for moving model parameters.

BitNet b1.58 is a particularly important representative of this line of work. It is a Transformer whose parameters are restricted to ternary values in the set \{-1, 0, +1\}. This ternary parameterization is the source of the “1.58-bit” characterization. BitNet b1.58 maintains the computational advantages of 1-bit BitNet while strengthening modeling capacity by explicitly allowing zero-valued weights, which support feature filtering. Empirically, beginning at the 3B scale and beyond, BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision baselines in perplexity and end-task performance under the same training tokens, while providing significant improvements in latency, memory, throughput, and energy efficiency.

# Why Ternary Weights Correspond to 1.58 Bits: The Role of $\log_2(3)$

The essence of the “1.58-bit” description lies in counting how many distinct values each parameter can assume. In BitNet b1.58, every weight takes one of three values: \{-1, 0, +1\}. When parameters are constrained to a discrete set of values, the number of bits required per parameter is governed by the number of distinct options. In binary systems, the number of bits reflects how many binary choices are needed to encode a given number of possibilities. If a parameter can take \(k\) distinct values, the number of bits needed to represent one such value is \(\log_2(k)\).

In BitNet b1.58, we have \(k = 3\) possible weight values, hence the bits per parameter is
\[
\log_2(3).
\]
Numerically, \(\log_2(3)\) is approximately \(1.58\). This value is not an arbitrary label; it is the precise binary information content of a ternary choice. It formalizes why allowing weights to be \(-1\), \(0\), or \(+1\) corresponds to roughly \(1.58\) bits per parameter in a binary encoding system. In other words, each ternary weight carries as much information as approximately \(1.58\) binary decisions.

This viewpoint clarifies the naming of BitNet b1.58: it is neither a 1-bit nor a 2-bit model in the literal binary sense. Rather, its parameters live in a ternary alphabet, and the exact binary-equivalent capacity is \(\log_2(3)\). In practice, systems typically store parameters in whole numbers of bits. However, from the standpoint of model design and information content, \(\log_2(3)\) captures the granularity of the parameter space and directly relates to the memory footprint and potential compression relative to full-precision models.

Beyond bookkeeping, the ternary alphabet has modeling implications. Moving from purely binary \(\{-1, +1\}\) to ternary \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\) adds the explicit capacity to zero out connections. This “feature filtering” capability strengthens the expressiveness of the architecture while keeping the information content per parameter at \(\log_2(3)\), and empirically this addition is key to matching full-precision baselines at larger scales.

# The BitNet b1.58 Architecture and Quantization

BitNet b1.58 adopts a Transformer backbone with LLaMA-alike components, including RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary positional embeddings, and removal of biases. Crucially, the model replaces standard dense linear layers with BitLinear layers and is trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. This design preserves the overall structure of modern LLMs while altering the numerical representation to achieve efficiency.

Constraining weights to \{-1, 0, +1\} is achieved via a specific quantization function. Let \(W\) be a weight matrix. The absmean quantization computes
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm} \sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
\]
scales by \(\gamma + \epsilon\), and then rounds and clips each entry to the nearest value in \([-1, 1]\) with integer rounding:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))).
\]
The outcome is a ternary matrix \(\widetilde{W}\) whose entries lie exactly in \{-1, 0, +1\}. This operation embodies the transition from continuous-valued weights to a discrete alphabet whose information content is captured by \(\log_2(3)\).

Activations are quantized to 8 bits with a per-token scaling to the symmetric range \([ -Q_b, Q_b ]\). Unlike earlier variants, there is no zero-point quantization in this design, which simplifies implementation and system-level optimization while retaining performance.

The net effect is a Transformer whose parameters are ternary and whose activations are 8-bit, preserving the familiar structure of LLaMA-like models while shifting the numerical regime to one where the dominant arithmetic is simple and energy-efficient.

# Computational Consequences of Ternary Weights

The computational cost of Transformer models is dominated by matrix multiplications inside linear layers. In full precision, these multiplications require floating-point additions and multiplications. In BitNet b1.58, the ternary weights convert these multiplications into operations that are effectively integer additions when implemented efficiently. Conceptually, multiplying by \(-1\), \(0\), or \(+1\) either negates, zeroes, or preserves an activation contribution; when paired with 8-bit activations and appropriate kernels, the dominant arithmetic reduces to integer additions.

This shift in arithmetic manifests in several ways. First, integer additions consume significantly less energy than floating-point multiplications and additions on contemporary hardware. Measurements based on established energy models show that, for matrix multiplications on 7nm chips, the arithmetic operations energy in BitNet b1.58 can be reduced by a factor of \(71.4\) relative to FP16 baselines. Second, because matrix multiplication is the primary cost driver in LLMs, reducing the cost of these operations yields noticeable wall-clock speedups. Empirically, BitNet b1.58 achieves speedups that grow with model size, reaching a factor of \(4.1\) at the 70B scale in direct comparisons with FP16 LLaMA-like baselines.

These gains are not merely theoretical. End-to-end inference latency improves substantially when using kernels tailored to low-bit operations. Even with a 2-bit kernel implementation, BitNet b1.58 demonstrates significant latency reductions compared to optimized FP16 baselines. As model size increases, the proportion of time spent in linear layers grows, amplifying the advantage of ternary weights and integer-addition-centric computation.

# Memory Footprint, Bandwidth, and the Meaning of 1.58 Bits

The memory benefits of BitNet b1.58 stem directly from the reduced information content per parameter. A full-precision parameter in FP16 encodes a continuous value in 16 bits. In contrast, a BitNet b1.58 parameter is selected from three values and carries \(\log_2(3)\) bits of information. While actual storage formats use whole numbers of bits and practical implementations may pack or align values differently, the conceptual compression ratio follows from the ratio between \(16\) and \(\log_2(3)\). This large reduction translates into smaller model checkpoints and dramatically reduced bandwidth needs for moving weights from DRAM to on-chip memory.

Inference further benefits because the time to load parameters can be a substantial component of latency. Lower memory bandwidth requirements help reduce this overhead, especially when deployment platforms have limited SRAM and must stream weights from DRAM. In measurements, BitNet b1.58 reduces runtime GPU memory by factors exceeding threefold at comparable model sizes, while simultaneously cutting latency. These improvements scale favorably with model size because embeddings remain in full precision and constitute a smaller fraction of total memory as the model grows.

This interplay between discrete parameter alphabets and system-level efficiency illustrates the practical significance of \(\log_2(3)\). The ternary constraint not only gives a precise bit-equivalent per parameter, it also unlocks tangible reductions in storage and transfer costs that compound across billions of parameters.

# Empirical Parity with Full Precision and the Benefits of Zero Weights

An essential question for any low-bit architecture is whether it preserves modeling quality. BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that the ternary alphabet can match full-precision LLaMA-like baselines in perplexity and zero-shot accuracy at scales beginning around 3B parameters when trained with the same number of tokens and comparable configurations. At 3.9B, BitNet b1.58 surpasses a 3B full-precision baseline while still being faster and using less memory. As the scale increases, performance parity is maintained while the efficiency gap widens in latency, memory, and energy.

A key factor is the inclusion of the zero weight. Moving from a purely binary alphabet \(\{-1, +1\}\) to a ternary alphabet \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\) adds explicit support for feature filtering. In practice, this allows the model to suppress unhelpful contributions by setting weights to \(0\), improving representational flexibility without abandoning the low-bit arithmetic regime. This improvement in modeling capability is achieved while preserving the 1.58-bit-per-parameter characterization determined by \(\log_2(3)\).

These properties carry over across a variety of tasks and scales. Evaluations across standard zero-shot benchmarks show narrowing performance gaps as model sizes increase, with BitNet b1.58 matching or exceeding full-precision baselines at relevant scales. When trained on larger token budgets, such as 2T tokens, BitNet b1.58 demonstrates strong generalization and superior end-task performance relative to a state-of-the-art 3B open-source baseline trained under the same token recipe.

# Scaling Laws and Practical Efficiency

BitNet b1.58 supports a new practical scaling relationship between model performance and inference cost. Empirical measurements indicate that larger 1.58-bit models can be more efficient than substantially smaller FP16 models across latency, memory usage, and energy consumption. For example, a 13B BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient than a 3B FP16 model, a 30B BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient than a 7B FP16 model, and a 70B BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient than a 13B FP16 model. These relationships arise because the cost dominated by linear layers scales with model size and benefits increasingly from the ternary, integer-addition-centric compute.

Throughput measurements reinforce this picture. On dual 80GB accelerators with pipeline parallelism, a 70B BitNet b1.58 model can accommodate much larger batch sizes than an FP16 baseline of the same parameter count, yielding nearly an order-of-magnitude higher throughput before exhausting memory limits. This throughput advantage ultimately traces back to the reduced information per parameter—quantified by \(\log_2(3)\)—which lightens both memory capacity and bandwidth demands.

Energy efficiency improves as well, both at the level of arithmetic operations and end-to-end inference. As models grow, the fraction of time and energy attributable to linear layers increases, which amplifies the benefits of moving from FP16 additions and multiplications to predominantly INT8 additions. Consequently, the overall energy per token decreases relative to FP16 baselines as the parameter count rises.

# Implications and Future Directions

BitNet b1.58 suggests several compelling directions for future work. First, sparse-activation architectures such as Mixture-of-Experts can benefit from the reduced memory footprint and communication overhead, potentially enabling deployment on fewer devices or even on a single chip when memory budgets allow. Second, the reduction of activation precision to 8 bits helps address the memory burden of long-sequence inference by shrinking KV caches; in the 1.58-bit regime, further lossless compression of activations may be possible, opening avenues for native long-context support. Third, the efficiency profile of BitNet b1.58 aligns well with edge and mobile constraints, where CPUs are prevalent and resource budgets are tight; lower memory, bandwidth, and energy demands expand the range of feasible on-device applications. Finally, the arithmetic characteristics of ternary-weight models invite hardware specialization. With the dominant operations being integer additions and with per-parameter information content of \(\log_2(3)\), new accelerators could be tailored to maximize throughput and energy efficiency for this computation regime.

At the conceptual core of these developments is the simple but powerful observation that constraining each parameter to three values yields an information content of \(\log_2(3)\) bits per parameter. This single quantity links the representational capacity of the parameter space to concrete system-level benefits in storage, bandwidth, arithmetic energy, and ultimately end-to-end performance. BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that with careful architectural and quantization design, one can realize these benefits without sacrificing model quality at scale.
