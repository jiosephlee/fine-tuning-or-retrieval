Chapter: Ensuring Fair Comparisons in 1.58-Bit Large Language Models

# Background: From Full Precision to 1.58 Bits

Large Language Models have advanced rapidly but at a significant computational and energy cost when trained and served in full precision, such as FP16 or BF16. The BitNet b1.58 model represents a different regime: it constrains every weight to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and trains from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. This design preserves the core Transformer architecture while replacing dense floating-point matrix multiplications with operations that can be implemented using integer addition. In doing so, it achieves substantial reductions in memory footprint, latency, and energy consumption, while maintaining accuracy comparable to full-precision baselines of the same size when trained on the same amount of data.

To enable broad adoption and comparability with established open-source systems, BitNet b1.58 incorporates LLaMA-alike components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary positional embeddings, and the removal of biases. It also adopts a specific quantization strategy for weights based on scaling by the average absolute value and then rounding and clipping to the ternary set, yielding $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}((W / (\gamma + \epsilon)), -1, 1)$ with $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$. For activations, it follows the BitNet approach but scales per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ to avoid zero-point quantization.

Against this backdrop, a central question arises: how should one compare a 1.58-bit LLM to an FP16 LLM in a way that is fair, credible, and interpretable? The paper addresses this by adhering to a strict experimental practice that controls confounding factors.

# The Principle of Fair Comparisons in Model Evaluation

When comparing models that differ in architecture, precision, and computation paradigms, it is easy to conflate gains due to better training data or recipes with gains due to the model’s intrinsic properties. The paper emphasizes a core experimental practice to ensure fairness: use the same pretraining data for both BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA baseline. By holding the data constant, the study isolates the effect of quantization and architectural modifications from the effect of data, allowing a meaningful assessment of whether the 1.58-bit formulation can truly match full-precision performance.

In practice, this means both models are pre-trained under the same data distribution and for the same token budget. The paper implements this by pre-training on the RedPajama dataset for $100$ billion tokens for both BitNet b1.58 and a reproduced FP16 LLaMA model. Moreover, the comparison explicitly aligns other configuration aspects, such as model size and training tokens, so that there is no advantage conferred by having more capacity or more exposure to data.

This approach yields comparisons where reported differences in perplexity, end-task accuracy, latency, memory, and energy can be attributed to the model design choices rather than to unequal pretraining conditions. In particular, the claim that BitNet b1.58 can match full precision starting from a $3$B size is grounded in experiments that remove data as a variable, reinforcing the validity of the observation.

# Implementing Data Parity: RedPajama and Token Budgets

The paper’s experimental setup fixes both the corpus and the token count across models. Specifically, both the BitNet b1.58 models and the FP16 LLaMA baselines are pre-trained on the RedPajama dataset for $100$ billion tokens. This decision ensures that each model sees the same distribution of examples and the same total amount of training signal measured in tokens.

The token count, denoted as a budget of $100$ billion tokens, is central. If one model were trained with a larger token budget than another, differences in learned representations, generalization, and robustness could be influenced heavily by data volume alone. The paper avoids this confound by matching the token count exactly. In addition, the text explicitly notes that claims of parity with full precision are made “when using the same configuration (e.g., model size, training tokens, etc.),” underscoring a broader commitment to controlled comparisons. By fixing both the data source and the token count, the experiments prevent unwarranted attributions of performance to the quantization regime when they could otherwise be explained by data advantages.

This methodological choice is also reflected in scaling observations. As model size grows, the study maintains consistent measurement protocols across systems, enabling statements such as BitNet b1.58 $70$B being $4.1\times$ faster in latency than the FP16 baseline to be interpreted in the context of matched training conditions rather than disparate data regimes.

# Evaluation Protocols Under Controlled Data Conditions

Fixing pretraining data supports a suite of evaluations that gauge both language modeling quality and downstream task performance. The paper evaluates zero-shot accuracy on a range of tasks, including ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ. It also reports validation perplexity on WikiText2 and C4. Under identical pretraining data, these metrics become meaningful comparators of model capability across the 1.58-bit and FP16 paradigms.

Runtime and system-level measurements are similarly conducted under consistent and transparent conditions. Latency and memory are measured using the FasterTransformer codebase, which is optimized for LLM inference latency on GPU devices. For BitNet b1.58, a 2-bit kernel from Ladder is integrated, and the key metric is time per output token, reflecting the dominant cost in inference. By applying the same measurement tools and by explicitly stating the use of the 2-bit kernel across reported latency and memory, the experiments avoid skewing the results in favor of one model due to unreported system optimizations.

Energy analysis focuses on arithmetic operations for matrix multiplication, the dominant component in LLM computation. The composition of energy for BitNet b1.58 is primarily INT8 additions, whereas the FP16 baseline involves FP16 additions and multiplications. The study reports that BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4\times$ arithmetic operations energy for matrix multiplication on $7$nm chips according to a cited energy model. Critically, because both models are trained on the same data and compared under consistent evaluation regimens, these energy savings can be connected to the model’s quantization and architecture rather than differences in learned function due to unequal pretraining data.

Throughput experiments at scale further control for deployment conditions. On two $80$GB A100 GPUs, using pipeline parallelism to fit a $70$B FP16 LLaMA baseline, BitNet b1.58 $70$B supports up to $11\times$ the batch size and achieves $8.9\times$ higher throughput. These results, interpreted alongside equal pretraining data, lend credence to the conclusion that the observed efficiency does not come at the expense of generalization performance attributable to data differences.

# Why Matching Pretraining Data Matters in This Study

The core claim of the work is that a 1.58-bit LLM with ternary weights can match full-precision LLMs in perplexity and zero-shot task accuracy from the $3$B scale onward, while offering substantial gains in latency, memory, throughput, and energy. Such a claim demands a rigorous experimental foundation because accuracy is sensitive to not only architecture and precision but also to the data used in pretraining.

If BitNet b1.58 had been trained on a different dataset or with a different token count than the FP16 LLaMA baseline, any parity or superiority in results could be attributed, at least in part, to data disparity. By fixing the pretraining corpus to RedPajama and matching the token count at $100$ billion across configurations, the study ensures that model performance reflects differences in how the architectures learn from the same information rather than differences in the information itself.

This is particularly important given the paper’s broader claims about a new scaling law for cost and performance. The stated equivalences, such as a $70$B BitNet b1.58 being more efficient than a $13$B FP16 LLM in latency, memory, and energy, are compelling only when the training data and token budgets are aligned. Otherwise, differences in pretraining could obfuscate whether cost efficiencies are achieved at comparable levels of model quality. Fixing pretraining data thus elevates the conclusions from suggestive to persuasive within the scope of the study’s methodology.

# From Data Parity to Interpretable Results

The practice of using the same pretraining data produces interpretable trajectories as model size increases. The paper notes that the performance gap between BitNet b1.58 and FP16 LLaMA narrows with larger models and that parity emerges at the $3$B scale under the matched setup. It also reports that a BitNet b1.58 model of $3.9$B parameters can outperform an FP16 LLaMA model of $3$B parameters in end-task performance while being faster and more memory-efficient. Because both are trained on the same data for the same number of tokens, these cross-size comparisons are not confounded by data advantages and can be understood as consequences of representational capacity, quantization-aware training dynamics, and architectural choices like explicit support for zero weights enabling feature filtering.

The paper extends the analysis to larger scales, presenting latency and memory trends up to $70$B parameters and documenting that speed-ups increase with size. The energy analysis similarly shows that efficiency gains compound as the percentage of computation devoted to linear layers grows with model size. These scaling observations rest on the foundation of a matched-data experimental design; without it, the trends could be dismissed as artifacts of different data regimes.

# A Controlled Extension: Training with 2T Tokens

The study also explores token scaling by training a BitNet b1.58 model with $2$ trillion tokens following a specific data recipe and comparing it to a state-of-the-art $3$B model trained with the same token budget. While this experiment targets generalization at large token counts, its design reflects the same fairness ethos: align the token budget and follow a defined recipe to enable meaningful comparisons. The reported superior performance of BitNet b1.58 on a benchmark including Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC-easy demonstrates that the benefits of the 1.58-bit regime extend to regimes with vast training exposure, again supported by controlled training conditions.

# Conclusion: Data Parity as the Basis for Credible Claims

The paper’s assertion that BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision performance from the $3$B scale onward while delivering substantial efficiency gains depends critically on the experimental practice of using the same pretraining data for both the 1.58-bit and FP16 baselines. By pre-training on RedPajama for $100$ billion tokens for both, and by aligning configurations such as model size and training tokens, the study ensures that differences in outcomes reflect model properties rather than data discrepancies.

This methodological discipline permeates the evaluation pipeline, from zero-shot tasks and perplexity to latency, memory, energy, and throughput measurements. The resulting evidence supports a coherent narrative: under matched pretraining data, BitNet b1.58 delivers a Pareto improvement in cost metrics without sacrificing model quality, and its advantages grow with scale.
