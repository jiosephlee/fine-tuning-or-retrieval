Chapter Title: Achieving Performance Parity with FP16 Baselines in 1.58-bit LLMs

# Framing Performance Parity: Equal Model Size and Token Count

A central premise in the paper “The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits” is that performance parity between a 1.58-bit Large Language Model and a full-precision FP16 baseline is assessed under matched conditions. Specifically, BitNet b1.58 is compared to FP16 Transformer LLMs with the same model size and the same number of training tokens. This assumption directly shapes the methodology and interpretation of results: when the architecture, parameter count, and data scale are aligned, the measured differences reflect the computational regime (1.58-bit versus FP16) rather than confounding factors such as larger training corpora or more parameters.

The paper’s experimental setup exemplifies this principle. The authors trained BitNet b1.58 and their reproduced FP16 LLaMA-alike models on 100 billion tokens from the same dataset and evaluated them using consistent protocols. Under these conditions, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 performance starting from a 3B parameter scale in terms of both perplexity and end-task accuracy. By holding model size and token count constant, the comparison isolates the impact of the 1.58-bit quantization and associated architectural choices, thereby enabling a clear attribution of observed parity to the modeling and computation paradigm rather than to training scale or data differences.

This framing also clarifies the meaning of claims such as “BitNet b1.58 can match full precision baselines.” Throughout the paper, “matching” refers to achieving equivalent performance metrics—perplexity and zero-shot accuracy—under identical configurations, including parameter count and training tokens. In turn, the cost advantages in latency, memory, throughput, and energy are measured relative to this controlled parity, demonstrating that reductions in precision to ternary weights do not inherently reduce model quality when the training and architectural conditions are appropriately designed.

# Architectural Choices That Enable Comparable Capacity

Ensuring that a 1.58-bit LLM can fairly compete with an FP16 baseline at the same scale requires careful architectural decisions. BitNet b1.58 is built on the BitNet approach, which replaces standard linear layers (\emph{nn.Linear}) with a specialized \emph{BitLinear}, and constrains the weights to ternary values in the set $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. The “1.58-bit” designation arises from the ternary representation: while binary weights have two states, the inclusion of $0$ introduces a third state and strengthens modeling capacity via explicit feature filtering.

To implement this ternary constraint, the model uses an absmean-based quantization function. For a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, the average absolute value is
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm} \sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
\]
and the quantized weights are computed as
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\!\big(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))\big).
\]
This mechanism scales the weights by $\gamma$ and then rounds them to the nearest integer in the allowable set, clipping to remain within $[-1, 1]$. The inclusion of $0$ is not merely a symbolic addition; it enables weights to precisely filter features by “turning off” certain connections, improving representational efficiency relative to pure binary $\{-1, +1\}$ weights.

In addition, activations are quantized to 8 bits, with per-token scaling to the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$. Unlike the original BitNet activation strategy, zero-point quantization is avoided by symmetric scaling, simplifying implementation while maintaining performance in experiments. The choice of 8-bit activations is salient: it supports the overall efficiency goals while preserving sufficient dynamic range to train the model end-to-end from scratch.

Crucially, the model adopts LLaMA-alike components—RMSNorm, SwiGLU feedforward blocks, rotary position embeddings, and removal of biases—to ensure a comparable backbone to widely used open-source FP16 LLM baselines. This alignment reduces architectural discrepancies that might otherwise obscure the effect of precision changes. By using a Transformer architecture recognizable to the community and integrable into common LLM toolchains, the comparisons remain faithful to practical deployments while ensuring that performance differences are attributable to precision and training methodology, not to divergent designs.

# Training Under Controlled Conditions: Matched Tokens

The paper’s training methodology underscores the importance of equal token counts for fair comparisons. BitNet b1.58 and the reproduced FP16 LLaMA models are trained on the RedPajama dataset for 100 billion tokens. The evaluation is conducted under a standard zero-shot protocol across multiple datasets, and perplexity is measured on established corpora. These choices lead to stable metrics that depend primarily on the learning regime and model capacity rather than on training scale advantages.

The reported results reveal a critical threshold: BitNet b1.58 begins to match the FP16 baseline at the 3B parameter scale. This observation would be ambiguous without the equal token count constraint. If one model were trained on more tokens or a different corpus, a performance gap could arise from data differences rather than the underlying modeling assumptions. By matching the training tokens, the paper shows that ternary weights with 8-bit activations, under a well-specified quantization and architecture, can achieve comparable generalization quality to FP16 at the same size.

The paper also explores training at larger token counts in a separate experiment, using a 2T-token data recipe for a 3B-scale model. Although this experiment targets generalization capability and compares against an external 3B model trained with a similar recipe, the principle remains intact: performance comparisons are meaningful when token budgets are aligned and the evaluation follows consistent procedures. In all cases, the methodology emphasizes that claims of parity are grounded in experiments where the token count is held constant between the models being compared.

# Empirical Evidence of Parity at Matching Scale

Under matched model sizes and token counts, the paper reports parity in two complementary measures: validation perplexity and zero-shot end-task accuracy. For perplexity, BitNet b1.58 achieves values comparable to the FP16 baselines starting from the 3B scale, with explicit mention that a 3.9B BitNet b1.58 surpasses a 3B FP16 LLaMA-alike model while being more efficient in latency and memory. For zero-shot tasks—ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ—the performance gap narrows with increasing model size, and parity is observed starting at 3B.

These findings rely on the controlled training setup. The models are trained on the same 100B tokens, and inference measurements use optimized implementations, including a 2-bit kernel integration for BitNet b1.58. The authors report time per output token, memory consumption, and energy metrics, emphasizing that BitNet b1.58 retains performance at matched sizes while delivering substantial efficiency gains.

The result is a complete picture at matched conditions: if the model size and token count are the same, BitNet b1.58 achieves competitive perplexity and task accuracy, and simultaneously exhibits improvements in latency, memory footprint, and energy consumption. The paper demonstrates that parity in quality is compatible with superiority in cost metrics when precision is reduced to 1.58 bits and the training is carefully designed.

# Cost Advantages Measured at Equal Capacity

With equal model size and token count, cost metrics reveal the core rationale for pursuing 1.58-bit LLMs. Inference latency improves significantly, with the speed-up increasing as model size grows. BitNet b1.58 at 70B parameters is reported to be 4.1 times faster than the FP16 baseline. Memory usage likewise declines because the dominant parameter storage shifts from 16-bit to ternary weights, and activations are reduced to 8 bits. In experiments, BitNet b1.58 uses up to 3.55 times less GPU memory in the mid-scale regime and scales favorably at larger sizes.

Energy consumption is analyzed by focusing on matrix multiplication, the dominant computational component in Transformers. BitNet b1.58 performs primarily INT8 addition operations, avoiding the FP16 multiplications that dominate energy cost in full-precision models. Based on the cited energy model, the paper reports a 71.4-fold reduction in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips. End-to-end energy measurements with 512-token sequences indicate that the efficiency advantage grows as model size increases, because the proportion of time spent in \emph{nn.Linear} layers becomes larger for bigger models.

Throughput measurements reinforce this story at matched parameter count. At 70B scale, BitNet b1.58 supports substantially larger batch sizes and achieves an 8.9-fold increase in throughput over the FP16 baseline when run with pipeline parallelism on the same hardware, demonstrating that efficiency gains translate into practical acceleration at inference scale.

# Interpreting Scaling Laws Under Matched Assumptions

The paper articulates a scaling law that connects model size, performance, and inference cost under the 1.58-bit computation paradigm. These statements are anchored in equivalence relationships showing that larger BitNet b1.58 models can be more efficient than much smaller FP16 baselines when measured in latency, memory usage, and energy consumption. For example, a 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 3B FP16 LLM, and a 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM in these cost metrics.

These scaling law observations presuppose that comparisons are made between models with matched architectural roles and workloads. While the scaling law focuses on efficiency rather than performance parity, the paper’s earlier findings—parity at 3B when trained on the same 100B tokens—provide the performance foundation for such cost analyses. In other words, once parity is established at matched size and token count, one can meaningfully compare how efficiency scales with size across precision regimes. This perspective makes clear why the assumption of equal model size and token count is central: it secures a fair baseline for performance, allowing the scaling law of efficiency to be interpreted without conflating precision effects with training scale or capacity differences.

# Implications for Design and Deployment Under Matched Conditions

By demonstrating that BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 performance starting from a 3B scale under the same model size and token count, the paper establishes a practical recipe for training cost-effective LLMs. The model’s ternary weights, quantized activations, and LLaMA-alike architectural components ensure that comparisons reflect the precision shift rather than architectural departures. This creates a consistent pathway for training and evaluating future 1.58-bit LLMs, where the expectation of parity is grounded in matched configurations.

The downstream implications discussed in the paper—improvements in memory footprint, latency, energy, and throughput—follow naturally from this matched-performance premise. As model sizes grow, BitNet b1.58 enables more efficient inference while maintaining accuracy, opening opportunities in scenarios where full-precision models are limited by hardware constraints. The paper highlights avenues such as mixture-of-experts architectures, native long-sequence support via reduced activation precision, and deployment on edge and mobile devices, all of which inherit feasibility from the foundational result: when trained at the same size and token count, a 1.58-bit LLM can perform on par with FP16 while delivering large cost advantages.

In summary, the assumption of equal model size and token count is not just a methodological detail but the backbone of the paper’s claims. It allows the authors to assert that BitNet b1.58 achieves parity in perplexity and end-task performance at the 3B scale, and then to quantify substantial and scalable efficiency gains. This establishes a clear and reproducible benchmark for future work: parity is achieved under matched conditions, and from that baseline, 1.58-bit LLMs define a new scaling law that is both high-performance and cost-effective.
