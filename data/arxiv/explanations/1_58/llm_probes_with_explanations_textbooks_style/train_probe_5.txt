Chapter Title: Power as the Bridge from Energy Efficiency to Faster Large Language Models

# The Landscape of 1.58-bit Large Language Models

The rapid growth of Large Language Models (LLMs) has delivered remarkable capability but at a steep computational and energy cost. Conventional LLMs use 16-bit floating-point parameters and activations (e.g., FP16 or BF16), and their core workload is dominated by matrix multiplications inside Transformer layers. This workload is expensive because it relies heavily on floating-point additions and multiplications, both of which carry substantial energy costs. The increasing resource demands impose challenges for deployment, including memory capacity, memory bandwidth, and energy consumption, which in turn constrain throughput and latency.

A fundamentally different path comes from architectures that train and run natively at 1–2 bits. BitNet b1.58 is a 1.58-bit variant in which every parameter of the model is ternary, taking values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. It is trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. Despite the drastically reduced precision, BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision (FP16 or BF16) Transformers of the same size and trained on the same number of tokens in both perplexity and zero-shot end-task performance, starting at scales of approximately 3 billion parameters. Beyond accuracy, BitNet b1.58 is significantly more cost-effective: it reduces latency, memory footprint, throughput limits, and energy consumption relative to FP16 LLM baselines.

This shift is not merely a matter of bit-width; it introduces a different computation paradigm. In BitNet b1.58, matrix multiplications essentially reduce to integer additions. The architecture explicitly quantizes weights via an absmean scaling-and-rounding function so that they lie in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, and it adopts 8-bit activations with per-token scaling to symmetric ranges $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, avoiding zero-point offsets. In aggregate, the model eliminates most multiplications and relies predominantly on highly efficient integer addition. As a result, it consumes far less energy in arithmetic operations than FP16 counterparts. Crucially, because the fundamental limit to compute performance in many chips is power, these energy savings can be translated into faster computation. This power-centric perspective is the key to understanding why reducing energy per operation enables higher throughput.

# BitNet b1.58: Architecture and Quantization for Efficient Computation

BitNet b1.58 builds directly on the BitNet family of 1-bit architectures but incorporates a zero weight, moving from binary to ternary parameters $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This addition strengthens modeling capacity through explicit feature filtering: weights of $0$ can suppress features, yielding representational benefits over strictly $\{-1, +1\}$ weights, while preserving the same energy profile and computational simplicity as the 1-bit design.

The model replaces standard dense layers with BitLinear layers and is trained end-to-end with low-precision constraints. Weights are quantized using an absmean function. If $W$ is a weight matrix, the quantization scales by the average absolute value
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|
\]
and then rounds and clips to the nearest value in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\quad
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\!\big(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))\big).
\]
Activations are quantized similarly to BitNet, except they are scaled symmetrically to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token to avoid zero-points, simplifying implementation and system-level optimization with negligible performance impact in experiments.

To maintain compatibility with the thriving ecosystem of open-source LLMs, BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components. It uses RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary position embeddings, and removes all biases. This design allows seamless integration into mature software stacks such as Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp, while departing fundamentally from FP16 computation in how arithmetic is carried out at the kernel level.

# From Floating-Point Multiplications to Integer Additions

The computational cost of LLMs is dominated by matrix multiplications inside attention and feedforward layers. In FP16 models, these multiplications rely on floating-point additions and multiplications. In contrast, BitNet b1.58 reorganizes the arithmetic so that matrix multiplication reduces largely to integer addition, with weights in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and activations in 8-bit integers. The 2-bit kernel integrated from Ladder for inference demonstrates how low-bit kernels can capitalize on this structure.

This shift dramatically lowers the energy cost per arithmetic operation. According to an energy model cited in the work, BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4\times$ arithmetic operations energy for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips compared to FP16 LLaMA baselines. A significant reason is that integer additions are far cheaper than floating-point multiplications and additions in terms of energy. Since matrix multiplication accounts for the bulk of computation in LLMs, reducing its energy footprint yields large end-to-end benefits as model size scales.

BitNet b1.58 also reduces the cost of moving data. Transferring parameters from DRAM to on-chip memory is a major overhead during inference. With 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations, BitNet b1.58 cuts both the capacity and bandwidth demands. This reduces memory transfer time and can alleviate bottlenecks that would otherwise dominate latency in memory-bound regimes. Furthermore, embeddings remain in full precision, but as models scale, embeddings constitute a smaller fraction of total memory, so overall memory savings grow with size.

# Power as the Fundamental Performance Constraint

The crucial link between energy efficiency and speed is the hardware reality that, in many chips, compute performance is fundamentally limited by power. The paper states this explicitly: as the fundamental limit to compute performance in many chips is power, the energy savings can also be translated into faster computation. This insight connects per-operation energy to achievable throughput under a fixed power budget.

When each arithmetic operation consumes less energy, a system operating under the same power envelope can perform more operations in the same time or run the same workload faster. In other words, reduced energy per operation relaxes the power-limited ceiling on computational activity. Because BitNet b1.58 replaces FP16 floating-point arithmetic with far cheaper integer additions and slashes the energy cost of matrix multiplications, it can execute more efficiently within the same power constraints, enabling lower latency and higher throughput.

This power-limited view also clarifies why benefits scale with model size. As models grow, the proportion of time and energy spent in matrix multiplications increases, while other components dominate less. Since BitNet b1.58 makes matrix multiplications particularly cheap, the share of end-to-end runtime and energy that it improves becomes larger at scale, allowing the power constraints to be exploited to achieve even higher relative speedups.

# Translating Energy Savings into Latency and Throughput Gains

The empirical results demonstrate that energy savings enabled by BitNet b1.58 translate into concrete performance gains. The model is consistently faster and more memory efficient than its FP16 LLaMA counterparts across sizes, with the speedup increasing as the model scales.

At around 3 billion parameters, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 perplexity and zero-shot task accuracy while being $2.71\times$ faster and using $3.55\times$ less GPU memory. A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model outperforms an FP16 3B baseline while being $2.4\times$ faster and using $3.32\times$ less memory. When scaled to 70B parameters, BitNet b1.58 is $4.1\times$ faster than the FP16 baseline under the same measurement framework. These improvements arise under kernels that already use low-bit implementations, suggesting that further system-level optimization remains possible.

Throughput measurements on two 80GB A100 GPUs with pipeline parallelism reinforce the point. With a sequence length of $512$ and batch size increased up to the memory limit, a 70B BitNet b1.58 model supports up to $11\times$ the batch size of the FP16 baseline, yielding an $8.9\times$ higher throughput. From the perspective of power-limited performance, the lower per-operation energy enables more concurrent or faster operations within the same power envelope, and the reduced memory footprint allows larger batches before saturation.

Energy analysis focused on matrix multiplications shows that BitNet b1.58 replaces FP16 additions and multiplications with predominantly INT8 additions. According to the cited energy model, this yields a $71.4\times$ reduction in arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips. End-to-end energy measurements with $512$-token sequences show that the efficiency gap widens with model size, consistent with the growth of the matrix multiplication share of total cost. This scaling behavior is precisely what one would expect if power is the fundamental limit and matrix multiplications are the major energy sink.

# Memory, Bandwidth, and Their Role in Performance

While arithmetic energy is central, memory capacity and bandwidth also play a crucial role in inference performance. Transferring weights from DRAM to on-chip memory can dominate both time and energy. BitNet b1.58 reduces the memory footprint of model parameters and activations drastically, shrinking both capacity and bandwidth requirements. This reduces the time to load weights and the pressure on memory systems during inference, which in turn reduces latency and increases throughput in memory-bound regimes.

The reduction of activations from 16 bits to 8 bits also has a multiplicative effect on the feasibility of long-sequence inference. By halving activation precision, BitNet b1.58 allows doubling the context length under the same resource constraints. The paper further notes that these activations can be losslessly compressed to 4 bits or even lower for 1.58-bit LLMs, suggesting additional headroom for memory and bandwidth savings. As with arithmetic energy, reductions in memory energy and bandwidth translate into better exploitation of the available power to accelerate useful computation.

# A New Scaling Law for Performance and Cost

BitNet b1.58 enables a new practical scaling law that relates model size to inference cost under power constraints. The paper summarizes equivalences that illustrate the point. A 13B BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient in latency, memory usage, and energy consumption than a 3B FP16 LLM. Similarly, a 30B BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM, and a 70B BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM, in the same dimensions. These equivalences reflect how lowering per-operation energy consumption unlocks higher performance per unit of power and per unit of memory bandwidth, especially as model sizes grow and matrix multiplications dominate cost.

The training side also supports this trajectory. Training a BitNet b1.58 model with 2T tokens following a state-of-the-art 3B data recipe yields superior zero-shot performance across multiple tasks compared to the FP16 baseline trained under the same token budget. This suggests that low-bit models do not just save energy; they scale in a way that preserves or even improves generalization under comparable training conditions.

# Implications for Systems and Hardware

The power-limited view of performance has direct implications for system design and hardware co-optimization. Because BitNet b1.58 defines a computation paradigm that relies almost entirely on integer additions, it paves the way for inference kernels and accelerators that are optimized specifically for 1–2 bit arithmetic and 8-bit activations. The paper highlights that 1.58-bit LLMs open the door to hardware that directly targets this new paradigm.

Reductions in model and activation precision alleviate inter-chip communication and memory bottlenecks, which is especially valuable for architectures such as Mixture-of-Experts that can otherwise be constrained by memory capacity and network overhead. With smaller memory footprints, models can fit on fewer devices or even a single chip, eliminating communication overheads entirely. For long-sequence inference, reducing activation precision directly reduces the burden of key–value caches, enabling longer contexts under the same resource limits.

Finally, by aligning computation with low-power arithmetic operations, BitNet b1.58 makes LLMs more amenable to edge and mobile devices, where CPUs and limited power budgets are prevalent. The lower energy and memory requirements allow deployment in environments that were previously impractical. While the paper calls for future work on specialized hardware tuned to 1-bit and 1.58-bit computation, the overarching principle is already clear: when power is the fundamental limit, reducing energy per operation is the lever that turns efficiency into speed.

# Conclusion: Power as the Fundamental Link

BitNet b1.58 shows that 1.58-bit LLMs can match the performance of full-precision models while drastically reducing energy, memory, and latency. The unifying reason these benefits translate into faster computation is the fundamental hardware constraint that compute performance in many chips is limited by power. By converting floating-point-heavy matrix multiplications into mostly integer additions and by compressing both weights and activations, BitNet b1.58 reduces the energy per operation and the energy per byte moved. Under a fixed power envelope, these reductions allow more operations per unit time and less time lost to memory transfers, yielding higher throughput and lower latency. As models scale, the advantages compound, establishing a new scaling law where larger low-bit models are more efficient than much smaller FP16 models across latency, memory, and energy consumption.
