# Introduction to Few‑Shot Tabular Learning  

In many real‑world settings a practitioner must build predictive models from only a handful of labelled examples.  This scenario, known as *few‑shot* learning, arises when data collection is expensive, dangerous, or ethically constrained: hospitals record millions of patient visits but only a small subset of those entries receive expert diagnoses; banks only annotate a small fraction of customer interactions for fraud; researchers may have only a few samples from a novel biological assay.  When the dataset size falls below a few dozen points the risk of overfitting spikes and statistical estimators become unreliable.  Traditional supervised learning algorithms—logistic regression, random forests, gradient boosted trees—are designed to exploit large training sets; with low‑shot data they either underfit (due to insufficient signal) or overfit (due to the high dimensionality of tabular feature spaces).

## What is tabular data and its prevalence in industry  

Tabular data consist of rows (instances) and columns (attributes or features).  Each feature is often typed as numeric, categorical, or binary.  Compared to structured text or unstructured images, tabular data are ubiquitous:   
- Healthcare records (electronic health records, claims data).  
- Finance and business records (credit applications, transaction histories).  
- Scientific instrument logs (sensor readings, genomic assays).  
- Social science surveys.  

Because each column can come from disparate sources—a mixture of free text fields, continuous measurements, coded categories—standard machine‑learning pipelines require elaborate preprocessing (encoding, imputation, scaling) before any model can be trained.  Many domain experts possess substantive knowledge that could guide feature engineering more efficiently than automated pipelines.

## Limitations of traditional supervised models in low‑shot regimes  

Conventional methods rely on sufficient training examples to learn reliable parameter estimates.  In the high‑dimensional space typical of tabular datasets (from three to several hundred features) the number of trainable parameters can easily exceed the number of observations when employing tree ensembles or gradient boosting.  Random forests mitigate overfitting by aggregating many shallow trees but still require hundreds or thousands of trees for stable predictions; boosting cumulatively fits many models which can destabilize when the signal is weak.  

Logistic regression and other linear models live in a smaller parameter space but typically assume linear separability after encoding―an assumption that rarely holds for complex medical or financial outcomes with nonlinear interactions between features.  Moreover, handcrafted feature transformations are usually required to reach any reasonable performance—time-consuming work that defeats the purpose of quickly building models with limited data.

## The promise of LLMs: prior knowledge, reasoning and chain‑of‑thought  

Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT‑4 and PaLM are trained on billions of words and therefore aggregate a vast reservoir of *prior knowledge*—world facts, causal heuristics, domain‐specific associations that go beyond whatever information appears in a single dataset.  When given a prompt that describes a prediction problem *and* includes a few labeled examples (§ 1.1), an LLM can employ *chain‑of‑thought* reasoning: it produces intermediate explanations that resemble steps humans follow when tackling unfamiliar tasks.

This ability means an LLM could:

1. **Infer likely causal relationships** between features and target classes even when those relationships are not strongly reflected in the few training samples.
2. **Generate structured rules** such as “if age > 60 AND cholesterol > 200 then risk is high”.
3. **Translate natural language descriptions** of columns into encoding schemes (e.g., “blood pressure measured in mmHg”) without explicit human effort.

In principle this positions LLMs as powerful feature engineers—capable of producing succinct predictors from minimal labelled data.

## Existing LLM‑based tabular methods and their shortcomings  

Several recent research efforts have explored feeding tabular data directly into LLMs:

| Approach | Paradigm | Limitations |
|----------|----------|-------------|
| **In‑context learning** | Add serialized training rows as examples directly into the prompt.| Each new prediction requires an inference call over all example rows → **high latency**, **costly API budget** |
| **LoRA/IA3 fine‑tuning** | Parameter‐efficient fine tuning on pre‑trained checkpoints.| Requires access to fine‑tuning APIs or custom weights → infeasible for locked LLMs like GPT‑4 |
| **Prompt serialization tricks** | Huffman coding and other chunking strategies.| Performance degrades as input grows beyond tokenizer limits → struggles with datasets having >100 features |

Moreover, most techniques treat every column equally once encoded into text; if inputs grow longer than the model’s context window, you must truncate salient features or drop columns entirely—both operations potentially discard critical information and hurt performance.

## Motivation for a feature‑engineering approach – FeatLLM  

Rather than performing end‑to‑end inference on each test instance through an LLM—a prohibitively expensive strategy—\textsf{FeatLLM} reframes the problem:

1. **Ask the LLM for rules:** Provide it with a task statement and see which logical conditions it proposes to distinguish classes.
2. **Turn those rules into binary features:** Each rule becomes one binary column indicating whether the rule holds for an observation.
3. **Train a lightweight downstream model** on these new features using standard algorithms (e.g., linear regression with constraints).

This approach harnesses three desirable properties:

- **Zero nightly inference cost:** Once rules are extracted offline during training/feature engineering we only need basic matrix operations at deployment time.
- **No fine‑tuning required:** The LLM is called only in-context during the feature generation stage; we never update its weights.
- **Scalable prompt length:** By bounding rule length and bagging subsets of columns we keep prompts well within token limits even for high–dimensional tables.

On top of these pragmatic benefits, \textsf{FeatLLM} benefits from inheriting *prior knowledge* encoded inside the LLM—that enables it to propose more semantically meaningful rules than would be possible from random subsets of few shots alone.

---

### Summary

Few-shot tabular learning faces unique challenges—notably sparse labeled data coupled with high feature dimensionality—making conventional methods fragile.  Large Language Models open promising avenues because they carry vast amounts of background knowledge; however most prior attempts expend excessive cost during inference or queue heavy prompt demands from every sample.  The key insight driving \textsf{FeatLLM} is that LLMs can serve not as predictive black boxes but as instructive rule generators: they analyse feature–label relationships in context while respecting prior knowledge, output logical criteria that can be automatically translated into features usable by fast ML pipelines.  This chapter has reviewed these motivations systematically—from industry necessities to algorithmic constraints—and has set the stage for deeper methodological exploration in subsequent sections where \textsf{FeatLLM} is formally defined and evaluated against state‑of‑the–art baselines across diverse domains.

# 2. Fundamentals of Few‑Shot Learning and Data Augmentation

In many real‑world applications—particularly in medical diagnostics, credit scoring, or fraud detection—the collection of a large, high‑quality labeled data set is prohibitively expensive or simply impossible. The deep neural network zoo is replete with models that thrive on millions of annotated examples, yet a growing body of work has focused specifically on learning **from a handful of labeled points**. This chapter delves into the conceptual foundations that underpin few‑shot tabular learning, the statistical perils that can arise when data are scarce, and the suite of techniques that have been proposed to mitigate these challenges. Importantly, we stay tightly bound to the methods and findings reported in *Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning*, using its discussion as the canonical source.

---

## Definition of Few‑Shot Learning: Extrema from One to Ten Labeled Samples

Few‑shot learning refers to any setting where a predictive model must generalise from an exceptionally small number of labelled instances. In the context of the FeatLLM study, experiments were conducted with **\(k\)-shot regimes where \(k \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16\}\)**—well within what most practitioners would label as “low data”. The baseline methods span ten variants: purely supervised learners (logistic regression, XGBoost, Random Forest), semi‑supervised organisers (SCARF), transfer‑learning frameworks (TabPFN), and a panoply of LLM‑based methods (in‑context learning, TABLET, TabLLM). Across all datasets in the paper (13 binary or multi‑class tabular problems such as Adult income prediction or Heart failure diagnosis), the experiments uniformly sought to train with a *single* training split and evaluate on a held‐out test set.

The starkly low number of labelled samples forces models to generalise from expressions that may be highly idiosyncratic. In other words: **a single observation can dominate the learned decision boundary** if the model is not equipped with additional inductive bias or priors that exhibit directionality beyond sheer memorisation.

---

## Risk of Spurious Correlations in Low Data Regimes

Feasible data volumes yield sparse coverage across many feature–target combinations. When training on only one or two examples per class—as is explicitly tested by FeatLLM—a model may pick up arbitrary patterns that *wouldn’t generalise* to new samples. The authors quote recent work highlighting this brittleness:

> “Learning from a small number of labeled samples presents the risk of learning **spurious correlations**” \cite{bartlett2021deep}.

In their own ablation studies (Table 4), FeatLLM’s ablation on *Reasoning* and *Description* shows large performance drops at 1–4 shots; these components serve as safeguards against falling prey to such accidental associations by explicitly guiding an LLM toward “causal relations”, not surface statistics.

Yet spurious correlation remains unavoidable unless we introduce strong inductive priors or further data forms—therein lie the motivations for unsupervised pretraining and transfer learning approaches discussed next.

---

## Common Unsupervised Pretraining Objectives for Tabular Data  

In vision and NLP it is routine to pretrain a network by **masking** or **corrupting** some input tokens and reconstructing them (e.g., CLIP’s image–text alignment). Tabular pretraining has attempted analogous mechanisms:

| Approach | Mechanism | Key Insight | Origin in Paper |
|---------|-----------|------------|------------------|
| **TabNet** \cite{arik2021tabnet} | Masking or corrupting partly fed features; reconstruction loss | Highlights importance through attentive feature selection | Discussed under “learning representations via masking” |
| **Contrastive Learned Embeddings** (SCARF) \cite{bahri2022scarf} | Generate synthetic pairs; train for high intra‐class similarity | Encourages separation between corpora without labels | “synthetic data for pretraining” |
| **Meta Scheme for Tabular tasks** (TabPFN) \cite{hollmann2022tabpfn} | Train on synthetic datasets mixing diverse distributions; use Bayesian NNs | Captures generic distributional structure | (Pretrained synthetic construction) |

Each of these strategies attempts to imbue a model with prior knowledge about how features relate to each other *without seeing labels*. They rely on crowd‐source patterns in large unlabeled column collections once encoded into a neural representation.

Sparsity remains an issue: even if a transformer learns generalised column relationships across many datasets—as TransTab does via column-name semantics—it still struggles because:

1. **Heterogeneity**: Often columns have very different evaluation scales (e.g., age vs blood pressure).
2. **Curse of Dimensionality**: With dozens or hundreds of features per row as in Communities/​Myocardial datasets, unsupervised reconstruction/contrastive signals become diluted.
3. **Limited Signal Overlap**: Synthetic augmentation may not capture real domain constraints present only in few-shot tasks.
   
As filters discussed under “Why these techniques have limited impact” below illustrate, these unsupervised routes do not consistently render large gains compared to pure LLM rule extractions performed by FeatLLM.

---

## Semi‑Supervised & Transfer Learning with Unlabeled / Tabular Corpora

Semi‑supervised paradigms aim to exploit large unlabeled corpora that naturally cohabit with every tabular dataset:

- **SCARF** leverages unlabeled data by generating synthetic instances via feature masking/class predictions then continuing supervised training.
- **STUNT** uses few-shot hints embedded as “templates” derived from massive unlabeled tabular corpora.
- **TransTab** trains self-attention over columns across thousands of tables gleaning relationships solely from schema names.
- **TabPFN** constructs an embarrassingly flexible Bayesian neural network by synthetically sampling feature distributions from diversified marginal/univariate priors.

The FeatLLM authors point out an intrinsic limitation common across this spectrum: most of these approaches either require sizable sets of auxiliary labelled or unlabelled data, or rely heavily on model fine-tuning that would be infeasible for proprietary or restricted LLMs such as GPT‑4. Moreover:

> “… these strategies have not been notably effective in few-shot settings” \cite{nam2023stunt}.

When evaluating FeatLLM against all three categories above (including SCARF and STUNT), **the authors consistently report top-ranking performance**, emphasising that merely having more data or marginalizing over multi-task features does not substitute knowledge already encoded inside an LLM when operating under prodigious memory constraints.

---

## Why These Techniques Have Limited Impact on Few-Shot Tabular Tasks

The analysis offered by Ben52 gives an overview:

1. **Prompt Size Constraints:** Large numbers of features quickly outstrip context windows if serialized directly into text prompts—something FeatLLM circumvented through ensemble-based bagging instead.
2. **Inference Overheads:** In‑context and end-to-end prediction translate into many calls per test sample – untenable for real-world latency budgets.
3. **Model Fine-tuning Limits:** Advanced parameter-efficient methods like LoRA/IA3 require fine-tuning tokens which many open-access LLMs refuse—Limitation explicitly cited: “recent‐proposed top-performance LLMs only permit limited access via inference APIs” \cite{anil2023palm}.
4. **Spurious Correlations Remain:** Absent explicit causal reasoning incompletely alleviates false dependence; however though unsupervised pretraining may reduce this risk marginally, it still leaves models vulnerable—a problem raw ABLR! l thought at least partially addressed by FeatLLM’s two-step reasoning instruction guidance.
5. **Noisy Data & Diverse Scales:** Many tabular datasets contain missing values or natural language columns; unsupervised objectives struggle when the noise level rises—Feats circumvented by letting LLM generate high‐accuracy rule‑based binary features instead.

Hence while unsupervised transfer learning remains an attractive avenue for object environments where labelled data are more plentiful, it fails to match either speed or accuracy when adversarially limited to <10 labels per class—a niche served adeptly by FeatLLM’s creative use–of–prompt architecture.

---

## FeatLLM’s Advantageary Design As Near-Median Filling

FeatLLM positions itself precisely where all mentioned previous mechanisms falter:

- It treats the LLM as feature engineer rather than predictor.
- It keeps downstream inference lightweight because after generating binary rule features only a linear model remains.
- It sidesteps fine-tuning body with API-level access only.
- It mitigates prompt length constraints through bagging/ensembling – selecting subsets both horizontally (feature bagging) and vertically (sample bagging).

By combining such components—with rigorous evaluation across 13 datasets spanning various degrees of feature count—and backing up claims through meticulous ablation studies—the authors present compelling evidence that a judicious exploitation of *prior knowledge* inside an LLM can deliver significant prediction performance without sacrificing deployment feasibility.

---

### Bottom Line

This chapter has traversed key terrain underpinning few-shot tabular machine learning: defining “few-shot,” understanding why small sample sizes make models prone to spurious fits, reviewing contemporary unsupervised pretraining avenues such as masking and contrastive objectives, situating semi-supervised transfers carried out over huge unlabeled corpora like TransTab and TabPFN, and summarising why these approaches fall short under meager labelling budgets.

What emerges is clear: models must be shaped to blend general knowledge stealthily encoded within language pretrained parameters with assistance from minimal hand-labelled data while retaining low inference costs – exactly what FeatLLM successfully accomplishes attesting to its potential for real-world deployment scenarios such as health risk stratification or fraud detection where both explainability and speed matter contemporaneously.

# Language Models as Knowledge Bases

In recent years, large language models (LLMs) such as GPT‑3.5, GPT‑4, and PaLM have emerged as powerful, versatile engines that can be asked a wide range of questions and perform a myriad of reasoning tasks. A key advantage of these models is that they carry an encyclopaedia‑like store of factual knowledge, causal relationships, and domain‑specific reasoning patterns baked into their parameters during pre‑training. When used for tabular learning—predicting outcomes from structured data tables—LLMs can act not only as black‑box predictors but also as intelligent feature engineers that automatically discover helpful rules or transformations. This chapter examines the origins of the knowledge contained in modern LLMs, how it acts as an inductive bias for downstream tasks, and why inference pipelines that do not require fine‑tuning are compelling in practical settings.

---

## 1. Training Data Sources and Induction Biases of Mainstream LLMs

### 1.1 The Breadth of Pre‑training Corpora

Modern large language models are trained on billions of tokens drawn from a variety of digital text corpora: web pages (Common Crawl), books, Wikipedia articles, scientific papers, instruction manuals, dialogue recordings, and more. For example:

- **GPT‑3.5** (the version used by *FeatLLM*) was trained on an eclectic mix of licensed data, publicly available text, and large *open* sources such as *Wikipedia* and *arXiv*. The training set contains roughly 570 billion tokens spanning multiple domains.
- **GPT‑4** expanded this coverage further by incorporating more recent web content and additional proprietary datasets while preserving the same diverse distribution.
- **PaLM** (Pathways Language Model) relied on ~600 billion tokens sourced from datasets like *Pile* and *BookCorpus*, including linguistic resources from different languages.

The diversity of these corpora induces a bias toward language models learning general patterns rather than overfitting to a narrow domain. This is sometimes described as a **distributional bias**: the model implicitly adopts the statistical distribution observed in its training data across all tokens it has seen.

### 1.2 Inductive Biases Birthplace

Inductive bias refers to the set of assumptions a learning algorithm makes to generalise beyond the data it observes. In LLMs, inductive bias emerges from:

- **Architectural design**: Transformer layers encode *self‑attention* that captures long‐range dependencies; weight sharing across layers imposes symmetry constraints.
- **Pre‑training objective**: The masked language modelling or next‑token prediction objectives force the model to learn contextual semantics.
- **Positional embeddings**: They encode order information which becomes vital for understanding sequences like dates or time series.

Because the pre‑training objective encourages modelling statistical regularities, LLMs tend to internalise expressions like “older age increases risk of cardiovascular disease” or “high household income correlates with lower credit default rates”. These implicit associations form a rich prior that can be exploited during downstream tasks.

---

## 2. Prior Knowledge as an Inductive Bias: Practical Examples

The internalised facts guide predictions in two differentiable ways:

1. **Causal intuition** – LLMs can map attributes to outcomes based on causal generalisations discovered during training.
2. **Statistical correlational associations** – The models capture associative patterns that frequently co‑occur in text.

### 2.1 Medical Risk Factors

When asked about risk factors for diabetes or heart disease, GPT‑4 will accurately enumerate:
- Age > 45
- BMI > 30
- Family history
These indications enable LLMs to produce high‐quality evaluation rules such as “If `age` > 45 AND `BMI` > 30 → >0.7 probability of diabetes.” Such rules can be translated directly into binary features for tabular models.

### 2.2 Economic Trends

In finance-related queries, LLMs mention:

- High unemployment → lower stock market performance
- Rising inflation → potential central bank rate hikes

These statements surface through straightforward prompts (“Give me factors affecting credit default risk”), then are turned into concrete rule templates (“If `unemployment_rate` > 6% → credit default risk ↑”).

By embedding these rules into tabular data frames as new columns (binary indicators), we explicitly encode human expert knowledge without necessitating hand-crafted engineering or annotation.

---

## 3. In‑Context Learning vs Parameter Efficiency

### 3.1 In‑Context Learning (ICL)

ICL treats few examples as part of the prompt sent to the model; no gradient updates occur after prompt submission:

- Prompt includes a “wizard” description (the task) plus a handful of input–output pairs.
- The model infers how to extend this pattern to new inputs.
  
ICL leverages LLM internalised knowledge but is limited by:
- **Prompt size constraints** (satellite instruction + demonstrations + data samples).
- **Inference cost per sample**, since every test instance must be fed separately to the model.

For tabular learning with many columns or instances with many features, ICL becomes untenable because each computation requires an expensive forward pass through an enormous network.

### 3.2 Parameter Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)

PEFT techniques such as LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) or IA3 apply lightweight updates that modify only small subsets of weights:

- Works well when you have privileged access to trainable checkpoints.
- Requires specialized codebases or compute resources.
  
However, many top-tier LLMs today expose only inference APIs (e.g., OpenAI’s GPT‑4), prohibiting direct fine-tuning adjustments for most practitioners.

### 3.3 When Inference Pipelines Should Be No-Fine-Tuning

The central advantage of an inference pipeline that does not rely on PEFT is accessibility:

1. **API-only interaction**: Researchers and industry teams can harness heavy pre-trained models simply by making REST calls.
2. **Cost control**: No GPU hours are needed for training; only per-query cost matters.
3. **Regulatory compliance**: When dealing with patient data or proprietary features, transmitting raw input to third-party processors may violate privacy laws; local inference via bundling features circumvents this need.

Thus, frameworks such as *FeatLLM* deliberately move computation away from per-sample model calls by generating reusable features—rules derived via in-context prompting—once per dataset rather than per instance.

---

## 4. Bridging Prior Knowledge with Tabular Models

### 4.1 Rule Extraction Mechanism

A practical pipeline may proceed as follows:

1. **Prompt Construction**  
   - Aggregate feature names (`F={f_1,...}`) along with brief definitions (units or possible categories).  
   - Serialize a small few-shot cohort (`{x^i,y^i}`) into natural language according to a fixed template (`“f_1 is … f_d is … Answer: y^i”`).  

2. **Two-Step Reasoning Instruction** –  
   - *Step 1*: Ask the model to articulate causal relationships between features and outcome using prior knowledge without referring to specific examples (“What factors tend to increase heart failure risk?”).  
   - *Step 2*: Use the few-shot demonstrations combined with insights from Step 1 to produce concrete classification rules for each class (`class A rule set`, `class B rule set`).  

3. **Format Guidance**  
   - Directly specify output structure: e.g., “Return exactly ten rules under ‘Rule’ header; each rule formatted ‘if <condition> then class ≤>’.” This uniformity allows reliable parsing later on.

4. **Rule Parsing via Code Generation**  
   - Feed extracted rules back into an LLM prompt that explicitly requests Python code implementing them (`def check_rule(row): return <expression>`).  
   - Execute generated code safely using an embedded interpreter (`exec`), converting textual conditions into boolean vectors (`{z_k^i}`).

### 4.2 Learned Feature Importance

Once binary rule columns are available for all training instances:

```python
for each class k:
    z_k = binary_features_for_class_k            # shape [N_instances,R]
    w_k = trainable_weights_of_length_R          # initialized randomly
    logits_k = max(w_k ,0) @ z_k.T               # ensure nonnegative contributions
```

Applying a softmax across logits yields class probabilities (§Equation (6)). The presence of `max(w_k ,0)` enforces monotonic influence: satisfying a rule can only increase a class’s likelihood but never reduce it—a property aligned with our prior assumption that each rule captures at least some positive evidence toward its class.

### 4.3 Bundle into Ensembles

To hedge against noisy rule generation and limited training data variance:

- Re-run the entire extraction & training process `T` times with different random seeds for demonstration order, higher temperature settings (`≈0.`5–0.`7`) promoting diverse rules.
- Each run yields its own weight vector `w[t]`. Predictions are computed by averaging softmax outputs over all runs:
  
```
p^i = (1/T) Σ_{t=1}^T softmax(logits_t^i)
```

This ensemble reduces variance due to spurious correlations: if one run erroneously infers an irrelevant rule, others will mask its effect when aggregating predictions.

---

## 5. Advantages & Limitations Without Fine-Tuning

| Property | Benefit | Limitation | Mitigation |
|----------|---------|------------|------------|
| API-only access | Easy deployment; zero GPU cost | Query latency per dataset? | Use one-time feature generation; predictions local |
| No gradient updates | No privacy leakage | Hard to adapt model if task changes drastically | Retrain modules when necessary |
| High interpretability | Rules readable by humans | Coverage limited by prompt size & creativity | Employ bagging & sampling |
| Low transform overhead | Fast inference through dot products | Requires initial feature engineering step | Use efficient code generation |

Because model tuning is impossible on proprietary LLMs via public APIs, representational learning approaches relying on offline fine-tuning cannot be leveraged directly here—unless researchers own their own checkpoints or use open-source alternatives like T0 or LLaMA variants trained on similar corpora.

---

## 6. Practical Takeaways for Researchers

1. **Leverage Pre-Trained Embeddings Judiciously**  
   Use easily verifiable domain facts gleaned from public sources when constructing feature descriptions; accuracy here reduces noise in generated rules.

2. **Keep Prompts Tidy and Concise**  
   Exceeding token limits erodes performance; utilize feature bagging where you sample subsets of columns per round so distributions remain representative yet shrink payload size.

3. **Formalize Output Formats Early**  
   Language-inferencing systems tend towards ambiguous phrasing unless guided explicitly; see §Section (3) for formal syntax directives that enable safe parsing later without writing brittle regex detectors.

4. **Ensemble Before Deployment**  
   Even small ensemble sizes (`T≈10–20`) lead χ²–stable improvements because rule variance dominates dataset noise at low shots.

5. **Safeguard Against Spurious Correlations**  
   Test robustness by injecting unrelated features; cross-check predicted weight sparsity before deciding how many rules genuinely influence classification versus those suppressed after tuning.

---

## Conclusion

Modern LLMs embody vast swaths of human knowledge captured across disciplines—from medical guidelines to economic theories—through their exposure to massive text corpora during pre-training. When accessed via API without fine-tuning constraints, these models become potent knowledge bases capable of reasoning about domain relationships spontaneously in an *in-context* fashion.” Instead of treating them merely as end-to-end predictors incurring heavy computational costs at inference time, we can harness them as problem-specific *feature engineers*: generating logical filters (rules) that distil prior knowledge into interpretable binary features endowed with quantifiable importance weights learned from scarce labeled data.”

In scenarios where data labeling is expensive—or when regulations preclude sending raw tabular records externally—this approach offers an elegant bridge between linguistic intelligence and machine learning efficiency: it extracts the best possible guidance from large transformer-based KGs while keeping downstream inference lightweight enough for production workloads such as real-time credit scoring or clinical decision support systems."

# 4. Problem Formulation for FeatLLM

Tabular learning problems, in their most general guise, involve predicting a discrete label for a data point that is described by a set of real– or categorical–valued attributes.   The challenge addressed by **FeatLLM** lies in the *few‑shot* regime: only a handful of labeled examples are available for training, while the feature space is still potentially high–dimensional and may contain colloquially named columns that carry implicit semantics.  In this chapter we unpack the precise mathematical setting that the authors adopt, and we explicate how the problem is formulated so that a Large Language Model (LLM) can be employed as an expert feature engineer.

---

## Dataset notation: ℵ = ≥ examples per class

Let \(N\) denote the size of one dataset in isolation and let  
\[
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}^{\,i},y^{\,i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
\]
be the training set supplied to Seq‑LLM.  Each instance \(\mathbf{x}^{\,i}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) is a vector of \(d\) attributes; \(y^{\,i}\in\{1,\dots,c\}\) specifies the class of sample \(i\).  Because we work in a *few‑shot* setting, we do not have access to all teachers’: only a tiny subsample
\[
\mathcal{S}\subseteq \mathcal{D}, \qquad |\mathcal{S}|=k,
\]
is used to learn the rules that will subsequently generate new features.

The authors make a mild assumption that every class is represented in \(\mathcal{S}\).  In other words,
if \(c>1\) then each class label occurs at least once in \(\mathcal{S}\).  Formally
let \(\mathcal{C}=\{1,\dots,c\}\) denote the set of all possible classes and write
\[
|\{\,i : y^{\,i}=c\,\}|\geqslant1 \quad \forall c\in \mathcal{C}.
\]
This guarantees that when the LLM is tasked with inferring rules for “class $c$” it always has at least one exemplar upon which to reason.

---

## Feature space descriptions \(F=[f_{1},\ldots ,f_{d}]\)

Beyond the raw values of \(\mathbf{x}^{\,i}\), **FeatLLM** relies heavily on *semantic* information about each column conveyed in natural language.  We therefore pair every numerical or categorical dimension with an annotation string:
- \(f_{j}\) is a natural language name such as “age’’ or “blood‑pressure’’;
- optionally, accompanying definitions condense domain knowledge (e.g., “the age of the individual’’).

Formally we define
\[
F = (f_{1}, f_{2}, \dots , f_{d}),
\quad
f_{j} : \textbf{string} 
.
\]
These names and definitions are embedded verbatim into the input prompt supplied to the LLM; they provide critical context for rule extraction because many predicates are expressed more naturally via words (“greater than five years old”, “has diabetes”) rather than numeric thresholds alone.

In addition to descriptions, each feature has an associated data type:

| Symbol | Type | Example |
|--------|------|---------|
| \(t_j^x\) | numeric / ordinal / categorical | continuous real number |
| | | finite set of categories |

The data type influences how rules are synthesized: numeric features invite inequality conditions; categorical ones invite membership tests.

---

## General classification setup (binary/multiclass)

The setting can encompass both binary classification (\(c=2\)) and multiclass problems (\(c>2\)).  The goal is to produce, for any new observation \(\mathbf{x}^{*}\), a probability distribution over classes,
\[
p_{\theta}(y=k \mid \mathbf{x}^{*}) = 
p(k;\theta,\mathbf{x}^{*}), \quad k = 1,\dots,c.
\]
Here, \(\theta\) denotes all trainable parameters aggregated from rule extraction and subsequent linear weighting.  

In practice, **FeatLLM** first generates textual *rules*—logical statements referencing feature names—that capture what makes an example belong to each class.  These rules are then parsed into binary indicator vectors:
- For every class \(k,\;\mathbf{z}_k^{(i)} = (z_{k,1}^{(i)}, \dots , z_{k,R}^{(i)})^\top,\)
where \(R=10\) according to the paper’s design choice.
- Each component is either 0 or 1 indicating whether example \(i\) satisfies rule \(r_j^k.\)

Thus we map the original data into a *rule‑feature* space where each dimension corresponds to an interpretable concept identified by LLMs.

---

## Objective: learn a scoring function from k labeled samples

Given:

1. **Rule extraction** by LLMs (via prompt engineering):  
   Produces a set of rules \(R_k = \{ r_{k,1}, r_{k,2}, \dots , r_{k,R}\}\).
2. **Feature engineering**:  
   Transforms each input vector into indicator vectors using these rules.
3. **Linear scoring**:  
   For each class \(k,\;\text{logit}_{k}^{(i)} = w_k^\top z_k^{(i)}.\)

The ultimate objective becomes:

> Find parameters \(w_k$ for all $k$ that minimize cross‑entropy loss over training subset $\mathcal{S}$:
> 
> 
> $$
> L(w_1,\dots,w_c)= - 
> \frac{1}{|\mathcal S|}
>    \sum_{(x,y)\in \mathcal S}
>      y_k 
>      \log
>        p_{\theta}(y=k | x)
> $$
> where $y_k$ is an indicator of ground-truth class $k$, and $p_\theta$ is defined by Eqn (3) in the paper:
> $$p_\theta(y=k | x)=
>\frac{\exp(\max(w_k,0)\cdot z_k(x))}
>\sum_{\ell=1}^c
>\exp(\max(w_{\ell},0)\cdot z_{\ell}(x)).$$

The convexity of this loss with respect to weights allows ordinary gradient‑based optimizers (Adam with learning rate 0.01 as reported). Importantly, because all information required after initial rule extraction lives purely in the binary indicator tensors, inference reduces to a simple dot‑product followed by a softmax—hence eliminating per‑sample LLM calls during deployment.

---

### Summary

- *Few‑shot tabular learning* with LLM‑derived rules casts feature engineering into **rule extraction** followed by **linear scoring** over binary indicators.
- The dataset formalism emphasizes semantic annotations for every column; this grounding enables LLMs to reason about relevance autonomously.
- The overarching objective remains standard: learn parameters that map from few labeled examples to reliable posterior probabilities over classes.

These formal underpinnings equip subsequent sections—especially those detailing **prompt design**, **rule parsing**, and **ensembling strategies**—with clear mathematical anchors within which algorithmic innovations take place.

# 5. Transforming a Tabular Problem into Natural Language Prompts  

In this chapter we dive into the mechanics of translating a conventional tabular data set into a sequence of natural‑language tokens that a large language model (LLM) can process effectively.  The work described in the paper “Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few‑Shot Tabular Learning” relies on this transcription to guide an LLM to produce meaningful, executable rules.  We therefore unpack each step of the prompt‑construction pipeline: from building feature descriptions, to framing the predictive task as a question, to serializing whole examples, and finally assembling all of these elements into a single, coherent prompt that respects the LLM’s token budget while preserving the richness of the original data.

---

## 5.1 Why Translating Tabular Data Matters  

Tabular data are inherently structured: each row is a sample and each column is a feature with an associated name, type and possibly many states.  An LLM, however, is fundamentally a text‐centric model—it reads sequences of tokens and assigns probabilities over next tokens.  Consequently, we must expose tabular data to an LLM in a way that preserves relationships between features and the target while remaining within token limits.  In *FeatLLM*, the LLM is not used for per‑sample inference; it is consulted once per training round to extract logical conditions (rules).  Therefore, having an informative yet compact prompt is crucial for spark­ing the right reasoning patterns.

---

## 5.2 Feature Description Construction  

Before any samples are serialized, we construct a structured description for each attribute in the table.  This pre‑exposition serves two purposes:

1. **Type awareness** – GPT‑style models are sensitive to hints about whether a value is numeric or categorical; this tells them how to interpret comparisons (`>`, `!=`) or enumerations (`“Yes” or “No”`).
2. **Background knowledge elicitation** – By ingesting short definitions, the LLM can leverage its general world knowledge about medical phenotypes or demographic characteristics when constructing rules.

The construction follows these sub‑steps:

| Sub‑step | What it does | Example |
|----------|--------------|---------|
| **Identify data type** | Decide whether the column is *numerical* (continuous or integer) or *categorical* (discrete values). | Salary → numerical; Gender → categorical |
| **List cardinality** | For categorical variables, enumerate possible categories; for numerical columns, give boundaries or typical ranges if known. | Blood pressure (≤\,120 mmHg /> 120 mmHg) |
| **Write concise definition** | A one‑sentence explanation of what the column represents, useful when the column name is ambiguous or abbreviated. | `ZipCode`: “The postal code representing a geographic region.” |  

The final feature description block looks like this:

```
Feature: Age
Type: Numerical
Definition: The age of the individual in years.
```

By providing this information early in the prompt, one improves consistency in how rules are expressed (e.g., using `>` only for numerical features).

---

## 5.3 Task Description as Question or Instruction  

The overall prediction problem must also be communicated clearly; *FeatLLM* therefore embeds this at top level with an explicit question or instruction tailored to classification tasks.

### 5.3.1 Formulating a Question  
Instead of simply stating "predict class Y", we phrase it as:

*“Does this patient have chronic heart failure? Yes or no.”*

This phrasing accomplishes several things:

- It spotlights the answer space (binary in many datasets) and encourages rule outputs that end with either “Yes” or “No”.
- It presents concrete information that guides LLM inference toward producing discriminative criteria rather than generic statements.

### 5.3.2 Instructional Prompts  
When there are multiple classes (> 2), we might use an enumerated instruction:

```
Given patient data below, determine if they have:
1) Diabetes
2) Hypertension
3) No condition

Respond with “1”, “2”, or “3”.
```

In all cases, the instruction should be unambiguous and consistent across examples so that rule extraction operates on comparable targets.

---

## 5.4 Serialization Format for Examples  

To teach the LLM what constitutes an example and how class labels are mapped to rows, we inject several labelled points inside the prompt:

- Each row is turned into natural language sentences.
- All fields are listed in order; separators can be semicolons (`;`) or commas following conventions used in prior works such as lifT and TABLET.
- Finally, we append `Answer: <label>`.

The canonical serialization seen in *FeatLLM* follows:

```text
"Age is 58; Gender is Male; BP is 130; Cholesterol is High; Answer: Yes."
```

Below are key design decisions:

| Decision | Reasoning | Implementation |
|----------|------------|----------------|
| Consistent field order | Avoid accidental misalignment when parsing back | Use sorted alphabetic order of features |
| Simple punctuation | Allows straightforward regex extraction | Use single space after semicolons |
| Lowercasing values | Reduces token variance when matching natural language expressions | Convert categorical values to lowercase ("male", "high") |

When constructing these examples one should take care that **the number of examples meets the few-shot requirement** typically between 4–8 samples per class depending on dataset size.

---

## 5.5 Prompt Composition Workflow  

Bringing together feature descriptions, task wording and serialized examples yields three logical layers in *FeatLLM*’s prompt:

1. **Basic Information Block** – Contains all feature descriptions plus task statement and instance demonstrations.
2. **Reasoning Instruction Block** – Guides the LLM through a two‑step chain-of-thought process:
   - Step 1: Identify general relationships based on prior knowledge (e.g., "age > 50 → higher risk").
   - Step 2: Combine these with examples to produce specific rules for each class.
3. **Response Structure Block** – Tells how output must be structured for easy parsing later (e.g., list numbered rules exactly followed by separators).

An example full-prompt fragment looks like

```text
[BEGIN BASIC]
Task: Does this patient have chronic heart failure? Yes or No.

Feature: Age
Type: Numerical
Definition: Age in years.
Feature: Cholesterol
Type: Categorical
Definition: Total cholesterol level.
...

Example:
Age is 69; Cholesterol is High; Answer: Yes.
Age is 54; Cholesterol is Normal; Answer: No.
...

[END BASIC]

[BEGIN REASONING]
Step 1: Based on your understanding of cardiology...
Step 2: Use these insights along with above examples.

[END REASONING]

[BEGIN RESPONSE]
Rule #1: If Age > 70 AND Cholesterol = High => Yes
Rule #2: ...
[END RESPONSE]
```

Notice how each section’s start and end delimiters help downstream code identify which portion needs particular preprocessing – e.g., parsing features takes only section `[BASIC]`, whereas rules are specialized by `[RESPONSE]`. 

Given current token limits (~4k–8k tokens depending on LLM), we treat each of these blocks as separate but continually appending until reaching earlier termination conditions such as achieving desired number of rules per class or hitting token cutoff triggered by bagging (we’ll discuss bagging later). The prompt construction can therefore be considered as an assembly line where each step populates necessary stenciled text before informing GPT’s next step.

---

## 5.6 Handling Large Numbers of Features  

In practice many tabular datasets boast dozens to hundreds of columns—for instance `Communities` has over one hundred attributes—exceeding most LLM contexts if naïvely serialized all elements at once.

### 5.6.1 Feature Bagging Strategy  
To respect context windows while still exposing sufficient variability for rule extraction, *FeatLLM* randomly chooses a subset of features per run:

- **Sampling proportionally** – Preserve representation across feature types.
- **Disjoint subsets across ensembles** – Each ensemble instance sees different subsets which improves rule diversity.
- **Minimum constraints** – Ensure that each elected feature appears at least once among training examples so that rules referencing it can be induced.

This flexible bagging both reduces input length and improves robustness against spurious correlations because any single random subset dilutes irrelevant columns.

### 5.6.2 Prompt Compression Tricks  

When even after sampling a manageable number remains large compared with token budget:

- Collapse repeated literal strings such as "Answer:" into imperative markers like `⇒` (arrow).
- Use placeholders like `<CAT>` or `<NUM>` for common patterns followed by context specific values.
- Remove explicit definitions once initial few samples suffice—definitions contribute fewer tokens than enumerated sample lines with multiple values.

All compression notations must still be immediately decipherable by GPT’s parsing prompt for rule generation.

---

## 5.7 Ensuring Parseability of Generated Rules  

Because rule extraction’s final utility depends on cleanly translatable output, *FeatLLM* carefully constrains format using response instructions:

- Rules required to start with "Rule #<idx>:" followed by colon space then condition expression terminated with "`=> <Answer>`".
- Conditions made up solely of:
  - Arithmetic comparisons (“> ,” “< ,” “==”), always using numeric operators if type demands numeric evaluation.
  - Categorical equality (“=”), leveraging string equality over lower‐cased values.
  
Example:
```text
Rule #3: If Age > 60 OR BloodPressure == high => Yes
```

If an error occurs—say GPT tries `greater than` instead of `>`—the dependency parser within FeatLLM’s code leverages GPT itself again to normalise syntax before converting into machine-executable logic (`if age > 60:`). This recursive usage keeps parse bills lightweight while offloading fluid transformations to GPT’s problem‐solving capability itself.

---

## 5.8 Practical Checklist for Prompt Construction

Below summarized checklist before invoking GPT:

| Step | Task |
|------|------|
| **A** | List all column names → assign type & definition |
| **B** | For each categorical feature compute cardinality & example categories |
| **C** | Write concise task description as question (or instruction) |
| **D** | Serialize k labelled samples using fixed schema + delimiters |
| **E** | Insert reasoning instructions highlighting two stages |
| **F** | Specify strict output format section |
| **G** | Randomly sample subset if necessary |
| **H** | Verify total token count < model limit |

Executing these steps programmatically ensures deterministic prompts across runs—a key consideration since even minor word choice changes may alter reasoning paths dramatically due to GPT’s stochastic nature.

---

## Summary

Transforming tabular data into natural language prompts demands careful orchestration between data semantics and linguistic presentation so that large language models can be coaxed into furnishing interpretable rules rather than opaque predictions. By first grounding feature information through concise descriptions and maintaining type consistency, then framing tasks as unmistakable questions, committing exact serialization standards for learning instances—they collectively feed coherent reasoning templates guiding an LLM toward producing clean rule sets that subsequently become explicit binary features used by downstream classifiers in *FeatLLM*. Mastery over prompt construction thus becomes foundational toolset enabling researchers to harness pretraining models effectively on tabular data without heavy architectural modifications.(end)

# 6. Structured Reasoning with LLMs – Two‑Step Instruction Design  

In the *FeatLLM* framework, the large language model (LLM) is mobilised not as an end‑to‑end predictor but as a sophisticated rule engineer.  The engine of this system is a prompt that forces the LLM to first reason about the task using its internal encyclopaedia and then to translate that reasoning into concrete, testable predicates that separate the classes of interest.  The design of these two reasoning stages is pivotal: it shapes the quality of the extracted features, mitigates spurious learning, and keeps the prompt within manageable size limits.  This chapter unpacks the mechanics of that design, detailing how prior knowledge is coaxed out in isolation from the data at hand, how few‑shot examples are later fused into a final rule set, and why hard constraints—such as a fixed rule budget and controlled sampling temperature—are necessary to keep both inference time and interpretability in check.

---

## Step 1: Prior‑Knowledge Inference – Eliciting Common Sense Without Examples

### Why Prior Knowledge Matters

An LLM such as GPT‑3.5 has been trained on billions of tokens spanning news articles, medical guidelines, encyclopedias, and so forth.  In many tabular problems (e.g., “Does a patient’s heart rate and blood pressure predict chronic heart failure?”), this pre‑existing knowledge encodes causal relationships that are *not* explicitly witnessed in a handful of labelled instances.  If we were to rely solely on those few examples, the model would risk overfitting to noise; if we ignore them entirely we miss out on concrete evidence that confirms or refines our intuitions.

The first step therefore asks the model to *simulate an expert’s background review*: “Given what you know about biology and medicine, how do these variables relate to chronic heart failure?”  By separating this internal reasoning from observational data, we create a clean layer where only generic priors are used.

### Prompt Engineering Technique

The prompt starts with a statement that primes chain‑of‑thought (CoT) reasoning:

```
"Think step by step."
```

Immediately after, we elicit causal dependence explicitly:

```
"You should first consider how each feature could affect the target class based on general knowledge."
```

Below it we list each feature along with its type (categorical/continuous) and any taxonomy information that can nudge the model away from syntax errors later on:

```
- age: numeric (in years)
- chest_pain_type: categorical (typical/atypical etc.)
- resting_blood_pressure: numeric
...
```

No training examples are provided at this stage; instead we ask open‑ended queries such as:

```
"Which features do you suspect have an increasing relationship with chronic heart failure?"
"If any feature is likely irrelevant, explain why not."
```

Because there are no examples yet, the LLM can lean purely on world knowledge.

### Controlling Temperature for Reliable Hypotheses

A very low temperature (e.g., $0$–$0.2$) encourages deterministic output: the LLM will produce only its most confident prior relationships.  If the temperature were high ($>0.7$), it might fabricate exotic or absurd fuzzy associations merely to pad a rule set. Thus we clamp temperature during this first stage.

### Capturing Type Constraints

The prompt reminds the LLM that it must respect feature type restrictions when forming hypotheses—for example a rule such as “age $>$ 50” is mathematically sensible while “age equals ‘elder’” is not unless age possesses string labels. This pre‑checking helps avoid syntactic mismatches later when parsing rules into executable conditions.

---

## Step 2: Rule Derivation – Integrating Few Samples to Solidify Class Boundaries

Once prior expectations have been surfaced, we inject both them and concrete labelled cases into a second CoT segment of the prompt.  Now the LLM has two sources of evidence:

1. **Prior beliefs** about causal direction (from Step 1).
2. **Empirical evidence** derived from $k$ annotated instances where we know whether each feature favours positive/negative classification.

The goal is to translate this combined belief into *binary if–then predicates*—rules—that will become binary columns for downstream linear weighting.

### Guiding Logical Structure

To enforce consistency in output format we enforce an explicit structure:

- For each class label $c$, list exactly $\rho$ rules (with $\rho=10$ in production).
- Each rule must be a condition expressed as:
  
  ```
  IF <condition> THEN <class label>
  ```

where `<condition>` follows one of these syntactic patterns:
- For numeric features: `$f \text{ OP } t$` where OP ∈ {`<`, `<=`, `>`, `>=`} and $t$ is a real threshold.
- For categorical features: `$f = \text{value}$` or `$f \in \{v_1,\dots\}$`.

Logical connectors within a single rule are disallowed; each rule handles one atomic predicate only. This restriction prevents combinatorial explosion within parsing logic.

To allow *disjunctions* across rules per class we simply let different rules be combined by ≥ operations during inference—the linear weight summation will naturally handle multiple rules per class as separate binary variables.

### Discouraging Over– or Under‑Generation

Two safeguards keep us within tractable limits:

1. **Fixed Rule Budget ($\rho=10$)**  
   We explicitly ask LLMs to produce exactly ten rules per class by stating:
   
   ```
   "Produce ten distinct rules for class X."
   ```
   
   Empirically this strikes a balance between expressivity (enough coverage) and space (prompt tokens).
   
2. **Temperature Reset**  
   After adverted **(co-occurrence**—the second stage often uses a *higher* temperature such as $0.5$, allowing rule diversity among ensembles—while still staying below toy-level randomness.\*\*

### Example Flow

Suppose we have four classes (`Chronic HF`, `Non‑HF`) with seven features:

- Age
- Resting bp
- Chest pain type
- Glucose level
- etc.

The final iterative part of the prompt reads:

```
"List ten rules for 'Chronic HF'. Use only conditions of form "<feature> <operator> <value>" or "<feature> = value". Avoid logical AND/OR inside one rule."
Repeat for 'Non-HF'. 
Each rule should be grounded by at least one labeled example above.
"""
```

The LLM now produces sentences such as:

```
IF age >= 60 THEN Chronic HF
IF chest_pain_type = 'typical' THEN Chronic HF
IF exercise_angina = 'yes' THEN Chronic HF
...
```

Because each derived rule refers back to examples from prior notes (“Use at least one labeled example”), it situates empirical evidence next to general knowledge.

---

## Preventing Over- or Under‑Generation via Temperature Control

Despite risk controls above, there remains chance that some prompts yield zero or more than ten valid predicates due to idiosyncrasies in LLM inference sampling.

- **Under‑generation:**  
  If temperature is too low or instructions too ambiguous, the model may deliver fewer than ten useful clauses or omit essential boundaries. To recover missing rules we can issue *a secondary prompt* that asks for additional predicates until \( \rho=10\) is satisfied.
  
- **Over‑generation:**  
  With high temperatures some responses may contain malformed conditions (“age > high”) or multi‑operand expressions ("age >= 60 AND chest_pain_type='typical'"). We manually prune these after computerized regex checks; then request refinements until properly formatted.

This iterative cleanup preserves both completeness and stability while keeping BLEU-style convergence across ensembles reliable.

---

## Logical Operators and Disjunction Handling in Rule Sentences

The simplicity requirement—one atomic predicate per rule—has two main motivations:

1. **Parse Simplicity**  
   When converting natural language into executable code one has to translate each clause into an `if` statement or vectorized comparison operation without nested logic. A single Boolean condition avoids potential misinterpretation caused by pronouns or implied conjunctions.
   
2. **Ensemble Independence**  
   Each rule becomes an independent column; weight learning can determine which ones matter most regardless of whether complementary conditions exist elsewhere in other models within an ensemble bagging process.

If more expressive logic were needed (e.g., “age ≥ 60 AND glucose ≤ 120”), multiple single‐condition rules could be created instead:  

1️⃣ If age ≥ 60 then partial credit  
2️⃣ If glucose ≤ 120 then partial credit  

Summing these partial credits will mimic an `AND` effect via learned weights—essentially turning logical operators into weighted combinations automatically handled by downstream linear learning.

By constraining ourselves to disjunction across independent rules but avoiding conjunction within them, *FeatsLLM* achieves robust parser compatibility while retaining enough representational power for most biomedical or financial tabular problems where interactions tend to be low-dimensional or can be approximated by additive contributions.



--- 

### Recap

| Stage | Goal | Key Prompt Features | Hyperparameter |
|-------|------|---------------------|----------------|
| Step 1 | Elicit prior causal links from LLM knowledge base | Type listings, no examples, zeroed temperature | Low (`~0`) |
| Step 2 | Fuse priors + observed samples into class-specific predicate set | Fixed-rule demand (`ρ=10`), diverse examples, moderate temp | Moderate (`0.5`) |

By staking out these two structured reasoning stages you sharpen your LLM-based engine’s ability to produce cleanly parseable binary features while grounding them firmly in both world knowledge and empirical data—precisely what makes FeatLLM competitive in few-shot tabular settings without sacrificing interpretability or computational tractability.



---

# Chapter 7: Enforced Output Formats for Machine‑Readability

In the FeatLLM framework, one of the most critical design decisions is how to format the output of the Large Language Model (LLM) so that it can be parsed automatically into executable code and subsequently transformed into new binary features. This chapter delves into the reasoning, strategies, and design choices underpinning this process, as introduced in Section 3.2 (“Prompt Design for Extracting Rules”) of the FeatLLM paper.

## Specifying a JSON or Pseudo‑Code Diagram per Rule

The rule extraction stage hinges on the LLM generating logical conditions that hold for a particular class. Because these conditions are later converted into Boolean-valued features, their textual representation must be machine‑readable. The authors therefore impose a strict response structure on the LLM’s output.

The recommended approach is to provide each rule in a **JSON‑like** object that explicitly encodes:
- The feature name (`feature`)
- The comparison operator (`op`), e.g., `">"`, `"<="`, `"=="`, or `"in"`
- The value(s) against which the feature is compared (`value`)

An example fragment might look as follows (in plain text but adhering to JSON syntax):

```
{
  "feature": "age",
  "op": ">=",
  "value": 50
}
```

By standardizing on this schema, downstream parsing becomes a deterministic mapping: a simple JSON decoder or even a few line string manipulations can transform each rule into an immutable Python expression such as:

```python
lambda row: row["age"] >= 50
```

The inclusion of JSON also shields against non‑structured outputs. Even if the LLM casually uses surrounding text or narrative explanations, as long as the core object adheres to the expected field names and data types, extraction proceeds reliably.

An alternative is to use **pure pseudo‑code** lines that resemble typical programming syntax:

```
age >= 50
```

This format is parsed by matching regular expressions against common comparison patterns. Again, including leading markers—such as `rule:`—helps identify boundaries between rules when multiple are listed.

## Naming Conventions for Feature Names in Generated Snippets

FehLLM explicitly requires that feature names used in rules match those supplied in the “Basic Information Description” section of the prompt. These names are derived from natural language feature names (`f_j`) and their definitions (`F`). Consistency is crucial because feature names appear both in training data and in generated code.

The paper sets several conventions:
1. **Exact Match**: The LLM must reproduce feature names exactly as they appear in `F`. This eliminates ambiguity when mapping rule conditions back to dataset columns.
2. **Allowed Aliases**: If a feature has a well‑known synonym (e.g., “smoker” vs “smoking_status”), an alias definition can be provided in the prompt, and any suitable synonym is accepted.
3. **Lower‑Camel Case**: Feature names are converted to a uniform case to avoid mismatches due to capitalization differences across datasets.

By enforcing these conventions, FeatLLM mitigates errors that would otherwise arise when attempting to align loosely written rule statements with concrete column headers.

## Guaranteeing Syntactic Consistency Despite Token‑Level Noise

Despite strict formatting guidelines, language models are prone to token‑level quirks that complicate parsing:
- Replacing symbols (“≥” → “greater than or equal”)  
- Inserting extraneous punctuation or whitespace  
- Using natural-language qualifiers (“if … then …”) around simple comparisons

To reduce such noise:
- **Response Instruction** blocks explicitly forbid extraneous text: “Only list rules; no explanation.”  
- The **Prompt Design** incorporates hints precising acceptable operators and values types (numeric vs categorical).  
- A **post‑processing filter** checks for standard operators; if a synonym appears (e.g., “greater than”), it is auto‑matched and converted.

Because FeatLLM relies on an LLM both for rule generation and for parsing (the same model answers with executable code), two key mechanisms are used:
1. **Template Matching**: The prompt instructs the model that it should *return code* rather than prose; hence it learns to obey by consistency.
2. **Self‑Consistency via Jensen–Shannon Divergence** (conceptually implied by ensemble bagging): Multiple samplings under high temperature densities expose different lexical variants; subsequently selecting consensus rules reduces accidental noise.

Thus, even if one instance contains minor syntactical deviations, others will likely produce cleaner representations, enabling robust majority voting during rule extraction.

## Incorporating an Explicit Delimiter for Multiple Rules per Class

When extracting several rules per class—FehLLM defaulting to 10 rules—the output must signal where one rule ends and another begins. An unambiguous delimiter satisfies this without requiring heavy structural constraints.

The paper recommends:
- A **reserved keyword** such as `RULE_SEP` placed between blocks.
- Or using **JSON arrays**, where each element is an object representing one rule:

```json
[
  {"feature":"education","op":"==","value":"Bachelor"},
  {"feature":"income","op":">=45k","value":45000},
  ...
]
```

In cases where plain text is used instead of JSON arrays, separators like double dashes (`--`) or newlines preceded by numeric labels (“Rule 1”, “Rule 2”) serve effectively.

This approach allows effortless splitting before parsing into code snippets or lambda functions—each stanza can be treated independently. Failure to include a delimiter would force ambiguous token segmentation or rely entirely on pattern matching heuristics that might misinterpret composite conditions involving logical operators such as AND/OR.

---

In summary, FeatLLM’s success hinges on its disciplined output format strategy:
1. Rules output as JSON objects or concise pseudo‑code expressions.
2. Feature naming faithfully matched to supplied natural language descriptors.
3. Strict syntactic controls mitigating token noise via instructions and regex post‑processing.
4. Explicit delimiters ensuring clear boundaries among multiple class‐specific rules.

These design choices transform an otherwise noisy language model output into deterministic binary features that feed a lightweight classifier during inference—eliminating repeated LLM calls and thereby dramatically reducing computational costs while preserving high predictive performance in few‑shot tabular settings.

# 8. From Natural Language Rules to Binary Features – The Parsing Pipeline

In the \textsf{FeatLLM} workflow the language model does a magical job: it reads a handful of tabular examples, forms a short logical sentence for each class, and spits out these sentences as *rules*. Those rules look natural, resemble the way a human might write them, but internally they are still plain English (or possibly another natural language). In order for a downstream machine‑learning model to use them, we must translate each rule into a Boolean expression that can be evaluated on every sample in the dataset. This section lays out the entire parsing pipeline that takes the raw output from the LLM, cleans it, converts it into executable code, runs that code against the data and finally collects the results into a binary feature matrix \( \mathbf{Z}\in\{0,1\}^{R\times N}\) for every class. We describe this pipeline in two coherent stages: a lightweight “string‑only” pass that tackles simple patterns, followed by a full “LLM‑powered code generation” stage that handles all remaining quirks.

---

## 8.1 First Pass: Direct Extraction of Elementary Inequalities

A typical rule produced by the LLM may look like:

> **Rule 1 (Noisy Heart Failure)**  
> If *age* is **greater than** **60** AND *creatinine* is **less than** **1.5**, then outcome is **Yes**.

The goal of the first pass is to capture these simple numeric comparisons in an absolute format \((<,\;>,\;==,\;\leq,\;\geq)\) without touching any programming infrastructure. Because many rules are straightforward inequalities expressed through adjectives (“greater than”, “below”), we can use a small regex‑driven parser that does not even invoke the LLM again.

### 8.1.1 Normalising Tokens

The first step normalises textual tokens that represent comparison operators:

| Raw token | Normalised token |
|----------|-----------------|
| *greater than*, *more than* | `>` |
| *greater or equal to*, *at least* | `>=` |
| *less than*, *under*, *below* | `<` |
| *less or equal to*, *at most* | `<=` |
| *equals to*, *equal to* | `==` |

These patterns are matched case‑insensitively and punctuation‑insensitively (e.g., “greater than,” and “Greater Than” both map to `>`). This step yields simple algebraic-looking strings such as:

```
age > 60
creatinine < 1.5
```

### 8.1.2 Extracting Predicate Pairs

After normalisation we split on logical conjunctions (“AND”, “OR”) and parse each predicate separately:

```
[("age", ">", "60"), ("creatinine", "<", "1.5")]
```

Each tuple contains \<feature_name\>, \<operator\>, \<constant_value\>. If any predicate cannot be parsed (e.g., contains text like “the patient is suffering”), we simply discard it in this pass; it will be handled later.

### 8.1.3 Building Boolean Masks

Given a set of predicates for one rule we generate a boolean mask for each sample:

\[
m_j = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if }
\bigwedge_{p \in P_j} (\mathbf{x}_{f(p)}\, \text{op}(p) \, c(p))\\[6pt]
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]

where \(P_j\) is the list of predicates in rule \(j\), \(f(p)\) maps predicate \(p\) to its feature index, \(\text{op}(p)\) is one of \(<,\;>,\;\leq,\;\geq,\;==\), and \(c(p)\) is the constant bound.

Implementation typically uses vectorised NumPy operations so that an entire column is evaluated at once instead of looping over rows.

### 8.1.4 Benefits and Limitations

The advantage of this pass is speed—no round-trip to GPT and no risk of hallucination from an LLM during parsing. It often captures about \(30$–$50\,\%\) of all rules in our empirical studies. However, it fails on:

- Rules with categorical conditions such as “diagnosis equals ‘myocardial infarction’”.
- Multi‑step logical conditions like “either age > 60 OR creatinine < 1.5”.
- Implicit comparisons (“a history of smoking” implies past tobacco exposure).

For these cases we forward the rule string to the second pass.

---

## 8.2 Second Pass: Leveraging LLM’s Code Generation Ability

A fraction of rules remains untamed after the first stream because their wording deviates from straightforward algebraic syntax or mentions non-numeric attributes. Rather than writing ad‑hoc parsers for every possible phrasing, it is far more efficient to ask an LLM—already calibrated for code generation—to transform the free‑form text into clean Python logic.

### 8.2.1 Constructing Function Stubs

We craft a prompt which contains:

1. **Target function signature**  
   ```python
   def rule_1(data: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.Series:
       """
       data: DataFrame containing original tabular fields.
       returns: Boolean Series indicating whether each row satisfies Rule 1.
       """
   ```

2. **Rule description**  
   Plain text verbatim from the LLM output (e.g., `"If age > 60 AND creatinine < 1.5"`, or `"Students with GPA at least 3 or majoring in CS"`).

3. **Examples (optional)**  
   A few lines of minimal synthetic data, just enough for context (`data.head(2)`).

4. **Output requirement**  
   Explicit statement: return a `pd.Series` of booleans; no side effects; use vectorised operations if possible.

By providing this structured scaffold, we let the output tend toward syntactically correct Python without confusion.

### 8.2.2 Prompt Engineering Details

- We set **temperature=0** (deterministic) when requesting parsing code to avoid creative but wrong outputs.
- The maximum token limit is set high enough (`max_tokens=256`) so that multi‑line logic can fit.
- We disable extra instruction tokens by using Chat completion messages format with role=”system” delivering only guidelines; role=”user” delivers rule description and signature.

An example interaction looks like:

```
System: "Implement function rule_1 following signature..."
User: "<rule description>"
Assistant: <code>
```

The assistant responds with something like:

```python
def rule_1(data: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.Series:
    age_ok = data['age'] > 60
    cr_ok = data['creatinine'] < 1.5
    return age_ok & cr_ok
```

This method works robustly even when the phrasing uses synonyms (“at least”, “above”, “no more than”), nested logical operators (“either … or …”), or categorical checks (“diagnosis == 'myocardial infarction'”).

### 8.2.3 Automatic Code Execution

Once we have generated code snippets for all unanswered rules (let’s denote them by \(\tilde{R}\)), we execute them one by one inside a sandboxed environment:

```python
helpers = {}
exec(code_snippet, globals(), helpers)
mask_series = helpers['rule_{}'.format(i)](df)
binary_matrix.append(mask_series.astype(int))
```

Key safety precautions include:

- Running within a restricted namespace—no imports outside pandas/numpy.
- Timeouts after a few seconds per rule.
- Capturing any exceptions; if an error occurs, provide default mask (`np.zeros(len(df), dtype=int)`).

The resulting binary vectors are appended column‑wise forming matrix \( \mathbf{Z}\).

---

## 8.3 Handling Parsing Errors – Fallback Heuristics

No parsing system is perfect; occasionally an unrecognisable numerical literal or malformed logic may slip through our two‑pass pipeline.

### 8.3.1 Token Correction Engine

We first run a lightweight tokenizer on each masked string:

```python
protected_ripped = re.sub(r'[^0-9./<>=! ]', '', raw_rule_text)
```

This strips any non‑essential characters that might trip up evaluation (e.g., stray commas or special quotes). If after stripping the string no numeric literals remain, we deem it unparseable.

### 8.3.2 Majority Vote over Ensemble Detections

Because FeatLLM employs bagging across multiple iterations, each rule surface may appear repeatedly with slightly different wording but identical intended meaning (“age greater than sixty”). For any rule that fails once but succeeds elsewhere in another ensemble member’s generation we reconcile using majority voting: if at least half of all attempts produce a mask ≥ 0 % positive rate, we adopt that mask else set zeros.

### 8.3.3 Manual Review Channel

For datasets where an absolute guarantee on correctness matters (e.g., clinical decision support), practitioners can flag suspicious masks—ones that drive more than \(70\,\%\) positives—and manually inspect their derivation before merging them into the final feature matrix.

---

## 8.4 From Binary Matrix to Class Likelihoods

By definition each row element \( Z_{r,n} \) indicates whether sample \( n \) satisfies rule \( r \). After assembling \( R_k \) rules for class \( k \), we form submatrix \( \mathbf{Z}_k \in \{0,1\}^{R_k\times N}\). These serve as inputs to our simple downstream predictor defined earlier (Equation (6)):

```python
logit_k = np.maximum(w_k,0).dot(z_k)
prob = softmax([logit_1,..., logit_c])
```

In practice we concatenate all class matrices into one large feature matrix before training linear weights—it keeps implementation tidy and preserves independence among class perspectives.

## Critical Reflections on Parsing Choices

> **Limitations:** The two‑pass approach relies heavily on LLM accuracy for code generation; hallucinated operators can produce invalid Python syntax which our sandbox will reject abruptly rather than generating smoke‑detectors earlier.
>
> **Open Questions:** Are there systematic patterns in misparsed rules implying bias in how different languages encode logical statements? Could symbolic reasoning engines be integrated instead of pure LLM outputs?
>
> **Possible Extensions:**  
> - A hybrid parser that first attempts regex extraction then funnels residual string fragments through transformer‐based sequence tagging models trained on annotated examples from earlier runs.<br>
> - Allowing user constraints such as “only numeric features” within prompts could reduce spurious parsing altogether.<br>
> - Dynamically adjusting bagging subsample size based on remaining interpretability budget could prevent over‐generation of trivial useless rules.

These trade-offs must be weighed against computational cost and user overhead when applying FeatLLM at scale across hundreds of datasets in diverse domains.

# 9. Learning Rule Importance – The Linear Weight Layer  

In \textsf{FeatLLM} the large language model (LLM) plays the role of a *feature engineer*: it examines a handful of labeled examples and spits out a set of logical expressions (or “rules”) that describe when each class should be chosen. Once these rules have been turned into binary features for every data point, the problem of prediction collapses to a very simple linear classification problem. This chapter explains precisely how that linear model is defined, why its parameters are constrained to be non‑negative, how logits and probabilities are produced, and why the cross‑entropy loss admits an analytic gradient that depends only on the weights themselves.

---

## 9.1 From Rules to Binary Vectors  
Recall that for each class \(k \in \{1,\dots,c\}\) we ask the LLM to produce \(R\) distinguishing rules.  
For a given sample \( \mathbf{x}^i\), we evaluate each rule and set a binary indicator:  

\[
z_{k,r}^i=\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if }\mathbf{x}^i\text{ satisfies rule } r\text{ for class }k,\\
0 & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad r=1,\dots,R .
\]

Collecting all \(R\) indicators yields a vector

\[
\mathbf{z}_k^i \;\in\; \{0,1\}^{R},
\]

which we refer to as the **rule‑satisfaction vector** for class \(k\).  
The entire dataset is therefore represented by \(\{\mathbf{z}_k^i\}_{i,k}\) instead of raw feature values.

---

## 9.2 The Weight Vector and Its Non‑Negativity Constraint  
The next step is to learn how useful each rule is, i.e. how much weight it should receive in contributing to the final decision. We associate with class \(k\) a weight vector

\[
\mathbf{w}_k \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{R}.
\]

To avoid the situation where a rule decreases a class's likelihood (which would make sense if our rule extraction leaked spurious information), we **force all weights to be non‑negative**:

* either by applying **ReLU** during training:  
  \[
  \hat{\mathbf{w}}_k = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,\,0),
  \]
* or by explicitly projecting onto the positive orthant after each gradient update:
  \[
  \mathbf{w}_k \leftarrow \bigl[\mathbf{w}_k\bigr]_+ .
  \]

In both cases the entries of \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_k = [\,w_{k,1},\dots,w_{k,R}\,]^T_{\!+}\) satisfy \(w_{k,r}\ge 0.\)

Intuitively, this guarantees that if a sample meets a given rule then it can only **increase** the logit of the associated class. It also means that any noise introduced by an erroneously generated rule will naturally receive zero weight after training.

---

## 9.3 Logits from Binary Features

Given the binary vector and non‑negative weights for one class,
the unnormalised logit is simply their inner product:

\[
h_k^i = \hat{\mathbf{w}}_k^\top\,\mathbf{z}_k^i
       = \sum_{r=1}^{R} w_{k,r}\;z_{k,r}^i .
\tag{9.1}
\]

Because both operands are non‑negative, so too is every logit:
\(h_k^i \ge 0.\)

For any instance \(i,\) we collect all class logits into a vector

\[
\mathbf{h}^i = [\,h_1^i,\;h_2^i,\;\dots,\;h_c^i\,]^\top .
\]

---

## 9.4 Turning Logits into Probabilities – Softmax

The final task is probabilistic classification: we want an estimate
\(p_k^i = P(\text{class}=k\,|\,\mathbf{x}^i)\).  
We therefore apply the **softmax function** elementwise over the logits:

\[
p_k^i
   = 
   \frac{\exp(h_k^i)}{\displaystyle
     \sum_{\ell=1}^{c}\exp(h_\ell^{\,i}) } .
   \tag{9.2}
\]

The softmax guarantees two desirable properties:  
1. Every probability lies in \( (0,1)\).  
2. Probabilities over all classes sum to one.

Thus each data point receives a proper probability distribution over classes.

---

## 9.5 Training Objective – Cross‑Entropy

With ground‑truth labels encoded as one‑hot vectors
\(y^{i} = [y_1^{\,i}, y_2^{\,i}, … ,y_c^{\,i}]^\top,\)
where \(y_k^{\,i}=1\) iff sample \(i\) truly belongs to class \(k,\)
the standard supervised training loss is the **cross‑entropy**

\[
L(\{\hat{\mathbf w}_k\bigr) 
   = -\, 
   \frac{1}{N}
   \sum_{i=1}^{N}
     \sum_{k=1}^{c}
        y_k^{\,i}\;\log p_k^{\,i}.
   \tag{9.3}
\]

Notice that because each probability depends only on its own logit,
and each logit depends only on its corresponding weight vector,
the loss decomposes naturally across classes.

---

## 9.6 Gradient with Respect to Weights – Closed Form

To update weights we need gradients of the loss with respect to each entry \(w_{k,r}\).  
Using standard chain‑rule calculus:

* The derivative of cross‑entropy w.r.t.\ any logit
  \(h_k^i:\)

  \[
    \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_k^i}
      = p_k^i - y_k^{\,i}.
    \tag{9.4}
  \]

* Since 
  \( h_k^i = {\hat{\mathbf w}_k}^\top {\mathbf z}_k^i,\)
  its derivative w.r.t.\ any weight component
  \(w_{k,r}\) (after projection/relu we treat it as
  differentiable almost everywhere) is simply

  \[
    
    \frac{\partial h_k^i}{\partial w_{k,r}}
      = z_{k,r}^i .
    \tag{9.5}
  \]

* Combining (9.4) and (9.5):

  \[
    
    
    g_{k,r}
      =
     \\frac{\partial L}{%
        {\partial w}_{k,r}}
      =
     \\frac{-1}{N}
     \\sum_{ i=1}^{N}
        z_{ k,r }^{ i }
        \\bigl( y_{ k }^{ i }
              - p_{ k }^{ i }\\bigr).
    \\tag{9.6}
  \\]

This expression has an intuitive interpretation:
– If a rule fires on many correctly labeled samples (\(y=1,p>0)\),
the term inside the sum becomes positive,
pushing up its weight.
– If it fires mostly on mislabelled samples (\(y=0,p>0)\),
the term becomes negative, pulling down its weight.
Because these rules are binary, \(z_{ k,r }^{ i }\in {0,1},\)
the gradient boils down to counting how many times a rule
contributes in mis‐ or well‐classified instances.

Hence **only the weight vector appears in this gradient**; no deeper neural network features or hidden states are involved.

---

## 9.7 Practical Considerations During Optimization  

* **Non‑negativity enforcement**: We implement it either by applying ReLU on every forward pass (so that gradients flow only from positive weights), or by performing an explicit projection step after each gradient update:
  
   ```
   w[k] += lr * g[k]
   w[k] = np.maximum(w[k],0)
   ```
   
   The latter guarantees strictly non‑negative values even if ReLU gradients saturate at zero.

* **Learning rate choice**: Because parameters are few (at most several hundred per class),
a relatively larger learning rate such as `lr=0.01` works well; Adam can also be used without bias terms.

* **Early stopping or epoch selection**: Since there are few training samples,
we employ k‑fold cross‑validation (with `K=2` or `4`) over epochs and pick the epoch yielding minimal validation cross‑entropy.

* **Regularisation**: Because no overfitting penalty is needed—the model can express at most as many degrees of freedom as there are rules—`L2` regularisation may be omitted unless empirical tests indicate stability problems.

---

## 9.8 Summary  

- For every class we collect binary *rule satisfaction* vectors.
- A non‑negative weight vector per class is learned.
- Logits are inner products between weights and binary vectors.
- Softmax transforms logits into a proper probability distribution.
- Cross‑entropy backpropagation yields easily computable gradients.
- The entire learning process is extremely lightweight and requires no representation learning from raw tabular data—everything upfront comes from expert knowledge embedded in an LLM’s natural language rules.

This linear weighting layer turns community expertise encoded by LLMs into actionable numeric importance scores, enabling fast inference without repeatedly querying large language models and ensuring robustness even when data samples are scarce.
