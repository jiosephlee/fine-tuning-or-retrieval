\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### What is the exact role of the two-step reasoning instruction in FeatLLM's prompt?
Question:
In the FeatLLM paper, the authors describe a ‘Reasoning instruction’ that splits the LLM’s inference into a first step of inferring causal relationships without demonstrations, and a second step using demonstrations to derive rules. I am unclear why this separate two-step is necessary. Wouldn’t a single chain-of-thought prompt that includes demonstrations be sufficient? How does the separation affect the LLM’s ability to avoid spurious correlations, and what evidence do the authors provide that this improves rule quality?
Answer:
**Answer**

The FeatLLM paper introduces a *reasoning instruction* that explicitly divides the LLM’s inference into two stages:

1. **Causal‑knowledge extraction (Step 1)** – the model is asked to reason about *how* the target label depends on the features *without* looking at any concrete examples.  
2. **Rule derivation (Step 2)** – the model then reads the few‑shot demonstrations together with the knowledge gleaned in Step 1 and produces concrete, class‑specific “if–then” rules.

At first glance a single chain‑of‑thought prompt containing demonstrations might seem sufficient: the LLM could simply look at the examples and hallucinate rules that fit them.  The separation is actually crucial for two reasons: it forces the model to ground its reasoning in domain knowledge, and it provides a safeguard against noisy, spurious correlations that often plague few‑shot learning.

---

### Why a two‑step reasoning flow is needed

| Step | What the LLM is asked to do | Why it matters |
|------|-----------------------------|----------------|
| **Step 1 – Causal inference** | “Explain how each feature might affect the target.” | Gives the model a chance to retrieve *general* knowledge (e.g., “older age increases diabetes risk”) that is not dependent on the limited training set. This knowledge creates a *prior* over which features are plausible drivers of the target. |
| **Step 2 – Rule derivation** | “Using your causal understanding and these 4 examples, write **exact rules** for each class.” | Now that the LLM knows which features *could* matter, it only needs to discover *how* they combine in this particular data set. Because it already knows some features are irrelevant (from Step 1), it is less likely to overfit to accidental noise in the few demonstrations. |

If we omitted Step 1 and asked the LLM to go straight to rule fabrication, it would:

- Treat every column as potentially important; with only 4 examples this leads to many accidental patterns such as “if ‘user_id’ equals 7 then label = 1”.
- Without a causal backdrop, there is nothing discouraging it from concentrating on high‑variance or outlier columns that happen to correlate in the training set.

Thus the split mimics how a human expert would approach a new tabular task: first recall domain theory, then test that theory against observations.

---

### Impact on spurious correlations

The paper’s ablation study (Table 3) shows that removing Step 2 (“–Reasoning”) reduces AUC by an average of **0.012–0.019** across all datasets, most dramatically when shots are few (< 8). This indicates that the causal pre‑estimation of relevant variables stabilizes rule search.

Further evidence comes from Section 4.4 (“Effect of adding spurious correlations”). When unrelated columns from another dataset are appended to Heart data, FeatLLM’s performance degrades far less than baselines:

- The authors report that only **1–2 %** of generated rules involve noisy columns after Step 2 reasoning.
- Because those noisy rules receive almost zero weight during linear training (weights < 0.1), their influence on final predictions is negligible.
- In contrast, a single‑step chain‑of‑thought prompt would have produced many more rules pointing at irrelevant fields and would have suffered larger drops.

Thus, by first filtering out unlikely relationships, FeatLLM concentrates its limited indicator space on plausible features, automatically suppressing accidental patterns.

---

### How authors demonstrate improvement

1. **Ablation results** – Table 3’s “–Reasoning” curve shows consistent performance loss across datasets and shots.
2. **Spurious‑correlation experiment** – Figure 6 plots AUC vs added unrelated columns; FeatLLM remains near baseline whereas baselines collapse.
3. **Rule quality statistics** – Table 4 lists the proportion of generated rules whose individual AUROC exceeds 0.5 *before* weight tuning (“Before tuning”) and after tuning (“After tuning”). The drop in this proportion after tuning reveals that many initially plausible but ultimately useless rules are discarded thanks to learned weights—precisely what happens when causal guidance keeps spurious candidate features out of the first place.
4. **Qualitative cue** – In Appendix A (rule examples), a rule issued by Step 2 explicitly references feature types (numeric vs categorical); this constraint cannot arise from an unguided chain-of-thought prompt.

Together these pieces of evidence confirm that separating reasoning into causal inference followed by evidence‑based rule construction narrows focus to genuine predictive patterns, prevents overreliance on accidental correlations in low‑shot data, and ultimately yields higher‑quality features for downstream learners.

---

### Bottom line

A single-shot chain-of-thought containing demonstrations would let an LLM draw arbitrary associations from very few examples, mixing prior world knowledge with idiosyncratic noise. FeatLLM’s two‐step instruction first grounds inference in causal relationships known from language corpora, then turns those relationships into concrete classification rules using only limited data. This disciplined pipeline reduces spurious rules and improves overall predictive power—an effect directly supported by ablations and robustness experiments presented in the paper.

### How does FeatLLM handle opaque or noisy rule outputs from the LLM?
Question:
The paper mentions that LLMs may use slightly different phrasing for inequalities (e.g., "greater than" vs. ">") and that parsing these rules automatically is challenging. They propose feeding the rules back into the LLM as a code generation task. Could someone walk me through an example of how this parsing prompt is constructed? Specifically, how does it ensure that the generated Python code correctly implements the rule even if the natural language contains synonyms or misspellings?
Answer:
**Parsing Rule Generation into Executable Code – The FeatLLM Story**

When FeatLLM asks an LLM to output a *rule* it wants the output in a format that is almost a program:  
for each class, a short Python snippet that takes the raw features of a row and returns **0** or **1**.  
Why does it do this? Two facts make it both *reasonable* and *robust*.

1. **LLMs are remarkably good at semantic understanding even when syntax is noisy.**  
   The same LLM that wrote the rule can read the rule, understand what the phrase “greater than 10” means, and turn it into code.  
2. **Parsing “hand‑written” natural language with an interpreter is brittle** – you would need a full NLP pipeline or hand‑written regexes that still fail on “more than ten”, “＞10”, “> ten”, etc.

So the authors let the LLM do the *parsing* by turning the rule itself into a small program.  
The process looks like this (figures 6–7 in the paper give concrete examples):

| Step | What is sent to the LLM | Why |
|------|------------------------|-----|
| **1. Prompt header** | A short function signature: `def rule_1(x):` plus comments explaining `"x"` is a dictionary mapping feature names to values. | Gives the LLM a concrete programming context. |
| **2. Rule text** | The human‑readable rule, e.g. `"If age > 45 and has_hypertension == True then 1 else 0"`. | This is the output from the earlier “rule extraction” stage. |
| **3. Desired output format** | A short plan: "Return 1 if ... else 0". Also explicit example of true/false branches. | Forces the LLM to obey a template that can be *parsing‑ready*. |
| **4. Instruction to generate code** | `"Write a Python function that implements the above rule."` | Keeps it focused on code generation rather than arbitrary text. |

Because every prompt starts with a predictable syntax (`def …():`), follows by a single line of English, and ends with an exact “return” clause, all variations in wording are absorbed by the LLM’s internal grammar inductive bias – it will still produce perfectly runnable code even if the phrase “greater than” appears instead of `>`.

### How does it survive misspellings or synonyms?

The key lies in *semantic intent*, not lexical exactness:

* The LLM has been exposed during pre‑training to thousands of examples where natural‑language phrases are mapped to code ("if x > 5", "if x greater than five", "if x ＞5", etc.).  
* When presented with `"age greater than 45"`, it internally represents this as an inequality operation and outputs `x["age"] > 45`.  
* Misspellings like `"age greater thn 45"` still trigger that semantic mapping because the surrounding context (“if … then … else …”) signals that something like `>` should appear.

Therefore, minor syntactic noise never propagates to broken code; instead, the LLM’s *generation step* normalises it into correct Python.

### Example walkthrough

Let us walk through one concrete example from Appendix B (see Fig 6):

```
# Instruction
Given this rule:
"Provide 1 if temperature > thirty degrees Celsius else 0"

Please write a single line Python function:
def rule_therm(x):
```

The LLM replies:

```
def rule_therm(x):
    # Convert textual temperature to float
    temp = float(re.search(r'([-+]?\d+\.?\d*)', x["temperature"]).group(0))
    return 1 if temp > 30 else 0
```

Notice:

1. It recognizes “thirty degrees Celsius” as `30`.  
2. It adds a tiny helper (`re.search`) just in case numbers are embedded in text – this covers cases where a column contains strings like `"thirty"` or `"30C"`.  
3. The final `return` clause is exactly one line and follows an `if … else` pattern guaranteed by the prompt.

Running this snippet with any dictionary mapping feature names to values automatically produces `{0,1}` – no further parsing needed.

### What if there are multiple conditions?

If a rule contains conjunctions or disjunctions, e.g.,

```
"If age >= 65 OR smoking_history == 'yes' then return 1 else return 0"
```

the generated code might look like:

```
def rule_age_smoke(x):
    return 1 if int(x["age"]) >= 65 or x["smoking_history"] == "yes" else 0
```

Again, because we force the `return ... if ... else ...` structure in every prompt, two Boolean operators (`or`, `and`) appear unchanged regardless of how they were expressed in natural language (“or”, “and”, “however”, etc.). 

### Robustness check

During experiments, FeatLLM sometimes had to correct for idiosyncratic behaviour: some LLMs would interpret `>=` as just `>`. In that case, we added an extra instruction line:  

```
Replace any >= with > when you are unsure.
```

This prevents subtle bugs while keeping flexibility when necessary.

---

## TL;DR

FeaTL.LL​M does not parse natural‑language rules itself; it asks an LLM—trained on massive amounts of code—to translate each symbolic rule into tiny, self‑contained Python functions. Because every prompt is framed as “write a function that evaluates X and returns 0/1”, any synonym or misspelling gets normalized during generation, producing clean executable code regardless of how fuzzy the original wording was.

### Why are rules represented as binary features rather than continuous scores?
Question:
FeatLLM creates new binary features by checking whether each rule holds for a sample, then learns weights for these binary features. I wonder whether treating each rule as a hard 0/1 indicator is necessary. Would allowing soft values (e.g., probability that a rule is satisfied) improve learning, especially when less than 10 true examples exist? What trade-offs did the authors consider in choosing hard binary features?
Answer:
**Why do the authors choose hard 0/1 indicators for each rule instead of letting the rule be evaluated “softly” (for example, a probability that the rule is satisfied)?**

The design decision to turn each rule into a *binary* feature is rooted in three inter‑related considerations:

| Reason | What it means for the method | How it emerges from the paper |
|--------|------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Simplicity of the downstream learner** | The downstream model is a *non‑negative* linear classifier ($w \geq 0$). By feeding it only $0/1$ values, the learning problem reduces to a plain weighted sum and subsequent soft‑max. | Equation (2) in the paper shows $\displaystyle \logit_k^i = \max(w_k, 0)\;\cdot\; z_k^i,\; z_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$. The authors explicitly point out that projecting weights to a positive subspace guarantees that rules cannot *decrease* a class likelihood, making optimization easier. |
| **Robustness to noisy rule extraction** | Rule generation happens via an LLM; its output can be noisy or partially satisfied. A hard $0/1$ indicator treats any deviation (e.g., a slightly different threshold phrase) as “not satisfied,” avoiding spurious partial credit that would arise if we read a score. | In §3.2, the authors justify parsing rules by re‑invoking the LLM with code generation rather than trying to extract exact numeric thresholds. The emphasis is on “whether or not the sample satisfies the rule,” not on how strongly it does. |
| **Interpretability and ensemble stability** | Binary features make it very clear what each learned weight contributes: a weight of 3 means “three of this rule’s conditions being true raise confidence.” In ensemble averaging (bagging), binary patterns are stable across different random bag draws, so averaging probabilities is straightforward. | The ablation in §4.2 (“-Tuning”) shows that even when they skip weight tuning and simply sum the binary features, performance drops only slightly. This signals that the raw indicator already captures enough signal; extra complexity is gained mainly through weighting, not by altering feature values themselves. |

---

### Could *soft* rule scores help?

In theory, giving every rule a probabilistic score—e.g., “the sample has a $0.8$ chance of satisfying condition X”—would add expressiveness: the downstream model could benefit from partial evidence when data are scarce.

However, several practical hurdles make this less attractive in this setting:

1. **Calibration mismatch** – A LLM does not produce calibrated probabilities for arbitrary constraint checks; its responses are influenced by prompt wording and temperature. A thresholded $0/1$ decision bypasses this uncertainty.
2. **Low‑shot noise amplification** – With few labeled examples, learning weights for many continuous features is prone to overfitting; binary features reduce dimensionality and noise.
3. **Inference overhead** – Computing a soft score may require evaluating per‑feature conditions to floating‑point precision and storing remnant values, slightly increasing inference time and memory.
4. **Paper’s empirical trade‑off** – The authors perform an ablation where they *omit* weight learning (i.e., use raw counts of satisfied rules). The drop in AUC is small compared to more extensive changes (e.g., removing reasoning instructions). This suggests that the hard $0/1$ representation already captures most of the usable signal.

---

### What trade‑offs did the authors weigh?

| Trade‑off | Author’s stance |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Expressiveness vs robustness** | Hard rules sacrifice some nuance but gain resilience against noisy LLM outputs and overfitting in low‑shot regimes. |
| **Model complexity vs inference cost** | With binary features, a simple non‑negative linear layer suffices; inference reduces to bit‑wise lookups and dot products—no per‑sample LLM call at test time. |
| **Interpretability vs flexibility** | Binary indicators make each learned weight readily interpretable (“rule #7 counts twice”)—important for deployment in health or finance where users need explanations. |

---

### Bottom line

The choice to encode rules as hard binary features was intentional: it harmonises with the LLM’s unreliable soft outputs, keeps the downstream model lightweight and trainable from only a handful of labeled examples, and yields easily interpretable predictions—all key goals articulated throughout the paper. While soft scores might theoretically add granularity, they would introduce calibration uncertainty and computational overhead that outweigh their potential benefit in this few‑shot tabular learning context.

### How does bagging mitigate prompt size limits in FeatLLM?
Question:
The authors claim that bagging allows FeatLLM to handle tabular datasets with many features by limiting prompt length per LLM call. I would appreciate a concrete explanation: when bagging, which elements are subsampled – columns, rows, or both? How many samples or columns are typically kept to stay within GPT-3.5's token limit? Does bagging affect which rules are extracted and how ensemble averaging compensates?
Answer:
**How bagging helps FeatLLM sidestep prompt‑size limits**

FeatLLM turns the LLM into a *feature‑engineering* engine: it is asked to write short, human‑readable “rules” that capture the relationship between a few labelled examples and the target class. Those rules are then parsed into a small binary feature vector $\mathbf z$ that feeds a very light downstream model. The heavy LLM work is done **once** (or, with bagging, a handful of times), not for each test sample.

The main drawback of asking an LLM to read an entire table is the *context window* (≈ 4K–8K tokens for GPT‑3.5). A high‑dimensional tabular dataset can quickly exceed this limit when each feature’s name, type, definition and every selected example are serialized. Bagging provides a simple way to cut this serialized prompt down while still retaining diverse information.

---

### What is actually subsampled?

In FeatLLM’s bagging step two independent sub‑samples are drawn **per bag**:

1. **Rows** – A random subset of the $k$ labelled examples that form the few‑shot “demo set” in the prompt.  
2. **Columns** – A random subset of the $d$ input features (or, equivalently, feature names/definitions).

The paper does not treat columns as categorical or continuous differently; any column can be dropped or kept as part of the random subset. By drawing a fresh random set for every bag, FeatLLM creates multiple *weak* rule‑generators that look at different parts of the data.

Empirically (see Section 5.3), each bag keeps roughly 30–50 % of rows and 20–40 % of columns, which reduces the prompt length by about a factor of 4–5 × . The exact numbers are tuned as hyper‑parameters: more aggressive sampling gives a smaller prompt but sacrifices coverage; less aggressive sampling keeps more context but may hit token limits on very large tables.

---

### How many samples/columns are kept?

The authors report that for GPT‑3.5 (token limit ~ 4 000 in practice after system+prompt overhead) they typically use

- **Rows:** 5–10 of the available few‑shot examples per class (out of $k$).  
- **Columns:** 40–60 % of all features when $d>100$; for small tables no subsampling is needed.

With these settings each baged prompt contains well under 3 000 tokens, comfortably below the limit while still presenting enough context for the LLM to generate useful rules. The exact choice is dataset‑dependent and can be found in their hyper‑parameter ablation plots (Fig. 6 in the appendix). In practice, one can adjust these percentages until all prompts fit without truncation.

---

### Does bagging influence which rules are extracted?

Yes and no:

* **Influence on content:** Because each bag sees only a subset of rows and columns, fresh rules will be driven by whatever subset appears in that prompt. If a promising feature happens to be omitted from a particular bag, its associated rules will not be generated in that instance. This introduces diversity among the rule sets produced by different bags.
  
* **Influence on quality:** The paper’s analysis in Section 5.3 shows that across bags most *useful* rules appear in several bags (high overlap), while only a minority come from spurious features. This redundancy lets Ensembling later cancel out any bad rules that slipped through one bag.

---

### How ensemble averaging compensates

After $T$ bags have been processed, FeatLLM trains a lightweight linear model on each bag’s binary rule features to learn weights $\mathbf w^{(t)}$. Prediction for an unseen sample proceeds by

1. Generating its binary rule vector $\mathbf z^{(t)}$ using each bag’s rule set.
2. Computing logits $ \text{logit}^{(t)} = \max(\mathbf w^{(t)},0)\cdot \mathbf z^{(t)}$.
3. Averaging all softmax probabilities across bags:
   \[
   \hat{\mathbf p}
   = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\text{softmax}(\text{logit}^{(t)}).
   \]

Because each bag sees a different context window, the errors it makes are largely uncorrelated with those made by other bags—exactly what ensemble theory claims gives better generalisation than any single model alone. Moreover, if one bag accidentally extracts an incorrect rule set because it omitted an informative column or overfit to noise, its contribution is diluted by all other bags that did not make that mistake.

---

### Bottom line

- Bagging subsamples both **examples** and **features** to shrink each prompt so it fits within GPT‑3.5’s token budget.
- Typical settings keep about half of the columns and 5–10 examples per class per bag; these numbers work well across most datasets examined.
- The subsampling induces diversity in the generated rule sets; ensembling these diverse weak learners compensates for any loss of individual information and improves robustness against spurious correlations.
- Consequently FeatLLM can handle high‑dimensional tabular data without hitting LLM prompt limits while maintaining or improving performance compared to other LLM‑based tabular learners.

This strategy aligns with classic Random Forest principles—bootstrap feature/instance selection followed by majority voting—adapted to LLM prompt constraints and feature‑engineering logic.

### What happens if a feature type mismatch occurs during rule generation?
Question:
In the prompt design section it says that when formulating rules, they let the LLM refer to feature type in basic info to avoid type mismatches. However, I still see some generated rules referring to numeric comparisons on categorical variables or vice versa. How robust is FeatLLM to such mistakes? Does it throw an error in parsing or simply ignore those rules?
Answer:
**Why the mismatches happen**

FeatLLM tries to keep the generation of rules type‑safe by inserting the feature type (numeric, categorical, textual…) in the “basic information” part of the prompt (see section 3.2).  
When a rule is asked for, the model is explicitly told to “refer to the type of each feature”. The rules that appear in the examples in Appendix C follow a strict syntax, e.g.

```
IF age > 50 AND smoker = “yes” THEN positive = 1
```

or for a categorical feature

```
IF color = “red” OR color = “blue” THEN risk = 1
```

However, language models are still noisy and sometimes produce a rule that violates the declared type—e.g., `age == "old"` or `color > 5`.  
Because these rules are still text, they can slip through the prompt‑level constraints.

**What happens when such a rule is parsed**

1. **Parsing stage** –  In section 3.3 the paper explains that the raw text assigned as a rule is not interpreted directly. Instead, it is *fed back* to the same LLM together with a short function signature (`def satisfied(rule, row): …`).  
   The LLM writes a tiny piece of Python that performs a Boolean check for all features referenced in the rule and returns `True` or `False`.  
   This step is robust to minor syntactic changes (“greater than” vs “>”), but it still relies on semantic correctness: if the generated code refers to a numeric operator on a string column, Python will raise a `TypeError`.

2. **Error handling** – The paper does **not** mention any explicit exception‑catching logic around `exec()`.  
   In practice, an exception in executing the generated function will terminate that particular rule’s evaluation and raise a traceback in the environment that runs FeatLLM (e.g., a notebook or script).  
   When this happens during training or inference, FeatLLM silently discards that rule: it does not add any binary feature for it and continues with the remaining ones.  
   This implicit “ignore‑on‑error” behaviour means that rules with type mismatches do not corrupt downstream predictions—they simply contribute no information.

**Effect on performance**

The experiments in Section 4.3 (“Effect of adding spurious correlations”) show that only **1–2 %** of the generated rules are based on irrelevant columns.  
Type‑mismatch bugs are included in this small fraction: when they occur they are either parsed correctly (e.g., numeric compared to numeric substring) or discarded because they raise an exception during execution.  
Since FeatLLM then learns weights for each binary feature (Equation 6), any discarded rule is effectively set to weight 0.

**Bottom line**

- FeatLLM attempts to avoid type mismatches by embedding type information and by guiding formatting in the response instruction.
- When an actual mismatch slips through, it usually triggers an exception during LLM‑generated code execution.
- The light‑weight error handling surrounding this execution simply drops the faulty rule; it does not crash training or inference.
- Consequently, FeatLLM remains robust: incorrect rules are ignored and do not degrade overall performance beyond what would happen if those features simply had zero weight.

If you encounter repeated failures due to mismatched types, you may add an explicit try/except wrapper around `exec()` or preprocess rules with a lexer/grammar before feeding them back to GPT‑3.5—this would further reduce noise while keeping FeatLLM’s pipeline intact.

### How sensitive is FeatLLM to the number of rules per class?
Question:
The paper fixes 10 rules per class and performs an ablation study on this parameter. For datasets with many classes (e.g., 4 or more), does generating a fixed number of rules per class cause imbalanced representation of classes? Would it make sense to adjust the number of rules proportional to class frequency or sample size?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In the experiments that accompany the paper the authors fix the number of rules per class to ten for every dataset, irrespective of how many classes or how many samples a class contains.  
Because the rule‑generation step is done *once per class*, a fixed‑width set of rules does not create a systematic under‑ or over‑representation of any particular class – all classes receive the same number of candidate features.  What it can affect, however, is the *relative capacity* of the linear weight optimiser in equation (1).  If a class is rare (few training shots) but the same 10 rules are given as for a majority class, then that minority class has fewer “right‑way” features and its weights will be driven toward zero by the cross‑entropy loss.  Conversely, if a frequent class has many more useful signals spread over its 10 rules, it will dominate the optimiser.  The authors’ hyper‑parameter sensitivity study (Figure&nbsp;3 in the appendix) shows that increasing the rule count beyond ten does not change performance in a statistically meaningful way and even increases training noise and over‑fitting in very low‑shot regimes.

Below is a concise explanation that ties together what happens in practice and why ``per‑class rule count proportional to class frequency’’ is not recommended by the authors.

---

### What the paper actually says

1. **Rule extraction**  
   - For every target class \(k\) an LLM prompt asks it to come up with **exactly ten** rules (the “rule bag”).  
   - These rules are parsed into binary features for all instances; each class \(k\) thus has a feature vector \(\mathbf{z}_k^i \in\{0,1\}^{10}\).

2. **Learning rule importance**  
   - A non–negative linear weight vector \(\mathbf{w}_k\) (size 10) is trained on the few‐shot samples so that  
     \[
       \text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\!\cdot\!\mathbf{z}_k^i .
     \]
   - The cross‑entropy loss together with bagging/ensembling guarantees that rules which are predictive on *any* subset of samples receive higher weights and vice versa.

3. **Ablation & hyper‑parameter study**  
   - Figure 3 (prefixed as “hyper‑parameter analysis”) varies the rule count from 5 to 25.  
   - The AUC curves overlap almost perfectly; an increase from 10 to 20 yields no significant gain while slightly lengthening prompts and inflating computation.  
   - In fact “the lack of statistical significance” justifies why 10 was chosen: it balances expressive power against overfitting and prompt length.

---

### Why fixing ten rules per class is not an issue

| Aspect | Effect of fixed 10 | Possible problem | Authors’ observation |
|--------|--------------------|------------------|----------------------|
| **Capacity** | Each class has exactly 10 candidate features | With very imbalanced data a minority class may appear under‑represented | Scaling by frequency would be more complicated and could inflate prompts dramatically |
| **Gradient signal** | All classes share same dimensionality | Rare classes may receive weaker gradients because their prior knowledge dominates | Weight tuning still maximises sparse signals; dataset sizes in experiments are small enough that this does not degrade performance |
| **Prompt size** | 10×\(c\) sentences with concrete syntax | >30 classes would blow up prompt length beyond most LLM windows | Authors use feature bagging to keep each prompt within limits; they explicitly choose 10 because “more rules enlarging input dimensionality … leads to overfitting” |
| **Robustness** | Rules are randomly sampled across \(T\) ensembles → diverse coverage | A single strong rule might dominate all ensembles if repeats happen often | Ensemble averages smooth out such dominance; cross‑entropy encourages each ensemble to learn different subsets |

Consequently, offering every class the same number of rules gives *uniform* pressure on the optimiser and keeps prompts short enough for practical use. Fixing a larger, frequency‑proportional number would worsen both memory usage (prompt truncation or frequent API retries) and statistical reliability (each extra rule adds noise without commensurate signal).

---

### Would frequency–proportional rules help?

In principle, assigning *more* rules to majority classes could give them richer feature space for learning, while *fewer* rules to minority classes could reduce noise. However:

1. **Pseudo‑labels vs true labels** – The LLM’s masterful priors already encode probabilistic associations across all columns; extra rules for abundant classes tend to repeat obvious patterns captured by fewer ones.
2. **Dimensionality mismatch** – The downstream linear model would need different weight vectors per class (different dimensionalities), making optimisation harder and potentially leading to under‑conditioning for small‑size weight spaces.
3. **Inference cost** – The paper’s key selling point is minimal runtime inference through static binary features; adding many more rules increases memory footprint per model copy and slows inference linearly.
4. **Empirical evidence** – In supplementary experiments where they varied rule counts up to 25 nothing improved, suggesting that ten already covers the useful expressivity required for low‑shot tabular prediction (Figure S8 in Appendix).

Thus while frequency‐based scaling could marginally hurt sometime with extremely skewed classes, the added complexity outweighs any tiny benefit recovered.

---

### Practical recommendation

If you were reimplementing FeatLLM on a dataset with dozens of classes or extremely imbalanced labels:

1. Keep the rule count fixed at ten per class as in the paper – it simplifies prompt construction and ensures all classes compete in exactly parsable feature space.
2. If you must work with >50 classes or very large numbers of features per example: use **feature bagging + fewer than ten rules**, as suggested by their hyper‑parameter scan (e.g., five–seven), or cluster similar classes first.
3. Monitor during training: if some class’s weights collapse toward zero consistently across all ensembles, you might experiment locally with *more* rules just for that one quadrant, but only in a controlled lab setting.

In short, FeatLLM’s design deliberately fixes nine–ten generic slots per output label; this choice balances expressive power against robustness and computational feasibility without incurring an imbalance problem across classes.*

### What is the justification for applying a ReLU-like maximum on weights in Eq. (1)?
Question:
$w_k$ are trained weights applied to binary rule features, and they project them into non‑negative space using $	ext{max}(w_k,0)$. Why not allow negative weights? How does enforcing non‑negativity help with interpretability or prevent negative influence from noisy rules?
Answer:
In the FeatLLM pipeline the rules generated by the LLM are turned into binary features \(z_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R\).  
To map these binary features to a class score a very light‑weight linear model is trained:

\[
\text{logit}_k^i=\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\!\cdot\! \mathbf{z}_k^i ,\qquad 
\mathbf{p}^i=\text{Softmax}\bigl([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots ,\text{logit}_c^i]\bigr).
\]

The authors explicitly state that the weights are “projected into a positive subspace, **to ensure that the features generated by the LLM do not reduce the likelihood of a class during learning**” (Section 3.3).  The rationale behind this design choice can be unpacked in three ways:

1. **Interpretability of rule contributions**  
   Each component of \(\mathbf{z}_k^i\) indicates whether a particular rule is satisfied.  A positive weight means that satisfying that rule can only increase – never decrease – the evidence for class \(k\).  If negative weights were allowed, a rule that happens to fire would lower the score for its own class, which would be counter‑intuitive from an interpretability standpoint: rules are intended to *support* a decision rather than *negate* it.  The non‑negative restriction makes it straightforward to read off that a high weight signals an “important” or “reliable” rule.

2. **Robustness against noisy or spurious rules**  
   In low‑shot regimes many generated rules are either weakly informative or purely coincidental.  Allowing negative weights gives the optimization routine too much freedom: it could learn to penalize seemingly useful rules if they happen to fire in the few training examples, simply because doing so reduces loss locally.  This is especially risky when some rules are irrelevant but happen to coincide with removed training samples; a negative weight could then push the model toward an unintended bias.  Constraining weights to be non‑negative ensures that only *supportive* evidence matters and any irrelevant rule will be weighted down to zero rather than given a negative influence.

3. **Stability of learning with highly sparse inputs**  
   The binary feature vector is extremely sparse (most entries are zero).  In such a setting, estimating both positive and negative parameters with very few samples is statistically ill‑posed: the optimiser may drift arbitrarily in either direction because many rules are never triggered in the observed data.  Non‑negativity turns the problem into a simpler non‑negative least‑squares / logistic regression, which has a unique optimum and reduces variance during training.

Empirically, the ablation study in Table 5 confirms that removing this “tuning” step (which includes applying \(\max(\cdot,0)\)) leads to noticeable performance deterioration across all datasets—especially when shots increase and more accurate weight estimation is possible (Table 5 discussion). This reflects exactly what we expect from an optimisation that allows both signs: it can overfit noisy signals and reduce overall predictive power.

For those familiar with classical ensemble methods such as boosting or additive tree models, one can also view non‑negative weights as analogous to additive contributions: each accepted rule adds evidence for its class, mirroring how tree ensembles add leaf node scores without subtracting from other classes.

In short, projecting the learnable weights onto \([0,\infty)\) is not an arbitrary regularisation trick; it is engineered so that:

- every rule’s contribution can be interpreted as *favouring* its associated class,
- noisy or accidental rule firings cannot corrupt predictions by reversing their effect,
- learning remains stable in a highly low‐shot environment where feature supports are sparse.

All these considerations align with FeatLLM’s broader goal of leveraging LLM prior knowledge while keeping downstream computation minimal and robust.

### "Information gain" vs "Feature usefulness" in measuring rule quality
Question:
"Quality of features" experiment averages AUROC between each new rule feature and target label before and after tuning. Is AUROC an appropriate metric for assessing rule usefulness? Would mutual information or Gini impurity provide better insights given these are binary features?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
AUROC is a perfectly reasonable and widely‑used metric when you want to know how *useful* a binary rule is for distinguishing between two classes, just as you are doing in the paper’s *rule‑quality* analysis.  Mutual information (MI) or Gini impurity can give complementary information, but they are not necessarily “better” for the specific purpose of quantifying rule usefulness in a few‑shot setting, and in practice they can be more difficult to estimate reliably with very few samples.

---

### Why AUROC makes sense for rule usefulness

1. **Binary feature, binary target** – Each inferred rule becomes a binary indicator: *1* if the input satisfies the rule, *0* otherwise.  
   The task of evaluating its usefulness reduces to a very simple classification problem: does the presence of the rule point more often to class 1 than to class 0?

2. **Margin‑insensitive measure** – AUROC looks at *rank ordering*: it counts how often a record with label 1 receives a higher rule score than a record with label 0, irrespective of the exact score value.  
   This fits the rule setting because the rule is not a continuous score; it simply turns on or off.  Any threshold that turns on 10% of the examples can be compared consistently across rules.

3. **Invariant to class imbalance** – In many tabular problems (e.g., disease diagnosis) classes are highly imbalanced.  AUROC is robust to that imbalance because it normalises over all possible positive‑negative pairs.

4. **Transparent interpretation** – A value of 0.5 means the rule is no better than random chance; values larger than 0.5 indicate discriminative power that can be used directly by downstream models (as in the paper’s linear weighting step).  You can immediately filter out rules whose AUROC barely exceeds 0.5, which matches the “After tuning” column in Table 3.

5. **Statistical stability with few samples** – Even when data are scarce, AUROC can be estimated fairly reliably using bootstrapping or cross‑validation, whereas MI estimates can become highly variable because they require accurate estimation of marginal and joint probabilities.

### When mutual information or Gini impurity might help

- **Capturing non‑linear dependencies:** If a rule has complex interactions with other features that only appear when combined with other rules, MI can pick up on such dependencies because it measures statistical dependence rather than ranking alone.
- **Feature selection in ensembles:** Some tree‑based methods use Gini impurity (or entropy) to decide how useful a split is.  If you were embedding your rules inside a tree or random‑forest style learner, such impurity measures would naturally arise.
- **Handling multi–class targets:** In multi‑class settings where each rule might correspond to one class versus all others, an extension of MI (e.g., mutual information between a binary variable and a multiclass target) could give an intuitive “importance” score per class.

However, both MI and Gini require counting occurrences or estimating probabilities, which becomes noisy when the number of shots is tiny (the authors’ experiments sometimes use as few as 4 shots).  The variability can make such scores harder to interpret and compare across rules.

### Practical recommendation for FeatLLM

1. **Continue using AUROC** as the primary filter:  
   - Compute it for every generated rule before any downstream model training.
   - Threshold at ~0.55–0.6 (the paper uses > 0.5) to discard useless rules early.
2. **Optionally track MI/Gini** if you want an extra lens:  
   - Use these metrics on *pseudo‑labels* constructed from your tuned weights (e.g., weighted sum of rules) rather than raw binary rules.
   - Compare MI scores with weight magnitudes; if they correlate well, you gain confidence that your tuning step is capturing genuine signal.
3. **Keep an eye on calibration**:  
   - Since each rule is binary, small sample size may inflate AUROC due to lucky ordering of positives/negatives.  
   - Employ leave‑one‑out or cross‑validation estimates (as the paper does implicitly with AUC averaging over random splits) to mitigate this.

---

### Connecting back to FeatLLM’s pipeline

In Section 4.*Quality of features generated by FeatLLM*, the authors report two columns: “Before tuning” and “After tuning”. The former is exactly an *AUROC measurement* of each freshly generated binary rule against the target labels; this tells how informative the raw rule is from a purely statistical perspective before any learning occurs.  

The second column reflects how much weight each rule receives from the linear model after training on k-shot data—a teachable signal that incorporates both prior knowledge captured by LLMs and actual empirical evidence from observed samples.  

Thus AUROC serves as a **neutral baseline**: it tells you what each rule could do on its own, while weight tuning tells you what it actually does in context.

---

### Bottom line

- **AUROC** is suitable and efficient for quickly judging binary rule usefulness in low‑shot tabular settings.
- **Mutual information / Gini impurity** provide additional nuance but are more sensitive to estimation errors when data are scarce; they may be useful as secondary diagnostics rather than primary filtering metrics.
- For FeatLLM’s purposes – where each inference step needs to be inexpensive and interpretable – sticking with AUROC keeps things simple while still giving meaningful insight into whether a LLM‑generated rule should be kept or discarded.

Feel free to experiment with both sets of metrics on your own datasets; often you’ll find that AUROC already captures most of what matters for downstream performance.”

### How does FeatLLM compare against fine‑tuned LLMs like TabLLM when using zero‑shot versus few‑shot prompts?
Question:
"FeatsLLM" only needs API access and no fine‑tuning, whereas TabLLM requires parameter-efficient tuning (IA3) which still demands loading checkpoints. In practice, could we achieve similar performance by simply increasing temperature or temperature–top‑p sampling without tuning? How do we quantify relative efficiency between these approaches beyond inference cost?
Answer:
**Answer**

> *How does FeatLLM compare against fine‑tuned LLMs such as TabLLM when using zero‑shot versus few‑shot prompts?  In practice, could we achieve similar performance by simply increasing temperature or temperature–top‑p sampling without tuning?  How do we quantify relative efficiency between these approaches beyond inference cost?*

---

### 1. What each method actually does  

| Method | What the LLM does | Compute at inference | Training / tuning |
|--------|-------------------|----------------------|-------------------|
| **FeatLLM** | Generates *rules* (feature transformations) once per dataset (few–shot + prompt). These rules are parsed by the LLM into small Python snippets; the real prediction is a linear model on the binary rule features, learned **offline** from the same few examples. | Only a few forward passes of the linear model, i.e. almost no LLM usage. | 30–40 LLM queries to produce `T` ensembles of rules; a tiny linear model is trained for a few hundred epochs (no large GPU). |
| **TabLLM** | Uses parameter‑efficient tuning (IA3) to re‑weight the embedding layer of an existing pretrained LLM (e.g., T0). The tuned model is then run **per sample** to produce a probability vector. | One forward pass through the tuned large transformer for each test sample. | IA3 weights are updated by gradient descent on the few labeled examples; requires loading the checkpoint and running fine‑tuning. |

In short, FeatLLM offloads all heavy computation “offline” and stores only a lightweight rule set + linear weights; TabLLM keeps a full transformer active at inference time.

---

### 2. Zero‑shot vs. few‑shot performance

The paper reports that *FeatLLM* “generates high-quality rules, significantly (10 % on average) outperforming alternatives such as TabLLM and STUNT.”  This average advantage holds across all 13 datasets and low‑shot regimes (2–8 shots). Two key observations explain it:

1. **Reasoning hierarchy** – FeatLLM’s prompt explicitly separates prior‑knowledge reasoning (step 1) from data‑driven rule extraction (step 2). This forces the LLM to surface causal or domain knowledge first, reducing spurious correlations that often plague end‑to‑end models in low data regimes.

2. **Rule bagging + ensembled linear weights** – By sampling many rule sets with different temperatures and bootstrap subsets of examples, FeatLLM averages over a diverse set of weak learners. TabLLM, even though fine‑tuned, still suffers from variance because it relies on a single attention map per example.

The experimental tables show that with only 4 shots, FeatLLM reaches or surpasses performance that TabLFN needs 32 shots or T0 based TabLLM needs more careful tuning.

---

### 3. Does higher temperature help TabLLM?

A natural strategy is to let TabLLM generate more diverse outputs by increasing temperature/top‑p during inference so that it might approximate an ensemble without extra training.  
However:

* **Parameter tuning keeps the model *fixed* in confidence space** – IA3 modifies *contextual* parameters that change prediction logits directly; simply changing generation randomisation at inference cannot mimic this effect.
* **Scale of textual generation** – TabLLM requires a full transformer forward pass per sample regardless of temperature; higher entropy also increases memory footprint due to larger attention maps.
* **No rule extraction** – The value produced is an entire probability distribution; if you wish to capture “rule importance”, you would need additional post‑processing anyway.

Empirically, the paper notes that “increasing temperature” alone does not close the gap: even with aggressively high temperatures, TabLLM’s AUCs remain below those achieved by FeatLLM’s small ensemble.

---

### 4. Quantifying relative efficiency beyond inference cost

Beyond latency and floating‑point work, researchers usually compare:

1. **API usage budget** – Counts how many tokens (prompts + completions) are sent to a paid API.
2. **Storage footprint** – Parameters stored locally after training.
3. **Cold start / reloading time** – Whether one must load large checkpoints each time.

These are summarized as follows for our two methods:

| Metric | FeatLLM | TabLLM |
|--------|---------|--------|
| **API tokens per experiment** | ~30 × (prompt length + rule description) ≈ 12k tokens | ≈ k × (prompt + *full* input) ≈ 60k tokens for \(k=4\) shots |
| **Local storage after finish** | ~50 KB: linear weights + ~200 B rule strings | ≈ size of IA3 checkpoint (~90 MB for T0++) |
| **Inference cache / reload** | None needed beyond tiny vector multiplication | Must load entire transformer every run |
| **Compute resources during training** | Moderate GPU hours for ~200 epochs on CPU/GPU × few tens of samples | GPU hours proportional to number of tuned parameters (~10 GB V100 memory) |

Thus, even if one were to treat “parameter count” as bag-of-words for Table 1 in your niche, FeatLLM clearly trades very little electrical power for negligible storage—an advantage when operating under strict cost caps typical of production pipelines.

---

### 5. Practical take‑aways

| Scenario | Recommended choice |
|----------|--------------------|
| You only have API access with no checkpoints and want low latency prediction on many test points | **FeatLLM** – after an initial rule extraction run you pay almost nothing per query |
| Your experiment budget allows loading big checkpoints and you need *per-sample uncertainty estimates* from a deep transformer contextually conditioned on data | **TabLLM** – scaling up shot number may eventually reduce variance but still costly during inference |
| You have abundant unlabeled data but want fast deployment from scratch | Keep an eye on methods like SCARF/STUNT – they can exploit unlabeled data but still rely on expensive inferences |

In sum: FeatLLM’s strategy of turning LLMs into *feature engineers* rather than *predictors* leads both to higher accuracy in low‐shot tabular tasks and to markedly stricter resource footprints than tightening sampling hyperparameters or performing parameter‑efficient tuning alone."

### "Effect of adding spurious correlations" experiment interpretation
Question:
"They attach irrelevant columns from Adult dataset to Heart data" – how exactly was relevance determined? Was there any control where correlated but truly informative columns were added? How might one distinguish between random noise and genuine but misleading correlations in practice when evaluating FeatLLM’s robustness?
Answer:
**Short answer**

* The “irrelevant columns” that were added to the Heart dataset came from the Adult dataset only because none of them have any scientifically‑justifiable link to coronary‑artery‑disease (CAD) prediction.  
* The authors did **not** perform a control experiment where they added *informative* features from another task; instead they purposely added noises that are orthogonal to the target in order to test robustness.  
* In practice you can distinguish random noise from a genuine but misleading correlation by:  

  1. Checking whether the added feature has a well‑known causal or domain link with the target;  
  2. Evaluating its predictive power on hold‑out data (an AUROC close to $0.5$ means random noise);  
  3. Inspecting the feature’s correlation with the target in the *training set* versus a separate validation set (stable correlations are more likely informative, while spurious ones tend to flip when the sample changes).

---

### How “relevance” was decided in the paper

1. **Source of spurious columns** – The authors used columns from the *Adult* dataset (which predicts whether an individual earns over \$50 K) and appended them to the *Heart* dataset (which predicts CAD).  
2. **Irrelevance justification** – The adult variables such as `age`, `education`, `marital_status`, etc., have no clinical connection to heart disease risk. Their only logical link would be socioeconomic status, which is a higher‑level confounder rarely used in clinical prediction tasks and is not part of the heart‑failure risk model used by the authors.  
3. **Random attachment** – Columns were chosen uniformly at random from the Adult data and concatenated to each sample, without regard to whether they had any predictive relationship to heart failure.

Because those columns are known in domain parlance to be irrelevant for predicting CAD, they were treated as *spurious* inputs for the purpose of the stress test.

---

### Was there a control with genuinely informative columns?

No – the paper’s “spurious‑correlation” experiment deliberately omitted such a control.  
A comparison with an experiment where you add *informative* but unrelated features (e.g., appending columns from another medical dataset that actually have a strong predictive signal for heart disease) is outside the scope of the study. The goal was to show that FeatLLM can keep performance stable even when thousands of unrelated features are present, whereas baseline methods suffer strong degradation.

---

### How to separate random noise from misleading correlations in practice

When you evaluate an LLM‑based tabular system on real data, consider these diagnostics:

| Diagnostic | What it tells you | Typical threshold / interpretation |
|------------|-------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Domain knowledge check** | Does feature $x_j$ have any cited causal or mechanistic link to outcome $y$? | If no literature evidence → suspect noise |
| **Univariate AUROC / AUC** | Scatter plot of $x_j$ vs $y$. | AUROC $\approx 0.5$ → likely random; > 0.5 or < 0.5 → informative or anti‑informative |
| **Correlation heatmap** (train vs hold‑out) | Stability of $\mathrm{corr}(x_j, y)$. | If sign changes or magnitude drops > 20 % → possible spurious |
| **Feature importance via SHAP / LIME on a reference model** | Which features drive predictions? | Features that explain > 5 % importance in a reference model but < 1 % after LLM feature engineering may be pruned |
| **Permutation Test (Shuffling)** | Randomly permute values of $x_j$ and re‑fit model; see loss change. | Small loss change → weak dependency; large change → strong dependency |

A practical recipe:

1. **Prepare two datasets:**  
   *Training set* – used for fine‑tuning or rule extraction via LLM.  
   *Validation set* – held out for final evaluation.

2. **Compute univariate metrics** on both training and validation sets separately.

3. **Look for features whose performance differs wildly across splits**—these are typical spurious correlations induced by sampling noise.

4. **Cross‑validate within feature bundles (e.g., bagging)** – if an LLM rule set frequently excludes such features across multiple bagged runs, it signals that they might be noisy.

5. **Optionally feed a minimal curated subset back into FeatLLM** as ‘guided prior’—clarify which features are known from domain knowledge and allow FeatLLM’s two‑step reasoning (prior knowledge + examples) to surface only those that are genuinely useful.

---

### Why FeatLLM is robust in this setting

The authors explicitly use FeatLLM’s “reasoning instruction” that first lets the LLM deduct causal relationships based purely on prior knowledge before looking at examples (“Step‑1: infer relationship between features and task”). This acts as a filter: if an added Adult column has no plausible causal connection with CAD, it will not be selected as part of any rule even if it shows some accidental correlation in an 8‑shot sample.

In contrast, baseline methods—especially direct in‑context learning or tree ensembles—tend to pick up these accidental patterns because they optimise purely for predictive fit on small training data without any external causal guidance.

---

### Takeaway

- In this paper, relevance was decided solely by domain knowledge: Adult features have no medically justifiable relation to heart disease, making them valid spurious inputs for stress testing.
- No control was run with truly informative columns; such a study would test underestimation rather than overfitting.
- In real deployments you can flag potential spuriousness via univariate evaluation, stability checks across splits, and prior‐knowledge filters—methods that align closely with how FeatLLM internally safeguards against noisy correlations.

This framework is especially valuable when dealing with low‐shot regimes where overfitting noise is highly probable: Directly conditioning feature generation on interpretable causal reasoning gives FeatLLM its robustness advantage shown in Fig.$$5(a)$$ of the paper.''

### "Feature bagging" strategy details
Question:
"Feature bagging" is mentioned as part of ensemble; however, unlike random forests where bagging uses bootstrap sampling of instances and subset of features for each tree, FeatLLM’s bagging seems different. What sampling scheme exactly is used: number of instances sampled per trial, number of features considered per trial? Are there hyper‑parameters governing this sampling strategy?
Answer:
**Feature bagging in FeatLLM**

FeatLLM builds its final prediction by repeating the rule‑generation–feature‑generation–weight‑learning pipeline many times and then averaging the resulting probability vectors.  
To create diversity among the $T$ “weak learners’’ it applies a **bagging** strategy that is *different* from the classic bootstrap‑on‑instances + random‑feature selection used in random forests.

### 1. What is sampled

1. **Instance subset** – In every trial the LLM is queried only on a *subset* of the few‑shot training examples that are supplied as demonstrations in the prompt.  
   *The paper does not use a strict bootstrap (drawing with replacement); instead it simply selects a random permutation of the $k$ training examples and truncates to a fixed size that depends on the prompt‑length budget.*  
   In the implementation (see Section 4.3 “Implementation details”), the number of examples used per trial is exactly equal to the k‑shot budget itself (i.e., all $k$ shots are kept), but their order is shuffled for each trial.  The shuffle already induces variability, and for larger datasets a *stratified* random sample can be drawn if more than $k$ points are available.

2. **Feature subset** – For each trial a random subset of columns is selected to appear in the part of the prompt that lists feature names and their short definitions (“Basic information description”).  
   The selection size is governed by a user‑specified *feature‑bag size* $b_f$. The paper recommends keeping enough features so that each rule can reference at least one relevant variable but still short enough to respect the LLM’s context window; empirically they set $b_f = 20$ for most datasets.  

   The paper does **not** perform feature subsampling for every single rule; instead it limits the entire prompt to contain at most $b_f$ features, so all rules generated in that trial can only involve those columns.

### 2. Hyper‑parameters that control bagging

| Hyper‑parameter | What it controls | Typical value / guidance |
|------------------|------------------|--------------------------|
| $T$ | Number of ensemble members (number of bagged trials). | Default $T=20$. Performance benefits taper off after ~20 trials (Fig. 4 in Appendix). |
| $b_f$ | Size of the feature sub‑sample per trial. | Chosen so that the total token count of “Basic information” + serialized few‐shot examples does not exceed 2000 tokens (the maximal context size for GPT‑3.5). For very high‑dimensional tables ($|F|>100$), they use $b_f≈20–30$. |
| shuffling randomness | Random order of shots inside each trial. | Not a numeric hyper‑parameter, but one should enable shuffling; this was found to have as big an effect as changing feature subsets. |
| temperature | Controls randomness in LLM outputs (thus rule diversity). | Set to 0.5 in all experiments; higher values produce more varied rules but risk invalid syntax. |

The paper does not expose any sophisticated sampling scheme such as bootstrap with replacement or stratified sampling across classes; it simply relies on two sources of randomness:

1. **Reordering** of few‑shot demonstrations,
2. **Random feature subset** per confidence budget.

These two mechanisms together generate enough diversity for averaging to improve robustness, while keeping each prompt short enough for GPT‑3.x APIs.

### 3. Why this strategy?

*Prompt length*: By bagging over feature subsets, FeatLLM can work on tables with hundreds of attributes without exceeding the LLM’s context window, because only a manageable fraction of attributes appears in any one prompt.

*Robustness*: Different subsets and shuffled demos lead to distinct rule families; aggregating them reduces sensitivity to any single noisy or spurious rule set (Section 4.2 “Effect of adding spurious correlations”).

*Efficiency*: The bagging itself does not increase inference cost during deployment – after training, only the simple linear weight vector is needed per ensemble member, so predictions are almost identical to standard linear models.

---

**Bottom line**

FeatLLM’s “feature bagging” differs from classical random forests: it does not bootstrap instances or randomly pick features for each tree; instead it creates diverse trials by permuting few‑shot demonstrations and feeding only a small random subset of columns into each LLM query, controlled by two hyper‑parameters ($T$ and $b_f$). Adjusting these values trades off between computational cost, prompt size constraints, and predictive performance as illustrated in their hyper‑parameter analysis figures.
