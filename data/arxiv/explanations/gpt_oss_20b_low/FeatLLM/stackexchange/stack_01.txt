\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### What is the exact role of the two-step reasoning instruction in FeatLLM's prompt?
Question:
In the FeatLLM paper, the authors describe a ‘Reasoning instruction’ that splits the LLM’s inference into a first step of inferring causal relationships without demonstrations, and a second step using demonstrations to derive rules. I am unclear why this separate two-step is necessary. Wouldn’t a single chain-of-thought prompt that includes demonstrations be sufficient? How does the separation affect the LLM’s ability to avoid spurious correlations, and what evidence do the authors provide that this improves rule quality?
Answer:
**Answer**

The FeatLLM paper introduces a *reasoning instruction* that explicitly divides the LLM’s inference into two stages:

1. **Causal‑knowledge extraction (Step 1)** – the model is asked to reason about *how* the target label depends on the features *without* looking at any concrete examples.  
2. **Rule derivation (Step 2)** – the model then reads the few‑shot demonstrations together with the knowledge gleaned in Step 1 and produces concrete, class‑specific “if–then” rules.

At first glance a single chain‑of‑thought prompt containing demonstrations might seem sufficient: the LLM could simply look at the examples and hallucinate rules that fit them.  The separation is actually crucial for two reasons: it forces the model to ground its reasoning in domain knowledge, and it provides a safeguard against noisy, spurious correlations that often plague few‑shot learning.

---

### Why a two‑step reasoning flow is needed

| Step | What the LLM is asked to do | Why it matters |
|------|-----------------------------|----------------|
| **Step 1 – Causal inference** | “Explain how each feature might affect the target.” | Gives the model a chance to retrieve *general* knowledge (e.g., “older age increases diabetes risk”) that is not dependent on the limited training set. This knowledge creates a *prior* over which features are plausible drivers of the target. |
| **Step 2 – Rule derivation** | “Using your causal understanding and these 4 examples, write **exact rules** for each class.” | Now that the LLM knows which features *could* matter, it only needs to discover *how* they combine in this particular data set. Because it already knows some features are irrelevant (from Step 1), it is less likely to overfit to accidental noise in the few demonstrations. |

If we omitted Step 1 and asked the LLM to go straight to rule fabrication, it would:

- Treat every column as potentially important; with only 4 examples this leads to many accidental patterns such as “if ‘user_id’ equals 7 then label = 1”.
- Without a causal backdrop, there is nothing discouraging it from concentrating on high‑variance or outlier columns that happen to correlate in the training set.

Thus the split mimics how a human expert would approach a new tabular task: first recall domain theory, then test that theory against observations.

---

### Impact on spurious correlations

The paper’s ablation study (Table 3) shows that removing Step 2 (“–Reasoning”) reduces AUC by an average of **0.012–0.019** across all datasets, most dramatically when shots are few (< 8). This indicates that the causal pre‑estimation of relevant variables stabilizes rule search.

Further evidence comes from Section 4.4 (“Effect of adding spurious correlations”). When unrelated columns from another dataset are appended to Heart data, FeatLLM’s performance degrades far less than baselines:

- The authors report that only **1–2 %** of generated rules involve noisy columns after Step 2 reasoning.
- Because those noisy rules receive almost zero weight during linear training (weights < 0.1), their influence on final predictions is negligible.
- In contrast, a single‑step chain‑of‑thought prompt would have produced many more rules pointing at irrelevant fields and would have suffered larger drops.

Thus, by first filtering out unlikely relationships, FeatLLM concentrates its limited indicator space on plausible features, automatically suppressing accidental patterns.

---

### How authors demonstrate improvement

1. **Ablation results** – Table 3’s “–Reasoning” curve shows consistent performance loss across datasets and shots.
2. **Spurious‑correlation experiment** – Figure 6 plots AUC vs added unrelated columns; FeatLLM remains near baseline whereas baselines collapse.
3. **Rule quality statistics** – Table 4 lists the proportion of generated rules whose individual AUROC exceeds 0.5 *before* weight tuning (“Before tuning”) and after tuning (“After tuning”). The drop in this proportion after tuning reveals that many initially plausible but ultimately useless rules are discarded thanks to learned weights—precisely what happens when causal guidance keeps spurious candidate features out of the first place.
4. **Qualitative cue** – In Appendix A (rule examples), a rule issued by Step 2 explicitly references feature types (numeric vs categorical); this constraint cannot arise from an unguided chain-of-thought prompt.

Together these pieces of evidence confirm that separating reasoning into causal inference followed by evidence‑based rule construction narrows focus to genuine predictive patterns, prevents overreliance on accidental correlations in low‑shot data, and ultimately yields higher‑quality features for downstream learners.

---

### Bottom line

A single-shot chain-of-thought containing demonstrations would let an LLM draw arbitrary associations from very few examples, mixing prior world knowledge with idiosyncratic noise. FeatLLM’s two‐step instruction first grounds inference in causal relationships known from language corpora, then turns those relationships into concrete classification rules using only limited data. This disciplined pipeline reduces spurious rules and improves overall predictive power—an effect directly supported by ablations and robustness experiments presented in the paper.
