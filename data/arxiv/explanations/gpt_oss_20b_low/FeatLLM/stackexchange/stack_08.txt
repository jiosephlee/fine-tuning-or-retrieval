\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### "Information gain" vs "Feature usefulness" in measuring rule quality
Question:
"Quality of features" experiment averages AUROC between each new rule feature and target label before and after tuning. Is AUROC an appropriate metric for assessing rule usefulness? Would mutual information or Gini impurity provide better insights given these are binary features?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
AUROC is a perfectly reasonable and widely‑used metric when you want to know how *useful* a binary rule is for distinguishing between two classes, just as you are doing in the paper’s *rule‑quality* analysis.  Mutual information (MI) or Gini impurity can give complementary information, but they are not necessarily “better” for the specific purpose of quantifying rule usefulness in a few‑shot setting, and in practice they can be more difficult to estimate reliably with very few samples.

---

### Why AUROC makes sense for rule usefulness

1. **Binary feature, binary target** – Each inferred rule becomes a binary indicator: *1* if the input satisfies the rule, *0* otherwise.  
   The task of evaluating its usefulness reduces to a very simple classification problem: does the presence of the rule point more often to class 1 than to class 0?

2. **Margin‑insensitive measure** – AUROC looks at *rank ordering*: it counts how often a record with label 1 receives a higher rule score than a record with label 0, irrespective of the exact score value.  
   This fits the rule setting because the rule is not a continuous score; it simply turns on or off.  Any threshold that turns on 10% of the examples can be compared consistently across rules.

3. **Invariant to class imbalance** – In many tabular problems (e.g., disease diagnosis) classes are highly imbalanced.  AUROC is robust to that imbalance because it normalises over all possible positive‑negative pairs.

4. **Transparent interpretation** – A value of 0.5 means the rule is no better than random chance; values larger than 0.5 indicate discriminative power that can be used directly by downstream models (as in the paper’s linear weighting step).  You can immediately filter out rules whose AUROC barely exceeds 0.5, which matches the “After tuning” column in Table 3.

5. **Statistical stability with few samples** – Even when data are scarce, AUROC can be estimated fairly reliably using bootstrapping or cross‑validation, whereas MI estimates can become highly variable because they require accurate estimation of marginal and joint probabilities.

### When mutual information or Gini impurity might help

- **Capturing non‑linear dependencies:** If a rule has complex interactions with other features that only appear when combined with other rules, MI can pick up on such dependencies because it measures statistical dependence rather than ranking alone.
- **Feature selection in ensembles:** Some tree‑based methods use Gini impurity (or entropy) to decide how useful a split is.  If you were embedding your rules inside a tree or random‑forest style learner, such impurity measures would naturally arise.
- **Handling multi–class targets:** In multi‑class settings where each rule might correspond to one class versus all others, an extension of MI (e.g., mutual information between a binary variable and a multiclass target) could give an intuitive “importance” score per class.

However, both MI and Gini require counting occurrences or estimating probabilities, which becomes noisy when the number of shots is tiny (the authors’ experiments sometimes use as few as 4 shots).  The variability can make such scores harder to interpret and compare across rules.

### Practical recommendation for FeatLLM

1. **Continue using AUROC** as the primary filter:  
   - Compute it for every generated rule before any downstream model training.
   - Threshold at ~0.55–0.6 (the paper uses > 0.5) to discard useless rules early.
2. **Optionally track MI/Gini** if you want an extra lens:  
   - Use these metrics on *pseudo‑labels* constructed from your tuned weights (e.g., weighted sum of rules) rather than raw binary rules.
   - Compare MI scores with weight magnitudes; if they correlate well, you gain confidence that your tuning step is capturing genuine signal.
3. **Keep an eye on calibration**:  
   - Since each rule is binary, small sample size may inflate AUROC due to lucky ordering of positives/negatives.  
   - Employ leave‑one‑out or cross‑validation estimates (as the paper does implicitly with AUC averaging over random splits) to mitigate this.

---

### Connecting back to FeatLLM’s pipeline

In Section 4.*Quality of features generated by FeatLLM*, the authors report two columns: “Before tuning” and “After tuning”. The former is exactly an *AUROC measurement* of each freshly generated binary rule against the target labels; this tells how informative the raw rule is from a purely statistical perspective before any learning occurs.  

The second column reflects how much weight each rule receives from the linear model after training on k-shot data—a teachable signal that incorporates both prior knowledge captured by LLMs and actual empirical evidence from observed samples.  

Thus AUROC serves as a **neutral baseline**: it tells you what each rule could do on its own, while weight tuning tells you what it actually does in context.

---

### Bottom line

- **AUROC** is suitable and efficient for quickly judging binary rule usefulness in low‑shot tabular settings.
- **Mutual information / Gini impurity** provide additional nuance but are more sensitive to estimation errors when data are scarce; they may be useful as secondary diagnostics rather than primary filtering metrics.
- For FeatLLM’s purposes – where each inference step needs to be inexpensive and interpretable – sticking with AUROC keeps things simple while still giving meaningful insight into whether a LLM‑generated rule should be kept or discarded.

Feel free to experiment with both sets of metrics on your own datasets; often you’ll find that AUROC already captures most of what matters for downstream performance.”
