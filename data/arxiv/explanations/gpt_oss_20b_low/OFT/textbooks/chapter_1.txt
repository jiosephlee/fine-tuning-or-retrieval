**Fine‑Tuning Text‑to‑Image Diffusion: Motivation and Foundations**

## 1 Introduction to Diffusion Models and the Need for Fine‑Tuning

Text‑to‑image diffusion models have recently burst onto the generative AI scene, delivering images that rival human illustration in both fidelity and diversity. The core idea behind these models is a stochastic denoising process: starting from pure noise, a neural network progressively removes noise conditioned on a textual prompt until a coherent image emerges. Landmark systems such as **Stable Diffusion**, **Imagen**, and **DALL‑E 2** have pushed this paradigm to new heights, thanks to large pre‑training corpora, sophisticated diffusion schedulers, and powerful vision–language embeddings.

Yet, despite their impressive capacity, these models often struggle to honor *fine‑level* user intent. A natural text prompt like “a red apple on a wooden table” can be interpreted in numerous ways—different lighting, angles, or surrounding objects. More critically, the text alone rarely constrains *identity* (e.g., generating a specific person) or *structure* (e.g., fitting an image to a pre‑specified sketch). Two families of downstream tasks have emerged to tackle exactly these limitations:

- **Subject‑Driven Generation** (DreamBooth style): One supplies only a handful of images of a particular subject—say, a cat or a celebrity—and wishes the model to render that very subject in novel contexts defined by arbitrary text prompts.
- **Controllable Generation** (ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter style): The user provides an auxiliary conditioning signal—canny edges, segmentation maps, depth maps—that the model must faithfully follow while still obeying the text description.

Both problems are fundamentally about *adapting* a gigantic pre‑trained diffusion backbone without eroding its already accrued generative prowess. Consequently, any effective fine‑tuning strategy must honor two high‑level desiderata:

1. **Training Efficiency** – Few trainable parameters and epochs. The method should sprinkle just enough learnable knobs so that the diffusion process can be nudged toward the new behavior without reinventing the wheel.
2. **Generalization Preservation** – After fine‑tuning it should still generate diverse, photorealistic images from arbitrary prompts. In other words, the model must not “forget” its original cinematic vision even as it learns a new skill.

Existing fine‑tuning approaches largely fall short of these goals. Typically they shrink learning rates to preserve stability or inject low‑rank adapters (LoRA) that only mildly perturb pretrained weights. Such tricks are ad hoc; they neither come with theoretical guarantees nor capture deeper structural properties of the underlying neural network.

In what follows we delve into why primitive methods falter, introduce the concept of *hyperspherical energy* as a structural safeguard for knowledge retention, and motivate orthogonal fine‑tuning as a principled alternative that marries robustness with flexibility.

---

### 1.1 Overview of Large Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

The field’s flagship models can be classified along two axes: **pixel-space vs latent-space generation** and **training paradigms vs use cases**.

- **Pixel-Space Diffusers** such as **StableDiffusion**\cite{rombach2022high} and latent image generators like **Imagen**\cite{saharia2022photorealistic} run their denoising U-Nets directly on raw pixels or compressed latents fed through an autoencoder (VQ-GAN for StableDiffusion). They rely heavily on powerful transformer-boosted cross-attention blocks that fuse textual embeddings (from CLIP or its descendants) into every layer.
- **Latent Diffusers** such as **DALL-E 2**\cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} encode images in latent spaces learned jointly with textual encoders (e.g., CLIP’s dual encoder). This reduces computational load while maintaining high sample fidelity.

All these backbones share an essential property: they were trained on *billion*-scale text–image pairs without domain-specific supervision beyond raw captions or tags. This enormous capability makes them excellent starting points for downstream specialization.

---

### 1.2 Limitations of Pure Text Guidance

The bottleneck arises when a user’s intent cannot be distilled into a concise caption:

- **Ambiguity** – Natural language is inherently underspecified: “a dreamy portrait” could imply lighting style, color palette, pose—many possibilities remain unexplained.
- **Lack of Precision** – When uniqueness is required (“the exact look of this specific woman”), vanilla text generalization falls flat because the model has never seen enough examples to resolve identity.
- **Structural Mismatch** – Designers often want concrete arrangements—edges, object placement—which are difficult to encode purely in language.

Thus we need additional modalities (images or sketches) or stronger adaptation mechanisms that go beyond naïve parameter updates.

---

### 1.3 Definition of Subject-Driven Generation

Subject-driven generation formalizes the problem: given \(N\) `anchor` images \(\{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^N\) depicting an entity \(S\) (e.g., "Mona Lisa" portrait), we wish to train—or adapt—a diffusion model \(f_{\theta}\) so that for any new text prompt \(\mathbf{p}\), sampling from \(f_{\theta}\) yields images \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) containing \(S\) rendered in the context specified by \(\mathbf{p}\).

Typical pipelines employ an inversion step: map each anchor image into latent space \(\mathbf{z}_i = E(\mathbf{x}_i)\), then fine-tune with reconstruction losses aligning \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_i\) close to \(\mathbf{z}_i\), while preserving generality via regularizers that penalize excessive deviation from pretrained weights.

The canonical example is **DreamBooth**, which latches onto subject embeddings via class token substitution and uses low-rank adapters (LoRA). Nonetheless its heavy dependence on carefully tuned learning rates leaves room for instability.

---

### 1.4 Definition of Controllable Generation

Controllable generation extends diffusion inference by accepting an extra *control signal* \(\mathbf{c}\):

\[
\mathbf{y} = f_{\theta}(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{c}) .
\]

Typical control signals include:

| Control Type | Example | Common Panel |
|--------------|--------|--------------|
| Canny edges | Edge map | Sketch |
| Segmentation | Class masks | Layout |
| Landmarks | Keypoints | Pose |

The goal is twofold:
1. For every semantic node in \(\mathbf{c}\), there must be visual correspondence in the output image.
2. The output must still honor extra attributes encoded in \(\mathbf{p}\).

Recent works like ControlNet add extra branching modules; T2I-Adapter injects adapters conditioned on \(\mathbf{c}\). Their approach is essentially *additive*: new small weights augment existing ones but may unintuitively swamp original semantics if not tightly constrained.

---

### 1.5 Key Desiderata for Fine-Tuning Methods

When adapting large diffusion backbones we seek:

| Attribute | Definition |
|-----------|------------|
| **Parameter Efficiency** | Minimize number of tunable parameters; ideally fewer than one per neuron or per layer block. |
| **Fast Convergence** | Reach performance plateau after few epochs; avoid oscillatory or catastrophic forgetting dynamics. |
| **Generalization Retention** | Preserve pre-trained zero-shot photo-realism; maintain diverse sampling ability across unseen prompts. |
| **Inference Cost Neutrality** | No extra overhead during generation; final finetuned model should run at same speed as base model.|

These constraints impose stringent requirements on any architectural perturbation we introduce during fine-tuning.

---

### 1.6 Shortcomings of Existing Fine-Tuning Strategies

#### 1.6.1 Small Learning Rate Updates
Classic stochastic gradient descent with tiny step sizes attempts to keep changes small (*implicit regularization*). But:

- No explicit geometric guarantee; weights can drift off manifold.
- Requires hyperparameter tuning per dataset/subject; highly fragile.
- Suffers from bias toward overfitting small data regimes if learning rates are too high.

#### 1.6.2 LoRA Low-Rank Adapters
LoRA injects two low-rank matrices \(A,B\):

\[
W' = W + A B^\top , \quad r' \ll d .
\]

Potential shortcomings include:

- **Fixed rank constraint** precludes representation power when higher rank changes are beneficial.
- The additive form can disrupt original weight orthogonality/geometry leading to semantic loss.
- Training stability heavily depends on selecting \(r'\), learning rate, and regularizer strengths.

In sum, both approaches provide hand-wavy explanations for stability but lack rigorous preservation guarantees against key structural properties—most notably those governing relationships among neurons across layers.

---

### 1.7 Why Preserve A Geometric Structure?

Neural network performance hinges not just on absolute weight values but on ***relative directions*** among neurons when viewed as vectors in high-dimensional space:

- Empirical evidence from work on angular loss functions shows cosine similarity—essentially angle between neuron vectors—is a compact proxy for semantic correspondence.
- Hyperspherical energy aggregates pairwise angular similarities over all neurons within a layer:
  
  \[
  \operatorname{HE}(W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1},
  \]
  
  where hats denote normalisation to unit norm.
  
A stable finetune should preserve this hyperspherical structure; otherwise inter-neuron relations—and thus higher-level features—are corrupted even if Euclidean weight change appears minimal.

Orthogonal transformations respect exactly this invariant: rotating every neuron vector within a layer preserves all pairwise angles—and thus `HE`. This observation motivates an approach where finetuning is cast as learning an orthogonal matrix per layer—a constrained yet expressive transformation that reveres neural geometry while allocating limited flexibility where necessary.

# 2 Fundamental Idea: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

In this chapter we dissect the key idea behind Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and place it within the broader landscape of model‑adaptation techniques. OFT departs from conventional wisdom—gradient–steep‑learning with a tiny learning rate—by focusing on a very particular geometric property of neural net weights: the set of pair‑wise angles among neurons, which we encode through **hyperspherical energy**. We show that by learning a **layer‑shared orthogonal matrix**, we can *re‑orient* the pre‑trained weight vectors without changing any intrinsic inner‑product relations, thereby preserving the semantic content that the large diffusion prior has learned while still allowing task‑specific adaptation.

The chapter is organized as follows. First we motivate why the *direction* of a neuron is the prime carrier of semantic knowledge (illustrated by a toy auto‑encoder experiment). Next we show mathematically why an orthogonal transformation keeps every pairwise angle intact and thus leaves hyperspherical energy invariant. We then derive the core OFT constraint, prove that any orthogonal transformation of the weight matrix keeps the hyperspherical energy equal to that of the pre‑trained weights, and therefore establish that minimizing hyperspherical‑energy drift forces the finetuned weight to be an orthogonal multiple of its original value. Finally, we contrast OFT’s **multiplicative** update with LoRA’s **additive** rank‑one​-style update, highlighting their different regularizing effects.

## 2.1 Why Neuron Direction Matters – Angular Information as Semantic Carrier

Before formalizing OFT we need to convince ourselves that *the angle between a neuron's weight vector and an input vector* carries more semantic load than its magnitude. This claim is rooted in the observation that neural networks can be trained successfully when all neuron vectors are forced onto a unit hypersphere: SphereNet \cite{liu2017deep} demonstrated that such normalization never degrades performance and often improves generalization. The intuition is simple: if all neurons lie on a sphere, then two neurons whose directions are close will respond similarly to most inputs, while those with far apart directions will activate on distinct subspaces; no magnitude scaling is necessary because all neurons have equal length.

To empirically confirm this intuition for image‐processing layers we design a toy convolution auto‑encoder on flower images. Two variants of activation are compared during testing:

1. **Standard inner‑product activation** (the training objective);  
2. **Angular activation only**, i.e.\ only using the cosine between input patch \(\mathbf{x}\) and filter \(\mathbf{w}\) (\(\cos(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|}\));  
3. **Magnitude activation only**, i.e.\ ignoring direction.

The results (Figure 2 in the paper) reveal that reconstruction quality drops by more than 80 % when magnitude information is discarded but remains nearly perfect when only angular information is kept—even though training never used cosine activation at all. This experimental fact supports our primary hypothesis: *direction alone suffices to encode most useful visual semantics*. It follows that an efficient adaptation technique should focus on changing directions rather than magnitudes.

## 2.2 Orthogonal Transformations Preserve Pairwise Angles – Hyperspherical Energy Invariance

Let us formalize what “changing direction” really means for an entire layer of neurons. Consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\). Each column \(\mathbf{w}_i^{0}\) denotes one neuron in \(d\)-dimensional space; we write \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{0}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_i^{0}}{\|\mathbf{w}_i^{0}\|}\) for its unit direction vector.

The **pairwise angular similarity** between two neurons \(i,j\) is simply their cosine:
\[
    \operatorname{cos}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{0},\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j^{0}) \;=\; \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{0\top} \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j^{0}.
\]
Summing over all unordered pairs yields what Liu et al. call **hyperspherical energy**:
\[
    E(\mathbf{W}^{0}) \;\triangleq\;
    \sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\ i<j}}^n
        \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{0}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j^{0}\|^{-1}.
    \tag{2.1}
\]
Because each term depends only on pairwise angles (distances on the sphere), \(E(\cdot)\) measures how uniformly distributed the neuron directions are on the sphere—a structural property tied to semantic expressiveness.

Now consider applying an arbitrary orthogonal matrix
\(\mathbf{R}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\), satisfying
\(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\!\mathbf{R}=\boldsymbol I_{d}\).
Define new weights
\[
    \widetilde{\mathbf W}
    = \underbrace{\bigl(\!R\,\,\widetilde\, W^0\bigr)}_{\textbf {new}}
      = R\,W^{0}.
\]
Because multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves lengths,
\(\|\tilde{\mathbf w}_i\|=\|\mathbf w_i^{0}\|\).
Furthermore,
for any two columns,
\[
    \frac{\tilde{\mathbf w}_i}{\|\tilde{\mathbf w}_i\|}
      = R\,\frac{\mathbf w_i^{0}}{\|\mathbf w_i^{0}\|}
      = R\, \hat{\bf w}_i.
\]
Hence
\[
    \tilde{\bf w}_i^\top R^\top R\, \tilde {\bf w}_j
      = (\hat {\bf w}_i)^\top R^\top R (\hat {\bf w}_j)
      = (\hat {\bf w}_i)^\top (\hat {\bf w}_j),
\]
which shows that all pairwise inner products—and thus all angles—are unchanged:
\(E(R\,W^0)=E(W^0)\).  
So *any* orthogonal reorientation leaves hyperspherical energy untouched.

Intuitively, rotating or reflecting all neurons by the same rigid motion borders hyper-sphere does not blur or sharpen their relative positions; it merely paradoxically re‐orients them in feature space while keeping everything else identical.

## 2.3 Derivation of OFT Constraint

With this geometric insight in hand, we can now formulate Orthogonal Finetuning as an optimization problem that preserves hyperspherical energy:

Minimize
\( \bigl| E(\widetilde W)-E(W^0)\bigr|
   =  |E(RW^0)-E(W^0)|,\)
subject to \(R^\top R=\boldsymbol I_{d}\).

Because any orthogonal \(R\) leaves \(E(RW^0)=E(W^0)\), *the minimum is zero*: 
the objective vanishes exactly when
\(R\) is orthogonal.
Thus Eq.(2.1) can be replaced by its algebraic version

> **OFT Principle**  
> Find parameters such that  
>   \[
>        {\widetilde W}=RW^0,\qquad 
>        R^\top R=RR^\top=\boldsymbol I_{d}.
>   \tag{2.2}
>   \]

This constraint states formally that *the finetuned weight matrix must be obtained by left‑multiplying the pretrained weight matrix by a single orthogonal matrix*. Equivalently, across *each layer*, OFT learns one shared rotation/reflection.

### Proof of Minimal Hyperspherical Energy Difference

Let us prove rigorously that enforcing Eq.(2.2) guarantees minimal hyperspherical-energy drift:

*Lemma.*  
Let \(W^0,W^*\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\). Then  
\( E(W^*)=E(W^0)\)   if and only if there exists an orthogonal \(R'\) such that
\( W^*=R'W^0.\)

*Proof.*  

(⇒) Assume \(E(W^*)=E(W^0)\). For each column define unit vectors
\(u_i={W_i}/{||W_i||}, v_i={W_i^*}/{||W_i^*||}\).
Because pairwise distances between all {u_i} equal those between {v_i}, there exists a rigid motion mapping each u_i to v_i: \(v_i=Ru_i,\;i=1,\dots,n.\)
Collecting these into matrices gives \(V=RU,\)
where \(U=[u_1,\dots,u_n]\),  
\(V=[v_1,\dots,v_n]\).
Both U and V have unit columns, hence both are partial isometries.
Then define \(R' = VU^\dagger,\)
where \(U^\dagger\) denotes pseudoinverse.
It satisfies \(R'U=W^*, R'^{\,T}R'=I_d.\)
Thus \(W^*=R'W^0.\)

(⇐) Conversely, if \(W^*=R'W^0,R'\in O(d)\),
then every column direction is rotated identically,
so distances on unit sphere remain unchanged.
Therefore \(E(W^*)=E(R'W^0)=E(W^0).\)

∎

Consequently, minimizing any normed difference between singe-layer weights and its rotated counterpart reduces to learning an orthogonal transform per layer:
\[ 
       W_{\ell}^{(final)} := R_{\ell} W_{\ell}^{(pretrained)} .
       \tag{2.3}
       \]
This completes the derivation.

## 2.4 Comparison with LoRA – Multiplicative vs Additive Updates

LoRA introduces low-rank perturbations to every linear layer:
\( W = W^0 + B A,\)
with learnable small–rank factors A,B.
Its main regularization comes from forcing B A to maintain low rank; intuitively it nudges weights slightly away from their pre‑trained positions while keeping overall spectrum stable.

OFT differs fundamentally in two ways:

| Property | LoRA | OFT |
|----------|------|-----|
| Update form | additive: Δ = BA (low rank). | multiplicative: Δ = (R – I) W⁰ (full rank but constrained). |
| Constraint | rank(Δ)=r′ small | Δ preserves pairwise angles → rotational transform |
| Regularization | scale bounded via LR & rank | strict geometric invariance → hyperspherical energy unchanged |
| Stability drives | depends on learning rate & rank choice | guaranteed by orthogonality |

Under Eq.(2.3), writing Δ=W−W⁰=(R−I) W⁰,
if (R−I) happens to be low-rank this resembles LoRA; otherwise it constitutes a full-rank update nevertheless restricted to preserve angles among columns---a substantially tighter constraint than simply restricting rank.

Thus OFT can be viewed as a generalized LoRA where “low-rank” has been replaced by “orthogonality:” both wisely constrain drastic changes while permitting adaptive reorientation of neuron directions—a plausible reason for OFT’s superior stability shown in experiments.

---

In summary, Orthogonal Finetuning operates by learning one jointly shared rotation per layer so as to keep every pairwise neuron angle identical to its pre‑trained state while allowing us to steer generations toward domain‐specific or controllable semantics. This elegant geometric insight leads not only to provable preservation of hyperspherical energy but also practical gains in training efficiency and stability—making OFT a compelling alternative to more conventional additive fine‐tuning methods like LoRA or vanilla gradient descent with tiny learning rates.
