\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 2: 3. Efficient Parameterization of Orthogonal Matrices

Controlling a large text‑to‑image diffusion model inevitably requires a difficult trade‑off: the finetuning mechanism must be expressive enough to inject task‑specific behaviour, yet compact enough that the number of trainable parameters remains tractable. The Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework achieves this by learning a **single orthogonal transform per layer**. Nonetheless, a naïve representation of an orthogonal matrix has quadratic space‑time complexity in the dimensionality of the layer and is impractical for the high‑dimensional weight matrices that appear in modern diffusion architectures such as Stable Diffusion or Imagen. This chapter develops a suite of practical parameterization tricks that dramatically reduce the memory and computational burden while preserving the theoretical guarantees that make OFT effective.

## 3.1 Cayley Transform for Differentiable Orthogonality

The Cayley transform offers an elegant way of constructing orthogonal matrices from unconstrained parameters. Given a **skew‑symmetric matrix** \( \mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) with \(\mathbf{Q}^\top=-\mathbf{Q}\), the Cayley map

\[
\mathbf{R}=\bigl(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\bigr)^{-1}
\tag{3.1}
\]

produces an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R}\in O(d)\). Two facts justify why (3.1) is attractive for fine‑tuning diffusion models:

1. **Differentiability**: All operations involved—matrix addition, inversion, and multiplication—are differentiable, allowing us to backpropagate gradients directly through \(\mathbf{R}\). Consequently, training does not require discrete search or projection steps; constraints are enforced implicitly by construction.

2. **Automatic enforcement of orthogonality**: Because \( \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-\top} (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^\top(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}= \mathbf{I}\), we need not add any penalty term or re‑orthogonalize after every update.

The only caveat is that the Cayley map only covers **special orthogonal matrices** (those with determinant \(+1\)), excluding reflections (\(\det \mathbf{R}=-1\)). Empirical experiments in OFT demonstrate that this limitation is benign: reflections do not afford any significant advantage over rotations for preserving hyperspherical energy, and every target transformation can be expressed up to multiplication by a diagonal reflection matrix—a negligible change for our purposes.

### 3.1.1 Implementing the Cayley Transform

A simple PyTorch implementation follows:

```python
class CayleyOrthogonal(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        # initialize Q to zero so R starts as identity
        self.Q = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(dim, dim))

    def forward(self):
        I = torch.eye(self.Q.size(0), device=self.Q.device)
        # enforce skew-symmetry
        Q_skew = 0.5 * (self.Q - self.Q.t())
        # compute (I - Q) inverse with stability guard
        inv = torch.inverse(I - Q_skew)
        R = (I + Q_skew) @ inv
        return R
```

The enforcement of skew‑symmetry is accomplished by averaging \( \mathbf{Q}\) and its transpose after each gradient step, guaranteeing that \(\nabla_{\!Q}L\) respects the constraint automatically.

## 3.2 Block‐Diagonal Decomposition

Although theoretically sound, directly parameterizing \(\mathbf{R}\in O(d)\) via equation (3.1) scales as \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\). In diffusion models, a typical transformer block might contain attention weight matrices of volume hundreds of millions when flattened into dense forms. To bring OFT into line with existing parameter‑efficient adapters such as LoRA, we adopt a **block–diagonal decomposition**:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{R}= 
\mathrm{diag}\bigl(
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
    \vdots & & \\
    & \boxed{\;\;}\; & \\
    & & \vdots
   \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{$r$ blocks}}\!
\bigr),
}
\tag{3.2}
\]
where each block \( \mathbf{R}_i \in O(d/r)\). The blocks are independent orthogonal transformations applied to disjoint groups of neurons (e.g., splitting channels in a linear layer or the heads in multi-head attention).

### 3.2.1 Why Block–Diagonal Works

- **Parameter Economy**: Each block requires only \( ((d/r)(d/r-1))/2 = \mathcal O(d^2/r^2)\) parameters via a skew­symmetric generator per block; thus total parameter count scales as \( r\times\nabla d^2/r^2=\mathcal O(d^2/r)\). Choosing \( r=8,\!16,\!32\) brings down the overhead by orders of magnitude relative to a full matrix.
- **Flexibility Preservation**: Even though all neurons within a block are transformed jointly, distinct blocks can rotate different subsets arbitrarily; empirical studies show this suffices to tailor the geometry of feature space to downstream tasks without compromising the model’s generative backbone.
- **Compatibility with Convolutional Structure**: In convolution layers each input channel can be assigned its own 2×2 rotation block – analogous to depth‑wise convolution – blurring the line between feature orientation and spatial filtering.

### 3.2.2 Sharing Blocks Across Layers

An optional yet powerful reduction achieves near elimination of additional parameters: we enforce **block sharing**, i.e., all layers share an identical set of block matrices:
\[
    \forall\,l,\;\;\;
    \{\!\boxed{\;\;}...\;\!\}= 
    r_{i}=\textbf{\[some shared transformation]\},   ~ i=1,\ldots,r.
\]
Consequently every layer learns no free parameters at all—the same orthogonal transformation is reused throughout—while still preserving overall flexibility because learning still shapes how this shared transform interacts with different weight tensors.

## 3.3 Parameter Count Analysis vs LoRA

To appreciate how OFT stacks against traditional low‑rank adapters such as LoRA, consider a fully connected layer of shape \( d\times n.\)

| Method | Trainable Parameters | Formula |
|--------|----------------------|---------|
| OFT (block‑diagonal, shared blocks) | \(d^2/(4r)\) | if each block off‑diagonal entries count half |
| LoRA (rank \(r'\)) | \(r'(d+n)\) | independent matrices \(A,B\)|

Assume typical settings where \( d,n=128,\; r'=8,\; r=8.\)

- OFT yields \(128^2/(4\times8)=512\) parameters per layer.
- LoRA supplies \(8(128+128)=2048.\)

Thus OFT reduces memory usage by roughly four times while retaining identical expressive power through global orthogonality.

When *block sharing* is introduced, OFT collapses further: only one set of blocks per parameter group contributes any free parameters—speeding up inference because the ran op becomes a fixed gate rather than per-layer learnable weights.

## 3.4 Practical Consequences During Training

### 3.4.1 Gradient Flow Through Orthogonal Matrices

Because every forward pass shapes weights as
\(W^{(t)} = R^{(t)} W_0,\)
the gradient w.r.t a particular hidden activations flows through an orthonormal mapping that preserves Fisher information norms harmonic on the hypersphere—thereby reducing catastrophic drift away from pretraining behavior.

Additionally, updating only an implicit rotation matrix cuts down numerical instability issues commonly plague neural networks when small learning rates are applied on full weight tensors; rotations keep magnitude stable while reorienting directions.

### 3.4.2 Fast Convergence via Smooth Manifolds

An orthogonally constrained optimization problem lives on a smooth manifold (the Stiefel manifold for rectangular transformations or orthogonal group for square matrices). Recent research has shown that first‑order optimizers perform more systematically when gradients remain tangent to this manifold instead of clinging to arbitrary directions in Euclidean space (see e.g., "Training Neural Networks Using Gradient Descent on Manifolds"). Empirically we observe that OFT reaches useful finetuned states within an order-of-magnitude fewer gradient steps compared to standard weight updates or low-rank adapters—exactly what our experimental section corroborates.

## 3.5 Summary and Takeaways

The efficient parameterization tactics presented here transform theoretically elegant but impractical “rotate all neurons” ideas into viable operators embedded in existing training pipelines:

* The **Cayley transform** guarantees unbroken orthogonality without extra regularizers.
* By harvesting symmetry via **block-diagonalization**, we scale from quadratic overhead down to linear-in-dimension levels.
* Optional **block sharing** thins down trainable parameters almost to zero while retaining adaptability across layers.
* The resulting architecture keeps training cheap yet expressive—a decisive advantage when finetuning massive models on limited data for subject-driven or controllable generation tasks.

With these ingredients in place, OFT stands poised to deliver both high-controllability performance and rigorous preservation of pre-trained generative semantics—a union rarely achieved by competing methods such as LoRA or control-netwidening adapters.
