\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Beyond Image Generation: Composability and Future Directions for Orthogonal Finetuning

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) has proven to be a lightweight, stable, and high‑quality way to adapt large text‑to‑image diffusion models to downstream tasks such as subject‑driven generation and controllable generation. By learning a layer‑shared orthogonal transformation that preserves hyperspherical energy, OFT keeps the pretrained model’s semantic knowledge intact while still giving it the flexibility to learn new skills. The next frontier for OFT research, however, is going to stretch its applicability far beyond single‑task finetuning. In this post we tease out three particularly promising directions: dealing with the computational bottleneck of matrix inverses, exploiting the composability of orthogonal transforms for multi‑task learning, and striking a better balance between parameter efficiency and model flexibility.

---

## 1. Scaling the Cayley Transform

The core of OFT is an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) that rotates the weight matrix of a layer \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) at finetuning time so that  
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!T}\mathbf{x},
\]
with an implicit orthogonality constraint \( \mathbf{R}^T \! = \! \mathbf{R}^{-1}\).  
A convenient way to parameterise \(\mathbf{R}\) is the Cayley transform
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric (\(\mathbf{Q}^T = -\mathbf{Q}\)). This guarantees that \( \mathbf{R} \in O(d)\) without having to run expensive Gram–Schmidt or QR routines.

### 1.1 Why matrix inverses become a bottleneck

The inverse \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) costs \(O(d^{3})\) per forward/backward pass when implemented naively. For modern diffusion models:

- The UNet backbone contains millions of parameters; many layers have very large projection dimensions (e.g., \(d=3200\) in a key‑value head).
- Training on high‑resolution latent grids doubles the effective batch size, further escalating memory pressure.

These factors push the inverse operation toward being one of the most time‑consuming parts of a single training step.

### 1.2 What we can do now

1. **Block‑diagonal Cayley** – The paper’s authors already proposed partitioning \( \mathbf{R} \) into smaller blocks so that each block’s inverse is cheap:  
   \[
   \mathbf{R}= {\rm diag}(\,\underbrace{\dots}_{r {\rm \ blocks}}\dots)
   \].  
   With \(r=8\), each block is only \(d/r\times d/r\), dropping complexity from \(O(d^{3})\) to roughly \(O((d/r)^{3})r = O(d^{3}/r^{2})\).

2. **Approximate inverses** – For small skew matrices \( \|\mathbf{Q}\|<1/2\), we can expand
   \[
   (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
      = \sum_{k=0}^{K} \! \mathbf{Q}^{k},
   \]
   truncating at low order \(K=2$ or $3$. This eliminates explicit inversion altogether.

3. **Implicit solutions** – Solving linear systems with conjugate gradient (CG) or MINRES on GPU can be far cheaper than inverting dense matrices, especially if the system is well conditioned (which it is almost always for small perturbations around identity).

Open research challenge: building a “differentiable CG” module that can fit inside PyTorch or JAX autograd graphs without losing performance.

## 2. Orthogonality as a Composable Module

One of OFT’s most exciting properties is that **orthogonal matrices form a group** under multiplication: if you finish training a diffusion head with an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R}_{1}\), then train another head with a different orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R}_{2}\), their product
\[ 
   \tilde{\mathbf R}=  \mathbf R_{2}  \!\cdot\!  \mathbf R_{1}
\]
is *still* an orthogonal rotation that simultaneously fulfils both constraints. This raises several tantalising questions:

| Question | Why it matters | Possible Approach |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|
| Can we learn one global transform per task and compose them lazily? | Enables multi‑task inference without retraining. | Reuse the product of per‑task matrices at inference time; equivalent to running sequential finite diffs on the same Latent space. |
| Do multiple tasks “interfere” when matrices are multiplied? | Could lead to loss of semantics if one rotate erases another’s structure. | Empirically evaluate ranking signals (e.g., CLIP similarity) after cumulative composition; design regularizers that penalize large angular drift between tasks' eigenvectors. |
| Could we train a *shared* subspace matrix that balances all tasks simultaneously? | Provides parameter sharing across tasks while preserving rich representations. | A joint objective:
  $$
     L = L_{\text{text}} + L_{\text{subj}} + L_{\text{control}} 
          + \lambda_{\text{dir}}\!\sum_{\ell}\!{\Vert}
           {\rm cotan}(  {\bf R}^{(\ell)}_{\text{text}}
                    {\bf R}^{(\ell)}_{\text{subj}}{}^{-1})
              {\Vert}^{2}
  $$
  where final term encourages alignment among all transforms.

Experimental direction: start with two tasks (subject vs control), train them sequentially but enforce an *orthogonality alignment loss* so that their final matrices are close in angle space but still optimised for each task separately.

Open problem: proving when such compositions preserve desired properties (e.g., generating crisp edges or keeping identity unchanged). An elegant geometric analysis could provide theoretical guarantees.

---

## 3. Parameter Efficiency vs Flexibility

In OFT, we observe a remarkable drop in trainable parameters compared to LoRA because only the orthogonal matrix needs updating:

- A full dense orthogonal matrix requires \( O(d^2)\) parameters.
- With block-diagonal decomposition and shared blocks we reduce this roughly to \(O(d^2/r^2)\).

However, constraints also introduce bias:

- **Block size choice** balances expressivity against overfitting: larger blocks allow more global rotation but reduce regularisation.
- **Fixed determinant** (Cayley transforms only produce matrices with det=+1). Although empirically benign, some use cases might benefit from allowing reflections (det=-1).

Future work should explore *hybrid* parameterisations:

| Strategy | Pros | Cons |
|----------|------|------|
| Low-rank perturbation on top of identity | Adds fine-grained control while keeping overhead small | Might not fully preserve hyperspherical energy |
| Sparse random projections | Fast multiplication and inference | Harder to guarantee strict orthogonality |
| Structured deep networks (e.g., equivariant layers) | Very compact representation | Needs domain-specific design |

A rigorous study measuring *semantic retention* (via CLIP or DINO similarity) as block size varies will answer whether the current trade-off is optimal or if new structures can push boundaries further.

---

## TL;DR

- **Scalability bottleneck:** Replace expensive Cayley inversions with block-oriented approximations or iterative solvers.
- **Composability:** Orthogonal transforms compose nicely; exploring multi-task pipelines could eliminate separate finetunes.
- **Efficiency–flexibility continuum:** Block size and parametrisation choices dictate how much freedom our matrices have versus preserving pretrained knowledge.

Orthogonal Finetuning already feels like a natural next step after LoRA for diffusion models—its geometric foundations give us guarantees that simple learning rate tweaking never has. As researchers grapple with these open questions, we’ll see whether OFT becomes a standard plug‑and‑play adapter for any vision–language system, unlocking even richer downstream capabilities while staying light on both computation and data.*
