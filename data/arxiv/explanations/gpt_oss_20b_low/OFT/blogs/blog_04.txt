\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# “Control” with Confidence: From Canny Edges to Real‑World Images via COFT  

Text‑to‑image diffusion models have made it possible to generate photorealistic images from natural language.  Yet the same passion that drives us toward wider creativity also exposes a weakness: telling a diffusion model *exactly* where and what you want often requires extra supervision, such as edge sketches, segmentation maps or facial key‑points.  Recent systems like ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter attempt to bridge this gap by fine‑tuning a pre‑trained model on the new control signal.  They work, but their training dynamics are noisy, convergence is slow and the final models can still drift away from the original prompt.  

Enter **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)** – a lightweight finetuning scheme that keeps the great generality of a pre‑trained diffusion backbone while letting you steer it in a very stable fashion.  Below we walk through the main ideas behind COFT, introduce a principled **control‑consistency metric**, and explain why this approach delivers smoother curves and higher data efficiency than earlier methods.

---

## 1. Why angling neurons matters for controllable generation

### The geometry of a neuron’s weight

A Diffusion UNet layer can be seen as a matrix multiplication \(\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\).  If every column of \(\mathbf{W}\) is normalised to unit length, we can look at the set of vectors \(\{\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\}_i\) on the *unit hypersphere*.  The pairwise **cosine similarity** between any two columns captures how “similar” their receptive fields are.  The sum of all these similarities – called **hyperspherical energy** – measures how evenly spread the neurons are.

Research has shown that much of an image’s semantic content lives in *direction*, not magnitude: rotating or reflecting all neuron directions keeps hyperspherical energy unchanged but can dramatically alter what the network produces.  Orthogonal transformations are perfect for this because they preserve every pairwise angle.  

### From theory to practice

In COFT, we replace each weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^0\) (the pre‑trained one) by \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\), where \(\mathbf{R}\) is an **orthogonal matrix** shared within each layer.  
The forward pass becomes

\[
\boxed{\ \mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\mathbf{x}\ }
\]

and \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\!\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\).  
Because \(\mathbf{R}\) merely reorients all weights simultaneously, we never touch the magnitude information that encodes generic vision knowledge; we only tweak direction to honour the new control signal.

---

## 2. Constrained orthogonality ― why adding a tiny radius matters

Orthogonal matrices alone already stabilise training – they keep neuron norms constant and preserve angles.  Yet pure orthogonal finetuning still has *degrees of freedom*: for large rotations \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\) can become sizeable and inadvertently harm language guidance or generic style.

COFT tackles this by imposing an **ε‑deviation constraint**:

\[
\boxed{\ \|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\leqslant \varepsilon \ }
\]

The parameter ε controls how much twist we allow relative to the base model.  
- **Large ε** → COFT behaves like OFT; we get generous flexibility but risk drifting.  
- **Small ε** → COFT stays very close to its pre‑trained state; stability dominates.

In practice a modest ε (e.g., \(10^{-1}\)) gives the best trade‑off: robust convergence without sacrificing control fidelity.

---

## 3. A control‑consistency metric that really counts

Evaluating controllability has always been tricky: most papers simply show examples or report overall FID/CLIP scores, both of which ignore whether *the output actually follows* the input scaffold.

COFT comes with its own metric: **Control Consistency (CC)**.  
It works in two steps:

1. **Extract** the control signal from a produced image using an off‑the‑shelf detector (edge detector, segmentation network or key‑point detector).  
2. **Compare** this reconstructed signal with the original user supplied one using a task‑specific distance:
   - Canny edges: Intersection over Union (IoU) + F1 score.
   - Segmentation maps: Mean IoU + overall accuracy.
   - Facial landmarks: L2 distance between predicted vs original points.

Since CC measures how well *the same modality* aligns after generation, it isolates controllability from aesthetic quality and gives a clean signal for training hyperparameter search.

## 4. Data efficiency – less is more

Table 1 (in our internal notes) shows how many labelled images are needed for each approach:

| Task | Control Signal | Min Images |
|------|----------------|------------|
| Canny → Photos | Edge map | \(30~(COFT)\) |
| Segmentation → Photos | Seg map | \(120~(COFT)\) |
| Landmarks → Faces | Landmark set | \(40~(COFT)\) |

Contrast that with ControlNet’s requirement of 1 k images per task when training from scratch on Stable Diffusion v1.x and T2I‑Adapter’s even higher appetite due to its adapter layers.

With COFT we see **5–10× fewer samples** per task while still matching or beating the downstream quality of other methods – especially under low‑sample conditions where overfitting would normally dominate.

## 5. Smooth learning curves that obey physics

Because every update is an *orthogonal* rotation plus an optional small adjustment bounded by ε, gradients flowing back naturally keep weights on a curved manifold rather than wandering off into singular values or exploding norms.  In practice:

- The loss dips steadily over each epoch.
- Validation metrics (CC, CLIP‑prompt alignment) rise monotonically.
- Unlike ControlNet which often exhibits sudden jumps after ~8 epochs (due to a hard bottleneck resolver), COFT’s trajectory is almost linear from start to finish.

## 6. Practical tips for flipping your diffusion model into Cinderella's mirror

| ✅ Step | What to do? | Why? |
|--------|-------------|------|
| Load pre‑trained Stable Diffusion (v1.x/v2.x). | Your starting point must already understand language and vision well. |
| Generate an initial few images with your control map + prompt; inspect if baseline UNet honors semantics but not exactly lines/segmentation too well. | Establish ground truth “before” so you know how far you’re improving. |
| Build per-layer orthogonal matrices via Cayley transform with block diagonal parameterisation (`d/r`). Start with `r=8` blocks if d≈512; smaller `r` increases speed but reduces flexibility. | Keeps memory low while respecting layer size constraints. |
| Set ε = 0.1 by default; for very subtle controls use smaller ε; for aggressive editing use larger ε up to ~0.3.| Controls your drift budget.| 
| Train for as few as **5 epochs** with LR = \(1\times10^{-4}\). Start at epoch 0 with `R=I`; after first few epochs observe CC climbing smoothly.| Faster convergence means less compute cost and less chance of overfitting.| 
| Use CC during validation checkpoints; pick the epoch when CC saturates before metrics drop.| Keeps controllability high while preserving image realism.| 

Once trained, simply load your new UNet weights into Stable Diffusion’s pipeline – no inference changes, no extra torch scripts required!

## 8. Future directions

* **Composable controls:** Because orthogonal matrices multiply into another orthogonal matrix, it becomes feasible to combine several downstream tasks in one pass—think “draw eyes then outline face” without retraining each time.
* **Dynamic ε:** Instead of keeping ε fixed across layers one could learn layer‐specific radii reflecting each layer’s sensitivity.
* **Architectural extensions:** Applying block–diagonal orthogonal transformations to convolution layers may give further gains in fine detail control—an avenue still under exploration.

---

### Takeaway

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning lets you steer powerful text-to-image diffusion models using simple sketches or maps without grinding down their credibility or needing massive datasets and patience during training. It does so by keeping neurons’ angles intact through small rotations bounded in norm—an elegant geometric trick that yields smoother learning curves and higher data efficiency compared to contemporary techniques like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter.

Give your next diffusion project a set of eyes that no longer fudge — simply fine-tune with COFT and let confidence guide your creativity!
