Answer:
**Intuition behind Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)**  

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) learns a *single* orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ for each layer and replaces the pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf W^0$ by $\mathbf W=\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0$.  
Because $\mathbf R$ is orthogonal, every neuron’s direction is merely rotated (or reflected) while its norm is unchanged, so the *pair‑wise angles* between neurons – and therefore the hyperspherical energy – stay exactly the same.  This guarantees that the “semantic geometry” encoded by the pretrained model is not destroyed.

In practice, however, an unconstrained $\mathbf R$ can still move the neurons very far from their original positions.  For a large \(\varepsilon\) the finetuned weights can drift into regions of parameter space that have very different behaviour from the pretrained model, which may give rise to instability or over‑fitting to a tiny target dataset.  Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) simply restricts that drift:

$$
\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|_2 \le \varepsilon .
$$

Here \(\mathbf I\) is the identity matrix; setting this as a bound requires every entry of \(\mathbf R\) to stay close to the identity, i.e., each neuron is rotated only a little.  In effect we add **two** regularisers to OFT:

1. **Preservation of hyperspherical energy** – keeps the internal angular relationships fixed.  
2. **Proximity to the pretrained weights** – keeps the finetuned model in a small neighbourhood of the source model.

The second constraint is what makes COFT more stable than vanilla OFT: it prevents large collective rotations that could break hidden symmetries or cause catastrophic forgetting.

---

### How we choose \(\varepsilon\)

The paper does not prescribe a universal rule; instead \(\varepsilon\) is treated as a hyper‑parameter tuned on a validation set.  Practitioners usually follow these guidelines:

| Scenario | Typical \(\varepsilon\) |
|----------|------------------------|
| **Few training images** | Large values (e.g., \(1.0 - 1.5\)). The model needs enough freedom to adapt to new data without over‑fitting. |
| **Large in‑domain dataset** | Small values (e.g., \(0.2 - 0.5\)). The pretrained model already covers most of the target distribution, so only gentle adjustments are needed. |
| **Very safety‑critical applications** | Very small (\(<0.1\)). Guarantees that outputs do not deviate much from pre‑training behaviour. |

The paper’s Figure 9 shows precisely this trend: as \(\varepsilon\) decreases, COFT stays closer to the pretrained weights, yielding *slower but steadier* convergence and less risk of mode collapse; as \(\varepsilon\) increases, COFT behaves similarly to OFT but may exhibit higher variance and occasional degradation.

---

### What does $\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|\le \varepsilon$ do “biologically”?

Think of each neuron as a point on a unit sphere in high‑dimensional space.  The orthogonal transform tilts all points simultaneously around some axis while preserving mutual angles.  If we constrain how far we can tilt—i.e., keep $\mathbf R$ near identity—each point moves only by a small angular amount: “small rotations around each neuron”.  This keeps the relative arrangement largely intact, much like keeping an animal’s gait slightly perturbed rather than completely altered when learning a new movement pattern.

In broader machine‑learning terms, this mirrors *parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning* philosophies such as LoRA: we keep most of the original parameters fixed but allow a tiny controlled shift in representation space. COFT provides an explicit mathematical guarantee for how far that shift may be.

---

### Why smaller ε → more stable but less expressive models

* **Stability:** With a tighter bound on $\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|$, gradient updates cannot push neurons far into regions where previously learned features have been scrambled or where numerical issues arise.  Hence training behaves almost like fine‑tuning with an extremely small learning rate; convergence is smooth and reproducible.

* **Expressiveness:** A stricter ε limits how much we can reorient neurons relative to each other.  In downstream tasks such as subject‑driven generation or control conditioning, we often need *new* directional patterns that encode subject identity or control semantics.  If ε is too small, those directions are basically already present in the base model and cannot be rearranged enough to capture the new nuance; consequently performance plateaus early.

Empirically this trade‑off appears in Figure 9: with ε≈0  (exactly identity), COFT reduces to plain static finetuning (no learning). With ε≈1 – 2 it behaves like OFT (roundabout full rotation freedom). Between them lies an optimum region where COFT gains extra stability without sacrificing much expressiveness.

---

### Bottom line

- COFT augments OFT with an explicit radius constraint on the orthogonal matrix.
- The radius bound controls *how far* neuron directions are permitted to move from their pre‑trained orientation.
- Choosing ε is mainly empirical; smaller values favour stability, larger values bring more flexibility at potential cost of instability.
- The biological intuition is simply that we allow neurons to “wiggle” only slightly around their original point on the hypersphere, preserving overall geometry while giving just enough room for task–specific re‑configuration.
