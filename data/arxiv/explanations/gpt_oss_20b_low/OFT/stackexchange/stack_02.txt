\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does orthogonal transformation guarantee preservation of pairwise angles but allow learning new directions?
Question:
Equation (3) in the paper states that if a weight matrix $W$ can be written as $W = R W^0$ with $R^	op R = I$, then the hyperspherical energy of $W$ equals that of $W^0$. Why does this imply that all pairwise cosine similarities between neurons stay identical? Is this property only true for a single layer, or does it extend to deeper networks with multiple layers where the output of one layer becomes the input to another?
Answer:
**Why an orthogonal transform leaves all pairwise angles unchanged**

Let a weight matrix of a single linear layer be  
\[
\mathbf W^0=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf w}_1^0 & \hat{\mathbf w}_2^0 & \dots & \hat{\mathbf w}_n^0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n},
\]
where $\hat{\mathbf w}_i^0$ is the *unit‑norm* vector corresponding to the *i*‑th neuron (the hat stresses that the vector has been normalised).  
The cosine similarity between any two neurons $i$ and $j$ is simply  

\[
\cos(\theta_{ij}^0)=\langle \hat{\mathbf w}_i^0,\hat{\mathbf w}_j^0\rangle
            =1-\tfrac12\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i^0-\hat{\mathbf w}_j^0\|^{2}.
\]

Now apply an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R\in \mathbb R^{d\times d}$ to every neuron:

\[
\mathbf W=\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0
        =[\hat{\mathbf w}_1,\dots,\hat{\mathbf w}_n],  \qquad  
        \hat{\mathbf w}_i=\frac{\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_i^0}{\|\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_i^0\|}.
\]

Because $\mathbf R$ is orthogonal we have  
$\|\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}\|=\|\hat{\mathbf w}\|=1$, so the normalisation step is not needed:  
$\hat{\mathbf w}_i=\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_i^0$.

The inner product of two transformed neurons is

\[
\langle \hat{\mathbf w}_i,\hat{\mathbf w}_j\rangle
  = \langle \mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_i^0,\;\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_j^0\rangle
  = (\hat{\mathbf w}_i^0)^{T}\underbrace{(R^{T}R)}_{=I}(\!\!-\!\!R)\,(\text{since }R^{T}R=I)\,.
$$

More concretely

$$

**Bottom line**

Equation (3) guarantees that **any orthogonal transformation applied uniformly to all neurons in one layer keeps that layer’s hyperspherical energy—and equivalently every pairwise cosine similarity—exactly the same as before**. This invariance is strictly local to that layer. When you chain many such layers together, each being rotated by its own orthogonal matrix, you obtain new directions for every weight matrix but still maintain each layer’s internal angular layout. Thus OFT can re‑code model parameters without erasing pretrained relationships, while deeper interactions between rotated layers allow new behaviours (e.g., better controllability or subject specificity).
