# Chapter Title

**Fine‑Tuning Text‑to‑Image Diffusion: Motivation and Foundations**

---

## 1 Introduction to Diffusion Models and the Need for Fine‑Tuning

Text‑to‑image diffusion models have recently burst onto the generative AI scene, delivering images that rival human illustration in both fidelity and diversity. The core idea behind these models is a stochastic denoising process: starting from pure noise, a neural network progressively removes noise conditioned on a textual prompt until a coherent image emerges. Landmark systems such as **Stable Diffusion**, **Imagen**, and **DALL‑E 2** have pushed this paradigm to new heights, thanks to large pre‑training corpora, sophisticated diffusion schedulers, and powerful vision–language embeddings.

Yet, despite their impressive capacity, these models often struggle to honor *fine‑level* user intent. A natural text prompt like “a red apple on a wooden table” can be interpreted in numerous ways—different lighting, angles, or surrounding objects. More critically, the text alone rarely constrains *identity* (e.g., generating a specific person) or *structure* (e.g., fitting an image to a pre‑specified sketch). Two families of downstream tasks have emerged to tackle exactly these limitations:

- **Subject‑Driven Generation** (DreamBooth style): One supplies only a handful of images of a particular subject—say, a cat or a celebrity—and wishes the model to render that very subject in novel contexts defined by arbitrary text prompts.
- **Controllable Generation** (ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter style): The user provides an auxiliary conditioning signal—canny edges, segmentation maps, depth maps—that the model must faithfully follow while still obeying the text description.

Both problems are fundamentally about *adapting* a gigantic pre‑trained diffusion backbone without eroding its already accrued generative prowess. Consequently, any effective fine‑tuning strategy must honor two high‑level desiderata:

1. **Training Efficiency** – Few trainable parameters and epochs. The method should sprinkle just enough learnable knobs so that the diffusion process can be nudged toward the new behavior without reinventing the wheel.
2. **Generalization Preservation** – After fine‑tuning it should still generate diverse, photorealistic images from arbitrary prompts. In other words, the model must not “forget” its original cinematic vision even as it learns a new skill.

Existing fine‑tuning approaches largely fall short of these goals. Typically they shrink learning rates to preserve stability or inject low‑rank adapters (LoRA) that only mildly perturb pretrained weights. Such tricks are ad hoc; they neither come with theoretical guarantees nor capture deeper structural properties of the underlying neural network.

In what follows we delve into why primitive methods falter, introduce the concept of *hyperspherical energy* as a structural safeguard for knowledge retention, and motivate orthogonal fine‑tuning as a principled alternative that marries robustness with flexibility.

---

### 1.1 Overview of Large Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

The field’s flagship models can be classified along two axes: **pixel-space vs latent-space generation** and **training paradigms vs use cases**.

- **Pixel-Space Diffusers** such as **StableDiffusion**\cite{rombach2022high} and latent image generators like **Imagen**\cite{saharia2022photorealistic} run their denoising U-Nets directly on raw pixels or compressed latents fed through an autoencoder (VQ-GAN for StableDiffusion). They rely heavily on powerful transformer-boosted cross-attention blocks that fuse textual embeddings (from CLIP or its descendants) into every layer.
- **Latent Diffusers** such as **DALL-E 2**\cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} encode images in latent spaces learned jointly with textual encoders (e.g., CLIP’s dual encoder). This reduces computational load while maintaining high sample fidelity.

All these backbones share an essential property: they were trained on *billion*-scale text–image pairs without domain-specific supervision beyond raw captions or tags. This enormous capability makes them excellent starting points for downstream specialization.

---

### 1.2 Limitations of Pure Text Guidance

The bottleneck arises when a user’s intent cannot be distilled into a concise caption:

- **Ambiguity** – Natural language is inherently underspecified: “a dreamy portrait” could imply lighting style, color palette, pose—many possibilities remain unexplained.
- **Lack of Precision** – When uniqueness is required (“the exact look of this specific woman”), vanilla text generalization falls flat because the model has never seen enough examples to resolve identity.
- **Structural Mismatch** – Designers often want concrete arrangements—edges, object placement—which are difficult to encode purely in language.

Thus we need additional modalities (images or sketches) or stronger adaptation mechanisms that go beyond naïve parameter updates.

---

### 1.3 Definition of Subject-Driven Generation

Subject-driven generation formalizes the problem: given \(N\) `anchor` images \(\{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^N\) depicting an entity \(S\) (e.g., "Mona Lisa" portrait), we wish to train—or adapt—a diffusion model \(f_{\theta}\) so that for any new text prompt \(\mathbf{p}\), sampling from \(f_{\theta}\) yields images \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) containing \(S\) rendered in the context specified by \(\mathbf{p}\).

Typical pipelines employ an inversion step: map each anchor image into latent space \(\mathbf{z}_i = E(\mathbf{x}_i)\), then fine-tune with reconstruction losses aligning \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_i\) close to \(\mathbf{z}_i\), while preserving generality via regularizers that penalize excessive deviation from pretrained weights.

The canonical example is **DreamBooth**, which latches onto subject embeddings via class token substitution and uses low-rank adapters (LoRA). Nonetheless its heavy dependence on carefully tuned learning rates leaves room for instability.

---

### 1.4 Definition of Controllable Generation

Controllable generation extends diffusion inference by accepting an extra *control signal* \(\mathbf{c}\):

\[
\mathbf{y} = f_{\theta}(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{c}) .
\]

Typical control signals include:

| Control Type | Example | Common Panel |
|--------------|--------|--------------|
| Canny edges | Edge map | Sketch |
| Segmentation | Class masks | Layout |
| Landmarks | Keypoints | Pose |

The goal is twofold:
1. For every semantic node in \(\mathbf{c}\), there must be visual correspondence in the output image.
2. The output must still honor extra attributes encoded in \(\mathbf{p}\).

Recent works like ControlNet add extra branching modules; T2I-Adapter injects adapters conditioned on \(\mathbf{c}\). Their approach is essentially *additive*: new small weights augment existing ones but may unintuitively swamp original semantics if not tightly constrained.

---

### 1.5 Key Desiderata for Fine-Tuning Methods

When adapting large diffusion backbones we seek:

| Attribute | Definition |
|-----------|------------|
| **Parameter Efficiency** | Minimize number of tunable parameters; ideally fewer than one per neuron or per layer block. |
| **Fast Convergence** | Reach performance plateau after few epochs; avoid oscillatory or catastrophic forgetting dynamics. |
| **Generalization Retention** | Preserve pre-trained zero-shot photo-realism; maintain diverse sampling ability across unseen prompts. |
| **Inference Cost Neutrality** | No extra overhead during generation; final finetuned model should run at same speed as base model.|

These constraints impose stringent requirements on any architectural perturbation we introduce during fine-tuning.

---

### 1.6 Shortcomings of Existing Fine-Tuning Strategies

#### 1.6.1 Small Learning Rate Updates
Classic stochastic gradient descent with tiny step sizes attempts to keep changes small (*implicit regularization*). But:

- No explicit geometric guarantee; weights can drift off manifold.
- Requires hyperparameter tuning per dataset/subject; highly fragile.
- Suffers from bias toward overfitting small data regimes if learning rates are too high.

#### 1.6.2 LoRA Low-Rank Adapters
LoRA injects two low-rank matrices \(A,B\):

\[
W' = W + A B^\top , \quad r' \ll d .
\]

Potential shortcomings include:

- **Fixed rank constraint** precludes representation power when higher rank changes are beneficial.
- The additive form can disrupt original weight orthogonality/geometry leading to semantic loss.
- Training stability heavily depends on selecting \(r'\), learning rate, and regularizer strengths.

In sum, both approaches provide hand-wavy explanations for stability but lack rigorous preservation guarantees against key structural properties—most notably those governing relationships among neurons across layers.

---

### 1.7 Why Preserve A Geometric Structure?

Neural network performance hinges not just on absolute weight values but on ***relative directions*** among neurons when viewed as vectors in high-dimensional space:

- Empirical evidence from work on angular loss functions shows cosine similarity—essentially angle between neuron vectors—is a compact proxy for semantic correspondence.
- Hyperspherical energy aggregates pairwise angular similarities over all neurons within a layer:
  
  \[
  \operatorname{HE}(W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1},
  \]
  
  where hats denote normalisation to unit norm.
  
A stable finetune should preserve this hyperspherical structure; otherwise inter-neuron relations—and thus higher-level features—are corrupted even if Euclidean weight change appears minimal.

Orthogonal transformations respect exactly this invariant: rotating every neuron vector within a layer preserves all pairwise angles—and thus `HE`. This observation motivates an approach where finetuning is cast as learning an orthogonal matrix per layer—a constrained yet expressive transformation that reveres neural geometry while allocating limited flexibility where necessary.

# 2 Fundamental Idea: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

In this chapter we dissect the key idea behind Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and place it within the broader landscape of model‑adaptation techniques. OFT departs from conventional wisdom—gradient–steep‑learning with a tiny learning rate—by focusing on a very particular geometric property of neural net weights: the set of pair‑wise angles among neurons, which we encode through **hyperspherical energy**. We show that by learning a **layer‑shared orthogonal matrix**, we can *re‑orient* the pre‑trained weight vectors without changing any intrinsic inner‑product relations, thereby preserving the semantic content that the large diffusion prior has learned while still allowing task‑specific adaptation.

The chapter is organized as follows. First we motivate why the *direction* of a neuron is the prime carrier of semantic knowledge (illustrated by a toy auto‑encoder experiment). Next we show mathematically why an orthogonal transformation keeps every pairwise angle intact and thus leaves hyperspherical energy invariant. We then derive the core OFT constraint, prove that any orthogonal transformation of the weight matrix keeps the hyperspherical energy equal to that of the pre‑trained weights, and therefore establish that minimizing hyperspherical‑energy drift forces the finetuned weight to be an orthogonal multiple of its original value. Finally, we contrast OFT’s **multiplicative** update with LoRA’s **additive** rank‑one​-style update, highlighting their different regularizing effects.

## 2.1 Why Neuron Direction Matters – Angular Information as Semantic Carrier

Before formalizing OFT we need to convince ourselves that *the angle between a neuron's weight vector and an input vector* carries more semantic load than its magnitude. This claim is rooted in the observation that neural networks can be trained successfully when all neuron vectors are forced onto a unit hypersphere: SphereNet \cite{liu2017deep} demonstrated that such normalization never degrades performance and often improves generalization. The intuition is simple: if all neurons lie on a sphere, then two neurons whose directions are close will respond similarly to most inputs, while those with far apart directions will activate on distinct subspaces; no magnitude scaling is necessary because all neurons have equal length.

To empirically confirm this intuition for image‐processing layers we design a toy convolution auto‑encoder on flower images. Two variants of activation are compared during testing:

1. **Standard inner‑product activation** (the training objective);  
2. **Angular activation only**, i.e.\ only using the cosine between input patch \(\mathbf{x}\) and filter \(\mathbf{w}\) (\(\cos(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|}\));  
3. **Magnitude activation only**, i.e.\ ignoring direction.

The results (Figure 2 in the paper) reveal that reconstruction quality drops by more than 80 % when magnitude information is discarded but remains nearly perfect when only angular information is kept—even though training never used cosine activation at all. This experimental fact supports our primary hypothesis: *direction alone suffices to encode most useful visual semantics*. It follows that an efficient adaptation technique should focus on changing directions rather than magnitudes.

## 2.2 Orthogonal Transformations Preserve Pairwise Angles – Hyperspherical Energy Invariance

Let us formalize what “changing direction” really means for an entire layer of neurons. Consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\). Each column \(\mathbf{w}_i^{0}\) denotes one neuron in \(d\)-dimensional space; we write \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{0}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_i^{0}}{\|\mathbf{w}_i^{0}\|}\) for its unit direction vector.

The **pairwise angular similarity** between two neurons \(i,j\) is simply their cosine:
\[
    \operatorname{cos}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{0},\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j^{0}) \;=\; \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{0\top} \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j^{0}.
\]
Summing over all unordered pairs yields what Liu et al. call **hyperspherical energy**:
\[
    E(\mathbf{W}^{0}) \;\triangleq\;
    \sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\ i<j}}^n
        \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{0}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j^{0}\|^{-1}.
    \tag{2.1}
\]
Because each term depends only on pairwise angles (distances on the sphere), \(E(\cdot)\) measures how uniformly distributed the neuron directions are on the sphere—a structural property tied to semantic expressiveness.

Now consider applying an arbitrary orthogonal matrix
\(\mathbf{R}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\), satisfying
\(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\!\mathbf{R}=\boldsymbol I_{d}\).
Define new weights
\[
    \widetilde{\mathbf W}
    = \underbrace{\bigl(\!R\,\,\widetilde\, W^0\bigr)}_{\textbf {new}}
      = R\,W^{0}.
\]
Because multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves lengths,
\(\|\tilde{\mathbf w}_i\|=\|\mathbf w_i^{0}\|\).
Furthermore,
for any two columns,
\[
    \frac{\tilde{\mathbf w}_i}{\|\tilde{\mathbf w}_i\|}
      = R\,\frac{\mathbf w_i^{0}}{\|\mathbf w_i^{0}\|}
      = R\, \hat{\bf w}_i.
\]
Hence
\[
    \tilde{\bf w}_i^\top R^\top R\, \tilde {\bf w}_j
      = (\hat {\bf w}_i)^\top R^\top R (\hat {\bf w}_j)
      = (\hat {\bf w}_i)^\top (\hat {\bf w}_j),
\]
which shows that all pairwise inner products—and thus all angles—are unchanged:
\(E(R\,W^0)=E(W^0)\).  
So *any* orthogonal reorientation leaves hyperspherical energy untouched.

Intuitively, rotating or reflecting all neurons by the same rigid motion borders hyper-sphere does not blur or sharpen their relative positions; it merely paradoxically re‐orients them in feature space while keeping everything else identical.

## 2.3 Derivation of OFT Constraint

With this geometric insight in hand, we can now formulate Orthogonal Finetuning as an optimization problem that preserves hyperspherical energy:

Minimize
\( \bigl| E(\widetilde W)-E(W^0)\bigr|
   =  |E(RW^0)-E(W^0)|,\)
subject to \(R^\top R=\boldsymbol I_{d}\).

Because any orthogonal \(R\) leaves \(E(RW^0)=E(W^0)\), *the minimum is zero*: 
the objective vanishes exactly when
\(R\) is orthogonal.
Thus Eq.(2.1) can be replaced by its algebraic version

> **OFT Principle**  
> Find parameters such that  
>   \[
>        {\widetilde W}=RW^0,\qquad 
>        R^\top R=RR^\top=\boldsymbol I_{d}.
>   \tag{2.2}
>   \]

This constraint states formally that *the finetuned weight matrix must be obtained by left‑multiplying the pretrained weight matrix by a single orthogonal matrix*. Equivalently, across *each layer*, OFT learns one shared rotation/reflection.

### Proof of Minimal Hyperspherical Energy Difference

Let us prove rigorously that enforcing Eq.(2.2) guarantees minimal hyperspherical-energy drift:

*Lemma.*  
Let \(W^0,W^*\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\). Then  
\( E(W^*)=E(W^0)\)   if and only if there exists an orthogonal \(R'\) such that
\( W^*=R'W^0.\)

*Proof.*  

(⇒) Assume \(E(W^*)=E(W^0)\). For each column define unit vectors
\(u_i={W_i}/{||W_i||}, v_i={W_i^*}/{||W_i^*||}\).
Because pairwise distances between all {u_i} equal those between {v_i}, there exists a rigid motion mapping each u_i to v_i: \(v_i=Ru_i,\;i=1,\dots,n.\)
Collecting these into matrices gives \(V=RU,\)
where \(U=[u_1,\dots,u_n]\),  
\(V=[v_1,\dots,v_n]\).
Both U and V have unit columns, hence both are partial isometries.
Then define \(R' = VU^\dagger,\)
where \(U^\dagger\) denotes pseudoinverse.
It satisfies \(R'U=W^*, R'^{\,T}R'=I_d.\)
Thus \(W^*=R'W^0.\)

(⇐) Conversely, if \(W^*=R'W^0,R'\in O(d)\),
then every column direction is rotated identically,
so distances on unit sphere remain unchanged.
Therefore \(E(W^*)=E(R'W^0)=E(W^0).\)

∎

Consequently, minimizing any normed difference between singe-layer weights and its rotated counterpart reduces to learning an orthogonal transform per layer:
\[ 
       W_{\ell}^{(final)} := R_{\ell} W_{\ell}^{(pretrained)} .
       \tag{2.3}
       \]
This completes the derivation.

## 2.4 Comparison with LoRA – Multiplicative vs Additive Updates

LoRA introduces low-rank perturbations to every linear layer:
\( W = W^0 + B A,\)
with learnable small–rank factors A,B.
Its main regularization comes from forcing B A to maintain low rank; intuitively it nudges weights slightly away from their pre‑trained positions while keeping overall spectrum stable.

OFT differs fundamentally in two ways:

| Property | LoRA | OFT |
|----------|------|-----|
| Update form | additive: Δ = BA (low rank). | multiplicative: Δ = (R – I) W⁰ (full rank but constrained). |
| Constraint | rank(Δ)=r′ small | Δ preserves pairwise angles → rotational transform |
| Regularization | scale bounded via LR & rank | strict geometric invariance → hyperspherical energy unchanged |
| Stability drives | depends on learning rate & rank choice | guaranteed by orthogonality |

Under Eq.(2.3), writing Δ=W−W⁰=(R−I) W⁰,
if (R−I) happens to be low-rank this resembles LoRA; otherwise it constitutes a full-rank update nevertheless restricted to preserve angles among columns---a substantially tighter constraint than simply restricting rank.

Thus OFT can be viewed as a generalized LoRA where “low-rank” has been replaced by “orthogonality:” both wisely constrain drastic changes while permitting adaptive reorientation of neuron directions—a plausible reason for OFT’s superior stability shown in experiments.

---

In summary, Orthogonal Finetuning operates by learning one jointly shared rotation per layer so as to keep every pairwise neuron angle identical to its pre‑trained state while allowing us to steer generations toward domain‐specific or controllable semantics. This elegant geometric insight leads not only to provable preservation of hyperspherical energy but also practical gains in training efficiency and stability—making OFT a compelling alternative to more conventional additive fine‐tuning methods like LoRA or vanilla gradient descent with tiny learning rates.

# 3. Efficient Parameterization of Orthogonal Matrices

Controlling a large text‑to‑image diffusion model inevitably requires a difficult trade‑off: the finetuning mechanism must be expressive enough to inject task‑specific behaviour, yet compact enough that the number of trainable parameters remains tractable. The Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework achieves this by learning a **single orthogonal transform per layer**. Nonetheless, a naïve representation of an orthogonal matrix has quadratic space‑time complexity in the dimensionality of the layer and is impractical for the high‑dimensional weight matrices that appear in modern diffusion architectures such as Stable Diffusion or Imagen. This chapter develops a suite of practical parameterization tricks that dramatically reduce the memory and computational burden while preserving the theoretical guarantees that make OFT effective.

## 3.1 Cayley Transform for Differentiable Orthogonality

The Cayley transform offers an elegant way of constructing orthogonal matrices from unconstrained parameters. Given a **skew‑symmetric matrix** \( \mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) with \(\mathbf{Q}^\top=-\mathbf{Q}\), the Cayley map

\[
\mathbf{R}=\bigl(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\bigr)^{-1}
\tag{3.1}
\]

produces an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R}\in O(d)\). Two facts justify why (3.1) is attractive for fine‑tuning diffusion models:

1. **Differentiability**: All operations involved—matrix addition, inversion, and multiplication—are differentiable, allowing us to backpropagate gradients directly through \(\mathbf{R}\). Consequently, training does not require discrete search or projection steps; constraints are enforced implicitly by construction.

2. **Automatic enforcement of orthogonality**: Because \( \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-\top} (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^\top(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}= \mathbf{I}\), we need not add any penalty term or re‑orthogonalize after every update.

The only caveat is that the Cayley map only covers **special orthogonal matrices** (those with determinant \(+1\)), excluding reflections (\(\det \mathbf{R}=-1\)). Empirical experiments in OFT demonstrate that this limitation is benign: reflections do not afford any significant advantage over rotations for preserving hyperspherical energy, and every target transformation can be expressed up to multiplication by a diagonal reflection matrix—a negligible change for our purposes.

### 3.1.1 Implementing the Cayley Transform

A simple PyTorch implementation follows:

```python
class CayleyOrthogonal(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        # initialize Q to zero so R starts as identity
        self.Q = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(dim, dim))

    def forward(self):
        I = torch.eye(self.Q.size(0), device=self.Q.device)
        # enforce skew-symmetry
        Q_skew = 0.5 * (self.Q - self.Q.t())
        # compute (I - Q) inverse with stability guard
        inv = torch.inverse(I - Q_skew)
        R = (I + Q_skew) @ inv
        return R
```

The enforcement of skew‑symmetry is accomplished by averaging \( \mathbf{Q}\) and its transpose after each gradient step, guaranteeing that \(\nabla_{\!Q}L\) respects the constraint automatically.

## 3.2 Block‐Diagonal Decomposition

Although theoretically sound, directly parameterizing \(\mathbf{R}\in O(d)\) via equation (3.1) scales as \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\). In diffusion models, a typical transformer block might contain attention weight matrices of volume hundreds of millions when flattened into dense forms. To bring OFT into line with existing parameter‑efficient adapters such as LoRA, we adopt a **block–diagonal decomposition**:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{R}= 
\mathrm{diag}\bigl(
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
    \vdots & & \\
    & \boxed{\;\;}\; & \\
    & & \vdots
   \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{$r$ blocks}}\!
\bigr),
}
\tag{3.2}
\]
where each block \( \mathbf{R}_i \in O(d/r)\). The blocks are independent orthogonal transformations applied to disjoint groups of neurons (e.g., splitting channels in a linear layer or the heads in multi-head attention).

### 3.2.1 Why Block–Diagonal Works

- **Parameter Economy**: Each block requires only \( ((d/r)(d/r-1))/2 = \mathcal O(d^2/r^2)\) parameters via a skew­symmetric generator per block; thus total parameter count scales as \( r\times\nabla d^2/r^2=\mathcal O(d^2/r)\). Choosing \( r=8,\!16,\!32\) brings down the overhead by orders of magnitude relative to a full matrix.
- **Flexibility Preservation**: Even though all neurons within a block are transformed jointly, distinct blocks can rotate different subsets arbitrarily; empirical studies show this suffices to tailor the geometry of feature space to downstream tasks without compromising the model’s generative backbone.
- **Compatibility with Convolutional Structure**: In convolution layers each input channel can be assigned its own 2×2 rotation block – analogous to depth‑wise convolution – blurring the line between feature orientation and spatial filtering.

### 3.2.2 Sharing Blocks Across Layers

An optional yet powerful reduction achieves near elimination of additional parameters: we enforce **block sharing**, i.e., all layers share an identical set of block matrices:
\[
    \forall\,l,\;\;\;
    \{\!\boxed{\;\;}...\;\!\}= 
    r_{i}=\textbf{\[some shared transformation]\},   ~ i=1,\ldots,r.
\]
Consequently every layer learns no free parameters at all—the same orthogonal transformation is reused throughout—while still preserving overall flexibility because learning still shapes how this shared transform interacts with different weight tensors.

## 3.3 Parameter Count Analysis vs LoRA

To appreciate how OFT stacks against traditional low‑rank adapters such as LoRA, consider a fully connected layer of shape \( d\times n.\)

| Method | Trainable Parameters | Formula |
|--------|----------------------|---------|
| OFT (block‑diagonal, shared blocks) | \(d^2/(4r)\) | if each block off‑diagonal entries count half |
| LoRA (rank \(r'\)) | \(r'(d+n)\) | independent matrices \(A,B\)|

Assume typical settings where \( d,n=128,\; r'=8,\; r=8.\)

- OFT yields \(128^2/(4\times8)=512\) parameters per layer.
- LoRA supplies \(8(128+128)=2048.\)

Thus OFT reduces memory usage by roughly four times while retaining identical expressive power through global orthogonality.

When *block sharing* is introduced, OFT collapses further: only one set of blocks per parameter group contributes any free parameters—speeding up inference because the ran op becomes a fixed gate rather than per-layer learnable weights.

## 3.4 Practical Consequences During Training

### 3.4.1 Gradient Flow Through Orthogonal Matrices

Because every forward pass shapes weights as
\(W^{(t)} = R^{(t)} W_0,\)
the gradient w.r.t a particular hidden activations flows through an orthonormal mapping that preserves Fisher information norms harmonic on the hypersphere—thereby reducing catastrophic drift away from pretraining behavior.

Additionally, updating only an implicit rotation matrix cuts down numerical instability issues commonly plague neural networks when small learning rates are applied on full weight tensors; rotations keep magnitude stable while reorienting directions.

### 3.4.2 Fast Convergence via Smooth Manifolds

An orthogonally constrained optimization problem lives on a smooth manifold (the Stiefel manifold for rectangular transformations or orthogonal group for square matrices). Recent research has shown that first‑order optimizers perform more systematically when gradients remain tangent to this manifold instead of clinging to arbitrary directions in Euclidean space (see e.g., "Training Neural Networks Using Gradient Descent on Manifolds"). Empirically we observe that OFT reaches useful finetuned states within an order-of-magnitude fewer gradient steps compared to standard weight updates or low-rank adapters—exactly what our experimental section corroborates.

## 3.5 Summary and Takeaways

The efficient parameterization tactics presented here transform theoretically elegant but impractical “rotate all neurons” ideas into viable operators embedded in existing training pipelines:

* The **Cayley transform** guarantees unbroken orthogonality without extra regularizers.
* By harvesting symmetry via **block-diagonalization**, we scale from quadratic overhead down to linear-in-dimension levels.
* Optional **block sharing** thins down trainable parameters almost to zero while retaining adaptability across layers.
* The resulting architecture keeps training cheap yet expressive—a decisive advantage when finetuning massive models on limited data for subject-driven or controllable generation tasks.

With these ingredients in place, OFT stands poised to deliver both high-controllability performance and rigorous preservation of pre-trained generative semantics—a union rarely achieved by competing methods such as LoRA or control-netwidening adapters.

# 4. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

In the previous chapter we introduced Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a method that adapts a pre‑trained text‑to‑image diffusion model by multiplying each layer’s weight matrix with a learnable orthogonal matrix. OFT preserves the hyperspherical energy of every layer, guaranteeing that the pairwise angles among neurons remain unchanged. While OFT already stabilises fine‑tuning compared to naïve weight updates and low‑rank adapters, we observed that some semantic drift can still occur when the orthogonal matrix departs too far from the identity. In many downstream tasks—subject‑driven generation and controllable image synthesis—a strict neighbourhood of the pre‑trained weight space often leads to better visual fidelity and faster convergence. This motivates a *constrained* version of OFT, named **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)**, where we explicitly bound the angular deviation of every orthogonal matrix from the identity.

The key idea is to augment the free‑rotation optimisation with an additional radius constraint
\[
\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \le \epsilon,
\]
where $\epsilon>0$ controls how far we are allowed to move from the original set of weights. COFT thus enforces
\[
\begin{cases}
      \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I},\\[4pt]
      \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \le \epsilon,
\end{cases}
\]
for every layer in which finetuning takes place.

Below we detail how this combined constraint can be efficiently optimised, how the radius parameter $\epsilon$ is chosen in practice, and what empirical effects it has on finetuning stability and flexibility.

## COFT Forward Pass

Given a pre‑trained weight matrix $\bm{W}^{0}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ belonging to some linear layer of a diffusion model, OFT replaces the forward operation
\[
\bm{z}=\bigl(\bm{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\!\top}\!\!\bm{x}
\]
with
\[
\boxed{\;\;\bm{z}=\bigl(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\!\top}\!\!\bm{x}\;}
   \qquad (1)
\]
where $\bm{x}$ is the layer input and $\bm{R}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix shared across all neurons in that layer.
COFT enforces two constraints on $\bm{R}$:

1. **Orthogonality**: $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ ensures that Gramian distances among neurons are preserved.
2. **Radius bound**: $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|_{F} \leqslant \epsilon$ confines $\bm{R}$ to a small $F$-norm ball centred at the identity.

The Frobenius norm is used for computational convenience and because it directly measures component‑wise deviations between $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{I}$.

The optimisation problem for COFT can therefore be written as
\[
   \min_{\substack{\mathbf param}}~  \mathcal L_{\text{task}}
   ~~~~~~~
   \text{s.t. }
   \begin{cases}
        \;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;
        (\star)\quad    (\mathbf R^\top)(\mathbf R)=\mathbf I,\\[3pt]
        (\star\star)\quad  \|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|\leqslant \epsilon .
   \end{cases}
   \tag{2}
\]
Here $\mathcal L_{\text{task}}$ denotes one of the loss functions used in subject‑driven generation (typically a reconstruction loss + prior preservation loss) or controllable generation (e.g., an auxiliary control consistency loss). The only parameters that need to be updated during training are those defining each orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$.

## Mapping the Radius Constraint onto a Skew Matrix

A straightforward way to maintain orthogonality is to factorise each orthogonal matrix by means of Cayley’s transform:
\[
    \mathbf R = (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)\bigl(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q\bigr)^{-1},
    \qquad
    = (\mathbf I - \mathbf Q)^{-1}\! (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q),
    \tag{3}
\]
where $\mathbf Q$ is a skew‐symmetric matrix ($\mathbf Q^\top = -\,\!\mathbf Q$). Any skew‐symmetric $\mathbf Q$ automatically generates an orthogonal $\mathbf R$, because both $(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)$ and $(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)$ are invertible provided $\|\mathbf Q\|<1$. Thus by learning only $\mathbf Q$, we reduce an $O(d^{2})$ optimisation over general matrices into learning a $O(d^{2}/2)$ set of parameters with an inherent algebraic constraint.

The radius bound expressed in terms of $\mathbf R$ is,
```
|| R – I ||_F <= epsilon.
```
To prevent paying a costly inverse every time we evaluate this constraint we replace $(3)$ with its first–order Neumann series expansion assuming that all entries of $\mathbf Q$ are small:
```math
(\mathsf {I}-Q)^{-1}=I+Q+Q^{2}+ … ≈ I+Q,
```
which yields
```math
R ≈ I+2Q.
```
Hence,
```math
‖R – I‖_F ≈ ‖2Q‖_F = 2‖Q‖_F.
```
Dropping constant factors gives us an equivalent constraint on the skew split:
```
||Q||_F <= ε',
```
with `ε'=`ε/2. In practice we simply clip or project `Q` onto this ball during each gradient update. Since `Q` remains skew-symmetric, any projected `Q` still produces an orthogonal `R` through Cayley’s transform.

Consequently, COFT training proceeds as follows:

1. Initialise `Q` with all zeros so that `R = I`.
2. Compute gradients w.r.t.`Q` from downstream loss gradients backpropagated through `(3)`.
3. Take a gradient step on `Q`.
4. Project `Q` onto the Frobenius ball of radius `ε'` if necessary:
   ```python
   if ||Q||_F > ε':
       Q = ε'* Q / ||Q||_F
   ```
5. Recompute `R` from updated `Q`.

Because projection is strictly convex and inexpensive ($O(d^2)$), it introduces negligible computational overhead while strictly guaranteeing that `||R–I||≤ε`.

## Practical Choices for ε

Choosing ε controls a trade‑off:

- **Small ε** keeps `R` very close to identity, limiting how much geometry can be altered in neuron space. This yields highly stable finetuning but may underfit, especially when converting more drastic visual styles or highly specific control signals.
- **Large ε** permits richer rotations (and reflections), potentially capturing more diverse downstream semantics but risking drift from pretrained manifold leading to hallucinations or loss of fidelity.

Empirically we found:
- For **subject-driven generation**, ε∈[0.1, 0.25] works well across datasets such as CelebA-HQ or personalized face portraits.
- For **controllable generation** tasks (Canny edges → image or segmentation → image), slightly larger values such as ε∈[0.3, 0.5] allow sharper alignment while still preserving overall image aesthetics.
- For hard pixel-level constraints like landmark-to-face alignment, even ε≈0.6 can be beneficial because small local perturbations need significant angular moves in feature space.

In practice one starts with default values (e.g., 0.25) and then performs binary search over validation data; due to COFT’s strict radius enforcement there is no clear sign of catastrophic failure when slightly overshooting.

## Empirical Impact of ε on Stability vs Flexibility

To illustrate how ε affects finetuning trajectories, see Figure X below (reproduced from the authors’ ablation plots). It shows:

1. **Convergence speed** – For all tested ε<0.5 our models reach target accuracy within ~8 epochs (≈800 SGD iterations). Increasing ε beyond this point yields diminishing returns: convergence stalls at similar speed because gradients become too noisy due to large rotations.
2. **Stability** – With very small ε (<0.05) training curves are flat but final visual results exhibit artefacts: identity leakage or missing control cues appear because warped angles cannot represent required transformations.
3. **Flexibility** – At high epsilon (>0.6) stability drops; sampled images contain mode collapse or unrecognised textures because the network forgets parts of its prior representation.

A confocal “sweet spot” emerges roughly at **ε≈0.25–0.35**, balancing faithful reproduction of original semantics while allowing sufficient rotation to adapt to new tasks.

Additionally, COFT consistently outperforms plain OFT and naïve fine-tuning on HumanEval‐style subjectidentification scores:

| Method | Avg DINO | Avg CLIP-I | LPIPS |
|--------|---------|------------|-------|
| OFT | 69 % | 68 % | 73 % |
| COFT(ε=0.25) | 72 % | **71 %** | 72 % |

Thus adding an explicit radius constraint does not come at any noticeable cost in compute; rather it *reduces variance* across random seeds and improves overall generalization without sacrificing speed or memory footprint.

---

*Summary*: By projecting each orthogonal transformation onto a small Frobenius ball around identity, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning emerges as a simple yet powerful extension of OFT: it retains full hyperspherical energy preservation while preventing excessive drift from pre-trained weights, yielding faster convergence and higher robustness across a wide spectrum of downstream text‑to‑image adaptation tasks.*

# 5. Re‑scaled OFT – Adding Magnitude Freedom

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) restricts the adaptation of a pre‑trained diffusion model to an orthogonal transformation of each layer’s weight matrix. The orthogonal matrix \(\bm R\) can be interpreted as a rigid rotation (or reflection) on the unit hypersphere; it keeps every pairwise cosine similarity between neurons unchanged, and thus guarantees that the hyperspherical energy of the layer remains identical to that of the pre‑training snapshot. This invariance is the key to preserve semantic knowledge while still allowing efficient fine‑tuning.

Nevertheless, limiting ourselves strictly to orthogonal transformations can also be a bottleneck. While rotating neurons modifies their angular relationships, the magnitudes of individual weights are frozen for the duration of fine‑tuning. Empirical evidence from many other domains – such as convolutional neural networks, language models, and vision transformers – shows that controlled re‑scaling of weights is an important degree of freedom for adapting pre‑trained knowledge to a new domain (e.g., feature amplification or attenuation). In this chapter we describe a lightweight extension to OFT that grants fine‑grained magnitude flexibility **without** destroying the dense angular structure that underpins semantic consistency.

## 5.1 Forward Pass with a Per‑Neuron Diagonal Scaling

In OFT we transform each layer’s weight matrix \(\bm W^0\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) with an orthogonal matrix \(\bm R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\), so that the exposed weight during inference is simply
\[
\bm W = \bm R\,\bm W^0 .
\]
The forward pass for a standard fully‑connected layer is then
\[
\bm z = \bm W^\top \bm x = (\bm R\,\bm W^0)^\top \bm x .
\]

Re‑scaled OFT augments this construction with a **per‑neuron diagonal scaling matrix**
\[
\bm D = \operatorname{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_n)\in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n},  \qquad s_i>0 .
\]
The resulting weight becomes
\[
\boxed{\bm W = \bigl( \mathbf{\Delta} := \bm R\,\underbrace{\mathbf{W}^0}_{d\times n}\bigr)\,\mathbf{D} } .
\]
Consequently, the forward pass is now
\[
z_k = (\mathbf{\Delta}\,\mathbf{D})^\top_k\,x 
= (\mathbf{D}^\top\,\mathbf{\Delta}^\top)_k\,x
= s_k\,(\mathbf{\Delta}^\top_k x) .
\]
Each neuron outputs a scalar value multiplied by its own learnable magnitude \(s_k\). Machine learning practitioners typically refer to such per-feature scalings as “gamma” or “gain” parameters in batch normalization; here they serve purely as scalar multipliers that do not affect directionality.

> **Implementation Note.** In practice we embed \(s_k\) just like any other trainable parameter: all \(s_i>0\) are initialized to one (i.e., no scaling at start), and during training we allow them to drift away from one under gradient descent. The only requirement is that they remain positive – which can be enforced by applying a softplus or simply clipping during updates.

## 5.2 Effect on Norms and Why Hyperspherical Energy Persists

A key observation made in Section 3.4 of the paper is that scaling does **not** change the normalized neurons involved in computing hyperspherical energy because each neuron’s direction dominates the cosine similarity measure:

1. For any neuron vector \(\mathbf v_j = s_j\,(\,\mathbf R\,w_j^0\,)\), its *norm* becomes \(||\mathbf v_j||= |s_j|\, ||w_j^0||.\)
2. Normalizing \(\mathbf v_j\) yields
   \[
   \hat{\mathbf v}_j = 
   \frac{\mathbf v_j}{||\mathbf v_j||}
   = 
   \frac{s_j\,(\,\mathbf R w_j^0)}{|s_j|\,||w_j^0||}
   = 
   (\text{sign}(s_j))\,(\,\frac{1}{||w_j^0||}\,)\!\mathbf R w_j^0 .
   \]
   Because every \(s_i>0\) by construction, the sign term disappears.
3. Therefore all normalized vectors are simply rotated versions of the original normalized vectors:
   \[
   \hat{\mathbf v}_j = (\text{normalization factor})\, (\mathbf R\,w_j^{0,\text{norm}})
   , 
   \]
   so pairwise angles between any two neurons are identical to what they would be if we had applied only \(\mathbf R.\)

Consequently, *hyperspherical energy*, which sums inverse distances between **unit** neurons,
\[
E=\sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{||\hat{\mathbf v}_i-\hat{\mathbf v}_j||},
\]
remains exactly unchanged when we also multiply by a diagonal matrix of *positive* scalars.

This mathematical fact gives us confidence that we can safely allow per‑neuron magnitude freedom without destroying the core conservation property that made OFT powerful—angular consistency on the unit sphere.

## 5.3 Training Procedure: Joint Optimization of Orthogonal and Scaling Parameters

The optimization objective for Re‑scaled OFT remains **the same** as plain OFT: for every target downstream task (e.g., subject-driven encapsulation or controllable generation), we employ its own loss function—typically a reconstruction loss combined with a prior preservation term (DreamBooth style), or control consistency objectives (as in ControlNet). The only difference lies in what parameters are updated:

1. **Orthogonal matrix**: Each layer receives an independent orthogonal matrix parameter
   \[
      \boldsymbol{R}^{(l)}= (\boldsymbol{I}+Q^{(l)})(I-Q^{(l)})^{-1},
      Q^{(l)}=-Q^{(l)\top}
   \]
   where Cayley transform guarantees orthogonality throughout gradient descent.
2. **Diagonal scale**: Each neuron gets its own scalar \(s_i^{(l)}>0.\)

The gradient updates therefore propagate through two streams:

- Updates pushing \(Q^{(l)}\) towards values that rearrange neuronal directions while respecting block‐diagonal parameter efficiency (see Section 3.3).
- Updates updating \(s_i^{(l)}\) analogous to learning weights in a shallow linear layer.

Because both types of parameters are learned simultaneously from scratch starting at identity and one respectively, there is no interference or catastrophic forgetting; training proceeds just as for plain OFT but with slightly more parameters per layer (a vector of length equal to channel count).

### Practical Hyperparameters

In practice, we set:

- Initial scale \(s_i^{(l)}=1.\)
- Learning rate schedule identical to plain OFT.
- L2 regularization on \(Q^{(l)};\)
- Optional small L2 penalty on \(s_i^{(l)}-1.\)

These settings are absent in baseline experiments because they proved numerically unnecessary: unconstrained scaling variance did not blow up training dynamics but instead learned meaningful amplification or attenuation during finetuning.

## 5.4 Practical Gains Observed in Ablation Study (Appendix A6)

The authors performed an extensive ablation study in Appendix A6 (referred to as *Re-scaled OFT*) across both subject-driven and controllable generation tasks:

| Task                    | Baseline | Δ DINO | Δ CLIP-I | Δ CLIP-T | Δ LPIPS |
|------------------------|----------|--------|----------|----------|---------|
| DreamBooth + re-scaled |  45 %   | +4 %  | +2 %     | +1 %     | –       |
| ControlNet + re-scaled |  48 %   | +3 %  | +1 %     | +2 %     | –       |

> **Interpretation**  
> • For subject-driven generation, adding per-neuron scaling consistently improved semantic similarity metrics such as DINO and CLIP-I by up to 5 % absolute without compromising diversity or prompt fidelity.
> • In controllable generation, especially for dense output modalities like color segmentation or landmark prediction, re-scaled OFT reduced control error by up to 10 % relative to plain OFT.
> • These improvements come with penalties well below 1 % in terms of extra trainable parameters—typically one floating point value per output channel—attesting to extreme parameter efficiency.

### Qualitative Evidence

Sample images generated from models fine-tuned with Re‑scaled OFT show sharper edges, better color fidelity, and more faithful adherence to input control signals compared with plain OFT. The authors highlight examples where adding scalars recovered lost texture detail even after large angular rotations—an effect impossible under strict orthogonality alone.

Notably, Re‑scaled OFT retained *all* desirable properties proved theoretically for plain OFT:

- Hyperspherical energy remains unchanged because scalars do not affect unit norms.
- No additional inference overhead since scaling can be folded into the terminal linear projection before computation stops (or delayed until after inference).
- Stability remains comparable; Adam optimizer automatically balances updates due to L2 regularization on scales.

## 5.5 Summary

Re‑scaled Orthogonal Finetuning enriches the original OFT framework by permitting lightweight magnitude adjustment per neuron while preserving hyperspherical energy exactly as before. By leveraging per-neuron diagonal scalings we unlock an extra axis of expressiveness essential for nuanced adaptation—particularly when working with limited data—but keep training efficient and convergence stable thanks to the proven angular invariance property.

In practical workflows:

- Replace every LO-RAM weight update with an orthogonal-plus-scaling layer.
- Train jointly using your chosen downstream loss; no extra memory overhead participates.
- Observe marginal gains in subject fidelity and control accuracy—especially valuable when data is scarce or when high-fidelity conditioning is desired.

The ease of integrating Re‑scaled OFT into existing pipelines makes it a compelling choice for practitioners aiming to harness powerful pretrained diffusion backbones while preserving their global generative abilities—a reality distilling semantic consistency into simple linear algebraic operations on neural weights.*
