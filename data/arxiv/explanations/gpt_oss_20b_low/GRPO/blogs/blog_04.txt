\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): A Memory‑Efficient RL Breakthrough for Math LLMs  

Mathematics remains one of the toughest areas for large language models.  Even when an open‑source model reaches the 50 % mark on the competition‑level MATH benchmark or handles most GSM8K questions, a new frontier has emerged: **reinforcement learning (RL)**.  Traditional RL methods for LLMs, like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), bring heavyweight memory demands because they require a separate *critic* to estimate the value of every token.  The recently released *Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)* sidesteps this bottleneck and delivers measurable gains on two of the biggest math benchmarks while making RL practical for 7 B‑parameter models.

---

## Why RL Matters for Math LLMs  

1. **Fine‑grained supervision** – Pure supervised fine‑tuning uses only labeled examples, but many mathematical problems can be answered with multiple plausible steps.  
2. **Reward consistency** – RL can align a model’s outputs with a reward model trained on comparisons (“Answer A is better than Answer B”).  
3. **Generalization to hard cases** – When the training data does not cover every type of problem, RL can push the policy toward stronger solutions without needing additional human annotations.

After training DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B—an instruction‑tuned base with deep mathematical reasoning—researchers applied PPO and saw noticeable improvements.  However, PPO’s *actor‑critic* architecture doubled GPU memory usage and slowed training by an order of magnitude.

---

## The Core Idea Behind GRPO  

> **Replace the critic with a group‑based baseline**.

Instead of learning to predict a value function \(V_\psi\) for each token (the classic actor‑critic trick), GRPO samples *multiple* outputs per question and uses their average reward as a baseline:

- For a question \(q\), sample \(G\) outputs \(o_1,\dots,o_G\) from the current policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  
- Compute rewards \(r_1,\dots,r_G\) using a trained reward model \(r_\varphi\).  
- Normalize these rewards by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the standard deviation.  
- The *advantage* at every token in an output is simply this normalized reward:
  \[
  \hat{A}_{i,t}= \widetilde{r}_i
  \]
  where \(\widetilde{r}_i=\frac{r_i-\text{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\text{std}(\mathbf r)}\).

The policy optimizer then maximizes the PPO surrogate objective using \(\hat{A}_{i,t}\) instead of value‑based advantages.  This eliminates the need to train and store a separate critic network.

---

## How GRPO Cuts Memory Overhead  

| Feature | PPO | GRPO |
|---------|-----|-------|
| Critic? | Yes (≈ model size) | No |
| Baseline estimation | Learned \(V_\psi\) per token | Group mean over > 64 outputs |
| GPU memory | Roughly double that required for pure policy updates | Same as pure policy updates |
| Training time per step | Longer (back‑prop through critic) | Faster (one forward pass per token) |

With GRPO, a batch that would have required ~16 GB just for its critic now fits comfortably on a single A100 GPU, enabling researchers to run multi–turn RL training cycles on models as large as DeepSeekMath-RL 7B.

## Concrete Gains on Algebraic Benchmarks  

The paper’s evaluation data show:

| Model        | GSM8K (CoT %) | MATH (CoT %) |
|--------------|---------------|--------------|
| DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B | 82.9 %  | 46.8 %   |
| DeepSeekMath‑RL 7B (GRPO)      | **88.2 %** (+5.3 pp) | **51.7 %** (+4.9 pp) |

Those numbers mean that *every* problem instance in GSM8K has more than five times higher chance of hitting the correct solution after just one round of GRPO RL fine‑tuning, while MATH sees an absolute double‑digit jump—no small feat given how many other open‑source models plateau around 40 %.

Because GRPO blocks overfitting to any particular answer format (the group baseline naturally penalizes wildly incorrect responses while rewarding subtle improvements), checkpoints remain robust when you try out *process supervision*—rewarding each reasoning step instead of only final answers—and still climb higher on out‑of‑domain tasks like CMATH or Gaokao question sets.

---

## How “Group” Makes it Work

You might wonder: why does averaging over dozens of outputs give a reliable baseline? Two key properties:

1. **Comparability** – The reward model is trained on pairwise comparisons of outputs for the same question; thus its scores already form a relative ranking rather than absolute scores.
2. **Variance reduction** – Subtracting the mean eliminates common bias; dividing by the standard deviation cancels out scale effects, turning raw rewards into standardized advantage signals that fit PPO’s clipping mechanism nicely.

Mathematically this is captured in Equation (6) from the paper’s Appendix:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{GRPO}(\theta)=\mathbb E_{q,\{o_i\}}\Bigg[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G
    \min\!\Bigg(
          \frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}\,
               \hat A_{i,t},
          \operatorname{clip}\!\big(...,\varepsilon\big)\,
               \hat A_{i,t}
    \Bigg)
    -\beta\, D_{KL}\!\big[\pi_\theta\,||\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\big]\Bigg].
\]

The only difference from classic PPO is that there's no learned value function \(V_\psi\); everything else pulls from sampling itself.

## Practical Tips for Implementing GRPO

1. **Select an appropriate group size** – Experiments used \(G=64\). Too few outputs yield noisy baselines; too many blow up compute without proportional gain.
2. **Use self‑consistency during sampling** – Sample diverse “chains” by varying temperature or nucleus cutoffs; this improves exploration.
3. **Update your reward model incrementally** – After each RL epoch, retrain/replay your preference model on freshly visited trajectories; otherwise it becomes stale.
4. **Add KL regularization explicitly** – In contrast to PPO’s implicit penalty inside rewards, GRPO plugs β⋅KL into the objective directly (Equation (6)), which is easier to tune and avoids reward signal corruption.

Following these steps keeps training stable even when you start from a heavily tuned instruction base like DeepSeekMath-Instruct.

## What Comes Next?

The paper lists several future directions that promise even more effective reinforcement learning:

- **Advanced sampling & decoding strategies** (e.g., tree search) to produce higher–quality trajectories without exploding GPU footprints.
- **Noisy‐reward robust algorithms** that can tolerate imperfect preference data—essential when scaling beyond next‐generation benchmarks.
- **Process reward models** that assess intermediate reasoning steps instead of only final answers, opening doors to truly stepwise improvement.

But even at its current form, GRPO proves that careful algorithmic simplification can unlock new frontiers: strong math reasoning at affordable compute cost.

---

## Takeaway

If you’re building or fine–tuning math-focused LLMs on commodity GPU infrastructure:

- Replace PPO by GRPO: drop half your memory budget and keep your policy updates streamlined.
- Expect +5–10 % absolute accuracy boosts on GSM8K and MATH with only a single RL loop.
- Scale up your training set gradually; even off‑distribution problems benefit from strengthened reward signals.

GRPO shows that deep math reasoning isn’t just about adding more parameters or fancy architected tokens—it’s also about smarter training procedures that let us train powerful models without choking our GPUs.

Happy training!
