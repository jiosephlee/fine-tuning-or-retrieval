\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: 2. The DeepSeekMath Pipeline Overview

DeepSeekMath is a research‑grade language model engineered specifically for mathematical reasoning. Its training pipeline is carefully sculpted: starting from a very large, high‑quality corpus of mathematical web pages, proceeding through targeted instruction tuning that teaches the model how to explain its reasoning, and finishing with a lightweight reinforcement‑learning algorithm that polishes the model’s behavior in practice. This chapter walks through each of those stages, explaining *why* they exist and *how* they interact to produce a model that scores competitively with state‑of‑the‑art proprietary systems.

---

## Pre‑training with the DeepSeekMath Corpus

### Why pre‑training matters in mathematical reasoning

Mathematics demands a precise, structured form of language—exact symbols, consistent notation, logical dependencies across many tokens. Classic language models trained on generic natural‑language data (e.g., books, news articles) rarely see enough of such patterns to learn them organically. Therefore, the authors first build a **domain‑specific corpus** that contains much larger *density* and *variability* of mathematical content. By feeding this data to the model during pre‑training, the parameters learn the statistical regularities of equations, number sequences, proof sketches, and even informal discussion about math that appears on public web pages.

### Constructing the DeepSeekMath Corpus

The corpus originates from Common Crawl (CC), which archives every publicly accessible page worldwide on a quarterly basis. Although CC is vast—hundreds of billions of tokens—the majority are irrelevant to mathematics: most pages are e-commerce sites or blog posts without any math. To sieve out truly math‑heavy content:

1. **Seed with OpenWebMath** – An existing curated set of high‑quality math web texts (≈13 B tokens).  
2. **Train a fastText classifier** – Using 500 K positive samples from OpenWebMath and 500 K negative samples chosen at random from CC, the authors train an efficient text classifier that scores how *math‑like* a page is.
3. **Iterate** – Run the classifier over deduplicated CC (≈40 B pages), keep only the highest scoring ones, then re‑annotate many newly found math domains (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`, `projecteuler.net`). With each iteration more diverse positives are added, tightening the classifier’s vocabulary.
4. **Stop when saturated** – After four iterations the authors exhaust ~98 % of discoverable math content and stop further crawling.

This yields **120 B tokens** in total—about seven times larger than Minerva’s math data and nine times larger than OpenWebMath’s—spanning dozens of languages but dominated by English and Chinese.

### Decontamination strategy

Benchmarks like GSM8K or MATH contain publicly available problems written by humans—if any overlap exists between these problem sets and the pre‑training data, evaluation would be unfairly inflated. Therefore:

- A sliding window (“10‑gram”) matcher removes any page whose text contains an exact copy of ≥10 consecutive tokens from any benchmark problem.
- For shorter segments (≤10 gams) but ≥3 grams they use exact string matching.
  
This guarantees that no direct plagiarism leaks into training.

### Effect on downstream performance

The paper reports training several 1.3 B parameter models on different datasets:

| Dataset | Tokens | Best metric on GSM8K / MATH |
|---------|--------|------------------------------|
| MathPile | 8.9 B | 48 % / 15 % |
| OpenWebMath | 13.6 B | 50 % / 18 % |
| Proof-Pile-2 | ~130 B (including arXiv) | **58 % / 30 %** |
| DeepSeekMath Corpus | 120 B | **61 % / 36 %** |

The improvement shows two things:
1. **Quality matters more than sheer size** – The authors’ fine–tuned classifier filters out noisy pages; irrelevant arXiv LaTeX snippets are largely removed.
2. **Multilingual diversity helps** – The corpus includes Chinese math content; models trained on English–only corpora actually degrade on Chinese benchmarks.

## Instruction Tuning (SFT) with CoT & PoT Data

### From “learned patterns” to “explainable reasoning”

Pre‑training alone configures a strong baseline flavor but does not teach the model to format solutions logically. For mathematics we need two properties:

1. **Chain-of-Thought (CoT)** – A step‑by‑step textual reasoning narrative that acts as both an intermediate representation and self–check.
2. **Program-of-Thought (PoT)** – A programmatic reasoning trace (typically Python snippets) that can rely on external libraries (`math`, `sympy`, etc.) to offload heavy computation.

The authors curate **776 k instruction pairs**, each consisting of a prompt and an answer in one of these three forms:

- **English**: Chain-of-thought explanations taken from annotated datasets such as MathInstruct; PoT solutions derived from HumanEval style programs; tool–integrated solutions where a program is executed-live and its output verbalized.
- **Chinese**: Handcrafted K–12 problems covering linear equations through calculus; solutions annotated with CoT or tool integration by bilingual experts.

These are fed into supervised fine–tuning with mean–squared weight decay \(5 \times 10^{-5}\), batch size 256, over only 500 gradient steps—a tiny fraction relative to pre‑training but rich in teacher signals.

### Concrete gains

With SFT alone `DeepSeekMath-Instruct` achieves:

| Benchmark | Accuracy (CoT) |
|-----------|----------------|
| GSM8K     | 82.6 %         |
| MATH      | 46.8 %         |
| CMATH     | 84.6 %         |

Notice how both numeric reasoning at grade school level (`GSM8K`) and college level (`MATH`) improve markedly over BERT-like baselines.

### Why CoT & PoT help

- **Attention cues**: Inserting intermediate tokens forces the model to allocate attention layers to structured slots (“Step 1”, “Compute…”) rather than packing everything together.
- **Curriculum easing**: Guided students style feedback creates implicit supervised rewards for each correct step or execution trace.
- **Tool integration bridging**: PoT uncovers a latent preference for leveraging code when arithmetic or algebra becomes messy.

## Reinforcement Learning using GRPO

### The insufficiency of pure SFT

Although SFT brings decent performance, it pinpoints one weakness: there is no *feedback loop* beyond dataset labels; errors can propagate unchecked inside longer chains if initial steps misleads subsequent tokens.

Hence reinforcement learning is used after SFT—fine‐tuning where outputs are *evaluated* and *reinforced* based on learned reward models.

### Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in LLMs

PPO turns policy gradients into a stable “clipped” objective:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb E \big[
\min (
\rho_t A_t,
\text{clip}(\rho_t ,1-\varepsilon ,1+\varepsilon ) A_t
)\big]
-\beta \,\text{KL}(\pi_\theta || \pi_{\text{ref}})
\]

where \(\rho_t=\frac{\pi_\theta(o_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(o_t)}\) is the likelihood ratio per token, \(A_t\) uses a learned value function \(V_\psi\), and \(\beta\) penalizes divergence from an older “reference” policy to keep changes conservative.

But PPO needs an extra critic network \(V_\psi\)—another massive model—that consumes GPU memory, delays training speed, and may distort gradients when only final token rewards exist (as is typical for math problems).

### Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

GRPO intelligently sidesteps the critic by reusing *group scores* as baselines:

1. For each question \(q\), sample \(G\) outputs \(\{o_i\}\) from \(\pi_{\text{old}}\).
2. Run a reward model \(r_\varphi(q,o_i)\). Reward might be judged “correct or not” or graded by degree of correctness.
3. Compute *relative advantage* per output:
   
   \[
   \hat A_{i,t}
   =
   \frac{r_i-\bar r}{\sigma}
   \]
   
   where \(\bar r\) is mean reward across group outputs and \(\sigma\) their standard deviation.
4. Use these per-token advantage values inside PPO’s clipped objective (modified slightly), enabling **no critic** time’s calculation:

   \[
   \mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
   =
   \mathbb E
   [
   \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G
   \min (
   \rho_{i,t} \hat A_{i,t}, 
   \text{clip}(\rho_{i,t},1-\varepsilon ,1+\varepsilon)\hat A_{i,t}
   )
   - 
   \beta\, D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta || \pi_{\text{ref}})
   ]
   \]

5. Regularize directly by adding KL divergence between current policy and reference policy rather than embedding it into rewards—this reduces variance in advantage estimates.

Because GRPO reuses actual rewards from multiple outputs for baseline estimation rather than modeling expected values via an extra network, memory usage drops dramatically while maintaining comparable performance boosts over PPO.

### Outcome vs Process Supervision

- **Outcome supervision** assigns support only at final token; every token gets same advantage equal to normalized final reward.
- **Process supervision**, more fine-grained process reward model gives reward after each sub-step (“Step k”), encouraging correct reasoning sequences midair; advantages become cumulative sums of future step rewards which help the model learn which partial states lead toward correct solutions.

Both variants are explored; GRPO + Process Supervision typically shines on harder benchmarks like MATH in non‐tool scenarios because it teaches multi‑step logic explicitly.

### Iterative RL Procedure

Because RL policies evolve during training, earlier reward models become stale quickly:

1. Sample new trajectories using current policy; store their scores under current reward model.
2. Periodically retrain reward model with fresh samples plus some rehearsal buffer (~10 % old demonstrations).
3. Update reference policy to current policy after each RL round — ensures KL penalty penalizes drift properly.

Thus the RL pipeline becomes *self improving*: better rewards lead to stronger policies that think even better—creating positive feedback loops ignored in pure SFT.

## Unified Training Paradigm for SFT/ RL

The paper proposes considering all training approaches under one mathematically coherent framework:

1. **Data source \(D\)**: Whether responses come from an unbiased offline collection or online sampling drawn from current policy dynamics (`online` vs `offline`).
2. **Reward function \(R(q,o)\)**: Determined either by a hard predicate rule (“answer matches ground truth?”) or through a neural reward model trained on pairwise preferences.
3. **Algorithm Λ(·)**: The procedure converting observations + rewards into gradient terms (`SFT`, `RFT`, `DPO`, `PPO`, `GRPO`).

Mathematically this yields:
\[ 
\nabla_\theta J
=
\E_{(q,o)\sim D}
[\, GC(q,o;t,R)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_t|q,o_{<t})\,] .
\]

Key insights arise:

- Algorithms that draw new examples online (GRPO/Online RFT) outperform those constrained to offline sets because they expose novel failures timely.
- Algorithms adopting continuous relative advantages (GRPO + Process) outperform those applying flat binary adjustments because they can shape gradients according to magnitude—a closer mimic of human instructor emphasis.
- Rewards produced by neural preferences dominate hard rules in high-dimensional neural spaces; their gradients provide more nuanced direction signals per token rather than just end reward binary signals used in RFT binaries penalization passes.

Overall this unified view explains why GRPO — blending online sampling with relative advantage scaling — pushes DeepSeekMath-RL beyond its already high base score stemming from SFT alone.



---  
In summary, DeepSeekMath’s pipeline begins by harvesting mathematically useful language in depth via homemade classifiers; it leverages synthetic instruction data teaching chain-of-thought logic; then refines it via lightweight yet data‐efficient RL and elegant gradient tricks rooted in reinforcement learning theory yet adapted for LLMs’ unique dynamics.</p>
