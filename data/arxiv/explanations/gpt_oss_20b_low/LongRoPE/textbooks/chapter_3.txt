Chapter 3: 3. Existing Positional Interpolation Strategies

Large language models (LLMs) embed position information through *rotary position‑embedding* (RoPE) in order to encode the sequential order of input tokens. Extending the context window of a pre‑trained LLM requires extending the effective span of its positional encoding without retraining the entire model from scratch. Several interpolation strategies have been proposed to achieve this, each dengan varying degree of success and underlying assumptions. In this chapter we review three representative approaches—Linear Positional Interpolation (PI), Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)‑based interpolation, and YaRN’s frequency‑grouped technique—highlighting both their motivations and limitations.

## Linear Positional Interpolation (PI) and Its Crowding Problem

Linear PI is perhaps the simplest method to extend RoPE when scaling a model from an original context length $L$ to an enlarged length $L'$. It is defined by a single rescaling factor $\lambda$, chosen as the extension ratio $s = \frac{L'}{L}$. The rotary‐angle for each dimension $i$ and token position $n$ is reduced linearly:

\[
\cos \!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda\,\beta^{i}}\right),
\qquad
\sin \!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda\,\beta^{i}}\right),
\]

where $\beta = \theta^{2/d}$ and $\theta = 10\,000$ is the default base used in RoPE implementations. In practice, PI sets $\lambda = s$ across **all** RoPE dimensions, meaning every frequency component is uniformly contracted by the same factor.

The intuition behind PI is straightforward: new token positions beyond $L$ are “compressed” onto the interval that the pre‑trained model has seen during training. However, this blanket contraction causes two intertwined problems:

1. **Crowding of positional information** – Positions that were previously widely spaced become densely clustered once compressed, impeding the model’s ability to distinguish tokens that are close together in space.
2. **Loss of high–frequency details** – Lower RoPE dimensions encode higher frequencies; uniform scaling down disproportionately erases fine‐grained positional cues that are critical for tasks requiring precise alignment.

Empirical evidence shows that while PI allows modest extensions (typically up to $2\times$), perplexity rises sharply beyond this limit because the model cannot adequately recover temporal relationships among newly inserted positions.

## NTK‑Based Interpolation/Extrapolation Theory and Limitations

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) framework models training dynamics of deep neural networks by linearising them around initial parameters; it has been applied to understand repurposing RoPE frequencies for longer contexts. NTK theory suggests that different RoPE dimensions have varying sensitivities: lower indices ($i$ small) correspond to higher angular frequencies and should be interpolated less aggressively than higher indices ($i$ large). Accordingly, NTK‐based interpolation proposes a dimension‑dependent scaling:

\[
\lambda_{i} = s^{f(i)},
\]

with an exponent $f(i)$ that typically decreases as $i$ grows, thereby applying *extrapolation* to high dimensions while *interpolating* more aggressively on low dimensions. Dynamic NTK variants further adapt $\lambda_i$ per token position so that positions further into a sequence receive larger extensions.

This strategy mitigates some crowding but depends on analytic conjectures about kernel behaviour rather than empirical optimisation of each dimension’s scaling factor. Consequently:

* **Limited extension ratios** – In practice, dynamic NTK methods cap extension ratios at roughly $4\times$. Beyond this, new positions become too distant in representation space, leading again to severe performance degradation.
* **Rigid functional form** – The fixed power‑law relationship between $s$ and $\lambda_{i}$ restricts flexibility; if an LLM’s spectrum behaves differently from theoretical assumptions, performance suffers.

Thus, while NTK improves upon plain PI by acknowledging dimensional heterogeneity, it still imposes strong prior constraints that can hamper optimal adaptation for very long context windows.

## YaRN’s Frequency‑Grouped Approach and Dependency on Hand‑Crafted Thresholds

YaRN (Yet another Rotary Position Embedding) refines the interpolation scheme by explicitly partitioning RoPE dimensions into three frequency bands:

1. **High‑frequency band** – Low indices; these are left *unaltered*, i.e., $\lambda_{i}=1$, preserving sharp positional detail.
2. **Mid‑frequency band** – Middle indices; these receive NTK‐style interpolation ($\lambda_i=s^f$).
3. **Low‑frequency band** – High indices; these are set with *extrapolation* ($\lambda_i=1$ or slightly larger), allowing them to span longer distances without compression.

The grouping thresholds are derived empirically by human designers based on visual inspection or heuristic tuning per LLM architecture. This heuristic grouping allows YaRN to reconcile both crowding mitigation and frequency preservation more flexibly than pure PI or NTK approaches.

However, YaRN brings its own drawbacks:

* **Human‑guided design choices** – The need to manually select group boundaries introduces brittleness: a suboptimal threshold may inhibit learning in certain frequency ranges.
* **Limited extrapolation range** – Even with judicious grouping it still fails to support extensions beyond $\sim 4\times$, as evidenced when evaluating YaRN on simulated 8k passages in LLaMA2: perplexity spikes midway through the extended window.
* **Inconsistency across architectures** – Different base models (LLaMA2 vs Mistral) exhibit distinct spectral properties; a single set of group thresholds may not generalise, forcing re‑tuning per architecture.

In summary, while YaRN demonstrates superior performance over earlier techniques up to moderate extension factors (\(s \le 8\)), its effectiveness diminishes when attempting dramatically longer contexts due to reliance on hand-crafted frequency groups and limited extrapolation capacity.

---

These three strategies illuminate distinct trade‑offs in extending RoPE: simplicity vs precision (PI), theoretically motivated dimension sensitivity (NTK), and pragmatic yet architecture‑specific grouping (YaRN). Their limitations—crowding, stringent extrapolation limits, and hand‑crafted hyperparameters—motivate newer methods such as LongRoPE that seek systematic optimisation over both dimensional and positional non‑uniformities without excessive tuning burden.

# Chapter 4: Non‑Uniformity in Positional Interpolation

Large language models (LLMs) have become indispensable tools for natural language processing, yet their impressive performance is often constrained by the size of the input context they can handle.  When a model encounters token positions beyond the range it has seen during pre‑training, its positional representations become out‑of‑distribution, leading to catastrophic degradation in downstream performance.  A popular solution has been to extend the *RoPE* (rotary positional embedding) used in Transformer attention layers, either by interpolating or extrapolating the rotary angles so that new token positions can be represented in the same coordinate space as the original ones.  Previous works such as PI (Linear Positional Interpolation), NTK‑based extrapolation, and YaRN have explored different ways of adjusting these angles, often relying on simple global scaling factors or hand‑crafted groupings of frequencies.  However, when we push beyond a few hundred thousand tokens—the realm where most LLMs have never been tested—a new set of challenges emerges: more than ninety percent of the seen positions are entirely novel, and naive scaling causes severe “crowding” of positional information.

Empirical investigation revealed that two distinct non‑uniformities exist in RoPE that, if properly addressed, can dramatically improve long‑context performance both with and without fine‑tuning.  This chapter presents a systematic study that uncovers these sources of non‑uniformity and shows how evolutionary search can discover near‐optimal interpolation schemes tailored to each RoPE dimension and for initial token positions.

---

## Evidence from Evolutionary Search Experiments

To probe whether the current rule‑based methods (PI, NTK, YaRN) fully exhaust the expressive power of RoPE interpolation, we carried out an evolutionary search that jointly optimizes all rotary scale factors and an initial–token cutoff threshold.  The objective function was perplexity on a handful of long documents; we used five random PG19 validation samples totaling roughly five million tokens. The search space consisted of:

1. **Per‐dimension scale factors** \(\lambda_i\)—one scalar per RoPE dimension \(i = 0,\dots,d/2-1\).  
2. **Initial‐token count** \(\hat{n}\)—the number of first tokens whose rotex angles remain unmodified.

Unlike earlier work that assumed all dimensions share a single factor or that grouped them into few frequency bands, we treated each dimension independently and allowed \(\lambda_i\) to range from \(1.0\) (pure extrapolation) up to \(s\times1.25\), where \(s= L'/L\) is the target extension ratio. The offset \(\hat{n}\) was searched over \(\{0,1,2,\dots,256\}\).

The evolutionary procedure started with a population of 64 candidates that included known solutions (PI, NTK, YaRN). In each iteration we evaluated perplexity for every candidate (subject to a **monotonicity constraint**: \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\)), then selected the top-left individuals as parents for mutation and crossover. This constraint was motivated by Neural Tangent Kernel theory, which predicts that higher RoPE frequencies (lower indices) should interpolate less than lower frequencies (higher indices). The search ran for 40 generations; typically it converged within eight to ten iterations.

Three striking results emerged:

* **Non‑uniform scaling outperforms uniform approaches**: The best candidates achieved perplexities twenty–thirty percent lower than PI at every window size up to sixteen thousand tokens.  
* **Large extensions are still feasible**: Even after nine generations an eightfold extension (4k → 32k tokens) was achieved without any fine‑tuning.  
* **Fine‑tuning benefits are amplified**: When we fine‑tuned the best candidate on extremely long documents (up to 64k tokens), its perplexity dropped significantly—again outperforming uniform baselines even after thousands of fine‑tuning steps.

These observations motivate two formal findings about non‑uniformities in RoPE.

---

## Finding 1: RoPE Dimensions Need Dimension‑Specific Scaling Factors

RoPE embeds position by applying sinusoidal rotations whose frequencies decrease exponentially with dimensional index:

\[
\mathrm{RoPE}_{i}(n)=
\Bigl[\cos(n\,\theta_i),\, \sin(n\,\theta_i)\Bigr],\qquad 
\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}, 
\]

with \(\theta=10^4\). Thus each pair of dimensions acts as a distinct rotating basis vector; lower indices correspond to higher angular frequencies while higher indices rotate more slowly.

In PI one scales every frequency by the same factor \(s^{-1}\); in NTK one applies powers of \(i\); YaRN lumps dimensions into three frequency groups and applies group‑specific rules. None of these respects the exact exponential decay in frequency; consequently many high‐frequency pairs end up heavily compressed while low‐frequency pairs may be over‐stretched or under‐represented.

The evolutionary search discovered that assigning *unique* scale factors per dimension aligns better with both the model’s learned geometry and downstream task objectives:

| Dimension index | Search optimum \(s/\lambda_i\) | Interpretation |
|-----------------|-------------------------------|----------------|
| 0–7             | ~\(s^{0.3}\) | Preserve high frequencies |
| 8–15            | ~\(s^{0.5}\) | Moderate interpolation |
| 16+             | ~\(s^{0.8}\) | Allow more compression |

These values differ from PI’s flat \(s^{-1}\), NTK’s linear trend (\(i^{-\alpha}\)), and YaRN’s stepwise bands. Crucially because each dimension now receives a tailored shift, **the overall aggregate rotation angle across all dimensions retains richer phase coverage over longer sequences**, which translates directly into lower perplexity at large window sizes.

---

## Finding 2: Initial Tokens Benefit from Reduced Interpolation

A sequence’s beginning tokens receive larger attention scores because they appear early in causal attention patterns and because subsequent layers often marshal contextual information back toward these tokens. Consequently they play an outsized role during language modeling.

The evolutionary run explored varying initial–token counts (\(\hat{n}\)). When \(\hat{n}=0\), all positions were scaled uniformly by their respective \(\lambda_i\), reproducing PT or NTK behavior; when \(\hat{n}>0\), those first tokens kept their original RoPE angles regardless of length expansion.

Empirically:

- With **no pooling** (\(\hat{n}=0\)), perplexity increased sharply once the sequence exceeded twice its base length.
- Thresholding at **\(128\)** or **\(256\)** tokens yielded consistent perplexities across all extensions up to \(16k\).  
- For each target window size there exists an optimal \(\hat{n}\); e.g., at an eightfold expansion (\(4k→32k\)) retaining \(128\) starting tokens yielded the lowest perplexity among all candidates.

This phenomenon is attributable to *attention crowding*: when many new positions are collapsed into a narrow angular band, early tokens risk being rendered indistinguishable compared to later ones—leading to ambiguous self‑attention keys/values and noisy gradient signals during fine-tuning. By preserving their original phase information we give the transformer a stable anchor on which longer context can be built.

---

## Finding 3: Non‑Uniform Interpolation Boosts Both Fine‑Tuning & Non‑Fine-Tuning Performance

The combination of dimensionally varied scaling plus initial-token preservation yields two complementary benefits:

1. **Zero-load extension** – By choosing scale factors that already keep much radial information intact (e.g., higher dimensions less compressed), one can achieve large context gains (\(4k→32k\)) *without any additional training*. Perplexity curves for LLaMA2-7B demonstrate this sharp drop relative to PI or YaRN baseline at exactly these lengths.

2. **Effective fine-tuning initialization** – When we perform standard gradient-based fine-tuning on long documents (e.g., training LLaMA2-7B for 400 steps on \(64k+L'\)), starting from an interpolated state as discovered above yields significantly faster convergence and lower final loss than starting from either PI or NTK interpolated states. The zero-one difference is not marginal—on PG19 evaluation it is approx 8% relative improvement after just 600 steps.

Because our method preserves key high-frequency signal components—critical for modeling local syntax—and retains stable low-frequency trends—useful for global coherence—it strikes a balance between *information retention* and *novelty accommodation*. This explains why non-uniform interpolation supports long contexts even *on pre-trained models*, yet further benefits *fine-tuned* models when extending beyond pragmatic limits like one million tokens.

---

### Summary

Through systematic evolutionary search guided by perplexity on realistic long documents, we uncovered two essential non-uniformities in Rotary Position Embedding that have been largely ignored by prior works:

1. **Dimensional heterogeneity** – Each RoPE frequency pair should receive its own scaling factor when adapting to longer contexts.
2. **Token position priority** – The earliest tokens warrant unsaccaded angular embeddings so they maintain their informational primacy throughout extended sequences.

Exploiting both aspects simultaneously yields interpolation schemes capable of extending LLM context windows several times larger than existing methods without expensive fine-tuning—and simultaneously provides excellent initialization points for further adaptation with modest additional training effort. These insights form the foundation upon which LongRoPE builds its scalable framework for extending contextual horizons beyond two million tokens while preserving or even enhancing performance at standard window lengths.\
