\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 2: 2. Positional Encoding in Transformers

Transformers are permutation‑invariant neural networks.  Without an explicit notion of token order, the model would treat the input as a bag of tokens, which is inappropriate for language, time series, or any sequential data.  Positional encodings are therefore a cornerstone of modern transformer architectures: they inject absolute or relative position information into the token representations so that the self‑attention mechanism can weigh interactions according to the relative ordering of words.

This chapter gives a thorough review of positional encodings with an emphasis on **Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)**.  We will motivate the need for position information, explain how the classic sinusoidal approach works, and then derive RoPE from first principles.  We will also look at how RoPE is affected when a model’s context window is stretched beyond its training length, introducing the concept of an **extension ratio** and how it resizes the underlying rotary angles.

### 2.1 Purpose of Positional Information in Transformers

A vanilla transformer consists of stacked multi‑head self‑attention layers followed by feed‑forward networks and residual connections.  
Self‑attention computes a weighted sum over all tokens in a sequence:

\[
\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\text{softmax}\!\Bigl(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\Bigr)V ,
\]

where each query \(Q_i\), key \(K_j\), and value \(V_j\) is obtained by projecting the embedding vector of token \(i\) or \(j\).  
Because the attention function only correlates vectors that have already been projected from the embeddings, if all tokens are identical up to permutation then every query will see every key with equal probability.  There would be no way for the model to reason about which token appears before another or at what distance.

Positional encoding supplies this missing piece by adding or concatenating a vector that depends solely on the absolute or relative position \(n\) of each token:

- **Absolute encodings** (e.g., learned position embeddings) give each index its own distinct vector.
- **Relative encodings** (e.g., learned bias terms, sine–cosine patterns) capture differences between positions only.

Once position vectors are incorporated (typically by addition to token embeddings), self‑attention can differentiate between permutations because queries/keys become position‑aware.

### 2.2 Classic Sinusoidal Positional Encoding

The original transformer paper introduced a deterministic, non‑learnable sinusoidal positional encoding:

\[
\mathrm{PE}_{(n,2i)}   = \sin \!\bigl(n\,\theta_i\bigr), \qquad
\mathrm{PE}_{(n,2i+1)} = \cos \!\bigl(n\,\theta_i\bigr),
\]

with
\( \theta_i = 10000^{-\,2i/d}\),
where \(d\) is the embedding dimension and \(i \in [0,\tfrac{d}{2})\).

For each fixed dimension pair \((2i,2i+1)\), the two components trace a circle as \(n\) varies.  
The choice of base \(10\,000\) produces distinct frequencies across dimensions: higher‑index dimensions rotate faster (higher frequency) than lower‑index dimensions (lower frequency).  
This design has two appealing properties:

1. **Continuity**: Holding all other fields fixed, rotating around the circle produces smoothly varying values as positions increase.
2. **Predictability**: Because weights are not learned separately for positions, new positions beyond those seen during training can be handled consistently simply by extrapolating \(n\,\theta_i\).

Nonetheless, when we attempt to use such static encodings for sequences much longer than those seen during pre‑training—say from 4 k tokens to millions—extrapolation can lead to “crowded” positions where different tokens share nearly identical embeddings.

### 2.3 Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)

RoPE was introduced as a simple but powerful way to angle-warps queries and keys while preserving multiplicative rotational symmetry across dimensions.  
Instead of adding sinusoids directly to token embeddings, RoPE rotates each query/key pair in complex‐valued space:

1. Split each hidden vector into even/odd pairs:
   \[
   h = [h_0,\dots,h_{d-1}], \
   h_{(2i)} , h_{(2i+1)}.
   \]
2. For each pair apply a rotation matrix defined by angle \(\phi_{n,i}=n\,\theta_i.\)

The rotation matrix for dimension pair \(i\) is

\[
R_{n,i} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \cos(\phi_{n,i}) & -\,\sin(\phi_{n,i})\\[4pt]
        \sin(\phi_{n,i}) &      \cos(\phi_{n,i})
    \end{bmatrix}.
\]

Applying this rotation yields rotated queries
\(q'=R_{n,i}\,[q_{(2i)},q_{(2i+1)}]^{\top}\)
and rotated keys
\(k'=R_{m,i}\,[k_{(2i)},k_{(2i+1)}]^{\top}\)
for tokens at positions \(n,m.\)

Using these rotated vectors in attention preserves a crucial property:

- The dot product between rotated queries and keys equals
  \[
  q'\!^\top k'=
     q^\top k + 
     q_{\perp}^\top k_{\perp},
  \]
  where the dot product captures both content similarity (\(q^\top k\)) and relative positional distance since rotations introduce cross terms depending on \(\sin(\phi_n-\phi_m).\)

Consequently, RoPE effectively imposes “rotational” relative attention: the farther apart two tokens are in position space, the more their rotated representations differ.

Because rotations live on a unit circle per dimension pair, RoPE can be seen as equipping each hidden dimension with its own periodic clock that ticks faster or slower depending on its frequency.

### 2.4 Applying RoPE Over Long Contexts

When extending a model’s context window—e.g., pushing from a pre‑trained length \(L=4096\) to an evaluation length \(L'\)—the linear index \(n\) multiplies each frequency:

- For standard RoPE over small contexts
  \[
    \phi_{n,i} = n\,{\beta}^{-\,i}, 
          ~~{\beta}=10^4,\quad i=0,\dots,\tfrac d2-1.
  \]
- If we naïvely keep this formula for very large grids,
the angles become so large that many consecutive positions wrap around multiple times on their respective circles.
Two distinct tokens may then land on almost identical coordinates,
making it hard for attention heads to discriminate them.
This phenomenon is often described as “crowding” or “positional interference.”

To alleviate this effect one can **downscale** all angles by a factor \(\lambda>0:\)

Let
\(s=\tfrac{L'}{L}\) be the *extension ratio*, i.e., how many times larger the target context is compared to training.
A naïve interpolation would simply replace each angular term with

*Linear PI*:
   \[
       \phi'_{n,i} = 
          \frac{n}{s}\,\beta^{-\,i},
   \]
equivalently,
\( n' = n/s.\)
Thus angles grow only proportionally to \(s^{-1}\).

However, linear downscaling applies uniformly across all frequencies.
Especially for low frequency (high‑dimension) components,
the shrinkage may still be too severe, leading to excessive crowding inside previously distinct regimes.

A more nuanced strategy allows *different* downscaling per frequency—a technique we will refer to as **non‑uniform positional interpolation**:

*Define*
\( R_i:= {\lambda_i}^{\,{-1}}\),
then
   \[
     \phi'_{n,i} = 
        R_i\, n\,{\beta}^{-\,i}
        = n\,{\lambda_i}^{-1}\,\beta^{-\,i}.
   \]
Each dimension receives its own scaling factor,
typically satisfying
\( 1 <= {\lambda_0} <= {\lambda_1} <= ... <= {\lambda_{\tfrac d2 -1}}\).
This direction comes from theoretical insights such as Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) analysis,
which suggests higher‑frequency dimensions should interpolate less than lower‑frequency ones,
because their angular spacing already distinguishes nearby indices more sharply.

Furthermore,
the scaling can also depend on *token index groups*:
an initial segment of tokens (\( n < n_0)\)
may be exempted from any interpolation (effectively setting \(R_i=1.\))
These two forms of non-uniformity—per–dimension scaling and per–position initialization—constitute a richer family of interpolations than previously explored methods such as plain PI or NA‑based extrapolation.

### 2.5 Impact on Attention Computation

Rotary encodings influence self‑attention through two intertwined ways:

1. **Graph structure**: The similarity between any pair depends on both content vectors and term
   σ(n,m; i)=cos(φ'_{n,i}-φ'_{m,i}).
   When φ’ differences become negligible due to overcrowding,
peak attention weights collapse, producing diffuse or ill‑behaved gradients during training/fine-tuning.

2. **Gradient flow**: Because attenuation in angle space directly modulates query×key inner products,
an inconsistent scaling can damage convergence properties: gradients become either too flat or too oscillatory if neighbouring angles are too close or too far apart.

Thus careful choice of λ–factors becomes essential when reaching contextual horizons far beyond what was seen during pre‑training.

### 2.6 Summary

| Requirement | Implementation Technique | Pros & Cons |
|-------------|--------------------------|-------------|
| Preserve logarithmic frequency spacing | Naïve linear interpolation | Simple but crowds low‐frequency dims |
| Exploit NTK insights | Dimension-wise exponential scaling | Better angular spread but hard to tune |
| Avoid scattering outside training range | Universal λ>1 scaling | Requires long sequences for search |
| Mitigate crowding while keeping original semantics | Non-uniform λ per dimension + optional exempted prefixes | Complex search but preserves key information |

In later chapters we will see how an *evolutionary search* over these λ–spaces yields dramatic gains when extending contexts beyond two million tokens,
all while keeping computation minimal by cleverly reusing existing pre-trained parameters.
The remaining sections will explore how this insight fits into holistic strategies for long‐context learning and evaluation.
