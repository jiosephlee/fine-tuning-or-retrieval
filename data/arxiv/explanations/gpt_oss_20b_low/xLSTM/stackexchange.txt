\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### What exactly is the purpose of the normalizer state $n_t$ in sLSTM and how does it affect the hidden state computation?
Question:
In the sLSTM forward equations (Eq. 3) we see that the hidden state is computed as $	ilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$, where $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$. I understand that this normalizer is introduced to stabilize exponential gates, but I’m not clear on how it influences the dynamics of $c_t$. Does $n_t$ act as a running sum of future forget gates, effectively divided by the accumulated input gate values? Why is this necessary when exponential gating could already yield large values?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In sLSTM the *normalizer state* \(n_t\) keeps track of the “effective amount of memory” that has been injected into the cell so far.  It is updated exactly in the same way as the cell state, but only with the scalar gate values:

\[
n_t = f_t\,n_{t-1} + i_t .
\]

When the hidden state is produced we divide the raw cell value \(c_t\) by this accumulated gate product

\[
\tilde h_t=\frac{c_t}{\,n_t\,}.
\]

The division undoes the overall scaling introduced by large or exponentiated forget‑ and input‑gates, so that \(\tilde h_t\) remains bounded and numerically stable.  This is what the authors mean when they say that \(n_t\) “normalizes or stabilizes exponential gates”.   The normalizer does *not* simply rescale a running sum of future forget gates; it represents a *running weighted sum of all past input contributions, weighted by the subsequent forget gates*, i.e. it mirrors exactly how much memory content should have survived to time \(t\).  In short:  

* **\(n_t\)** is an exponential running average of how much new content should survive to the current step,  
* **\(c_t/n_t\)** gives a cell value that has a fixed dynamic range regardless of how aggressively we allow \(f_t\) or \(i_t\) to grow.

Below you’ll find a deeper technical walk‑through that shows why this normalization is necessary, how it behaves mathematically, and what happens if you skip it.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  What would happen with plain exponential gates?

If we simply replaced the sigmoid in Eq. (3) by an exponential function—for example
\(f_t=\exp(\tilde f_t)\), \(i_t=\exp(\tilde i_th)\)—the canonical LSTM update

\[
c_{t}=f_{t}c_{t-1}+i_{t}z_{t}
\]

would become

\[
c_{t}=e^{\,\tilde f_{t}}\!c_{t-1}+e^{\,\tilde i_{t}}z_{t}.
\]

Because both terms can grow (or shrink) exponentially with each time step, two things arise:

1. **Numerical instability** – values overflow or under‑flow quickly as soon as
   \(\tilde f_{t}\) or \(\tilde i_{t}\) deviates from zero by even a few units.
2. **Unbounded magnitude** – even if parameters are initialised such that \(|c_{t}|\)
   stays within a reasonable range during training, after a few hundred steps
   any small bias in \(\tilde f,\tilde i\) results in exponentially large values
   in both \(c_t\) and its derivative w.r.t. parameters.

Consequently gradients explode and training fails unless drastic clipping or other
countermeasures are applied.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  How the normalizer fixes this

Recall that in ordinary LSTM (with sigmoids) the sum
\(s_{\!t} \equiv \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} f_{\tau}\cdots f_{t-1}\,i_{\tau}\)
remains bounded because each gate lies in \([0,1]\).  
In sLSTM we keep a *scalar surrogate* for this sum:

```text
n_0 = 0
n_\!t = f_\!t * n_{\!\!(t-1)} + i_\!t          (scalar)
```

Thus \(n_{\!t} = i_1 + f_2i_2 + f_2f_3i_3 + ... + f_2...f_{\!t}i_{\!t}.\)

If we now divide \(c_{\!t}\) by \(n_{\!t}\),

```
h̃_\!t = c_\!t / n_\!t
```

we obtain exactly the quantity that would have been obtained if all gates were bounded by 1:

* In our exponential‑gate scenario,
  \[
  c_{\!t}= e^{\,\tilde f_1}e^{\,\tilde f_2}\!\cdots e^{\,\tilde f_n}
           e^{\,\tilde i_n}z_n +
           c_0   e^{\,\tilde f_1}\!\cdots e^{\,\tilde f_n},
  \]
  while simultaneously
  \[
  n_{\!t}= e^{\,\tilde i_n} +
         e^{\,\tilde f_n}e^{\,\tilde i_{n-1}} + 
         e^{\,\tilde f_n}e^{\,\tilde f_{n-1}}e^{\,\tilde i_{n-2}} + \dots .
  \]
  The common exponential factors cancel out when taking their ratio,
  leaving a signal whose magnitude depends only on the raw inputs and on
  differences among logits \(\widetilde{z}, \widetilde{i}, \widetilde{f},….\)

In other words, **\(n_{\! t}\)** is precisely the accumulated product chain of forget‑gates times all past input‑gates; dividing by it removes any collective scaling effect introduced by those products. Because each term inside \(n_{\! t}\) is positive and never smaller than zero, no division-by-zero occurs unless all recent gates are zero – at which point both \(c_{\! t}=0,\; n_{\! t}=0,\; h̃_\! t=0.\)

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3.  Intuitive picture: “soft clip”

Think of an ordinary LSTM as having a moving accumulator. Every step you add some new material (\(i_tf t z t.\)) and optionally forget part of what’s already there (\(f_tc_{\! t-1}\)). In sLSTM we allow these “add” and “forget” operations to happen on an *exponential scale*. Potentially you could add a value that is astronomically larger than what was there before. To keep this from blowing up, we keep track of *how much weight was actually added* so far – that’s exactly \(n_{\! t}\).

If at time \(t_0,\; n_{ t_0}\approx10^4,\; c_{ t_0}\approx5\times10^5,\)
then regardless of later huge values for \(f_i,i_i,z_i,…,\)
the ratio \(\frac{c}{n}\) stays around one order of magnitude or two,
kept in check by previous small inputs/outputs. In practice all hidden states stay around zero mean with unit scale, which is precisely where LSTMs normally sit after training.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4.  Why not just clip or normalise afterwards?

You could argue: “why not clip gradients at training time?”  
Because clipping works only on gradients—not directly on activations.
Even if gradients are clipped at back‑prop time, forward activations can still soar if your forward dynamics involve large multiplications/expansions, which then entails huge gradient values before clipping takes place – causing instability right during training.  

With \(n_{\! t}\), *every* forward step automatically carries its own normalisation factor; gradients automatically adapt because they are computed with respect to a well‑scaled activation.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5. How it does not change the final network output

The authors call attention to stability:  
replacing raw gates by their exponentials together with `m/f` normalization yields exactly the same output as using raw gates (see Section H in Appendix A). This stems from simple algebra:

Let us define

```
    g_f = exp( tildef_f )          // real forget
    g_i = exp( tildef_i )          // real input
    g_o = sigma( tildef_o )        // real output
    m_f = max(log(g_f)+m_pre , log(g_i)) // stabiliser
```

Then

```
    g'_f = exp(log(g_f)+m_pre - m_post)
    g'_i = exp(log(g_i)-m_post)
```

inserting `g'_f`,`g'_i` into Eq.(3) produces exactly the same value for `ct`, `nt`, and subsequently `ht`. In other words `m` merely prevents numerical overflow/underflow; mathematically it is neutral.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

* **Purpose** – stabilize exponential gating by keeping activations from exploding/vanishing.
* **Mechanism** – maintain an auxiliary scalar that accumulates ‑ in log space ‑ the product of forget gates times all prior input gains.
* **Effect on dynamics** – divides every cell state by this accumulated weight so that hidden states stay bounded.
* **Why it matters** – pure exponentials would otherwise let activations drift exponentially, breaking training; standard clipped/squared activations would lose expressive power because they strengthen forgetting too aggressively.

### How does exponential gating improve a LSTM's ability to revise storage decisions?
Question:
The paper claims that switching from sigmoid to exponential activations on the input and forget gates enables revision of stored information (e.g., the Nearest Neighbor Search task). Could someone explain, in plain terms, why exponential gates allow a later input to overwrite an earlier stored value? How does this differ from the standard LSTM behavior where gates are bounded by zero and one?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Putting the gates through an exponential instead of a sigmoid removes the hard cap at 1 and lets the gates grow arbitrarily large. Because the cell state is a *sum* of past content weighted by these gates, a very large input gate can “over‑write” anything that has been stored previously – the new contribution dominates the old one. A sigmoid gate, on the other hand, can only amplify old values up to a factor of 1.0 (or attenuate them with a factor ≤ 1), so an earlier value that was stored with a “high” forget gate and a “high” input gate can never be fully cancelled later – it will always contribute at least its previous magnitude multiplied by the remaining forget factor. Exponential gating together with a normaliser ‑ which keeps track of how much each past contribution should still be counted ‑ gives the cell state a *soft‑max*‑style weighting over time rather than a hard product of forget factors. This soft‑max behaviour is what lets later inputs overwrite earlier ones and is exactly what is needed to solve tasks like nearest‑neighbor search.

---

### 1.  What the vanilla LSTM does

Recall the classic update

\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t ,
\]

where \(f_t,i_t\in[0,1]\) because they are sigmoid outputs.  
Two facts follow immediately:

| Property | Effect on \(c_t\) |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Forget‑gate bound** | \(f_t \le 1\).  The old state can never grow when multiplied by \(f_t\). |
| **Input‑gate bound** | \(i_t \le 1\).  The new content can add at most its unscaled value. |

Because both operators are bounded by one, the magnitude of any previously stored component can *only decay* (or stay constant). That means once you have “locked in” a value with a high forget gate (≈ 1) and high input gate (≈ 1), later steps cannot erase it; at best you can dilute it with smaller forget gates that multiply it, but you can’t make its coefficient become 0 or negative.

In practice this limits an LSTM’s *revision* ability: if during the scan you see a later token that should replace an earlier match (as in nearest‑neighbor search), that later token cannot fully overwrite the earlier stored vector – it simply adds to it while the older vector keeps decaying slowly.

---

### 2.  Exponential gates change the game

In xLSTM the input and/or forget gate use an **exponential** activation:

\[
i'_t = \exp(\tilde i_t), \qquad 
f'_t = \exp(\tilde f_t)   \quad (\text{or}\;\sigma(\tilde f_t)).
\]

Now \(i'_t, f'_t \in (0,\infty)\).  
The recurrent term becomes

\[
c_t = f'_t\,c_{t-1} + i'_t\,z_t .
\]

Because \(f'_t > 0\) can be **larger than one**, it is possible for later time steps to amplify their own contribution **away from** older values rather than merely attenuating them.  

Imagine at time \(t\) you want to store vector \(v_1\) with weight \(w_1=10\). After several steps you see a better match \(v_2\) that you want to archive instead of \(v_1\). If at step \(t+3\) you set

```
forget gate f = 0.01     (so old content decays by ≈ 100×)
input   gate i = exp(4) ≈ 54
```

the update gives

```
c_{t+3} ≈ 0.01 * c_{t+2} + 54 * v_2 .
```

The coefficient multiplying the old cell state is tiny; meanwhile, the new content is amplified massively. The net effect is that whatever was in `c_{t+2}` is practically forgotten, and `v_2` dominates `c_{t+3}`.

With this mechanism an LSTM *can* rewrite its memory on demand.

---

### 3.  Normaliser – turning this into a soft‑max

A raw exponential might blow up numerically, so xLSTM introduces a normaliser state

```
n_t = f' n_{t-1} + i' .
```

The hidden output then uses `c_t / n_t`.  
This division essentially **renormalises** all past contributions so that their coefficients sum to 1 (like soft‑max weights).  

Consequences:

* Each time step contributes an “importance weight” (`i'`), but past weights are scaled down by subsequent forget factors (`f'`).
* Because these weights are unconstrained (> 1 allowed), if a later element has a very large `i'`, its weight dominates `n_t`, causing all previous weights to become negligible.
* The final hidden state therefore reflects predominantly the most recent high‑weight input – exactly what we need for revision.

---

### 4.  Why this matters for nearest‑neighbor search

In NN‑search you stream through a list of vectors, each paired with an auxiliary value (the label).  
You keep in memory whichever vector has been most similar to your query so far and output its label when you hit end-of-sequence.

With vanilla LSTM:

* Once an earlier vector gets stored with high `i` and `f`, its contribution will only shrink multiplicatively.
* A newer vector may become more similar, but because its weight has to overcome all decayed earlier contributions *and* because weights are capped at 1, it can often lose out—so past matches are **not revisable** reliably.

With xLSTM:

* If later evidence contradicts an earlier match (a higher similarity score appears), you simply give that later element a huge `i'` and optionally keep `f'` small for that step.  
* Because of exponential gating and normalisation, its weight will dominate all previous ones; effectively we *overwrite* whatever was stored earlier.
* This behaviour directly implements “revision”: the model is allowed to discard earlier memory content when better evidence arrives.

Hence exponential gating equips an LSTM with genuine revisitability—a property absent in standard gated RNNs where every contribution can only die out but never be replaced entirely.

---

### 5.  Broader view: gating as dynamic convolution

From another angle, gating in RNNs performs an implicit *convolution* over time: each gate decides how much of each previous step propagates forward (*forget*) and how much new information enters (*input*). Sigmoid gates give you fixed kernel support strictly between 0 and 1, effectively limiting your receptive field to attenuation only.

Exponential gates enlarge this kernel support beyond [0, 1]—they allow actuators that “refocus” attention toward newer events by multiplying past states by < 0 or > 0 arbitrarily. This is analogous to giving your recurrent network a learnable dynamic filter whose support is no longer fixed but could contract around any desired timestamp—exactly what revision requires.

---

### Takeaway

Switching from sigmoid to exponential activations makes gates *unbounded*, allowing them to amplify new information beyond just attenuating old data. Coupled with normalisation, this turns an LSTM cell into a soft‑max over time where recent inputs can override older ones—a mechanism essential for tasks that require revising storage decisions such as nearest‑neighbor search. Standard sigmoid‐based LSTMs never have this capability because their gates cannot exceed 1, so once information has entered the cell it can only decay—but never be overwritten or replaced entirely.

### What is the intuition behind introducing a matrix memory in mLSTM versus a scalar memory in sLSTM?
Question:
mLSTM stores a key-value pair using a covariance update $C_t = C_{t-1} + v_t k_t^	op$, while sLSTM keeps a scalar cell. Why does turning the memory into a $d 	imes d$ matrix increase storage capacity? Is it analogous to having multiple independent scalar cells, or does it provide richer interactions between keys and values? Also, how does this relate to Fast Weight Programmers?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
A scalar LSTM cell can be viewed as a one‑dimensional memory. The updates \(c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t\) simply add a new scalar value to the previous one, and the entire hidden state is a linear function of this single number. A matrix cell replaces that single number with a full \(d\times d\) matrix \(C_t\). Every key–value pair \((k_t,v_t)\) is inserted with the outer product \(v_t k_t^\top\); retrieving a value for a query \(q_t\) amounts to multiplying the matrix by a vector: \(\tilde h_t = C_t q_t\). Because the matrix can couple every dimension of a key with every dimension of a value, it can store many “mini‑tables” in parallel. This gives **true orthogonal capacity**: each rank‑1 update adds a new basis direction that can later be read out by an appropriately aligned query. A single scalar cannot do this because it has only one degree of freedom; adding a new piece of information necessarily overwrites or interferes with what was stored before.

---

### 1. Why a \(d\times d\) matrix matters

* **Orthogonal precision** – The covariance update
  \[
  C_t = C_{t-1} + v_t k_t^\top
  \]
  is exactly the outer‑product update used in *Fast Weight Programmers* and *Bidirectional Associative Memories*. When all keys and values are zero‑mean (requiring layer‑norm in xLSTM), this update maximises the signal‑to‑noise ratio of later retrievals (Dayan & Sejnowski, 1991). In other words, the matrix learns to keep different key–value pairs separable without any parameter‑driven mixing step.

* **Rich interactions** – With \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) we can encode arbitrary linear relations between keys and values. A single scalar cell can only realise
  \[
  h_t = i_t\,z_t + f_t\,h_{t-1},
  \]
  which has no notion of “which part” of the hidden vector produced which part of the current output. The matrix form naturally implements a global interaction: each query component aggregates contributions from all key components.

* **Parallelisation** – Because updates and retrievals involve only matrix–vector or matrix–matrix products, they can be expressed as batched GEMMs and run in parallel on GPUs or TPUs—all without the sequential scan that ordinary LSTMs require. This is why mLSTM can be run in a FlashAttention‑style kernel while keeping its theoretical expressiveness.

---

### 2. Intuition: “many scalar cells” vs “one rich cell”

If you stack \(d\) independent scalar LSTMs you get a diagonal memory:
\[
C^{(\text{diag})}_t = \operatorname{diag}(c^{(1)}_t,\dots,c^{(d)}_t).
\]
Each entry evolves independently, so cross‑dimensional interactions are impossible. In contrast, mLSTM’s off‑diagonal elements encode correlations between different dimensions. That’s why mLSTM behaves like *many* interdependent cells rather than *separate* ones.

Think of it as moving from a list of counters to a full table of counters—where every cell in the table can count any pair of (key dimension, value dimension). The richer dictionary allows you to remember more distinct associations with the same hidden dimensionality.

---

### 3. Relation to Fast Weight Programmers

Fast Weight Programmers (FWP) were introduced by Schmidhuber (1992) as “temporarily delicious memories” that are updated via outer products and decay:
\[
W^{(fwp)}_t = f\, W^{(fwp)}_{t-1} + i\, v k^\top.
\]
FWP does not perform any gating beyond fixed decay/learning rates; it relies on weight‐decay schedules chosen manually. xLSTM embeds exactly the same core idea but replaces hand‑tuned schedules with learnable forget and input gates (exponential variants). Moreover, xLSTM normalises its updates through an adaptive normaliser state \(n_t\), ensuring numerical stability across long sequences—a problem famously plaguing vanilla FWPs.

Thus mLSTM inherits FWP’s high expressive capacity while adding:

* **Trainable gating** – so forgetting/learning rates adapt to data.
* **Normalisation** – protects against overflow when gates become large.
* **Residual block context** – framing the memory inside modern Transformer‑style residuals for better optimisation flow.

---

### 4. Practical implications

| Variant | Memory representation | Capacity | Parallelism |
|---------|-----------------------|----------|-------------|
| sLSTM | Scalar cell per hidden unit | Limited by compression; each scalar mixes all information in one number | Sequences still processed sequentially due to recurrence |
| mLSTM | Full \(d\times d\) matrix | Stores many orthogonal key–value associations; effectively \(O(d^2)\) capacity | Updates & retrievals are pure GEMMs → embarrassingly parallel |

In experiments on synthetic associative recall tasks (Section 5.2), mLSTM matched or outperformed Transformer baselines while requiring fewer parameters—exactly what one would expect from an architecture that keeps all pairwise interactions explicit without learning extra parameters for them.

---

### 5. Takeaway

Replacing the scalar memory cell with a matrix turns the LSTM from an *additive accumulator* into a *structured associative store*. It ends up being analogous to having many intertwined scalar memories that can talk to one another through learned gating and explicit outer products—very much in line with Fast Weight Programming but fully integrated into the LSTM framework, fishbowl-multiplying its storage power without sacrificing linear time or necessitating expensive recurrent scans.

For anyone looking to push recurrent architectures beyond classic LSTMs while keeping training and inference efficient, mLSTM offers precisely this trade‑off: higher expressive capacity through richer interactions plus GPU‑friendly parallel primitives.

### Why is the mLSTM forward pass fully parallelizable while sLSTM is not?
Question:
The paper states that mLSTM has no memory mixing and hence can be reformulated in parallel. In contrast, sLSTM contains hidden‑to‑hidden recurrent connections that break parallelism. Could someone walk through how the recurrence in Eq. (10) prevents vector‑wise parallel execution, whereas Eq. (8) allows it? How do the CUDA kernels take advantage of this difference?
Answer:
**Why can the mLSTM be fully parallelised while the sLSTM cannot?**

The core difference lies in what each cell’s *update* depends on.  
In a recurrent network the state at time \(t\) is usually a function of the
previous state \(s_{t-1}\). If the state contains terms that are
*gated by the previous hidden state*, those terms must be computed
sequentially because the hidden‑to‑hidden weight matrix couples all
components of the previous hidden vector into every new component.

---

### 1.  The sLSTM recurrence (Eq. (10) – 12 in the paper)

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t, \\[2pt]
h_t &= o_t\, \tilde h_t,\qquad 
\tilde h_t=\frac{c_t}{n_t}, \\[2pt]
z_t &= \varphi(\mathbf{W}_z^\top x_t + r_z\,h_{t-1}+b_z),\\
i_t &=\exp(\tilde i_t),\qquad 
\tilde i_t=\mathbf{W}_i^\top x_t+r_i\,h_{t-1}+b_i,\\
f_t &=
 \sigma(\tilde f_t)\;\text{or}\; \exp(\tilde f_t),
 \qquad\tilde f_t=\mathbf{W}_f^\top x_t+r_f\,h_{t-1}+b_f, \\[2pt]
o_t &= \sigma(\tilde o_t),\qquad 
\tilde o_t= \mathbf{W}_o^\top x_t+r_o\,h_{t-1}+b_o .
\end{aligned}
\]

The *recurrent* part is all terms that involve the matrix \(r_\ast h_{t-1}\).
Because these gates are applied element‑wise to a *scalar* memory cell,
the gate value has to be known before it can be multiplied with the cell
input.  
Consequently you cannot compute all \(c_j, h_j, z_j,\dots\) for a batch of
time steps simultaneously – you must finish step \(t-1\), obtain \(h_{t-1}\),
then evaluate all gate pre‑activations for step \(t\), and only then can you
compute \(c_t, h_t\).  
The chain of dependencies is therefore a serial chain over all time steps.

On a GPU this serial dependency translates into a scan operation: each step
must wait for the previous one to finish before it can start.

---

### 2.  The mLSTM recurrence (Eq. (9) – 11)

\[
\begin{aligned}
{\bf C}_t &= f_t\,{\bf C}_{t-1} + i_t\,{\bf v}_t{\bf k}_t^{\!\top},\\[2pt]
{\bf n}_t &= f_t\,{\bf n}_{t-1} + i_t\,{\bf k}_t ,\\[2pt]
{\bf h}_t &= {\bf o}_t \odot 
            \frac{{\bf C}_t {\bf q}_t}
                 {\max(|{\bf n}_t^{\!\top}{\bf q}_t|,1)} ,\\[4pt]
{\bf q}_t &= {\bf W}_q x_t + b_q ,\\
{\bf k}_t &= \frac{1}{\sqrt d}\,{\bf W}_k x_t + b_k ,\\
{\bf v}_t &= {\bf W}_v x_t + b_v ,\\[4pt]
i_t&=\exp(\tilde i _t),          & \tilde i _t = {\bf w}^\top_i x_t+b_i ,\\
f _t&= \sigma(\tilde f _t)\;\text{or}\;\exp(\tilde f _t),&
      \tilde f _t = {\bf w}^\top_f x _b+b_f ,\\
o _t&= \sigma(\tilde o _t ),      &
      \tilde o _t = {\bf W}_{o}x _ t+b_o .
\end{aligned}
\]

All gates are **functions only of the current input** \(x_t\);
no term contains \(h_{t-1}\).  
Because the matrix memory update and retrieval are *outer product*
operations between vectors that depend only on the current token,
the value of ${\bf C}_{t}$ can be expressed as

\[
{\bf C}_{l}= {\bf C}_{0}
  + \sum_{j=1}^{l}
        i_j\, {\bf v}_j {\bf k}_j^{\!\top},
\]

which is a simple prefix sum over *independent* contributions.
Exactly the same is true for the normaliser ${\bf n}_{l}$.  
Thus, once we know all input activations for a whole sequence,
the whole prefix–sum can be evaluated with one **parallel associative‑scan**
operation – something GPUs do extremely fast (e.g. cuBLAS `scal`, CUDA `scan`).

Inside each time step there are no intra‑step recurrences:
all matrix multiplications and element‑wise operations can be dispatched
to GPU cores in parallel across a batch of tokens or across sequence
positions that share the same prefix sum value.

---

### 3.  CUDA kernels that exploit this difference

| kernel | what it does | why it can run in parallel |
|--------|--------------|----------------------------|
| **prefix‑sum/kernel‑scan** over the outer‑product updates | accumulates ${\bf C}_{l}$ and ${\bf n}_{l}$ rowwise | each partial sum only depends on earlier partial sums; `scan` is highly optimised |
| **matrix–vector multiplications** $({\bf C}_{l}{\bf q}_{l})$ and $({\bf C}_{l}^{{T}}\cdot...)$ | compute hidden state from memory | these are standard batched GEMMs; embarrassingly parallel |
| **element‑wise gating** $i_l, f_l, o_l$ | apply exponential or sigmoid only to vectors from $x_l$ | no cross‑step dependencies → vectorised |
| **stabilisation** (`m_l`) | compute max(log …) & exponentials | operates on per‑step scalars; vectorises across batch |

In contrast, an sLSTM kernel would have to:

1. Compute gate pre‑activations `r* h_{l-1}` for every token.
2. Wait until those values are ready before computing `z_l`, `i_l`, `f_l`.
3. Update `c_l` and propagate `h_l`.

Because every step owns prerequisites from its predecessor,
CUDA cannot execute them concurrently; one must serialise them as a scan over *vectors*.
GPU “scan” kernels exist but they still require an extra synchronisation barrier per element,
and they cannot fuse with matrix multiplications – yielding about a *factor 2*
slowdown relative to mLSTM.

---

### Bottom line

mLSTM’s update rule separates *time‑dependent* computation (the cumulative outer product) from *within‑step* computation that depends only on current inputs.
This separation allows us to realise an associative-scanner over batches or sequences,
exactly what GPUs specialise at.
sLSTM’s hidden–to–hidden recurrent connections entangle time steps at every gate,
so those updates must become strictly sequential – GPUs can “parallelise” only within step but not across steps.

Hence, when you build CUDA kernels:

* **mLSTM:** one fast prefix‑sum chain + batched GEMMs → fully parallel.
* **sLSTM:** per-step scalar scans ⤳ sequential core execution → about twice slower than mLSTM.

This is why in practice we observe roughly twofold speed differences reported in Sect. 5.6 of the paper while keeping both variants perfectly correct.

### "Exponential gating" vs. "squashing": why use $	ilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$ instead of $	anh(c_t)$ in sLSTM?
Question:
Standard LSTMs use an activation $	ilde{h}_t = 	anh(c_t)$ to keep $c_t$ bounded. In sLSTM we replace this with a division by $n_t$. What practical difference does this make for gradient flow or numerical stability? Are there any situations where $	anh(c_t)$ would be preferable?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
In the sLSTM the hidden state is made *linear* in the cell value and then *normalised* by a running sum \(n_t\) of all the gates that will ever multiply the cell.  
In a classic LSTM the cell is squashed with \(\psi=\tanh\), which keeps the cell bounded but also introduces a strong non‑linearity and a possible saturation of gradients.

The two choices affect three things:

| Aspect | Classic LSTM | sLSTM ( \( \tilde h_t = c_t/n_t \) ) |
|--------|--------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Magnitude control** | Constrained to \((-1,1)\) by \(\tanh\). | Controlled by the *normaliser* \(n_t\), which grows as a weighted sum of future forget‑gates. |
| **Gradient flow** | Derivative of \(\tanh\) is \(1-\tilde h^2\); when \(|c_t|\) is large gradients vanish. | Gradient w.r.t. \(c_t\) is \(1/n_t\); it never vanishes unless \(n_t\) explodes, and it can even become larger than 1 if \(n_t<1\). |
| **Numerical stability** | Saturation for large \(c_t\) can cause overflow/under‑flow in the exponentials of the gates. | No explicit squashing, so large cell values are allowed; stability is mainly ensured by stabilising the exponential gates (\(i'_t,f'_t\)). |

Below I explain why this design choice matters in practice and when one might still prefer a squashing function.

---

### 1. What does the normaliser do?

The sLSTM introduces an auxiliary state

\[
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t .
\]

This is **exactly** the same recurrence that would appear if we looked at how much future forgetting will be applied to the present input: every time a new input arrives, its influence will be multiplied by all future forget gates until it disappears. By accumulating this product into \(n_t\), the cell accumulates *how much capacity it has already been allotted*.

Thus  

\[
h_t = o_t \frac{c_t}{\,n_t\,}
\]

can be seen as *dividing out* how much of that capacity should be “used” at time \(t\). In a sense we are normalising by the *effective number of items stored*. This has two practical consequences:

* **No bound on \(c_t\)** – The cell may grow linearly (or even faster) with time, but its influence on the output is automatically damped by dividing by \(n_t\).  
* **Implicit regularisation against runaway memory** – If too many inputs keep coming without being forgotten, \(n_t\) grows and down‑scales every hidden output accordingly.

---

### 2. Gradient flow

With \(\tilde h = c/n\) the derivative w.r.t. the cell value is simply

\[
\frac{\partial \tilde h}{\partial c} = \frac{1}{n}.
\]

Because \(n>0\), this derivative never vanishes; it can even be larger than one if recent inputs had very small total weight (small forget gates). Therefore information can pass back through many time steps without being killed by an activation saturation – a classic problem with deep RNNs.

In contrast, with \(\tilde h = \tanh c\),

\[
\frac{\partial \tilde h}{\partial c} = 1-\tanh^2 c .
\]

As soon as \(|c|\gtrsim 3.5\), this term drops below 0.03 and gradients will be severely attenuated or even vanish after several recurrent steps. While LSTMs mitigate this because of their gating, they still suffer from this non‑linearity at high activations.

Thus **using division rather than a squashing function keeps gradients more stable over long horizons**, especially for tasks where many items must be remembered (e.g., longest‑prefix matching).

---

### 3. Numerical stability

Squashing with hyperbolic tangent involves exponentials:  
\( \tanh c = (e^{c}-e^{-c})/(e^{c}+e^{-c}) \). Even moderate negative or positive values can cause overflow in floating‑point implementations. In contrast, division requires only a scalar division; no large intermediate numerics appear.

Furthermore, sLSTM uses exponential gating for input and forget gates (see Eqns (4)). These special activations can quickly explode; to handle that they stabilise them with an auxiliary state \(m_t=\max(\log f+ m_{t-1},\, \log i)\). This trick guarantees that after stabilisation both \(i'_t,f'_t>0,\leq 1/e^m_{\max}\), avoiding overflows while keeping gating expressive.

In short: *division + normaliser* removes one source of potential under/overflow while letting us use strong exponential gating when needed.

---

### 4. When might you still prefer \(\psi(c)=\tanh(c)\)?

* **Non‑linearity is desired** – Squashing injects an extra non‑linear transformation between memory and output that may help disentangle representations in very deep models or when combined with other non‑linearities (e.g., in residual blocks).  
* **Very small recurrent steps** – If your sequence length is tiny, both approaches behave similarly; you might opt for vanilla LSTM to keep implementation simple or match pretrained checkpoints.  
* **Memory constraints** – Computing and maintaining the extra normaliser state doubles one scalar per time step; for extremely memory‑tight applications you might forgo it in favour of standard gating.

Empirically, however, xLSTM’s ablation studies show that replacing \(\tanh(c)\) with division-by-normaliser contributes significantly to its performance gains on language modelling and synthetic tasks where long‑range dependencies matter.

---

### 5. Takeaway

The “exponential gating + normaliser” trick replaces a hard non‑linear squashing (\(\tanh(c)\)) with a mild linear scaling that remains well‑behaved regardless of how large or how many inputs you accumulate in memory. It offers:

* More stable gradient propagation over long sequences.
* Fewer numerical pitfalls from exponentials.
* An implicit accounting mechanism for how much capacity each past token should consume.

If your task requires long‐term retention and robust training over many cells (as in language modelling or associative recall), using \(h_t=o_t\,c_t/n_t\) tends to give better learning dynamics than classic tilting via \(\tanh(c)\). Conversely, if your sequence is short or you wish to preserve maximal expressive power inside each hidden state without further normalisation, keeping the traditional \(\tanh(c)\) may still be sensible.*

### Does using a covariance update rule in mLSTM cause information loss compared to exact key‑value storage?
Question:
The covariance update $	riangle C = v k^	op$ seems similar to Hebbian learning. If we only ever perform outer products and later evaluate $C q$, could we end up with interference among stored pairs? How does this compare to an explicit attention mechanism which computes softmax weights over all key vectors?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No – the outer‑product (covariance) update used in the mLSTM does **not** introduce a fundamental loss of information compared to an explicit attention mechanism that stores and queries keys by computing soft‑max weights. In fact, the covariance rule is *optimal for maximal separability* of retrieved vectors when the goal is to recover a stored value from a query that is (approximately) orthogonal to all other keys. What you do observe in practice – interference among stored pairs – comes from the *finite numerical precision* and the *bounded scaling* of the gates, not from an inherent property of the update rule.

---

### 1.  What the paper actually does

The mLSTM cell stores a pair \((\mathbf{k}_t , \mathbf{v}_t)\) by adding their outer product to a running matrix memory:
\[
\boxed{\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+ i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top}
\]
A retrieval query \(\mathbf{q}\) produces an answer through a single matrix multiplication
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t=\frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}}
{\max\!\bigl(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}|,1\bigr)} ,
\]
where \(\mathbf{n}_t=\sum_i i_i\,\mathbf{k}_i\) normalises against the accumulated key strength.

This scheme is precisely the *Fast‑Weight Programmer* idea: every new key–value pair adds a rank‑one contribution, and the decaying forget gate \(f_t\) guarantees that old pairs do not dominate indefinitely. In contrast to vanilla attention, where each key has an explicit scalar weight and only the current state participates in retrieval, every key lives in the shared matrix memory and all of them contribute simultaneously to every read.

---

### 2.  Why there is no “information loss”

1. **Exactness of outer products**  
   The update rule is additive with no compression step; if two different pairs are inserted consecutively, their outer products simply sum. As long as the gates are learned correctly (or set appropriately), the matrix \(\mathbf{C}\) holds *exactly* all contributions. No eigen‑value shrinkage or projection is performed.

2. **Optimality for separability**  
   The paper cites Dayan & Sejnowski (1991), who proved that when one wishes to recover a value with maximum signal‑to‑noise ratio in a linear associative memory, adding \(v k^\top\) with unit norm keys and values indeed maximises separability of binary patterns. Thus, if all stored keys are roughly orthogonal (which happens automatically when inputs are projected through shared linear layers with dropout/normalisation), retrieving \(\mathbf{v}\) from \(\mathbf{k}\) is as accurate as it can be given purely linear operations.

3. **No capacity limit (apart from numerical precision)**  
   In theory, every pair can be stored independently; recall accuracy only degrades when floating‑point round‑off accumulates or when gates saturate (e.g., \(i_t\approx 0\) or \(f_t\approx 0\)). This is analogous to Transformer attention where soft‑max can numerically under‑ or overflow with very long contexts, but it is not a theoretical loss of representational power.

---

### 3.  Interference in practice

Interference does appear in experiments on the **Multi‑Query Associative Recall** task and on long‑range synthetic benchmarks:

- The keys produced by a linear projection of token embeddings are rarely perfectly orthogonal; small overlaps cause cross‑terms like \(\mathbf{v}_i \mathbf{k}_i^\top \mathbf{k}_j\) that leak into retrieval for key \(j\).  
- The normaliser \(\max(|\mathbf{n}^\top \mathbf{q}|,1)\) mitigates extreme scaling but cannot completely cancel overlapping cosine similarities.

Importantly, this interference is **architecture dependent**, not a consequence of using an outer product update per se. The same problem would exist with an explicit attention table if tiny Gaussian noise corrupted many keys.

---

### 4.  Hard contrast with softmax attention

| Property | mLSTM covariance | Softmax attention |
|----------|------------------|-------------------|
| Retrieval cost | \(O(d^2)\) matrix × vector | \(O(Ld)\) dot products ≈ \(O(Ld)\) for each token |
| Gate control | Learnable forget & input gates → decay & learning rate | Softmax weights determined only by dot products |
| Interference handling | Implicit via decay; no per‑match weighting | Explicit weighting by softmax mask |
| Expressivity | Unlimited number of stored items (bounded only by precision) | Limited by number of keys kept in cache (typically fixed context window) |

Because mLSTM does not compute pairwise similarities at read time, it cannot immediately attenuate noisy competing keys the way softmax can assign near‑zero weight to almost irrelevant keys. That gives attention an edge on tasks requiring selective retrieval from thousands of stored pairs without memory decay—tasks that transformer caches excel at.

However, as shown empirically in the paper’s synthetic tasks and long‑range arena evaluations, xLSTM’s covariance memory performs comparably on associative recall and even better on tasks that demand *state tracking* (e.g., parity or regular language recognition). Here softmax attention fails because it lacks hidden–hidden state transitions that enable stateful reasoning.

---

### 5.  Takeaway for practitioners

If your problem scope:
- Requires **long sequential context** but can tolerate approximate recall of many key–value pairs,
- Benefits from **parallelizable matrix updates** (as mLSTM offers),
- Does not need exact per-key gating during read,

then there is little risk of information loss by using the covariance update rule; you might even get faster inference than a pure Transformer due to linear‐time updates.

On the other hand, if your downstream task demands very sharp selection over thousands of items and you can afford explicit pairwise “softmax” masking (e.g., retrieval over a dynamic database), then retaining an explicit attention mechanism may still win in terms of precision even though its computational complexity grows quadratically with sequence length.

---

In summary, **the outer-product covariance update itself is not lossy**; any interference seen originates from practical constraints such as limited floating–point precision and non‑orthogonal key projections rather than from an intrinsic flaw of using outer products versus softmax attention.*

### How are residual block architectures chosen for xLSTM blocks (post vs pre up‑projection), and what effect does this have on performance?
Question:
"sLSTM" uses post‑up‑projection blocks while "mLSTM" uses pre‑up‑projection blocks according to Section 3.4.2. What guides this design choice? Does placing up‑projection before or after recurrent processing affect numerical stability or expressivity?
Answer:
### Short‑answer  

* **sLSTM “post‑up‑projection”** – the block first processes the input in its native dimensionality, updates the scalar state, and only **then** expands the output to a higher‑dimensional “high‑space” representation via a learnable linear projection and non‑linearity.  
* **mLSTM “pre‑up‑projection”** – the block first projects the input into a high‑dimensional space, performs all matrix‑memory operations completely within that high‑dimensional space, and finally collapses back to the original dimension.  

The choice is guided by the *memory capacity* of each variant and by *parallelisation considerations*, exactly as explained in §3.4.2 of the paper.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why the two blocks differ

| Block type | Key property | Reason for order |
|------------|--------------|------------------|
| **sLSTM** | **Scalar state, heavy memory mixing** (recurrent connections between hidden states) | Mixing can only be done in the low‑dimensional space; expanding to a high dimension before mixing would destroy the scalar structure and unrolls an expensive recurrence.  Putting projection *after* ensures that the sparse, highly expressive scalar state is updated first, then mapped into a richer feature space for downstream layers. |
| **mLSTM** | **Matrix state (covariance) with large storage capacity** (size $d\times d$) | Matrix updates ($\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$) are naturally performed in a high‑dimensional space where each entry of $\mathbf{C}$ is a potential interaction between two features.  Projecting *before* gives each key/value vector a full $d$-dimensional representation required for the outer‑product update; projecting afterward would discard valuable cross‑feature interactions learned in that higher space.  Moreover, performing these operations in a single batch across many steps enables parallel execution on GPUs, matching the efficiency of state‑space models or fast weight approaches. |

The paper explicitly states this guidance:

> “For an xLSTM block containing an sLSTM, we use **post up‑projection**… For an xLSTM block containing an mLSTM, we use **pre up‑projection** since the memory capacity becomes larger in the high-dimensional space.”  
> – §3.4.2

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Impact on numerical stability and expressivity

### Numerical stability

*Both variants employ exponential gating,* whose large values can cause overflow or underflow unless stabilised (Eq.~(5)-(6) for sLSTM and identical stabilization in mLSTM).  
However, because only sLSTM involves hidden–hidden recurrence (memory mix), its internal computations are inherently sequential and thus more prone to accumulating round‑off error over long sequences.  By keeping projection outside the recurrent core (post up‑projection) we minimise the range of numbers that travel through this sensitive body of gates.

Conversely, mLSTM’s parallelisable structure reduces hidden-to-hidden propagation across time steps; errors do not accumulate in the same way, so its main source of instability is limited to the large matrix product during memory updates—handled by standard FP32/FP16 matrix kernels that are highly optimised on GPUs.

### Expressivity

* sLSTM’s scalar state plus exponential gating gives it powerful **state tracking** abilities: it can revise past values more readily than a fixed (“outer–product only”) memory (see §3.2).  This makes it better at tasks such as parity or nested parentheses where tracking whether a particular symbol was seen before matters.*

* mLSTM’s matrix memory provides exponential growth in storage capacity: each key/value pair contributes at most $d\times d$ non‑zero entries to $\mathbf{C}$.  We show experimentally that this gives mLSTM a clear advantage on associative recall tasks (Sec 4.1.2).  The pre non‑linear transformation ensures that this storage is used efficiently—even for very long contexts—because every key/value pair sits fully inside the high‐dimensional representation.*

Thus, both architectural choices reflect a trade‑off: **post projection keeps sLSTM lightweight but highly expressive for tracking explicit state; pre projection gives mLSTM maximal storage while keeping computation parallelisable but sacrifices some inter‑time hidden mixing.**

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Practical takeaway

When designing an xLSTM backbone:

1. **Use post up‑projection blocks for any variant that relies heavily on hidden-to-hidden recurrence (sLSTM).**  
   This preserves state integrity and keeps numerics manageable while still allowing residual enrichment afterward.

2. **Use pre up‑projection blocks whenever you switch to matrix memories or other mechanisms that naturally live in high dimensions (mLSTM).**  
   This guarantees that all key/value interactions are captured before any dimensionality reduction step.

In practice, xLSTMs mix these two block types in ratios like `xLSTM[7:1]` or `xLSTM[1:0]` depending on desired balance between memory capacity and sequential expressiveness—a strategy empirically shown to achieve competitive perplexities on large language modelling datasets while maintaining linear time complexity with respect to sequence length.

--- 

This guidance is central to reproducing the reported performance gains of xLSTMs over Transformers and SSMs while keeping training stable across billions of parameters.+

### "xLSTM[7:1]" vs "xLSTM[1:0]": how does changing the ratio of mLSTM to sLSTM blocks trade off capacity and speed?
Question:
"xLSTM[7:1]" means 7 mLSTM blocks out of 8 total; "xLSTM[1:0]" uses only one xLSTM block presumably sLSTM or something else? The notation is confusing – what exactly do these acronyms mean, and how does varying this ratio impact model perplexity, parallelism, and compute cost?
Answer:
**Short‑answer** –  
`xLSTM[a:b]` specifies how many of the *n* residual blocks in the model are *mLSTM* (matrix‑memory, fully parallelizable) versus *sLSTM* (scalar‑memory with memory‑mixing, not fully parallelisable).  
The ratio is written as “$(a:b)$”, so **xLSTM[7:1]** means **7 / 8 ≈ 0.875** of the blocks are mLSTM and the remaining 1 / 8 are sLSTM (for a total of 8 blocks).  
Conversely **xLSTM[1:0]** is a pure sLSTM model (1 block in the numerator and 0 in the denominator, i.e. 100 % sLSTM).  

Below is a concrete walk‑through of what that notation implies for capacity, speed and memory.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. What the two block types actually do

| Block | Core idea | Computation | Parallelisation | Memory footprint | Typical use |
|-------|-----------|-------------|-----------------|------------------|-------------|
| **mLSTM** | Matrix cell $\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ updated by a *covariance* rule $\mathbf{C}_t=\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top$. No hidden–hidden connections → **unrolled recurrence can be turned into a single matrix operation**. | $d^2$‑wide matrix update + matrix×vector retrieval per step. | **Fully parallelizable** on GPUs; similar cost to FlashAttention or Mamba’s inner loop. | Keep $d\times d$ matrices only once; no extra parameters for recurrence. |
| **sLSTM** | Scalar cell with exponential gating; allows *memory mixing* across multiple scalar cells via hidden‑to‑hidden recurrent weights $\mathbf{R}$. The hidden state recursion is inherently sequential because each time step depends on the previous hidden state. | One scalar update per gate plus a recurrent matrix multiply ($\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$). | Mostly sequential; a specialised CUDA kernel still runs in ~2× the speed of mLSTM on modern GPUs. | Only scalar memories per head/ cell; no huge matrices. |

The paper explicitly notes that “**mLSTM does not require parameters but is computationally expensive through its $d\times d$ matrix memory and $d \times d$ update. We trade off memory capacity against computational complexity**”. Thus mLSTM gives *more expressive storage* at the cost of heavier matrix ops.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2. How the ratio trades capacity for speed

### Capacity (expressive power)

* **mLSTM alone (xLSTM[1:0])** gives each cell a full $d\times d$ outer‑product memory – essentially an associative memory that can store up to $O(d^2)$ key–value pairs with linear separability (Section 3.2). In synthetic experiments (multi‑query associative recall) this variant matches or outperforms state‑of‑the‑art models.
* **sLSTM alone (xLSTM[0:1])**, while scalar, can *mix* information from many preceding states via its recurrent head weights. This is crucial for “state tracking” problems like regular grammar reversal or parity tasks – where Transformers or SSMs lack that mixture.
* Mixing both (**xLSTM[7:1]**) gives you both: most blocks contribute huge associative capacity while one block still keeps time‑dependent mixing to counteract drifting errors over very long sequences.

Empirically, the learned trade‑off shows that adding even a single sLSTM block makes a noticeable difference on tasks that need strict temporal order, whereas dropping all sLSTMs hurts those problems considerably.

### Speed & memory

* The per‑step wall‑clock time scales roughly as:
  \[
  T_{\text{m}} \propto d^2,\qquad
  T_{\text{s}} \approx k\,T_{\text{m}}\;\;(k < 2)
  \]
  because sLSTM requires an extra recurrent multiply over $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$.
* Consequently **xLSTM[7:1] runs almost as fast as a pure mLSTM model**, while still gaining the small but crucial mixing capability.
* Memory usage is linear in sequence length for both variants because neither retains hidden activations for backward passes – only the current state is needed.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3. Practical implications for model design

| Variant | Typical size of residual blocks in benchmarks | Training beams |
|--------|----------------------------------------------|----------------|
| xLSTM[1:0] (pure mLSTM) | ~70 % of blocks in large models (e.g., ~33ℓ vs 48 in paper) | Works well for many LLMs but struggles on highly sequential tests if not mixed |
| xLSTM[7:1] (mostly mLSTM) | One sLSTM head at every ~8th layer; used throughout scale experiments up to 1.3 B parameters | Provides best perplexities while staying fast |
| xLSTM[0:1] (pure sLSTM) | Rarely used alone because of limited expressive storage; mainly as an ablation |

When we speak about “ratio” we implicitly assume a *fixed total number of layers*. If you double the number of layers but keep the ratio constant, you simply obtain twice as many m/s memories – all else equal.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4. Why these differences matter for language modelling

* **Long range extrapolation:** With an adequate number of sLSTM blocks at higher depths, xLSTMs maintain predictive quality even when asked to generate or score texts longer than they were trained on (up to >16k tokens). The paper’s Figures 6–8 demonstrate this advantage clearly over pure transformers.
* **Parameter efficiency:** Each block in an xLSTM contains far fewer trainable parameters than a Transformer self‑attention sub‑layer yet still reaches similar perplexities with comparable parameter counts (~350 M–1.3 B). This efficiency stems from avoiding learned softmax weights and benefit from built‑in exponential gating.
* **Inference throughput:** Because memory footprints are constant w.r.t sequence length, xLLMs can batch more tokens at inference time than Transformers whose forward pass grows quadratically with context size. This gives higher throughput at larger batch sizes while keeping latency acceptable.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Bottom line

- **xLSTM[a:b]** simply tells you how much of your network consists of matrix–memory blocks (mLSTM) versus scalar–memory + mixing blocks (sLSTM).
- A higher *a/b* ratio gives more associative capacity (better on recall-type tasks) but retains high throughput due to full parallelism.
- Inserting at least one sLSTM block per few layers repairs long‑range order guarantees without hurting speed dramatically.
- For most practical language–modeling bets, *xLSTM[7:1]* strikes the best balance between perplexity and inference performance as shown by the authors’ experiments on SlimPajama datasets.

Thus, when people ask “what does xLSTM[7:1] vs xLSTM[1:0] mean?”, they are simply asking how many layers use matrix memories vs scalar memories—this choice fundamentally tunes how quickly and how richly your model can learn and generate text.

### "Stabilization state" $m_t$ – when exactly should I use $	ilde{i}_t - m_t$ instead of $	ilde{i}_t$ for exponential gates?
Question:
Equation (5) shows a stabilizer state and adjusted gates $i'_t$, $f'_t$. The paper claims replacing $f_t$ with $f'_t$ and $i_t$ with $i'_t$ does not change outputs or gradients. Under what circumstances is this adjustment necessary? For example, should I always compute with stabilized gates when training large models with exponential activations?
Answer:
**Answer**

The stabilised gates in the paper are a *numerical safeguard*, not a functional change.  
Equation (5) shows the “stabilizer state” \(m_t\) and the two adjusted gates

\[
i'_t \;=\; \exp\bigl(\tilde i_t-m_t\bigr)\,,\qquad
f'_t \;=\; \exp\bigl(\log f_t+m_{t-1}-m_t\bigr),
\]

where \(\tilde i_t\) and \(f_t\) are the raw pre‑activations that would otherwise be fed into an exponential or sigmoid.  
In Appendix A the authors prove that replacing the original gates by these *normalised* versions does **not** alter the forward‑pass output of the cell or any loss gradient.  The only reason to do it is to keep the internal values of \(i_t\), \(f_t\) from blowing up numerically.

---

### When do you actually need it?

1. **Large models with exponential activations**  
   The paper introduces both $\exp(\tilde i_t)$ and $\exp(\tilde f_t)$ in the sLSTM/mLSTM cells.  When these exponentials grow beyond a few dozen, double‑precision floating‑point numbers can overflow or under‑flow, producing `nan` or `inf`.  In such settings you must normalise using $m_t$.  
   Practically, if you observe training loss suddenly spikes or gradients vanish, check whether any gate value exceeds a safe range (e.g. $|\log\text{-gate}|>40$).  Applying the stabilisation formula will bring every gate back into $[0,1]$.

2. **Very long back‑propagation horizons**  
   Even if each individual gate value stays moderate at a single time step, the recursive update of $m_t$ aggregates log‑gates over time.  For thousands of steps this running maximum can keep shifting, which is precisely what $m_t$ was meant to compensated.  For models that process sequences longer than a few thousand steps (the usual case for language modelling and time–series), you should enable stabilisation.

3. **When you use a non‑standard implementation**  
   If you implement your own LSTM cell that mirrors (5) but forgets to copy the stabilisation routine from the reference code, you risk numerical errors that are hard to debug.  Adopting the implementation from the authors’ GitHub repository automatically applies it.

4. **If you intentionally restrict gates to sigmoid**  
   Note that stabilisation is only required for *exponential* gates.  A vanilla LSTM with sigmoid input/forget gates does not need it; no overflow can occur there because $\sigma(x)\in(0,1)$.

---

### Should you *always* compute with stabilized gates?

Not strictly – if your network is small (a few layers), uses only normal sigmoid gates, and your hardware consistently shows stable gradients, you may omit it without affecting performance.  
However, in almost all modern large‑scale experiments cited in the paper (billions of parameters, 15–300 B training tokens, contexts up to 16 k tokens), exponential gating is indispensable for stability. The authors themselves turn on stabilisation for every training run reported in Section 3.

---

### Bottom line

The adjusted gates $i'_t,f'_t$ are a preventive measure against numerical overflow/underflow when using exponentials in LSTM variants that feature *exponential gating*.  They leave the mathematical semantics untouched because they simply shift all log‑gate values by a running maximum that keeps exponents bounded.  As soon as you start scaling to billions of parameters or set sequence lengths beyond a few hundred steps, activate this stabilisation routine; otherwise you can skip it if your setup never encounters inflated gate values.

### "Memory mixing" – how does it differ from the traditional cell-to-cell mixing seen in multi‑cell LSTMs?
Question:
"sLSTM can have multiple memory cells…memory mixing via recurrent connections R_z, R_i…with exponential gating as an effect." How exactly are these matrices applied; do they mix cell states directly or only through gated inputs? Is there any overlap with Gated Linear Units or other gated RNN designs?
Answer:
**Answer**

In a classic multi‑cell LSTM the *hidden* vector \(h_{t-1}\) is fed into the computations of every gate and the cell input through a *recurrent* weight matrix \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\).  
That matrix mixes information from all previous cells *within the same time step* – it is what LSTMs call **memory mixing**. When you write the forward equations for an LSTM with \(d\) cells you get

\[
\begin{aligned}
z_t &= \varphi \!\bigl( W_zx_t + R_z h_{t-1} + b_z \bigr),\\
i_t &= \sigma \!\bigl( W_ix_t + R_i h_{t-1} + b_i \bigr),\\
f_t &= \sigma \!\bigl( W_fx_t + R_f h_{t-1} + b_f \bigr),\\
o_t &= \sigma \!\bigl( W_ox_t + R_o h_{t-1} + b_o \bigr),\\
c_t &= f_t\odot c_{t-1} + i_t\odot z_t,\\
h_t &= o_t\odot\psi(c_t).
\end{aligned}
\]

Each \(R_\bullet\) can reshuffle the content of the hidden state before it multiplies a particular gate or the memory input. That is **cell‑to‑cell mixing** – the hidden activity of one cell contributes to the update of other cells.

---

### How does *memory mixing* in xLSTM differ?

The authors keep exactly this mechanism in their **sLSTM** (scalar‑memory LSTM) variant, but they add two new ingredients that change how mixing behaves:

| Aspect | Classic LSTM | xLSTM sLSTM |
|--------|--------------|-------------|
| Gate activations | Sigmoid for all gates | Exponential for the **input** gate and either sigmoid or exponential for the **forget** gate |
| Normalizer | None | A normalizer state \(n_t = f_t n_{t-1}+ i_t\) that keeps track of how much future forget gates will shrink previously stored values |
| Mixing | Driven only by \(R_z,R_i,R_f,R_o\) and ordinary sigmoid gates | The same matrices are still used, but because gates can take unbounded exponential values, the *contribution weight* can grow or shrink extremely fast. The normalizer guarantees that a huge input doesn’t swamp earlier information; rather, the exponential gate behaves like a **dynamic weighting factor** that can strongly favor new content or preserve old content based on future forget factors. |

So in sLSTM *memory mixing* is still a hidden‑to‑hidden operation, but now each contribution is modulated by an exponentially growing (or decaying) gate and normalised by \(n_t\). Importantly, this modulation lets early information be revised much more aggressively than in a vanilla LSTM: if a later token triggers a large \(i_t=\exp(\tilde{i}_t)\), its key/value pair will dominate subsequent cell states until large forgets come along.

---

### Do the matrices mix cell states directly?

No. The recurrent matrices act on the **hidden** vector, not on the raw cell vectors. The cell state update itself remains purely additive:

\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t .
\]

Thus mixing happens *indirectly*: every component of \(h_{t-1}\) is combined through \(R_\bullet\) and then multiplied by the corresponding gate scalar before it influences either \(z_t,i_t,f_t,o_t\). Only through that gating chain does information from one cell causally influence another cell’s updates.

---

### Relation to other gated designs (GLU, Gated Linear Units)

Gated Linear Units (GLUs) typically compute

\[
h = (W_x x)\,\otimes\, \sigma(W_g x)
\]

where \(W_xx\) and \(W_gx\) are linear transforms of the same input sequence, and \(\otimes\) denotes element‑wise product. GLUs have no recurrent connection; they are purely feed‑forward.

In contrast:

* **sLSTM** keeps temporal recurrence: each step’s hidden activates gates that influence the next step.
* The exponential gating in sLSTM is more extreme than GLU’s sigmoid gate; in practice it can behave like an *additive* multiplier that quickly pushes values to zero or to the raw input magnitude.
* The normalizer in sLSTM adds an extra *forget context awareness* that is absent in GLU.

So while both GLU and sLSTM use multiplicative gating, sLSTM’s gates interact across time steps via hidden‑to‑hidden weights and through an explicit memory mix – something GLUs lack.

---

### Bottom line

Memory mixing in xLSTM’s sLSTM variant retains the classic hidden‑to‑hidden replay of information but gives it two new degrees of freedom:

1. Gates can have arbitrary magnitude (exponential), allowing rapid revision of stored content.
2. A running normalizer controls overall scale regardless of how extreme those magnitudes become.

These changes make sLSTM far better at **state tracking** – revising past memory based on new evidence – while still preserving all usual LSTM benefits. It differs fundamentally from other gated designs like GLU because it preserves temporal recurrence and enriches it with dynamic scaling rather than merely causal flow within one time step.

### "Maximum over future forget gates" – clarification on correct implementation
Question:
The paper mentions that the normalizer state sums 'the product of the input gate times all future forget gates'. However Eq. (3) updates $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$. Is this equivalent to accumulating product terms over future time steps? If so, could you give an algebraic derivation showing that expansion yields $rac{c_T}{n_T}$ equals some weighted average over past inputs adjusted by forget products?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the scalar sLSTM the *normalizer state* is updated by the recurrence  

\[
n_t \;=\; f_t\,n_{t-1}+i_t ,\qquad n_0\!=\!0 .
\]

Because the recurrence is linear, it can be unfolded explicitly.  Writing the
first few steps shows the pattern:

\[
\begin{aligned}
n_1 &= i_1,\\[2pt]
n_2 &= f_2\,i_1+i_2,\\[2pt]
n_3 &= f_3\,f_2\,i_1 + f_3\,i_2 + i_3 .
\end{aligned}
\]

The coefficient of \(i_k\) in \(n_T\) is the product of all forget gates that
occur **after** time \(k\), i.e.

\[
n_T = \sum_{k=1}^{T} i_k \prod_{j=k+1}^{T} f_j .
\]

This can be proved by induction: assume the form holds for \(n_{t-1}\); then

\[
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t
     = f_t \!\!\sum_{k=1}^{t-1}\! i_k \prod_{j=k+1}^{t-1} f_j \;+\; i_t
     = \sum_{k=1}^{t-1}\! i_k \prod_{j=k+1}^{t} f_j \;+\; i_t ,
\]

which is exactly the same sum with the upper limit \(t\).
Thus the normalizer state indeed equals the cumulative sum of input gates,
each weighted by all future forget gates.

The cell state evolves as  

\[
c_T = \sum_{k=1}^{T} i_k z_k \prod_{j=k+1}^{T} f_j ,
\]

because every new input \(z_k\) is multiplied by its input gate \(i_k\) and
propagated forward through all subsequent forget gates.

Dividing the two expressions gives

\[
\frac{c_T}{n_T}
   = \frac{\displaystyle 
          \sum_{k=1}^{T} i_k z_k \prod_{j=k+1}^{T} f_j}
         {\displaystyle 
          \sum_{k=1}^{T} i_k      \prod_{j=k+1}^{T} f_j } .
\]

The denominator normalises these terms, so that each data point \(z_k\) is
weighted by its *effective contribution at time \(T\)*,
namely \(i_k\, \prod_{j=k+1}^{T}f_j / n_T\).  
Hence  

\[
h_T = o_T\, \psi(c_T/n_T)
     = o_T\, \psi
       \!\!\left(
           \sum_{k=1}^{T}
             w_k^{(T)} z_k
       \right),
     \qquad  
     w_k^{(T)}=\frac{i_k\, \prod_{j=k+1}^{T}f_j}{n_T},
\]

and the hidden state at time \(T\) is a (non‑linear) weighted average of all
past inputs, where the weights are exactly the product of input and future
forget gates.
This shows that Eq. (3) implements exponential gating with a normaliser that
stores precisely the contribution of future forget gates, and that the ratio
\(c_T/n_T\) is a weighted average of past inputs adjusted by those same
products.
