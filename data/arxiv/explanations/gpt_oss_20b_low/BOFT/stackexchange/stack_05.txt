\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Is the multiplicative dropout scheme in BOFT equivalent to standard dropout applied after weight multiplication?
Question:
Multiplicative dropout replaces randomly selected butterfly components or diagonal blocks with identity matrices during training. When training proceeds, these components are removed multiplicatively. How does this affect gradient flow compared to standard dropout which zeros out activations or weights additively? What is the mathematical motivation behind choosing identity matrices rather than scaling factors or random signs?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes, the multiplicative‑dropout scheme used in BOFT is mathematically equivalent to applying a conventional dropout mask *after* the weight‑multiplication, provided that the mask is applied to the individual butterfly components (or the fine‑grained diagonal blocks inside them) rather than to the input activations. The key difference lies in *where* and *how* the mask is applied: normal dropout zeros out either weights (LoRA‑style additive drop) or activations (standard dropout), whereas BOFT’s multiplicative dropout zeroes out entire (sub)matrices of the *orthogonal transform itself*. This preserves orthogonality during training while still regularising the effective number of active parameters.

---

### 1.  The BOFT weight‑update and its multiplicative dropout

A forward pass in BOFT takes the form  

\[
\mathbf z \;=\;\bigl(\mathbf R(m,b)\,\mathbf W^0\bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf x,
\qquad
\mathbf R(m,b) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf B}^b_{(d,i)} ,
\]

where every factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}^b_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component that can be
permuted into a block–diagonal matrix with $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks.  
Initially each component is set to identity (Cayley skew–symmetric initialized to zero).

**Multiplicative dropout** chooses a random subset of these components (with probability
$p_1$) and, inside each chosen component, a random subset of its diagonal blocks
(with probability $p_2$). The selected blocks are replaced by **identity matrices**.
Because identity acts as a neutral element in matrix multiplication,
the overall product $\mathbf R(m,b)$ still remains a valid orthogonal matrix – only its
effective rank changes during training.

---

### 2.  Why this is equivalent to standard dropout applied after multiplication

Consider a single butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf B}$.  
Without dropout its contribution is

\[
\tilde{\mathbf B} = (\text{permute})\,\text{diag}\!\bigl(\operatorname{rot}_1,\dots,\operatorname{rot}_{d/2b}\bigr),
\]

where each $\operatorname{rot}_k$ is a $2b\times2b$ rotation matrix parameterised by a skew‑symmetric block.

If we first multiply the pretrained weight matrix by this component and later apply
standard **activation** dropout, we are effectively masking elements of $\mathbf R( m,b)$
after it has acted on all subsequent layers.  
In contrast, applying dropout **inside** the component (i.e., replacing some rotations by
the identity matrix) is mathematically identical to multiplying $\mathbf R(m,b)$ with a
diagonal mask matrix $\mathbf M$ whose entries are either $0$ (identity block) or $1$
(full rotation). That is,

\[
\text{Dropout}( \tilde{\mathbf B}) \;=\; \tilde{\mathbf B}\,\mathbf M
          = (\text{permute})\,\underbrace{
            \text{diag}\!\bigl(\underbrace{\operatorname{rot}_1}_{(0-1)},\, …\,,
            \underbrace{\operatorname{rot}_k}_{(0-1)} , …\, \bigr)}_{\text{subblocks}} ,
\]

and since orthogonal matrices satisfy $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{-1}= \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,T}$,
the whole product remains orthogonal as long as every retained block stays orthogonal.
Thus the mask behaves exactly like a conventional dropout applied *after* the full
matrix multiplication: it zeros out certain linear transformations but leaves the rest
unchanged.

---

### 3.  Intuition behind using *identity* instead of random scalars or signs

Uniformly sampling random scalars or signs would break strict orthogonality of each factor;
the product would no longer preserve pairwise angles between neurons—a core motivation
of OFT/BOFT. By replacing an entire sub‑block with an **identity**, we perform one of two things:

1. **Preserve exact orthogonality**: an identity block keeps its column space unchanged,
   so multiplying with it does not alter distances or angles.
2. **Reduce effective rank gradually**: during training many sub‑blocks will be turned into
   identities, automatically shrinking the capacity of the overall transform without
   introducing ill‑conditioned scaling factors.

If we had used random signs (say $+1,-1$), we would distort rotations and potentially
introduce reflections that are not smoothly learnable under Cayley parametrisation.
If we had used random scalars $c_\ell\in[0,1]$, we would disturb the unitary spectrum,
making gradients unstable (orthogonality relies on all singular values being exactly one).

Thus identity blocks are the minimal way to “switch off” parts of an orthogonal factor
while still allowing back‑propagation to adjust the remaining degrees of freedom.

---

### 4.  Gradient flow comparison

Because all components retain their orthogonality properties after masking,
back‑propagation proceeds exactly as if no mask were applied:  

* The gradient w.r.t. each retained block follows from  
  \[
  \frac{\partial L}{\partial (\text{rot}_k)}
     = 2\, (\text{rest of }\mathbf R)^T\, 
         \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf z}\,\mathbf x^T .
  \]
* For masked blocks, gradients are zero because those blocks are frozen at identity and do not participate in learning.

Hence, gradient flow behaves identically to standard dropout applied after weight multiplication,
but with an additional guarantee that no intermediate non‑orthogonal maps creep in during training—a key advantage for maintaining spectral properties and stability.

---

### 5.  Broader connections

* **Orthogonal fine‑tuning**: BOFT extends OFT by replacing dense orthogonal matrices
   with products of few sparse butterfly factors; multiplicative dropout gives it similar regularisation power as LoRA’s additive drop, but with less chance of damaging angular geometry.
* **Dropout as structural regularisation**: While standard activation dropout injects noise at feature level, multiplicative dropout enforces sparsity at structural level (e.g., halving communication links in a butterfly network).
* **Spectral preservation**: Because all retained transformations stay orthogonal, spectral norms stay unchanged—exactly what one expects from “dropout after multiplication”.

---

### Bottom line

Multiplicative dropout in BOFT can be seen as *masking* particular branches of an orthogonal beam splitter before they act on data. This mask behaves just like conventional dropout applied after full weight multiplication but preserves exact orthogonality by substituting entire sub-blocks with identities rather than shuffling scalars or signs. The result is stable gradients, clean spectral properties and an elegant regularising mechanism that keeps training robust while reusing most of the pre‑trained knowledge.
