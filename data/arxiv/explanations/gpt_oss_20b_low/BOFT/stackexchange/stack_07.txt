\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does preserving pairwise angles between neurons lead to better generalization?
Question:
Both OFT and BOFT claim that maintaining pairwise angles between neuron vectors preserves pretraining knowledge and improves generalization. Intuitively I see that an orthogonal transform preserves dot products up to sign. Could someone articulate why this property is more beneficial than simply using small learning rates or L2 regularization? Are there theoretical results linking angle preservation to lower information loss during fine‑tuning?
Answer:
**Why preserving pair‑wise angles—i.e. keeping the weight matrix on the orthogonal group—helps fine‑tuning**

---

### 1.  What does “angle preservation” really mean for a linear layer?

For a frozen pre‑trained weight matrix \(W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\) let  
\(w^{0}_{i}\) be its \(i\)-th column (a *neuron*).  
The *angles* between any two neurons are captured by the inner product
\[
\langle w^{0}_{i},\,w^{0}_{j}\rangle
   = (W^{0})_{:,i}^{\!\top}(W^{0})_{:,j}.
\]
Intuitively, these inner products encode the *semantic similarity* that
has been learned during pre‑training: neurons that activate together on
the same kind of input patterns will have a large positive dot‑product,
while unrelated neurons will have a small or negative one.

In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its parameter‑efficient variant BOFT we
replace the linear map by

\[
z=(RW^{0})^{\!\top}x,\qquad R\in O(d),
\]

so that all neurons are rotated by the same orthogonal matrix
\(R\). Because \(R\) is orthogonal,
\[
\langle w_{i},\,w_{j}\rangle
   =(Rw^{0}_{i})^{\!\top}(Rw^{0}_{j})
   ={w^{0}_{i}}^{\!\top}w^{0}_{j},
\]
which guarantees that **every pairwise angle is preserved exactly**.
Thus the full relational geometry learned in pre‑training is kept intact
and only an overall rotation (or reflection) of the neuron space is used
to adapt to a new task.

---

### 2.  Why an exact angle preservation is preferable to small learning
rates or \(L_{2}\) regularisation

| Technique | What it changes | What it keeps | Consequence for generalisation |
|-----------|-----------------|---------------|--------------------------------|
| Small LR | Shrinks the step size of **every** weight update | Leaves *all* angles untouched | Slower adaptation; risk of under‑fitting |
| \(L_{2}\) penalty | Forces individual weights toward zero | Alters inner products arbitrarily | Can change semantic geometry cheaply |
| Orthogonal finetuning | Adds a single **orthogonal** transformation | *All* pairwise angles stay exactly the same | Preserves pre‑trained geometry without diminishing representational power |

The key point is that small learning rates and \(L_{2}\) regularisation act *independently* on each element of \(W\); they have no built‑in awareness that two weights participate together in defining a direction in feature space. Consequently,

* The optimisation may drift toward a region where some important directions are collapsed or overly amplified, even though the overall magnitude stays small.
* The set of reachable weight matrices grows with many degrees of freedom, which makes overfitting easier on a small downstream dataset.

In contrast, OFT/BOFT forces the new matrix to lie on an *orbit* of the orthogonal group:
\(RW^{0}\). This orbit is a low‑dimensional manifold whose size grows linearly
(in \(\log d\) parameters for BOFT). Each point on this manifold still has exactly the same angles as \(W^0\), so the search space becomes highly structured and intrinsically regularised – a situation that empirical studies show leads to better generalisation.

---

### 3.  Information preserving + spectral stability

Orthogonality preserves not only inner products but also the Euclidean norm:
for any vector \(x,\; \|Rx\|=\|x\|\).
This guarantees that the spectral norm (largest singular value) of the
weight matrix does **not** change:
if \(\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_r)\) are the singular values of \(W^0\),
then \(\operatorname{span}(RW^0)=U\,R\,V^\top\Sigma V^\top=U'\Sigma V^\top,\)
and \(\sigma_{\max}(RW^0)=\sigma_{\max}(W^0)\).

Preserving spectral norm is known to stabilise optimisation:
large singular values amplify gradients, leading to exploding gradients,
while very small ones deflate them and slow learning. By keeping the same spectrum,
Orthogonal finetuning avoids these pitfalls without any tuning of learning rates.

---

### 4.  Intuitive connection to “learning bilinear similarity”

Writing each row of \(RW^0\) as a bilinear form gives  
\(z_i = {w^{0}_i}^{\!\top}R\,x.\)
Thus fine‑tuning learns a *single* bilinear similarity function between
the original neurons and inputs, with a very strong prior: \(R\) must be orthogonal.
This is reminiscent of distance–metric learning where one learns a rotation/reflection so that similar points stay close.
Because rotations preserve all pairwise distances inside any subspace,
the model retains the continuum of similarities it already knows while answering a new query efficiently.

---

### 5.  Empirical evidence & theoretical backing

The authors report several experiments to confirm that:

1. **Smaller parameter counts** (BOFT uses \(\mathcal O(d \log d)\)
   versus \(\mathcal O(d^2)\) for vanilla OFT and many others for LoRA)
   already give higher downstream accuracy on Vision Transformer, GPT–like language models,
   and Stable Diffusion adapters.

2. **Lower training loss plateau** – because most degrees of freedom are already fixed by orthogonality,
   optimisation concentrates on meaningful rotations.

3. **Theoretical limit** – Theorem 4.1 shows that with enough butterfly layers BOFT can approximate *any* orthogonal matrix,
   so we are not sacrificing representational power.

More importantly, literature such as Liu & Zhou (2021) links hyperspherical energy – which depends only on pairwise angles – to better generalisation in large models.
Since Orthogonal finetuning holds this energy constant, it directly preserves whatever level of hyperspherical spread was discovered during pre‑training.

---

### 6.  Bottom line

Preserving pairwise angles via an orthogonal transformation keeps intact:

- The full geometric structure learned during pre‑training.
- The spectral properties that stabilise optimisation.
- A low‑dimensional but expressive hypothesis class that acts as an implicit regulariser.

Small learning rates or simple penalties cannot guarantee any of these simultaneously; they merely shift weights without caring about their mutual geometric relationships.
Hence ORTHOGONAL FINETUNING/NOMORALLY BOFT can achieve better generalisation while keeping trainable parameters minimal – exactly what parameter‑efficient adaptation needs.
