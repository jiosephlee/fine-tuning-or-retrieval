# Foundations of Model Finetuning

Large foundation models—such as GPT‑3, Vision‑Transformer backbones, and Stable Diffusion—have become the bedrock of modern artificial intelligence. Their parametric scale (hundreds of billions of weights) drives unprecedented performance but simultaneously imposes severe barriers on training costs, storage requirements, and deployment feasibility. Consequently, the community has turned to *parameter‑efficient finetuning* (PEFT) as a practical pathway for adapting a pretrained backbone to a downstream task without incurring the expense of full retraining.

In this chapter we establish the theoretical and practical landscape that motivates Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). We first outline the architectural and training difficulties that arise with large foundation models. Then we survey the three core families of PEFT—**weight‑finetuning**, **adapters**, and **prompt tuning**—highlighting how each family seeks to balance fidelity to the pretrained knowledge with computational tractability. Finally, we sketch how OFT fits within this ecosystem, introducing its key intuition: preserving pairwise angles between neurons through an orthogonal transformation.

---

## Large foundation models and scalability challenges

Complexity in foundation models scales super‑linearly with the size of their parameter space. Even modest increases in depth or width result in a combinatorial explosion in memory consumption and floating‑point operations per forward pass. Training a model like GPT‑3 requires distributed multi‑GPU clusters, days of compute, and terabytes of dataset storage. When such a model is released publicly or for research purposes, it is prohibitive for most practitioners to fine‑tune it from scratch because:

1. **Compute cost**: Each backward pass involves gradients for every parameter; the gradient accumulation overhead grows with the square root at least of parameter count.
2. **Memory constraints**: Gradient buffers must hold either activations or partial derivatives during backpropagation; larger layers quickly saturate GPU DRAM.
3. **Energy consumption**: A full training run draws significant electricity, rendering it economically unfeasible for many labs.

Because these obstacles are inherent to the *scale* rather than any specific architecture design, researchers have focused on *efficiency enablers* that allow existing foundation weights to be repurposed. The rise of PEFT addresses this directly.

---

## Three PEFT families: weight‑finetuning, adapters, and prompts

The field has stratified PEFT into three broad families based on where trainable parameters appear in the computational graph.

### 1. Weight‑finetuning

Here we keep the network architecture fixed and selectively adjust a subset of the original weights. Classic fine‑tuning inflates every matrix column by adding an adjustment vector; however, modern weight‑finetuning methods reduce overhead by:

- Applying a low–rank update (LoRA): \( W^{\text{ft}} = W^0 + A B^\top \) where \( A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\), \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}\), and \( r \ll d,n\).
- Employing group sparsity or block structures that force many weights to remain frozen while updating only strategic degrees of freedom.

The main advantage is that there is no inference latency; from a deployment standpoint we can directly replace \(W^0\) with \(W^{\text{ft}}\). The downside is that by discarding or oversimplifying certain parameters we risk losing useful representation capacity.

### 2. Adapters

Adapters augment each transformer block (or other submodule) with small feed‑forward networks (or linear bottlenecks) that are independently trainable:

- A tiny two–layer MLP receives the block’s hidden state, processes it through new weights \(A,B\), then mixes it back into the original stream.
- The pretraining weights remain frozen; only adapter parameters are learned.

Adapters introduce negligible computation compared to full fine‑tuning yet provide an extra transformation capacity tailored per downstream task. Their expressiveness depends on adapter depth and dimension tunables.

### 3. Prompt tuning

Prompt tuning regulates input representations rather than internal weights:

- Prefix tokens (special learnable embeddings) are concatenated to hidden states at certain layers.
- These prompts act as task instructions without touching any pretrained weight.
- It is particularly useful for language models where “prompt engineering” can guide generation.

Prompt tuning requires far fewer parameters but only modulates early representations; its effect on deeper semantic layers can be limited unless higher–level prompts are used.

---

## Motivation for preserving pretrained knowledge

All three PEFT schemes implicitly assume that we would like *intact* pretrained knowledge: parameters have already learned robust statistical correlations from massive corpora or image collections; we wish downstream tasks to exploit most of this prior physics without destabilizing it.

A common heuristic is to use very small learning rates so mistakes will barely shift any weight away from its initial pretraining value. But even small updates can dramatically alter pairwise distances between neurons if those distances are not explicitly constrained.

Orthogonal Finetuning addresses this by formally preserving all pairwise angles between neurons throughout adaptions:

- An orthogonal matrix \(R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) satisfies \(R^\top R = I_d\).
- Applying \(R\) left–multiplication on every linear layer maps neuron columns (weight vectors) through a rigid rotational field.
- Distances and angles survive because rotation does not alter inner products: \(\langle w_i R , w_j R\rangle = \langle w_i , w_j\rangle\).

In contrast to additive updates like LoRA which may stretch or skew representations arbitrarily, OFT guarantees that all hidden features remain on the same hypersphere surface as before training—only their orientation changes.

---

## Overview of related work: LoRA, BitFit, etc., leading up to OFT

| Method | Paradigm | Core idea | Parameter count | Typical use case |
|--------|----------|-----------|-----------------|------------------|
| LoRA | Weight finetuning | Low–rank additive update | \(\mathcal{O}(dr)\) | Language & vision fine‑tuning |
| BitFit | Weight finetuning | Bias update only | \(\mathcal{O}(N)\) | Minimalistic tuning |
| Adapter families | Adapter tuning | Small bottleneck MLPs added per block | Depends on bottleneck dim | Vision & language adapters |
| Prompt tuning | Prompt-based | Learnable prefix embeddings | Very small (\(<0.1\%\)) | Few-shot prompting |

Orthogonal Finetuning enters this tableau by reparameterizing *each* linear transformation as \(W^{\text{ft}} = R^\top W^0\). The fully dense orthogonal matrix contains roughly \(d^2/2\) free parameters (due to skew–symmetric encoding), which yields superior generalization at an unreasonable parameter cost relative to LoRA or adapters when applied to very high dimensional layers (\(d=10^4\)).

This mismatch underlies our motivation: we'd like an orthogonal parameterization that remains fully expressive yet keeps parameter budgets comparable to low–rank approaches. That goal motivates butterfly factorization—an elegant way to compose dense orthogonal matrices from sparse building blocks—framed later in this text as BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning).

---

> **Key Takeaway**  
> Orthogonal Finetuning preserves angle geometry across downstream adaptation but forfeits scalability due to dense orthogonal matrices’ quadratic parameter size. By seeking structured factorizations such as butterfly matrices (introduced subsequently), one can retain orthogonality while achieving nearly linear parameter scaling—opening a new dimension in PEFT research.

# Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) – Concept & Mechanics

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled model‑finetuning paradigm that replaces the usual additive weight updates used in protocols such as LoRA with a multiplicative orthogonal transformation. The core motivation is to preserve the structure of the pretrained network—specifically, the pairwise angles between neurons—while still allowing enough flexibility for downstream task adaptation. Below we unpack the theory, implementation tricks, and practical trade‑offs that make OFT both powerful and parameter‑efficient.

---

## OFT Objective and Its Relation to Spectral Norm Preservation

### The Refinement Import  

A linear layer in a neural network can be written as
\[
\mathbf{z} \;=\; (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x},
\]
where \(\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb R^{d\times n}\) is the pretrained weight matrix, \(d\) is the input dimension, \(n\) the output dimension, \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb R^{d}\) is an input vector, and \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb R^{n}\) is the layer’s pre‑activation output.

During finetuning we normally replace \(\mathbf{W}^0\) with a new training matrix \(\mathbf{W}\). In OFT we constrain this replacement to be *multiplicative*: 
\[
\boxed{\;\; \mathbf{W} \;=\; \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0,\;\;}
\]
where \(\mathbf{R}\in \mathbb R^{d\times d}\) is an *orthogonal* matrix (\(\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}_d\)). The forward pass becomes
\[
\mathbf{z} \;=\; (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\, \mathbf{x}.
\]

This simple change hides a deep intuition: multiplying a column vector of weights \(w_i^0\) by an orthogonal matrix does **not** alter its Euclidean norm or its pairwise angles with other columns. In other words,
- The **singular values** of the weight matrix stay exactly as they were in the pretrained state.
- The *geometry* of neuron subspaces remains intact.

The second fact is crucial because many generalization results rely on maintaining relative distances and angles in weight space. By preserving these properties, OFT encourages the fine‑tuned model to stay close to the pretraining manifold while still being able to adapt functionally to new data.

### Spectral Norm Preservation

If we perform a singular value decomposition (SVD)
\[
\mathbf{W}^0 = \mathbf{U}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{V}^\top ,
\]
then
\[
\mathbf{W}
= \underbrace{\bigl(\underbrace{\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{U}}_{\text{orthogonal}}\bigr)}_{\equiv\,\tilde{\mathbf U}}\ ,
   \underbrace{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\text{diagonal}}\,
   \underbrace{\bigl(\underbrace{\boldsymbol V}_{\,n\times n}}_{\text{|?}}\bigr),
\]
i.e., only the left factor is twisted by an orthogonal matrix. Because orthogonal multiplications do not change singular values,
the maximum singular value (spectral norm) satisfies
\[
\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf W)\;=\;
\sigma_{\max}(\tilde{\boldsymbol U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}) 
= \sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol U\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
=  \sigma_{\max}(\mathbf W^0).
\]
This invariance yields empirically lighter training dynamics—because gradients are not amplified or dampened by spectral changes—and provides a natural regularizer that stabilizes convergence.

---

## Cayley Parameterization of Orthogonal Matrices

The optimization variable in OFT is an orthogonal matrix. Directly optimizing over such matrices is inconvenient because any change must respect the orthogonality constraint. The *Cayley transform* offers a powerful parametrization that automatically guarantees orthogonality while allowing unconstrained optimization over skew‑symmetric matrices.

### Skew‑Symmetric Representation

Let \(Q \in \mathbb R^{d\times d}\) be skew-symmetric:
\[
Q = -Q^\top.
\]
Define
\[
R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}.
\]
The above mapping from \(Q\) to \(R\) has two key properties:

1. **Orthogonality**: For any skew‑symmetric \(Q\), the result \(R\) satisfies \(R^\top R = I_d.\)
2. **Zero Initialization**: Setting \(Q=0.\) immediately yields \(R=I_d.\)

Thus we can choose any real entries for the half entries of \(Q,\) let them be our trainable parameters, and trust that \(R(Q)\) will always lie on the Stiefel manifold of orthogonal matrices.

**Why not directly parameterize using Euler angles?**
Because Euler angles involve trigonometric nonlinearity that can trap gradients in local minima and cause degeneracies when dimensions exceed 3. Cayley’s method treats all dimensions uniformly and preserves convexity in small neighborhoods around identity—a crucial property for fine-tuning where changes are modest.

### Practical Considerations

- For a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix, $Q$ contains $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters.
- Backpropagation through $(I-Q)$ requires solving linear systems each step; efficient libraries (e.g., using Cholesky factorization because $I-Q$ is nonsingular near identity).
- Because $R$ is defined via inverses, it may encounter numerical instability when $I-Q$ approaches singularity. In practice this rarely happens because initialization at identity keeps $||Q||_F$ small throughout finetuning.

---

## Block‑Diagonal OFT: Construction, Zero‑Initialization, Trainable Parameters

The benefit of OFT comes at a computational cost: learning a full dense orthogonal matrix demands $O(d^2)$ parameters—often prohibitive for large embeddings ($d$ in tens of thousands). To control model size while retaining most benefits of multiplicative updates, **block‑diagonal OFT** proposes to constrain $\mathcal R$ as a direct sum of smaller orthogonal blocks:

### Construction

Let 
\(d = r\,b,\)
i.e., split each neuron vector into $r$ contiguous groups each of length $b$. Then define:
```text
          ┌───────┐
          │ R1    │
          │       │
          ├───────┤
          │ R2    │
          │       │
          ├───────┤
      …   │ ...   │
          ├───────┤
          │ Rr    │
          └───────┘
```
Mathematically:
\( 
   R_\text{{block}} = 
   \operatorname{diag}(R_1,R_2,\dots,R_r)\quad 
   ,\, R_i\in {\mathbb R}^{b\times b}, 
   ~\,  i=1,\dots,r .
  \)

Each block $R_i$ is independently parameterized via Cayley transform on its own (skew-symmetric submatrix). Because block-diagonal matrices preserve orthogonality within each block but ignore interaction across different blocks, we conserve expressiveness while cutting parameter count drastically:

- *Trainable Parameters*: For each block size $b$, there are $\frac12 b(b-1)$ learnable parameters. With $r$ blocks we get  
  \(P_\text{{block}} = r\, {b(b-1)\over2}
     = { d\, (b-1)\over2 }.\)

When compared to:
- dense number: ½$d(d-1)$ ≈ $O(d^2)$,
- block-diagonal size with block equal to feature dimension: corresponds to full OFT ($r=1$, $b=d$).

Thus by choosing modest block sizes (e.g., $b=32$ or $64$), we achieve orders-of-magnitude reduction in learnable weights without blowing up computation.

### Zero Initialization and Curvature

Initializing every skew-symmetric entry at zero yields identity blocks:
\( Q_i = 0 ~⇒~ R_i = I_b.\)
Consequentially,
\(R_\text{{block}}(t=0)=I_d.\)
This means *at startup,* all neurons are effectively left unchanged from their pretrained state—a desirable trait for finetuning expensive models.

Moreover, since every update takes place through skew-symmetric perturbations, which are inherently small near zero due to Cayley’s arctangent-like mapping, gradient magnitudes naturally stay stable across layers—a boon for deeper models or very large learners such as vision transformers or GPT-like language models.

### Parameter Efficiency vs Expressivity

You might ask: does this reduced freedom hurt performance? Empirical evidence suggests:

| Method | Trainable Params | Angle Preservation | Spectral Norm | Typical Performance |
|--------|------------------|---------------------|---------------|---------------------|
| Full OFT | ≈ `½*d*(d−1)` | Exact | Unchanged | Slightly higher |
| Block-OFT (`b` small) | ≈ `½*d*(b−1)` | Approximate (within blocks only). | Unchanged | Comparable if `b` not too tiny |
| LoRA add-on | `k*d + k*n` (rank `k`) | None | Power-law modification | Competitive on many tasks |

Thus block-diagonal OFT sits neatly between naive additive fine-tuning and fully multiplicative updates in terms of both inductive bias and parameter budget.

---

## Summary & Takeaways

Orthogonal Finetuning offers an elegant principled alternative to conventional additive updates by ensuring that downstream training does *not* distort inter-neuron relationships or spectral norms—two keys behind transferability from pretraining. The key ingredients are:

1. Multiply all weight columns by an orthogonal transform more stable than adding arbitrary tensors.
2. Use Cayley transform to faithfully encode all possible infinitesimal rotations.
3. Reduce parameter count via block-diagonal factorization without compromising core geometry preservation.

For researchers keen on deploying large pretrained models on memory-limited hardware or who want tighter control over fine-tune dynamics, OFT—in its vanilla form or via its block-diagonal variant—provides a robust starter point that merges mathematical rigor with practical tractability.

# The Parameter‑Efficiency Problem in OFT

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled way to adapt a frozen foundation model by learning an orthogonal matrix \( \bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) that is applied to every weight matrix in the network. The orthogonality of \(\bm{R}\) guarantees that the pairwise angles between neurons—an essential geometric property that encodes the knowledge learned during pre‑training—are preserved throughout fine‑tuning. This fidelity gives OFT a dramatic advantage over ad‑hoc linear adaptation schemes such as LoRA. However, the cost of this generality is steep: a fully dense orthogonal matrix requires \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) trainable parameters, which quickly becomes prohibitive for modern foundation models whose layers can have dimensionalities in the tens of thousands. This chapter scrutinizes the source of this parameter burden, compares it with the block‑diagonal variant of OFT that reduces the parameter count at the expense of expressivity, and formalizes the design desiderata—dense connectivity, minimal free edges, and orthogonality—that must be jointly satisfied to achieve truly parameter‑efficient yet expressive finetuning.

## 1. Parameter Count in Dense vs Block‑Diagonal OFT

### 1.1 Dense Orthogonality and Its Parameter Storm

An arbitrary real orthogonal matrix \( \bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) satisfies  
\[
\bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}=\bm{I}_{d},
\]
and its upper triangular part has \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\) free degrees of freedom (the equivalent number of independent parameters), while the remaining entries are determined by orthogonality. In practice, one still stores all \(d^{2}\) entries during training, because most implementations rely on explicit dense matrices; each entry may be updated and differentiated independently. Even though only half of them are free, every entry must participate in forward and backward passes, incurring a linear cost in memory and a quadratic cost in computation if naïve matrix multiplications are used.

Concretely, consider a single Transformer encoder layer with a hidden size \(d=4096\). A full orthogonal matrix would need to store \(\approx 16.8\times10^{6}\) floats (assuming 32‑bit precision). Scaling to a 175 B parameter model with hundreds of such layers would swamp any regular GPU memory budget.

### 1.2 Block‑Diagonal Bottlenecks and Savings

The block‑diagonal variant expresses \(\bm{R}\) as
\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_{1}, \bm{R}_{2}, \dots , \bm{R}_{r}),
\]
where each block \(\bm{R}_{i} \in \mathbb R^{b\times b}\) is itself an orthogonal matrix and \(b\,r = d\). Now each block introduces at most \(\frac{b(b-1)}{2}\) free parameters; summing across all blocks gives
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{r} \frac{b(b-1)}{2} = r\,\frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{b(d-b)}{2},
\]
which is linear in \(d\). For instance, with \(b=64\), we reduce the number of trainable values from about 16 M down to merely \(64\times(4096-64)/2 = 128\,512\)—a savings factor of over one hundred.

The **catch** is that this savings comes from imposing a skeleton on how neurons are transformed: neurons belonging to distinct blocks cannot communicate via cross‑block mixing. The representation thus becomes an ensemble of isolated subspaces—a rigid structure that may deviate from the original pretraining geometry.

## 2. Costs of Fixed Sparsity Patterns

### 2.1 Hidden Inductive Biases

A fixed sparsity pattern implicitly forces the learned transformation into a highly constrained hypothesis class. In OFT, all matrices share the same sparsity mask but differ only by their values inside that mask. While this sharing reduces variance and stabilizes training, it simultaneously injects an inductive bias: *how* information propagates between neuron dimensions is predetermined by the mask rather than data‑driven.

For certain tasks where inter‑dimension interactions are critical—say, multi‑modal alignment or generative modeling—such rigid masks can severely limit performance because they block necessary cross‑neuron correlations that pretraining has encoded.

### 2.2 Fragmented Expressivity

The capacity to approximate an arbitrary orthogonal transformation depends on how richly structured these pretrained weights can become after learning. A block‑diagonal \(\bm R\) can only generate transformations that respect block boundaries: any linear combination mixing two different blocks cannot arise spontaneously because no non‑zero connections exist between them.

Consequently, without additional mechanisms (e.g., adding cross‑block gates or learning separate off‐diagonal surrogates), OFT with block‐diagonal constraints cannot emulate transforms such as rotations mixing all dimensions simultaneously or canonical transforms like Fourier or Hadamard transforms—classic examples frequently invoked in signal processing but impossible under strict block separation.

### 2.3 Overhead vs Savings Trade‑off

While block‐diagonal sparsity cuts parameters dramatically, it also necessitates extra bookkeeping: at training time one must enforce zero constraints on off‐diagonal elements to preserve sparsity.
Moreover, since blocks are processed independently during forward passes, GPU kernels cannot exploit fused operations across blocks as efficiently as dense kernels do.
In practice this can offset some gains from reduced parameter counts when training throughput improves substantially for large models.

## 3. Expressivity vs Regularity Trade‑off

### 3.1 Defining Expressivity

Expressivity here refers to the ability of your learned transformation to approximate **any** orthogonal matrix within the spaces spanned by your parametrization class:
- **Full** OFT (no sparsity): universal approximator within orthogonal group \(O(d)\).
- **Block–diagonal** OFT: subset where each subspace remains pristine; approximation quality depends on how many blocks we carve out.

A natural way to quantify expressivity is via an *approximation error* metric:
given a target orthogonal matrix \(\bm T\), we define
\[
e_{\text{s}} = \min_{\hat{\bm R} \in S_{\text{s}}} \|\hat{\bm R}-\bm T\|_{F},
\]
where \(S_{\text{s}}\) denotes our structured hypothesis space at sparsity level “s” and \(\|\cdot\|_{F}\) is Frobenius norm.
The smaller \(e_{\text{s}}\), the more expressive our representation for that task.

### 3.2 Regularization Through Structure

Regularity emerges from restricting parameters so that they obey structural constraints—orthogonality on one hand and fixed sparsity patterns on another.
This endowments both Gamma & Beta regulatory properties:
- **Stability** – Orthogonality guarantees spectral norm preservation (spectral stability).
- **Controllability** – The more compact our mask (larger `b`, fewer blocks), the smoother interpolation we get between identity and generalized rotation.

In practice finer‐grained masks (small `b`) afford higher model flexibility but risk overfitting; coarser masks reduce variance but may incur underfitting if blocked modal dependencies become necessary.

### 3.3 Balancing Act: “Desiderata” for an Efficient Orthogonal Transform  

To be parameter efficient yet expressive enough for downstream transfer tasks we enumerate three key desiderata:

| # | Desideratum | Why It Matters | Implication |
|---|-------------|----------------|-------------|
| ① | **Dense Connectivity** | Every input dimension should influence all output dimensions (full rank). | Even if intermediate factors are sparse, their composition must lead to a fully connected final transform |
| ② | **Minimal Free Edges** | Total number of trainable non‐zero entries should be minimized | Reduces memory footprint while maintaining expressivity |
| ③ | **Orthogonality Constraint** | Preserves angles & spectral norm throughout fine‐tuning | Keeps pretrained geometry intact and prevents explosion/vanishing during backprop |

Any candidate factorization scheme must satisfy these concurrently:
> *“An efficient parameterization exists if one can factorize any dense orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse orthogonal matrices that together encode full connectivity (dense mapping) while using only O(d log d) trainable values.”*

By formulating OFT through this pragmatic lens—akin to designating information links across a network graph—we open up design spaces far beyond simple block partitioning. One naturally wonders whether there exist sparse graph topologies more powerful than naïve partitions yet still feasible computationally; what follows set up this investigation meticulously.

# 4. Information Transmission Framework for Matrix Factorization

Large‑scale foundation models are typically adapted to downstream tasks by fine‑tuning a subset of their parameters.  
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a particularly principled approach in which a *dense* orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is learned and applied multiplicatively to the frozen pretrained weight matrix \(\bm{W}^{0}\).  
While OFT preserves the angles between neuron vectors and therefore the semantic knowledge encoded in \(\bm{W}^{0}\), a full orthogonal matrix involves \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) free parameters, far too many for parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning.  
The paper “Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization” introduces a compelling viewpoint to address this problem: **viewing the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix as an information transmission process over a grid‑structured graph**.  
In this chapter we develop that idea in depth, illuminating how the sparsity patterns of factorised matrices correspond to directed edges on a graph, how connectivity constraints enforce density, and how counting *free* edges yields an intuition for parameter efficiency.

---

## 4.1 Grid‑Structured Graph Representation of Matrix Multiplication

Consider a sequence of \(m\) *sparse* square matrices \(\{\bm{B}_{1},\bm{B}_{2},\dots,\bm{B}_{m}\}\) with common dimension \(d\times d\).  
Their product defines a dense matrix
\[
\bm{R} = \bm{B}_{m}\,\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.
\]
A naïve way to think about this multiplication is purely algebraic: each entry of \(\bm{R}\) is a dot product of rows of \(\bm{B}_{m}\) with columns of \(\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_{1}\).  
Instead we interpret each multiplication as *information flowing* from one layer of nodes to the next, inducing a **directed graph** that has \(m+1\) layers, each consisting of \(d\) nodes.

*Layer construction.*  
We denote the nodes in layer \(k\) by \(v^{(k)}_{i}\) where \(k = 0, 1,\dots,m\) and \(i=1,\dots,d\).  
The output vector after multiplying all factors can be regarded as the signal emitted by the nodes in layer \(m\).

*Edges induced by sparse factors.*  
The matrix \(\bm{B}_{k}\) is viewed as providing directed connections from every node in layer \(k-1\) to every node in layer \(k\).  
Specifically, if the entry \((i,j)\) of \(\bm{B}_{k}\) satisfies
\[
(\bm{B}_{k})_{ij} \neq 0,
\]
then there exists a directed edge
\[
v^{(k-1)}_{j} \;\longrightarrow\; v^{(k)}_{i}.
\]
Conversely, if this entry is zero then no edge exists.
Thus, sparsity translates *exactly* into missing edges.

*Visualising the graph.*  
Figure 4.1 (conceptual; not reproduced here) illustrates an example where \(d=4,\; m=5\); each arrow indicates allowed transmission from an earlier node to its neighbours in the next layer.  
The grid shape arises because each layer contains exactly \(d\) nodes and connections only occur between consecutive layers—hence “grid‑structured”.

This representation immediately renders two crucial insights:
1. **Dense entries** in \(\bm{R}\) manifest when there is *at least one path* from every source node on layer 0 to every sink node on layer \(m\).
2. **Free parameters** correspond to the *non-zero* entries used in weight learning; zero entries are either predetermined (by structural constraints such as orthogonality or sparsity pattern design).

---

## 4.2 Directed Edges as Non‑Zero Entries and Connectivity Constraints

To clarify the role of directed edges we formalise two key relationships.

### 4.2.1 Non‑zero Pattern ↔ Edge Presence

Let us define indicator functions
\[
E_{k}(i,j)=
   \begin{cases}
   1 & \text{if } (\bm{B}_{k})_{ij} \neq 0,\\
   0 & \text{otherwise}.
   \end{cases}
    \qquad (i,j=1,\dots,d)
\]
Then the adjacency matrix of directed edge set between layers \(k-1\) and \(k\) is precisely \(E_{k}\).
If we demand that every row and column have at least one non-zero element, then each node has at least one incident edge either outgoing or incoming.

### 4.2.2 Path Existence ↔ Density of Product

Consider any entry \(R_{ij}\) in the product \(\bm{R}\).
Unrolling the multiplication reveals that
\(R_{ij} = 
   (\bm{B}_{m})_{i,a_{m-1}} (\bm{B}_{m-1})_{a_{m-1},a_{m-2}}
   \cdots (\bm{B}_1)_{a_1,j},
   )
   ) ,
   )
   ) ,
    )
 where indices run over valid sums.
Hence **\(R_{ij} = 0\)** iff for **every possible intermediate path** from node \(j\in v^{(0)}_j\) to node \(i \in v^{(m)}_i\), at least one factor along that path contains a zero entry (and thereby kills that path).

Therefore:
- **Dense connectivity** (all entries non-zero):
  Every source node must have at least one directed path reaching every sink node.
- **Sparse factorization**:
  Achieved by choosing sparsity patterns carefully so that collectively they still satisfy dense connectivity.

Thus designing sparse factors becomes tantamount to designing graphs where every pair of source–sink nodes is linked via *at least one* path.

---

## 4.3 Dense Connectivity Requirement for Full Matrix Generation

The requirement “every element of $\bm{R}$ must be non‑zero” translates into a combinatorial condition on our graph:

> **Claim (Dense Connectivity Condition).**  
> The product $\prod_{k=1}^{m}\mathbf B_k$ yields a fully dense matrix iff for all $i,k$, there exists at least one directed path
> $\bigl(v^{(0)}_j,v^{(1)}_{\ell_1},v^{(2)}_{\ell_2},\, … ,\, v^{(m)}_i\bigr)$ such that each consecutive pair $(v^{(r-1)}_{\ell_{r-1}},v^{(r)}_{\ell_r})$ corresponds to an existing edge $E_r$.

Graphically this means: from any bottom‐layer vertex $v^{(0)}_j$ we keep following edges upward; we should never touch an “isolated” region that cannot reach any vertex on top layer $v^{(m)}$.

It turns out that many classical fast linear transforms automatically satisfy this: for instance, the Cooley–Tukey FFT factorisation places permutation graphs (the butterfly structure), which guarantee dense reachability with relatively few edges.

### Example: Butterfly Graph

Consider $d=8$, i.e., $N=3$ subdivisons ($8=2^3$).  
The butterfly structure intertwines levels such that each source node communicates with half of the next level’s nodes then merges with its partner older counterpart and so on.
If you trace any bottom vertex through successive levels you will meet all upper vertices eventually—this yields full connectivity while using only $\mathcal O(d\,N)=O(d\,log d)$ edges instead of $d^2$.

Such insight suggests that not all sparse‐factor structures are equally effective: some patterns may preclude reaching all pairs and thus inevitably force zeros into $\mathbf R$, while others can guarantee density with fewer trainable connections.

---

## 4.4 Counting Free Edges vs Parameter Efficiency

Having established how edges encode non‑zero parameters, we now quantify how many free edges are necessary or sufficient under different constraints.

### 4.4.1 Edge Count per Sparse Factor

A general sparse matrix $\mathbf B_k$ has exactly $D_k$ non‑zero entries; these correspond to $D_k$ directed edges between layers $k-1$ and $k$.  

When we require **orthogonality**, additional constraints arise:
An orthogonal matrix $\mathbf O$ satisfies $\mathbf O^\top = \mathbf O^{-1}$.
If $\mathbf O$ contains zeros in its pattern, these zeros are not independent—they impose relationships among remaining entries.
However, when designing structured sparse orthogonal matrices—for example using Cayley parametrisation on block–diagonal submatrices—the number of *free* trainable parameters equals roughly $(b^2-b)/2$ per block where $b$ is block size.

In practice we treat:

| Factor |\# free trainable params |
|--------|---------------------------|
| Dense orthogonal | $\frac12 d(d-1)$ |
| Block–diagonal | $\frac12 b(b-1)\times r$ with $br=d$ |
| Butterfly component size k | $\frac12 k(k-2)\times d/k = O(k\,d)$ |

Thus *free edges* are those whose values are freely adjustable during training; other edges are fixed either by identity or symmetry conditions implied by orthogonality or by permuted zero pattern.

### 4.4.2 Edge Tradeoffs for Expressiveness versus Efficiency

Suppose we fix total number of free edges \(E_{\text{free}}\).
A stricter sparsity pattern yields fewer free edges but risks losing ability to approximate arbitrary dense matrices.
Conversely, more free edges allow greater expressiveness but increase memory/computation overhead during fine‑tuning.

Analyzing example patterns:

| Pattern | Free edges | Approximate runtime factor vs full MATR multiplication | Comment |
|---------|------------|------------------------------------------------------|---------|
| Dense | $\Theta(d^2)$ | One multiplication | Baseline OFT |
| Block‑diag ($b=d/32$, say r=32)| $\Theta(b^2 r)$ ~$\Theta(b\,d)$ | constant factor < 10% | Lower expressiveness |
| Butterfly ($b=2$, m=$log_2 d$)| $\Theta(d\,log d)$ | Log×cost of small mat^mul | Balances dense connectivity |

One can imagine hybrid constructions: e.g., use several butterfly components interleaved with block–diagonal ones; count their contributions accordingly.

### 4.4.3 How Counting Edges Guides Design Choices

The information transmission framework suggests two design levers:

| Lever                              | What it changes                                |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Number of stages / depth m        | Controls length of paths -> more opportunities for compensation over zeros |
| Size per stage k (block size b ) | Influences number-of-trainable per stage; smaller k → fewer parameters but potentially more stages |

An optimal configuration aims for:

- Sufficient depth so paths can “mix” enough bits,
- Small blocks so training overhead stays modest,
- Permutations/separable structures (butterfly-like swaps), preserving dense reachability while keeping sparsity high.

In practice researchers adjust these hyperparameters empirically guided by theoretical bounds like those above: e.g., set m ≈ log₂ d and b ≈ 2 ensures ℰ=Θ(d log d).

## Summary

This chapter showed that constructing dense orthogonal matrices required for Orthogonal Finetuning can be reframed as propagating information over grid graphs induced by sparse factors. The positions where factors have non‑zero entries become directed edges; requiring all pairwise paths leads to dense outputs while letting us count exactly how many trainable connections are needed. By understanding connectivity constraints and counting free edges it becomes possible to systematically design parameter‑efficient factorizations—most notably butterfly structures—that preserve full expressivity without exploding memory usage. This viewpoint not only clarifies why certain structured sparsities work well but also provides guiding principles for future adaptive finetuning strategies in large foundation models.

# 5. Butterfly Structures – From FFT to Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) requires the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) that is parameter‑efficient: it should use far fewer parameters than an arbitrary \(d\times d\) matrix while preserving the orthogonality constraints that make OFT stable and generalizable.  In this chapter we show how a celebrated numerical algorithm – the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) – provides the mathematical blueprint for an extremely sparse factorization that still yields a **dense** matrix.  The resulting “butterfly” factorization uses only \(O(d\log d)\) non‑zero entries, a vast reduction from the naïve \(O(d^{2})\).  We dissect the Cooley–Tukey recursion, formalise the butterfly factors, show how recursion builds a full‑dimensional butterfly matrix, demonstrate that each factor can be guaranteed orthogonal with a Cayley transform, and explain how these ideas combine into a practical parameterization for OFT called **Orthogonal Butterfly** (BOFT).

## 5.1 Cooley–Tukey Recursive Factorization and the Butterfly Factor

The Cooley–Tukey algorithm is the classic method for evaluating the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) in \(O(d\log d)\) operations.  Its essence is a repeated **divide‑and‑conquer** decomposition: an \(d\)-point DFT is expressed as two \(\tfrac{d}{2}\)-point DFTs followed by a small mixing stage that couples pairs of outputs.  If \(d=2^{N}\) is a power of two, this decomposition unfolds recursively until one‑point DFTs are reached.

Mathematically, let us denote by
\[
\mathbf{F}_{d}
\]
the \(d\times d\) DFT matrix.  For simplicity we work with permutations of \(\mathbf{F}_{d}\) that preserve its unitary property; the exact form of complex exponentials is irrelevant for our purposes because we wish to build **real** orthogonal matrices later.  

### 5.1.1 The Butterfly Factor \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\)

At every recursion step the algorithm mixes pairs of points using a small \(k\times k\) block that contains only diagonal terms.  For an even block size \(k=2m\), the canonical *butterfly factor* takes the form
%
\[

\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[4pt]

\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{d}_{i}\in \mathbb{R}^{m},

Thus \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\) is highly structured: it contains exactly \(4m=k\times 2m/2 = k^{2}/2\) non‑zeros, half of all entries in a dense matrix.

A key observation is that **every** entry on either diagonal can be chosen arbitrarily; these scalars will later be made to satisfy orthogonality constraints.

# 6. Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) – Design & Implementation

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is a principled, scalable, and highly parameter‑efficient finite‑tuning method that extends the orthogonal finetuning (OFT) paradigm by employing a **butterfly factorization** of orthogonal matrices.  In BOFT, the left‑hand side weight modifier is not a single dense orthogonal matrix but a product of many sparse, structured orthogonal blocks.  This construction enables us to manipulate an entire $d$‑dimensional linear layer with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ trainable parameters while preserving the key desirable properties of OFT: identity initialization, controlled distance preservation via orthogonality, and stable training dynamics.

Below we present a rigorous exposition of BOFT’s mathematical formulation, its implementation details—including initialization and dropout regularisation—hyperparameter choices, and a complexity analysis that contrasts it to its OFT predecessors and LoRA.

---

## 6.1 Mathematical Formulation

### 6.1.1 Butterfly Components

Let \(k \geq 2\) be an integer power of two.  
A **butterfly factor** \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\in\mathbb R^{k\times k}\) is defined as

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1)   & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2) \\
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3)   & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]

where each \(\mathbf{d}_i \in \mathbb R^{k/2}\) is a trainable vector (or a block matrix when we generalise to \(b>2\)).  
The non‑zero pattern of this factor precisely matches the two‑way “butterfly” connections typically used in radix‑2 Cooley–Tukey FFTs: every input node pairs with exactly two output nodes.

For any dimension \(d=2^N\), we build the \(d\times d\) **butterfly matrix** \(\mathbf B(d)\) recursively:

\[
\mathbf B(d)
=
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf B(d/2)&0\\
0&\mathbf B(d/2)
\end{bmatrix}
=
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
   \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d/2)\cdots
   \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2).
\]

Here  
- \(\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)\in \mathbb R^{d\times d}\) places the butterfly factor \(\mathbf B^{F}(k)\) along the diagonal 
  (\(b=\frac dk=1,\dots,d/2,\dots , d/2^N=1\)).
- The recursion ensures that each butterfly component operates on contiguous subspaces of size \(k\).

Consequently, the overall butterfly matrix is a product of \(N = \log_2 d\) sparse block‑diagonal matrices.

### 6.1.2 Orthogonal Butterfly Parameterisation

In BOFT we treat each butterfly component as an **orthogonal** matrix.  
To guarantee this, each small block (\(j\times j,\; j=2b,\dots , 2b\,k /(k/2)) is parameterised via the Cayley transform:

\[
\bR_{j}
=\bigl(\bI+\bQ_{j}\bigr)\bigl(\bI-\bQ_{j}\bigr)^{-1},
    \qquad
    \bQ_{j}=-\,{\bQ_{j}}^\top,
\]
where each skew‑symmetric matrix 
\( \bQ_{j}\in \mathbb R^{j\times j}\)
encodes \(j(j-1)/2 = b(b-1)/2\,k/k=O(b)\) free parameters.
When \(k=2^n b\), the sparsity pattern of every component matches that of a standard FFT butterfly.

Collecting all components yields the **orthogonal butterfly matrix**
\(R(m,b)\):

```
R(m,b)=prod_{i=1}^{m} tildeB^b_(d,i)
```

with 

- **m** – number of butterfly layers (depth);
- **b** – base block size per component.
  
The actual forward pass for adapting a pretrained linear layer
\( W^0 \in \mathbb R^{d\times n}\)

```
z  = ( R(m,b) · W^0 )^T · x            (x ∈ ℝ^d , z ∈ ℝ^n)
```

is L = (total layers)*C operations plus equivalent cost for pre-multiplying
\(W^0^\top x.\)

### 6.1.3 Parameter Count

Each component introduces
\( O((b-1)d)\) effective trainable parameters because only the skew‑symmetric part
of every block is free: 
- For a block-size \(b\times b,\;
   |\text{skew-symmetric}| = b(b-1)/2.\)
- Each component has \( d/b \) such blocks,
  so total per component:
  \( (d/b)\times b(b-1)/2 = (b-1)d/2.\)

With **m** components we obtain

> **#parameters** = 
> ½ (b – 1)d m.

Specialisations:

| BOFT version | Special case | #parameters |
|--------------|--------------|-------------|
| BOFT(1,b)    | Block–diagonal OFT | ½(b–1)d |
| BOFT(1,d)    | Full OFT | ½(d–1)d →
approx λ*d² |
| BOFT(log d , 2)| Full butterfly | ≈ d log ₂ d |

Thus, by choosing **b = 2** and **m = log₂ d** we minimise parameter usage while preserving a dense transformation.

---

## 6.2 Implementation Details

### 6.2.1 Identity Initialization

For any orthogonal flyweight \(R(m,b)\), we initialise *every* skew‑symmetric matrix as the zero matrix:
\( Q_j =0.\)
The Cayley transform then reduces to the identity:
\( R(m,b)=I_d.\)

This choice guarantees:
- Initial loss exactly matches that of the frozen backbone;
- No bias or drift in early optimisation steps;
- Easier transfer learning from large pre-fitted models.

### 6.2.2 Multiplicative Dropout

Regular dropouts used in LoRA act additively on weight updates.
In BOFT we must preserve orthogonality after dropout; hence we introduce *multiplicative* dropout that targets specific components or blocks and temporarily replaces them with identity.

Algorithmic steps:

1. **Component Dropout:**
   - With probability \(p_1,\; i.i.d.\), replace entire butterfly component
     `tildeB^b_(d,i)` by identity.
   
2. **Block Dropout inside a Component:**
   - With probability \(p_2,\; i.i.d.\), replace each diagonal block
     within `tildeB^b_(d,i)` by an identity block.
   
Both dropouts are applied during training but omitted at inference.
They encourage sparsity in the effective transformation,
leading to improved generalisation in low‑data regimes.

### 6.3 Hyperparameter Choices

| Hyper‑parameter | Typical value | Effect |
|-----------------|---------------|--------|
| `m`            | log₂ *d*      | maximal expressive capacity; raising m beyond log₂ *d* yields diminishing returns because extra layers mainly reparametrise already dense map |
| `b`            | { 2,4,8,… }   | smaller `b` increases expressivity per parameter; larger `b` flattens structure but reduces param count |
| `p₁`            | [0,0.3]       | higher dropout induces stronger regularisation |
| `p₂`            | [0,0.3]       | fine-grained block dropping for local sparsity |
| Optimiser       | AdamW         | weight decay applied only to γ parameters (Cayley parameters). |

Practical guidelines based on empirical studies:
- For vision transformers (`image size`: >100M parameters),
  setting `m = log₂ d` and `b = 4` balances accuracy and runtime well.
- For language models (`hidden size` ≈12k),
  using `m = log₂ d`, `b=8` usually suffices for near‐full performance while keeping parameter count <10×10³.

## 6.4 Computational Complexity Analysis

Time complexity scales linearly with both depth *m* and dimension *d*:
  
> **Per-layer forward/backward cost** ≈ O(m · n · log₂ d),
> where n is output dimension.
  
Compared to:

| Method        | Parameters per layer        | Runtime vs training step      |
|---------------|----------------------------|------------------------------|
| Full Fine‑tune | O(d²)                      | baseline                     |
| OFT (block-diag)| O(b·d)                      | ~ same as full               |
| LoRA           | O(r·(n+b))                  | slightly cheaper             |
| BOFT(log ₂ d , 𝑏=𝟐 ) | O(d log ₂ d )                | higher than OFT but << full |

Memory overhead: Each butterfly component stores its skew‑symmetric parameters,
so total buffer ≈ ½(b−1)d m floats – modest compared to full fine-tuning.

The extra cost at training stems from repeated sparse matrix multiplications which can be efficiently implemented via BLAS *gemm* with custom kernels exploiting known sparsity patterns.
At inference, after training one can absorb the learned $R$ into $W$ ahead of time (`$W_{\text{finetuned}} = R W^0$`), incurring no run‑time penalty relative to baselines.

## 6.5 Special Cases Revisited

It is illuminating that several intelligent specialisations recover familiar freezing paradigms:

| Specialisation                           | Equivalent Known Method                                     |
|------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| **BOFT(1,b)**                           | Block-diagonal OFT – each neuron group gets its own small orthogonal transformation |
| **BOFT(1,d)**                           | Conventional OFT – one global dense orthogonal matrix        |
| **BOFT(log₂ d , 𝑏=𝟐)**                   | Full FFT-like butterfly – dense orthogonal with ~log₂ d factor depth |

## 6.6 Concluding Summary

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) leverages structured sparse factorizations derived from fast Fourier-like butterflies to efficiently represent dense orthogonal transforms needed for model finetuning:

- The method employs an α-exponential cascade of small orthogonal blocks (`R(m,b)`), thereby reducing learnable parameters from $O(d²)$ down to $O(d\,log\, d)$ or less.
- Identity initialization guarantees starting point fidelity with pretrained weights.
- Multiplicative dropout further regularises by momentarily nullifying entire components or blocks without violating orthogonality constraints.
- Hyperparameter engineering (`m`, `b`, dropout rates) allows practitioners to navigate between speed, memory footprint, and task performance while staying within principled mathematical bounds.

Through this design lens one sees how BOFT synthesises ideas from numerical linear algebra (Cayley transforms), signal processing (FFT butterflies), and deep learning finetuning workflows into a single coherent framework compelling for modern foundation model adaptation tasks​

# 7. Theoretical Insights into BOFT Expressivity & Stability  

Large‐scale foundation models such as vision transformers, language models, and diffusion generators contain dozens of linear layers whose weights are first learned on massive corpora or image collections. When a downstream task demands only a small degree of adaptation, the most natural approach is to keep the original weights frozen and inject a small, trainable transformation that can reconfigure the model to the new distribution. Two pioneering paradigms for such a transformation are **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** and its parameter‑efficient descendant **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)**. In this chapter we analyse the mathematical properties that explain why BOFT not only subsumes OFT but also reaches a strictly larger hypothesis class while preserving favorable spectral attributes.

We proceed in four phases:

1.  Revisit OFT in matrix‑multiplicative form.  
2.  Show how BOFT generalises OFT by means of butterfly factorisation and prove **Theorem 4.1** – that BOFT is strictly more expressive than OFT when the block size is held constant.  
3.  Extend to the *generalised orthogonal composition* that can express every orthogonal matrix (the **kaleidoscope hierarchy**).  
4.  Discuss two important spectral consequences: preservation of singular values and their impact on training stability, followed by interpreting BOFT as learning a bilinear similarity.

Throughout we assume all matrices are real; all notation follows standard conventions: $\bm{I}_d$ is the $d\times d$ identity, $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the Euclidean norm, and $\sigma_{\max}(\bm{M})$ designates the largest singular value of $\bm{M}$.

## 7.1 Orthogonal Finetuning Revisited  

OFT reparameterises a frozen linear layer $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ with an *orthogonal* left multiplier $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$:

\[
\boxed{\; \bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0 \;}
\tag{7.1}
\]

The forward pass becomes

\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^{\!\top}\,\bm{x}
= (\bm{W}^0)^{\!\top}\,\underbrace{\!\! \bm{R}^\top}_{=\bm{R}^{-1}} \,\bm{x},
\quad \forall\, \bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d.
\tag{7.2}
\]

Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal ($\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}= \mathbbm{\! 1}_d$), it preserves:
- pairwise angles between rows (or columns) of $\bm{W}^0$, hence the *geometric* relationship among neurons;
- singular values of $\bm{W}^0$, as desired for training stability (see § 7.3).

The bottleneck in OFT lies in parameter count: an unconstrained orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2 = O(d^2)$ degrees of freedom. To reduce this cost, OFT imposes a *block–diagonal* structure

\[
\boxed{\;
   \bm{R}= \operatorname{diag}\!
   (\underbrace{\!\!\mathbbm{\! R}_1}_{b\times b},
             \underbrace{\!\!\mathbbm{\! R}_2}_{b\times b},
             \ldots,
             \underbrace{\!\!\mathbbm{\! R}_r}_{b\times b}),}
   \qquad rb=d,
   \tag{7.3}
\]

where each block $\mathbbm{\! R}_i$ is an independent $b\times b$ orthogonal matrix.
The total number of trainable parameters reduces to $r\, b(b-1)/2 = O(b\, d)$.

While block–diagonal OFT makes finetuning cheaper, it dramatically restricts expressivity: neurons whose coordinates lie in different blocks are rotated independently, precluding any coupling across blocks.

### Theorem 4.1 (Expressivity of BOFT)

> **Statement**  
> Let $\Omega_{\text{OFT}}(b)$ denote the set of all block–diagonal orthogonal matrices with block size $b$. Let $\Omega_{\text{BOFT}}(m,b)$ denote the set obtained by multiplying $m$ butterfly components as above, each with block size $b$. Then  
> \[
>    \Omega_{\text{OFT}}(b)\;\subsetneq\;\Omega_{\text{BOFT}}(m,b),
>    \qquad m>1,
> \]
> in particular for any fixed $b$, boosting $m$ yields strictly larger hypothesis class.

#### Proof Sketch  

We prove inclusion first: every block–diagonal matrix can be expressed as exactly one butterfly product with `$m=1`'—this coincides with standard OFT ($B_{(d,1)}=\operatorname{id}$). Hence $\Omega_{\text{OFT}}(b)\subseteq\Omega_{\text{BOFT}}(1,b)$. To establish strictness for any larger `m`, we construct an explicit example that lies in `Ω_BOFT` but not in `Ω_OFT`.

Let `d = 4` (`N=2`) and pick `b=4` so a single factor covers all dimensions (`B_{(4,1)} = I`). For `m=3`, choose butterfly factors such that each has non‑trivial action across different coordinate pairs—specifically,

```
B_3 = [[0 1], [1 0]] ⊗ I_2          (swap)
B_2 = diag(I_2, diag([0 -1; 1 0]))    (block rotation)
B_1 = I_4                          
```

The product yields an orthogonal matrix that couples coordinates from different blocks (e.g., mixes elements `x₁` with `x₃`), which cannot be realised by any block–diagonal matrix with block size `4`. Therefore this product lies in Ω_BOFT(3,4) but not in Ω_OFT(4). □

> **Remark**  
> While **block–diagonal** OFT forbids inter‑block mixing even at initialization (`I_d`), **BOFT** allows such mixing through successive butterfly stages.

This theorem demonstrates that for any given computational budget (fixed number of trainable parameters), increasing `m` while shrinking individual blocks can strictly expand the representational power beyond plain OFT.

---

## 7.3 Generalised Orthogonal Composition and Full Orthogonal Group  

The ultimate aim is to parameterise *any* orthogonal matrix using as few parameters as feasible while preserving structural regularity. The **kaleidoscope hierarchy** achieves this by alternating *right* and *left* multiplications with independent butterfly products:

\[
\boxed{
  R^{G}(l)=
     R_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\,
     R_{l,2}^{\,T}(m_2,b_2)\,
     R_{l-1,1}(m_1,b_1)\,
     R_{l-1,2}^{\,T}(m_2,b_2)\,
     \dots
     R_{0,?}(~).
}
       \tag{7.4}
       \]

Here:
- `$R_{i,j}`' are orthogonal matrices from BOFT families (`+` for right multiplication, `$^{T}$` indicates left multiplication).  
- Choosing `$m_1=m_2=\log d`, `$b_1=b_2=2`, and `$l=d-½$, one obtains

$\displaystyle R^{G}(l)=
      B_{d-½}(d)\,
      B_{d-¾}^{\,T}(d)\,
      B_{...}\,$

which forms a basis spanning $\mathrm O(d)$; i.e., every orthogonal matrix can be written as such a product.

Thus BOFT is not merely an efficient approximation; it sits inside a family capable of representing the *full* special orthogonal group via relatively shallow compositions (`$\mathcal O(\log d)$` factors). In practice we stop far earlier—usually one or two butterfly layers suffice—providing a sweet spot between expressivity and parsimony.

---

## 7.4 Spectral Norm Preservation  

A striking advantage of left-multiplication with an orthogonal matrix stems from SVD invariance:

Let  
$\displaystyle
 W^{0}=U\,Σ\,V^{\top}
$
be Munch’s decomposition where:
- `U∈ℝ^{d×r}`, `V∈ℝ^{n×r}` contain left/right singular vectors,
- `Σ∈ℝ^{r×r}` has non‑negative descending singular values σ₁≥σ₂≥…≥σ_r>0.

After finetuning:

$\displaystyle
 W=W^{t}=R\,U\,Σ\,V^{\top},
$
so that

$\displaystyle
 (W)^\top W=V Σ U^\top R^\top R U Σ V^\top = V Σ^␰Σ V^\top.
$

Therefore all singular values {σ_i} remain unchanged: σ_i(W)=σ_i(W⁰). The spectral norm

$ 
\sigma_{\max}(W)=σ₁(W),
$

is preserved exactly whenever `R` remains strictly orthogonal throughout training.

### Practical Consequences  

| Scenario | LoRA | OFT / BOFT |
|----------|------|------------|
| Initialisation | Zero δ ≈ → W² ≈ W⁰² | Identity | Identity |
| Spectral Norm Change | perturbs σ₁ slightly | preserved |
| Training Stability | may suffer from exploding/vanishing gradients when σ₁ shifts | robust |
| Generalisation | less control over spectrum | tighter control |

This analytical property explains why experiments show stable training dynamics when employing BOFT compared to LoRA or other additive-to-weight methods: perturbations never push past stronger units or dampen weaker ones inadvertently.

Recall formula:

For any input vector x∈ℝⁿ,

$\displaystyle z_i=(w_i^0)^\top(Rx)

$

so optimizing over `R` simultaneously adjusts all outer products without individually meddling each column `w_i`. In other words,

$\displaystyle z_i <-> x^\top K w_i^0,
$

with `K:=R`. We see this as learning a global bilinear similarity weighted by an *orthogonally constrained* matrix—a regularisation akin to metric learning where each transform maps both input and weight columns onto some latent similarity space whilst preserving distances because `K†K=I`.

# 8. Practical Recipes for Training with BOFT  

The previous chapters introduced the theory behind Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT), proved its expressivity, and outlined its mathematical structure. In practice, however, the devil lies in the details: how do we hook a BOFT layer into an existing transformer or diffusion pipeline, what learning‑rate schedule guarantees stability, how do we pick the factorisation hyper‑parameters \(m\) and \(b\), and how do we keep training time reasonable?  This chapter is a step‑by‑step guide that covers all those practicalities, with recipes that have worked on large vision transformers (ViTs), language models (LLaMA‑7B and GPT‑Neo‑2.7B), and text‑to‑image diffusion models (Stable Diffusion 2.1).  By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

* Insert a BOFT module into any feed‑forward or attention sublayer without changing the inference graph.  
* Select a good \((m,b)\) pair for a given layer size and parameter budget.  
* Choose learning rates, optimizers, and dropout schedules that keep training stable while maximizing generalisation.  
* Monitor runtime overhead and exploit caching / fused operations to mitigate it.  

---

## 8.1 Overview of the BOFT Forward Pass

Given a frozen weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) that appears in one of the linear projections of a transformer block:  

\[
\mathbf{z} \;=\; (\mathbf{R} \mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

BOFT replaces \(\mathbf{R}\) with a product of \(m\) orthogonal butterfly components

\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b) \;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) being a block–sparse matrix consisting of \(b\times b\) orthogonal blocks embedded in a permutation defined by the butterfly pattern at level \(i\).  The forward pass is therefore

1. **Cache** the frozen product \(\mathbf{W}^0_{\text{frozen}} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\).  
2. **Apply** each butterfly component in reverse order:  
   \[
   \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0 = \mathbf{x}, \quad
   \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i} = (\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\, )^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i-1}\quad (i=1,\dots,m).
   \]
3. **Multiply** by cached weight:  
   \[
   \mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}^0_{\text{frozen}}\;\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{m}.
   \]

During inference we can fuse steps 2–3 into one operation by pre‑computing the final dense transformation  

\[
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\;=\;\bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\,\bigr)\,\mathbf{W}^0,
\]
so that only a single dense matmul is required at run time.  The cost trade‑off is that all butterfly parameters must be updated during training but not during inference.

---

## 8.2 Wires: Mounting BOFT on Existing Architectures

| Backbone | Target Module | BOFT Hook | Comment |
|----------|---------------|-----------|---------|
| Transformer/LLaMA | Q/K/V projections (3×\(d_{\text{head}}\times d_{\text{model}}\)) | Replace each projection matrix with BOFT | Each head may use its own \((m,b)\) for extra flexibility |
| ViT / Swin | MLP layers (two linear layers per block) | Insert BOFT before each linear layer | For attentive embeddings you may wish to mix with KLﬁt weights |
| Stable Diffusion 2.x | UNet down-/up-sampling Linear/Conv layers | Use BOFT on any \(d\times d'\) linear layer | Convolution → flatten → linear → reshape – this wrapper works after flatten |

**Implementation tip:** In PyTorch you can create a custom `nn.Module` called `BOFTLayer` that takes in `weight0` as a buffer and owns only the `skew` parameters of each butterfly component as trainable variables:

```python
class BOFTLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, weight0, m, b):
        super().__init__()
        self.weight0 = nn.Parameter(weight0, requires_grad=False)
        self.butterflies = nn.ParameterList(
            [create_cayley_skew(d=weight0.shape[0], b=b)
             for _ in range(m)])
    def forward(self, x):
        # reverse order multiply
        z = x
        for skew in reversed(self.butterflies):
            B = cayley_from_skew(skew)
            z = torch.matmul(z.unsqueeze(-1).permute(1,0,2).squeeze(-1), B.T)
        return torch.matmul(self.weight0.t(), z)
```

Be sure to register `weight0` as `nn.Parameter(torch.tensor(...), requires_grad=False)` so it is frozen.

## 8.3 Selecting Hyperparameters \(m\) and \(b\)

**Understanding the trade‐off**

• **Parameter count**:   
 \(P(m,b)=\frac12(b-1)d\,m.\)

• **Expressivity**: Larger \(m\) allows richer transformations; smaller \(b\) keeps matrices sparse.

• **Runtime overhead**: Each butterfly application adds one dense‑by‑sparse matmul per stage.

### Practical Grid

| Layer Size | Desired Params |\(m,b)\)| Rough Complexity |
|------------|-----------------|--------|-------------------|
| \(d=512,\ n=2048\) (ViT MLP first linear)| ~10 k params | (4,8)|(~6×ℓ¹?). |
| \(d=1024,\ n=4096\)(ViT MLP second linear)| ~20 k params | (5,4)|(~10×ℓ¹?)|
| Transformer QKV (\(h=16,\ d_{\text{model}}=2048,h_{\text{k}}=128,d=\!128,n=128)\)| ~4 k params | (6,2)| minimal overhead|
| Diffusion UNet Conv fan‑out (\(d=768,n=768)\)| ~40 k params (max budget)| (9 ,32)| larger but still fine|

A simple rule of thumb:
* Fix total param budget **\(P_{\max}\)**.  
* Solve for minimal integer pair that satisfies both:  
 \(P(m,b)\le P_{\max},\, m\,b\,d^{-1}<2^{-\gamma}\).  

where \(2^{-\gamma}\) ensures enough butterfly stages to mix information across all positions.

### Scheduling

Often we start training with all layers *cold* at initialization:

```
learning_rate = LR * sqrt(b/d);      # smaller b tighter constraint
schedule = {"phase1": Seq(epochs/4),
            "phase2": Seq(epochs/4),
            "phase3": Seq(epochs/4),
            "phase4": Seq(epochs/4)}
```

Modulate LR per phase: starting small to avoid destabilising pretrained weights; then gradually increase.

## 8.4 Optimizer Choices & Learning Rate Strategy

BOFT trains *only* the skews of butterfly blocks—roughly O(d log d / 2). Since these skews are often nearly orthogonal after every update due to Cayley transform clipping, we can safely use:

| Optimizer | Rationale |
|-----------|-----------|
| AdamW     | Handles sparse gradients nicely; default betas {0.9, 0.999}. |
| LAMB      | If you need larger batch sizes; slows divergence due to Adam’s over‑aggressive second moment handling.|

**Learning rate bounds**

```
lr_min = lr_init * 10^{-5}
lr_max = lr_init
scheduler = MultiStepLR(
    optimizer,
    milestones=[round(epoch*ratio) for ratio in [0.25, 0.5, 0.75]],
    gamma=0.5)
```

`lr_init` is usually `5e-5` for language models and `1e-5` for vision models when training from scratch but should be *lowered* if adding many butterflies (`m >= 6`). A good practice is to run a quick two‑epoch learning‐rate warmup (`warmup_steps = min(dataset_size//batch_size ,400)`).

---

## 8.5 Multiplicative Dropout Scheduling

Standard dropout discards features; multiplicative dropout masks *blocks* of each butterfly component at training time:

```python
def multiplicative_dropout(skew_list,
                           p_blocks: float,
                           p_butters: float,
                           training=True):
    if not training:
        return skew_list
    new_skews=[]
    for skew in skew_list:
        if random.random() < p_blocks:
            # replace entire block with identity via zero skew
            new_skew = torch.zeros_like(skew)
        else:
            # mask individual diagonal blocks inside this component
            mask=torch.bernoulli(torch.full_like(skew,p_butters))
            new_skew = skew * mask
        new_skews.append(new_skew)
    return new_skews
```

Typical schedule:

```
p_blocks_start ≈ 0., p_blocks_end ≈ 10% 
p_butters_start ≈ 50%, p_butters_end ≈ 30%
Fade linearly over first half epochs.
```

These probability values can be tuned per dataset; higher dropout accelerates regularisation but may stall convergence if too aggressive.

## 8.6 Runtime Overhead Mitigation

The major cost arises from repeatedly applying \(m\) butterfly matrices during each forward/backward pass.

### Fused Butterfly Libs

Using libraries such as *PyTorch BFV*, which implement efficient batched matrix–vector multiplications for binary permutation-structured matrices yields up to **30 % speedup** over naïve PyTorch operations.

### Cache-Gauged batched matmul

Instead of applying each butterfly sequentially:

```python
def batched_butterfly(x):
    # x shape: [B,N]
    x_perm=x.clone()
    for skip in powers_of_two():
        x_perm[:, :skip] ... ←apply_permutation_and_scale...
    return x_perm
```
This reduces constant overhead dramatically.

### Mixed Precision + BFLOAT16

Because butterfly matrices are orthogonal small blocks (~\(2\times2\)), using BFLOAT16 doesn't degrade orthogonality much but halves memory footprint.

## 8.7 Inference Pipeline After Finetuning

At inference time you never apply any butterflies in real time – you fuse them into standard dense weights:

```python
def fuse_weights(layer_weight_frozen: Tensor,
                 skew_list):                       
    R_mat=torch.eye(layer_weight_frozen.shape[1], device='cuda')
    for skew in reversed(skew_list):
        R_mat=cayley_from_skew(skew).t() @ R_mat
    new_weight=(R_mat @ layer_weight_frozen.T).t()
    return new_weight.contiguous()

# Replace all layers' parameters after training epoch                                      
# This step is optional if you configured `torch.no_grad()` wrappers inside your model.
```

After fusion your model contains standard `nn.Linear`, thus enjoying identical inference speed as vanilla pretraining models without any additional latency or memory overhead.

## 8.8 Troubleshooting FAQ

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Fix |
|---------|--------------|-----|
| Gradient explosion early on? | Butterflies not orthogonal due to large skews near singularity.| Reduce initial activating function or use smaller learning rate (`lr <= 5e-6`). Apply gradient clipping (`clip_norm=<7`). |
| Loss stagnates after few epochs? | Too few butterfly stages (`m`) or overly small block size causing lack of expressive power.| Increase `m` or reduce `b`; verify with approximation error analysis from §8.3 “Expressivity graphs.” |
| Training memory > GPU capacity? | Butterfly components PAIR up with large hidden dim not allowing fused caching.| Use gradient accumulation (`accumulate_steps`) or switch to mixed precision BFLOAT16 via amp autograd. |
| Accuracy lower than LoRA baseline? | Dropout too aggressive causing underfitting.| Reduce multiplicative dropout rates or disable them when validation loss shows improvement drop.| 
| TorchScript export fails? | Requires loops over list of skew parameters which JIT cannot trace.| Precompute fused dense weights before scripting or mark loops using `torch.jit.ignore`. |

# 10. Discussion – Inductive Biases & Limitations

The Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) paradigm extends the principled orthogonal finetuning framework by endowing the orthogonal transformation with a butterfly‑shaped sparse factorization. Within this chapter we analyze the inductive biases that arise from this structural design, compare them to the implicit regularization discovered in other parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT) families such as low‑rank or sparse adapters, and identify practical barriers that warrant further investigation. All insights presented here are derived from the original paper and its empirical or theoretical findings; no external materials are invoked.

## # Implications of Structured Sparsity on Generalization

BOFT inherits a rich family of classical fast linear transforms through its butterfly construction. The butterfly graph—originally introduced in the radix‑2 Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm—encodes a set of power‑of‑two‑size diagonal blocks \(\mathbf{B}^F(k)\) that are recursively interleaved and permuted across repeated layers. Because many familiar transforms (discrete Fourier, Hadamard, sine/cosine) can be expressed as a product of such butterfly layers with appropriate parameter choices, BOFT is capable of reproducing this family of operations exactly.

When a pretrained model is edited by an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\), the model’s weight columns are rotated in a highly constrained subspace of the full orthogonal group \(\mathcal{O}(d)\). This constraint exhibits two complementary properties:

1. **Preservation of Pairwise Angles**  
   Orthogonality guarantees that for all vectors \(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\),
   \[
   \langle\mathbf{Ru},\,\mathbf{Rv}\rangle = \langle\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{v}\rangle,
   \]
   which shows that the bilinear similarity—and hence the underlying distance metric—between all neuron embeddings remains unchanged. Empirically, this sharp spectral preservation translates into more stable training trajectories and reduced overfitting compared to additive rank‑\(k\) updates.

2. **Implicit Regularization via Exploreable Transform Families**  
   Because BOFT’s hypothesis space is effectively a low‑complexity manifold formed by the successive composition of sparse orthogonal blocks, random initializations of these blocks lie close to identity matrices but can explore richer regions as training progresses. This structured exploration aligns the finetuned model space with well‑studied signal processing bases, which may act as a form of *implicit inductive bias* favouring representations that respect both locality (due to the sparsity pattern) and global coherence (achieved after several butterfly stages). Such properties have already been shown to improve generalization in neural networks trained from scratch; BOFT transfers these benefits to fine‑tuning without incurring extra inference cost.

The paper validates these intuitions empirically across vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion generative models. Across all domains, BOFT consistently outperforms block‑diagonal OFT and LoRA when operating under identical parameter budgets, suggesting that the inductive biases encoded by butterfly factorization indeed generalize beyond a single architecture type.

## # Comparison to LoRA Low‑Rank Training

LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) is arguably the most widely adopted PEFT technique for large language models. LoRA formulates a weight update as
\[
\mathbf{W}^\ast = \mathbf{W}^0 + \Delta \mathbf{U}\Delta \mathbf{V}^\top,
\]
where \(\Delta \mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\) and \(\Delta \mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}\) are trained with \(k = O(1)\). The key desiderata for LoRA are twofold: 
* Low per‑layer parameter cost proportional to \(O(k(d+n))\), and 
* Simple additive updates that preserve most of the pretrained weight magnitude.

While LoRA’s low‑rank structure inherits an implicit bias towards low-frequency or smooth perturbations in weight space—due to its small effective rank—it does not explicitly enforce orthogonality or pairwise angle preservation. Consequently, LoRA can inadvertently introduce drifts in spectral norms, which may impair training stability or lead to feature collapse if not carefully regularized.

BOFT achieves comparable sparsity benefits but through an *orthogonal* multiplicative transformation rather than an additive decomposition:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^\ast = (\prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)})\,\mathbf{W}^0.
\]
Here each multiplicative factor is itself an \(O(b)\)-by-\(O(b)\) rotation embedded in a larger \(d\times d\) space via block diagonals or butterfly patterns. The net parameter count scales as
\[
O(dm(b-1)/2)
\]
for general \(m,b\), which yields roughly \(O(d\,b)\) parameters when \(m=1\) (block‑diagonal OFT). In contrast to LoRA’s linear spectral perturbation, BOFT preserves any singular value due to orthogonality:
\[
\sigma_i(\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^\ast)=\sigma_i(\mathbf{W}^0),
\]
ensuring that training dynamics remain controlled even under extensive adaptation.

From a representation standpoint, whereas LoRA perturbs weight columns additively—effectively moving them within an affine subspace—BOFT rotates columns within a closed manifold defined by butterfly chains. These rotations cannot interpolate arbitrary linear combinations but can express any rotational transform expressible by a product of sparse orthogonals; such transformations tend toward preserving feature manifold geometry rather than shrinking or exploding axes.

Moreover, numerical experiments reported in the paper show that for identical parameter budgets (e.g., 4 % of total weights), BOFT achieves higher downstream task performance across vision recognition benchmarks and text generation perplexities than LoRA variants with either fixed or adaptive rank settings.

## # Potential Use of Alternative Network Topologies

The information transmission view introduced in Section 3 reframes sparse orthogonal factorization as constructing directed graphs whereby messages pass between successive layers of nodes. Under this abstraction, butterfly graphs represent one instance where dense connectivity over two dimensions can be achieved using only \(O(d\,\log d)\) edges: each level connects each node to just two nodes in the next level according to recursively defined permutation patterns.

Given that this framework does not preclude other topologies with comparable sparsity but different connectivity properties, several alternatives merit consideration:

1. **Expander Graphs**  
   Expander graphs possess strong connectivity properties while maintaining extremely small degrees per node (often constant). Utilizing expander-based adjacency matrices as butterfly factors might allow faster mixing between source and target nodes with even fewer non-zero entries per matrix while still guaranteeing dense overall connectivity after \(O(\log d)\) stages.

2. **Random Sparse Orthogonal Matrices**  
   Generating each factor via random permutations followed by randomized Givens rotations could produce diverse connectivity patterns possibly hardening generalization against specific preprocessing biases inherent to fixed butterflies.

3. **Hierarchical Tree-Like Factorizations**  
   Substituting lower-triangular or upper-triangular sparse matrices arranged in hierarchical trees yields alternative combinatorial structures that may offer asymmetric yet dense transformations suitable for attention-based architectures where directional flow matters.

While robust experimental evidence is absent at present—since all evaluations reported rely on coherent butterfly sequences—the graphical framework permits systematic exploration: any proposed topology could be encoded as directed edges between successive node layers; verifying dense reachability reduces to checking whether every source node can connect to every sink node via paths composed solely from those edges across all levels. Balance between edge count and guarantee of density would then motivate theoretical analysis similar to results shown for butterflies: e.g., determining minimal path-length conditions or cycle-free constraints necessary for full invertibility under orthogonality constraints.

## # Runtime Overhead vs Inference Savings

A salient practical advantage presented in the paper is that BOFT incurs no inference latency once the learned orthogonal matrix has been merged into the pre-trained weights—multiplication by an already applied transformation carries no additional computational cost during deployment. However this convenience comes at two distinct costs:

- **Training Overhead**  
  Each forward and backward pass now requires additional matrix multiplications by factor matrices \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\). Since each factor is sparse but involves permutations and small block-wise rotations, Jacobians are more expensive than vanilla forward passes over frozen weights alone but still cheaper than full fine-tuning where all weights must be updated directly. Empirical runtime studies indicate that training speed degrades roughly proportionally to \(m=b=O(1)\); yet exact wall-clock penalties remain unquantified for extremes such as \(m=\log d,\ b=2.\)

- **Memory Footprint**  
  Storing each factor matrix along with intermediate activations requires additional GPU memory proportional to \(O(m\,db^2)\). While still far below storing full weight updates as in low-rank adapters (which require storing two dense rank-\(k\) tensors), memory constraints become nontrivial when scaling BOFT beyond midsized models (e.g., > 11 B parameters).

There is thus an inherent trade-off: increasing depth \(m\) improves expressivity but adds linear overhead; enlarging block size \(b\) reduces number of factors yet boosts per-factor complexity quadratically due to larger block rotations within each butterfly component.

Future work may involve identifying optimal pairs \((m,b)\) per layer automatically through hyperparameter search or using adaptive mechanisms analogous to Rank-Adaptive LoRA where low-rank dimension adapts during training—here “block size” could adapt instead.

## # Open Research Questions  

Several important questions naturally arise given what has been demonstrated:

| Question | Significance | Possible Direction |

| What is the minimal number of butterflies required (\(m_{\text{opt}}\)) for different architecture depths? | Guarantees sufficiency without excessive overhead | Theoretical bounds using graph expander properties; empirical profiling |
| Is there an optimal *per-layer* block size \(b_\ell\) that balances expressivity vs memory? | Tailors induction bias per layer | Layer-wise validation curves; learning-to-learn meta-learners |
| Can we replace butterflies with learned sparse topologies while still guaranteeing dense overall transformations? | Expands design space beyond radix‑2 patterns | Search over permutation families; evolutionary algorithms |
| How does BOFT behave under extremely hostile tasks (e.g., domain shift or adversarial perturbations)? | Assesses robustness conferred by structured rotations | Adversarial fine-tuning experiments; worst-case analysis |

Addressing these will deepen understanding not only of BOFT itself but also elucidate how strict structure versus flexibility affect finite-sample performance in foundation model adaptation.

## # Future Research Directions

Drawing from both empirical successes and identified limitations, several lines warrant further inquiry:

1. **Dynamic Butterfly Depth Adaptation**  
   During early training epochs, lightweight factors might suffice; later epochs could incorporate additional butterflies if loss plateaus earlier than desired—a “curriculum” style approach mirroring adaptive rank selection in LoRA variants.

2. **Hybrid PEFT Schemes**  
   Combine BOFT’s rotational layer with low-rank additions from LoRA: 
   \[
   \tilde{\mathbf{W}}^\ast = (\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)})(\,\mathbf{W}^0 + U V^\top).
   \]
   This could harness benefits from both spectral preservation and local perturbation flexibility.

3. **Hardware Acceleration via Structured Sparsity**  
   Investigate custom kernels exploiting diagonal-plus-butterfly sparsity patterns—for example shared memory banks aligned with block rotations—to reduce runtime overhead further.

4. **Theoretical Exploration via Matrix Product States**  
   Recasting butterfly factorizations as tensor network sketches may reveal hidden hierarchies capturing multi-scale correlation structures—potentially aligning well with transformer attention mechanics.

5. **Cross-Modal Generalizability Studies**  
   Extending BOFT beyond vision-language pairs into multimodal task suites (e.g., video-language generation) will test whether inductive biases derived from classic transforms continue to confer benefits when data modalities exhibit markedly different statistical characteristics.

In conclusion, Orthogonal Butterfly finetuning introduces a principled way to embed classical signal processing priors into modern deep learning finetuning pipelines while preserving computational simplicity at inference time. Its success invites deeper theoretical interrogation into structured sparse transformations within large model adaptation yet simultaneously underscores practical considerations such as runtime overhead and latent inductive bias tuning—all fertile territories for future research endeavors.

# 11. Conclusion – The Path Forward in Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning

In this chapter we revisit the central ideas introduced throughout the manuscript, crystallize the practical benefits that stem from viewing orthogonal finetuning through the lens of sparse factorization, and sketch a roadmap for the next wave of research in parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT). The discussion is organized into four coherent parts: a concise recap, an examination of real‑world impact, an exploration of broad research implications, and a forward‑looking perspective on open challenges and promising directions.

## Summary of Contributions

**Dense orthogonal transformations from sparse building blocks**  
The most headline‑grabbing result in this work is the demonstration that a *dense* orthogonal matrix—typically requiring $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ trainable degrees of freedom—can be constructed from only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters by multiplying $\log d$ sparse orthogonal factors that take the form of butterfly matrices. This composition preserves the defining property of orthogonality (columnwise or rowwise unit norm and pairwise orthogonality) while dramatically shrinking the learnable set. Importantly, each butterfly factor admits a cheap inverse (itself being its transpose) which means forward and backward passes remain computationally cheap relative to full dense orthogonal reparameterizations.

**Information transmission framework for factorization design**  
To substantiate why butterfly structures are a natural fit for constructing dense transformers in less space, we introduced an information‑theoretic view: representing an $d\times d$ matrix as a grid‑graph where edges encode non‑zero entries allows us to reason about *connectivity* and *edge redundancy* between successive factor matrices. The framework formalizes two desiderata—dense reachability and minimal free edges—and shows how butterfly graphs satisfy them with logarithmic overhead. This abstraction invites cross‑disciplinary borrowings from network topology design and can be leveraged to evaluate other factorization motifs (e.g., tree or expander graphs).

**Generalized Orthogonal Finetuning Framework (BOFT)**  
The culmination is BOFT itself, a modular algorithm that subsumes the earlier block‑diagonal OFT as a limiting case, yet grants users flexible knobs: block size $b$ (controlling regularity), number of factors $m$ (controlling expressiveness), and optional multiplicative dropout (controlling overfitting). In practice, setting $b=2$, $m=\log d$ offers a sweet spot where parameter count is $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ yet the final transformation can approximate any orthogonal matrix arbitrarily closely when sufficient depth is used. Numerical experiments across vision, language, and diffusion models showed consistent performance gains over LO RA and vanilla OFT under tight parameter budgets.

**Theoretical insights on expressivity and spectral properties**  
Beyond empirical headroom, we provided formal arguments for why BOFT is more expressive than its block‑diagonal counterpart: a product of alternating butterfly components can span the entire orthogonal group when paired with additional transposed components (illustrated by Theorem 4). Moreover, because all transformations are orthogonal left–multiplications, spectral norms are preserved exactly; this yields both training stability and better generalization on downstream tasks—a property traditionally associated with carefully regularized architectures rather than parameter counts alone.

## Practical Impact on Foundation Model Adaptation

### Parameter Budget vs. Performance Trade‑off  
Large pretrain checkpoints often contain tens or hundreds of billions of weights. Adapting such models without full finetuning demands extreme parameter efficiency. BOFT supplies just enough freedom to rotate neuron embeddings in each layer while keeping the upstream activation distribution close to what was learned during massive pretraining. In practical settings—e.g., adapting GPT‑3 for domain‐specific question answering or Stable Diffusion for niche artistic styles—this translates to reaching task performance comparable to full finetuning but with few hundred thousand trainable parameters instead of billions.

### Zero Overhead at Inference  
Unlike adapter or prompt tuning, which introduce additional modules or tokens into every forward pass, BOFT rewrites the weight matrix itself at training time by composing precomputed orthogonal block matrices. Once training concludes, the resultant product can be absorbed into a single weight tensor at negligible GPU memory cost. Hence there is no inference slowdown—a key selling point for deployment on edge devices or latency‑critical services.

### Robustness Across Domains  
Harnessing inherent structural properties of butterfly transforms provides implicit inductive bias: many classic signal transforms lie close to these structured subspaces and have good conditioning. Consequently, BOFT tends to avoid drastic shifts in learned representations that would otherwise lead to catastrophic forgetting or overfitting on small downstream datasets—an issue commonly observed in LoRA or naive fine‑tuning.

## Broader Implications for Future PEFT Research

1. **Structured Reparameterization beyond Orthogonality**  
   While BOFT focuses on preserving angles via orthogonal permutations, similar sparse factorizations could enforce other desirable constraints like sparsity or positivity. Extending the information transmission framework to these constraints opens up new frontiers for designing “bidirectional” PEFT schemes where both clean initialization and hard regularity bounds coexist.

2. **Adaptive Depth-Wise Hypothesis Spaces**  
   The parameter trade‑off theorem invites research into **layer-wise adaptive factor counts**: shallow layers might benefit from fewer butterflies (higher regularity), while deeper layers could use more due to richer expressiveness demands. Automatic mechanisms such as reinforcement learning controllers or Bayesian optimization could discover optimal configurations per layer per task.

3. **Cross-Modal Factor Sharing**  
   Today’s multimodal foundation models often contain intertwined encoder–decoder pathways across vision-language modalities. An intriguing question is whether sharing butterfly factors across modalities further compresses cross‑modal adapters while preserving joint coherence—a direction that could unify vision transformers with language LLMs within a single PEFT umbrella.

4. **Hardware-Aware Implementation**  
   Butterfly factorizations align well with existing vectorized linear algebra routines because they require only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ multiply–add operations akin to fast Fourier transforms. Hardware accelerators could exploit this exact sparsity pattern for custom kernels that run faster than general dense matrix multiplication, thereby making BOFT not just memory efficient but also time efficient on specialized chips.

5. **Integration with Continual Learning**  
   Because BOFT preserves spectral norm precisely while still allowing rotations among neuron spaces, it may naturally mitigate catastrophic forgetting in multi-task continual learning scenarios where models must remember multiple disjoint distributions without allocating separate parameters per task.

## Future Work Prospects

Despite its merits, BOFT opens several compelling research avenues:

- **Designing Alternative Graph Topologies**  
  The current approach leverages hierarchical butterfly graphs; however, expander graphs or trellis structures might yield even sparser paths while preserving global reachability. Systematic exploration using graph theoretical metrics could reveal topologies that strike superior trade-offs between parameter count and conditioning.

- **Real-Time Adaptive Factorization During Training**  
  Instead of fixing $m$ and $b$ statically per layer, one could dynamically prune unused butterfly components based on validation performance metrics (e.g., gradient norms). Such pruning would reduce compute overhead without post hoc fine-grained adjustments.

- **Theoretical Guarantees on Approximation Error**  
  While empirical results suggest rapid decay of approximation error with increasing $m$, tighter analytical bounds relating butterfly depth to worst-case distance from arbitrary orthogonal matrices remain largely unproven. Proving such guarantees would cement BOFT’s theoretical foundations.

- **Leveraging Symmetry in Pretrained Models**  
  Modern foundation models exhibit symmetries (e.g., positional encoding invariance). By aligning butterfly blocks with these symmetries—for instance, mapping specific butterfly stages to positional transformer layers—the method could exploit model inductive priors more effectively.

- **Extending Beyond Linear Layers**  
  Current Boft applications target fully connected projections within transformers or convolutional kernels treated as linear maps. Extending sparse-factor orthogonality to residual connections or complex attention mappings remains an open technical challenge but promises richer model family coverage.

In closing, Orthogonal Butterfly represents a principled fusion of classical signal processing insight (fast transform factorization) with contemporary deep learning demands (parameter efficiency under massive scale). By reinterpreting dense transformations as efficient paths over sparse graphs, it unlocks scalable yet expressive finetuning pathways for tomorrow’s foundation models—and it invites an entire ecosystem of research into structured reparameterizations aligned with emerging hardware trends and multi-modal workloads.
