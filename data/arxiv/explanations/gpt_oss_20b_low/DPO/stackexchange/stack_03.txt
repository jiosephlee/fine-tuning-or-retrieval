\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why is a linear layer added on top of a transformer to predict rewards in the reward‑model phase?
Question:
In the reward‑model phase the paper mentions that the reward network \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) is initialized from the SFT policy and then a linear layer is added on top of the final transformer layer to output a scalar reward (Section 3.1). What is the justification for this architectural choice? Does it directly correspond to the way the policy logits are used, or is it simply a convenience to produce a single score?
Answer:
**Why do RLHF papers add a linear head on top of the transformer to produce a scalar reward?**

The reward model in the “reward‑model phase” is nothing more than a **neural network that takes a prompt–completion pair \((x,y)\) and outputs a real‑valued scalar** $r_{\phi}(x,y)$.  
In practice that network is the same language‑model backbone that was used for the SFT policy, but its final token‑level logits are discarded.  
Instead, the hidden representation that the transformer produces after its last layer is fed into a *single linear layer* (followed by a bias) and the scalar result is treated as the reward.

There are two intertwined reasons why this design is adopted:

1. **Convenience for the static‑logit loss**  
   In the reward‑model phase we want to maximise the likelihood of the observed human preferences under a Bradley–Terry (BT) model

   \[
   p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
   =\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
          {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+
           \exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)} .
   \]

   The maximum‑likelihood loss for a parametric reward $r_{\phi}$ is

   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{R}(\phi)
   =-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log\sigma \bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\bigr)\right] .
   \]

   This loss operates on **scalar differences**.  Therefore, to obtain a scalar for every \((x,y)\) pair a lightweight head that converts the transformer’s last‑layer activations to one number is sufficient; no extra architecture is required.  If we had simply used the raw language‑model logits, we would have to aggregate over all tokens or train a more complex network, which would add computational cost and risk overfitting.

2. **Alignment with the policy’s probability space**  
   The SFT policy is also a transformer that produces token‑wise logits $\ell_{\theta}(x,y_{t})$.  The final hidden state at the last token (or the average of all hidden states) is a natural summary of the sequence’s likelihood under the policy.  
   By attaching a linear head we obtain a *direct mapping* from that sequence representation to a real number that can be interpreted as a reward.  The linear head is effectively a *parameterised function* that is flexible enough to fit the preference data while still being simple to train (just a few dozen parameters).  Importantly, because the head is a linear transformation, its gradients are cheap to compute and back‑propagate through the entire transformer, enabling efficient training of the reward model.

---

### How this ties into the rest of the pipeline

1. **Reward‑model initialization** – The linear head is initialised from the fine‑tuned policy parameters $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$.  Thus, if the policy already assigns higher likelihood to high‑reward completions, the initial reward scores already have the correct sign, which speeds up convergence.

2. **Reward normalisation** – After training, the authors usually normalise $r_{\phi}$ so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\mathcal{D}}[r_{\phi}(x,y)]=0$ for each prompt $x$.  Because the head is linear, this is just a matter of adjusting the bias term; per‑prompt normalisation does not affect the relative order of completion scores, so it preserves the BT likelihood.

3. **Connection to the policy** – In the RL or DPO stages the reward is combined with the policy log‑probabilities:

   \[
   r_{\text{eff}}(x,y)=r_{\phi}(x,y)-\beta\bigl(\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr).
   \]

   The linear head produces $r_{\phi}$ that can be added directly to the log‑probabilities.  If we had used a more complex head, we would need to be careful to keep the reward on the same scale as the log‑probabilities, which a single linear transformation guarantees.

---

### Bottom line

Adding a single linear layer on top of the transformer in the reward‑model phase is **not an arbitrary architectural flourish**.  
It is a pragmatic choice driven by two constraints:

* The reward loss operates on scalar differences, so a lightweight mapping from the transformer’s last‑layer activations to a single number is all that is needed.  
* Using the same backbone as the policy means the reward head can be initialised from the policy parameters and trained efficiently, while the linearity keeps the scale of the reward compatible with the log‑probabilities that will later be combined with it.

Thus, the linear head is the simplest, most efficient way to convert the pretrained transformer into a scalar reward function that can be optimised with a cross‑entropy loss and later plugged into RL or DPO updates.
