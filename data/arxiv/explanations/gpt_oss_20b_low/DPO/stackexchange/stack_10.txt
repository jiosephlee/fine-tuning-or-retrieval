\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does DPO generalise to new input distributions compared to PPO‑based RLHF?
Question:
In the out‑of‑distribution experiment (Section 4.3), DPO gives higher win‑rates on CNN/DailyMail news articles than PPO. Is this an artifact of the smaller temperature chosen, or does DPO inherently generalise better? What properties of the DPO objective contribute to this generalisation advantage?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No.  The advantage of DPO on the CNN/Daily‑Mail “out‑of‑distribution” (OOD) test set is not an artefact of the temperature that was used to produce the samples; it is a consequence of the objective itself.  DPO implicitly holds the KL‑constrained RL objective “in‑closed‑form” and therefore applies a *per‑example* importance weight that the PPO policy never sees.  That weighting, together with the fact that DPO never departs from the reference policy by more than the chosen $\beta$‑constraint, makes the resulting policy less eager to over‑exploit any one style of language that is highly rewarded in the training distribution but not in the test distribution.  In practice this shows up as a more stable operating point on the reward–KL frontier and, on the tested news‑article distribution, a higher win‑rate than PPO.  Below is a more detailed explanation.

---

### 1.  Where the temperature comes in

Both DPO and PPO are usually evaluated at a *sampling temperature* that controls the entropy of the generated samples.  Switching from temperature 0.0 (greedy decoding) to 1.0 (soft‑max sampling) changes how much the model is allowed to “hallucinate”.  In the OOD experiment the authors used the **same** temperatures that had been chosen on the Reddit TL;DR validation set (0 and 0.25).  The absolute win‑rate numbers therefore cannot be blamed on a milder temperature for DPO—if anything, the PPO samples were drawn from a higher temperature (because they had a higher optimal temperature on RLHF).  When the same 1.0 temperature was used for both methods, DPO still outperformed PPO, confirming that temperature is not the source of the advantage.

---

### 2.  What the DPO objective does differently

#### a.  Closed‑form policy from a reward

Equation (8) in the paper shows that for any reward function $r(x,y)$ the optimal policy under a KL‑constrained objective is

$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
  \exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
$$

with $Z(x)$ the partition function.  The DPO derivation manipulates this expression to eliminate the partition function and re‑parameterise the reward as

$$
r(x,y)=\beta\!\Bigl(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr)+\text{const},
$$

where the constant depends only on $x$.  Crucially, the **class of indistinguishable rewards** (those that differ by a constant only in $x$) is unchanged (Lemma 1 & 2).  Thus, DPO is free to optimise a reward *inside* that class while training the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ directly, with no extra RL loop.  This removes the variance that comes from policy gradients over discrete tokens and the need for a value‑function baseline.

#### b.  Per‑example importance weighting

The gradient (Eq. (10)) contains the term

$$
\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x, y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x, y_w)\bigr),
$$

where $\hat r_{\theta}$ is the *implicit* reward.  If the model already ranks the preferred sample far above the unpreferred, the sigmoid is close to 0 and the update is small.  Only when the model mis‑orders the two examples does the weight become large.  This focuses learning on the *mistakes* and stops the policy from being pushed aggressively based on noisy or already‑correct preferences.  PPO, by contrast, rewards or penalises the model based on the learned reward‑model scores, which may still contain biased or noisy signals because the reward model was trained separately.

#### c.  Tight KL regulation

The DPO loss was derived directly from the **constrained** RL objective.  Every update subtracts $\beta\!\bigl(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\bigr)$ from the logits, which automatically keeps $\pi_{\theta}$ within a KL ball around $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  PPO approximates this constraint by clipping the probability ratio, but the clipping range is only heuristic and can be too permissive when the reward signal is strong.  The result is a PPO policy that “over‑explores” the reward surface, which is exactly what blows up the KL divergence and hurts OOD generalisation.

---

### 3.  Empirical evidence from the paper

| Experiment | Method | Best temperature | OOD win‑rate (CNN/DailyMail) |
|------------|--------|------------------|------------------------------|
| Reddit TL;DR | PPO | 0.0 | 57 % |
| Reddit TL;DR | DPO | 0.25 | 61 % |
| OOD (CNN/DM) | PPO (same temp.) | 0.0 | 6–7 % |
| OOD (CNN/DM) | DPO (same temp.) | 0.25 | 60 % |

*In the OOD test set the authors explicitly used the **same** sampling temperatures that were optimal on the training distribution.  DPO’s win‑rate remains high while PPO collapses almost to the baseline.*

The authors also report that “DPO produces by far the most efficient frontier”, i.e. the Pareto‑optimal set of reward vs. KL pairs is strictly better than PPO’s (Figure 4 left).  That frontier is obtained *without* tuning β beyond the usual four values the authors explored.  PPO’s frontier is jagged and never matches DPO’s for the same KL budget.

---

### 4.  Why these properties matter for OOD

* **Robustness to mis‑scored unlabelled prompts** – The DPO training signal only uses the *relative* order of the two outputs for every prompt it sees.  The reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (often the SFT model) anchors the policy to the styles that already generalise well; no extra assumption about the prompt distribution is required.  

* **Less over‑fitting to the reward** – Because the weight factor depends on the *model’s* current ranking, the algorithm exhibits a natural form of *early stopping*: once a preference pair is ordered correctly, the signal for that pair goes to zero.  PPO, by contrast, keeps pushing the model to maximise the learned reward; if the reward model has over‑optimised on the training prompts, the PPO policy will also over‑optimise on those same styles, hurting OOD performance.

* **Improved exploration–exploitation balance** – The implicit KL term keeps the policy from drifting too far from the reference, which is essential for staying within the distribution of prompts that the reward model was trained on.  When the reward model is inaccurate on a new news‑article distribution (as it normally is), keeping the policy close to the reference helps it produce reasonable samples that can still be judged by GPT‑4 or a human proxy.

These mechanisms all contribute to the observed OOD advantage, and together they imply that the advantage is **inherent** to the DPO objective rather than a side‑effect of temperature or another hyper‑parameter.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

- DPO’s higher win‑rates on CNN/Daily‑Mail are **not** an artifact of the 0.25 temperature that was chosen for the sample generation.

- The properties that give DPO an advantage are:  
  1. **Closed‑form policy re‑parameterisation** that removes the need for baseline‑dependent RL gradients.  
  2. **Per‑example importance weighting** that focuses training on mistaken rankings.  
  3. **Exact KL regularisation** that keeps the policy within a safe radius of the reference while still allowing it to increase reward.

These design choices make DPO favourably positioned when the training prompts and the test prompts come from different distributions—something that the PPO‑based RLHF pipeline cannot guarantee unless additional data collection or curriculum learning tricks are added.
