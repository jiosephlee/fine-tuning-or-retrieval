\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What are the implications of the equivalence class of reward functions for policy learning?
Question:
Definition 1 introduces an equivalence relation on reward functions: two rewards are equivalent if they differ by a function of \(x\). Lemma 2 says that equivalent rewards yield the same optimal policy. How does DPO ensure that it does not arbitrarily pick a reward from the equivalence class that might lead to numerical instability? Does this relate to the normalisation of rewards (zero mean) mentioned earlier?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Because the DPO objective is invariant to adding a state‑only term \(f(x)\) to a reward, the algorithm never has to decide *which* reward in an equivalence class it is learning—any member would induce the same optimal policy and the same preference likelihood. In practice, DPO implicitly chooses the *normalised* reward whose partition function is one, i.e. the reward that satisfies

\[
\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)=1 .
\]

This is exactly the same normalisation that is done when training the reward model in standard RLHF (zero‑mean or other baselines). Thus, DPO does not suffer from arbitrary numerical drift; the log‑ratio term automatically keeps the reward within a stable scale and the optimiser has no free parameter that could blow up. The “normalisation” you see earlier in the paper is simply the statement that the underlying reward model is fitted under a constraint (e.g. \(\mathbb{E}[r(x,y)]=0\)). DPO respects that same constraint by construction, because the implicit reward is a difference of log‑probabilities and therefore already centred and bounded.

---

### 1.  What does the equivalence relation mean?

The paper defines two rewards \(r\) and \(r'\) to be equivalent if \(r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)\) for some function of the state only. In a preference‑based setting the human probability of a preference depends only on the **difference** of rewards:

\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\bigr).
\]

Adding a state‑only term to both rewards leaves every difference unchanged, so the full distribution of human preferences is unchanged. The same operation does not alter the KL‑constrained RL value either, because the optimal policy of the constrained problem satisfies

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).
\]

The added term cancels in the ratio of two exponentials, hence the induced policy is unchanged.  
That’s why Lemma 2 holds: the entire equivalence class leads to the exact same optimal policy.

### 2.  Why DPO never “chooses” a bad reward

DPO replaces the explicit reward \(r_\phi(x,y)\) with the *log‑ratio* reward

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} .
\]

This expression already satisfies the normalisation property: substituting \(\pi_\theta=\pi_r\) in the constraint yields

\[
\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)e^{\tfrac{1}{\beta}\hat r_\theta(x,y)} = \sum_{y}\pi_{r}(y\mid x) =1.
\]

Thus \(\hat r_\theta\) lives in the *canonical* representative of its equivalence class (the one that makes the partition function equal to one). That is the same representative that would result from the usual reward‑model normalisation step (zero‑mean, etc.) in standard RLHF. Consequently, DPO does **not** wander into the extreme members of the class that could cause numerical blow‑ups.

### 3.  Connection to the earlier normalisation comment

When the paper talks about “normalising the rewards so that \( \mathbb{E}[r_\phi(x,y)]=0\) for all \(x\)”, it is describing a typical identifiability constraint for the MLE of a Bradley‑Terry model. DPO’s implicit reward is already centred implicitly:

\[
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\bigl[\hat r_\theta(x,y)\bigr]
=
\beta \,\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)-\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr]=0,
\]

because the expectations of the two log‑probabilities cancel. Therefore, the equivalence‑class choice made by DPO aligns exactly with the normalisation that is usually imposed on explicit reward models. In short, the two statements are consistent: DPO is automatically operating in the normalised sub‑space of reward functions.

### 4.  Practical implication

Because the DPO loss

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\Bigl[
\log \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}
-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}\Bigr)
\Bigr]
\]

only contains *differences* of log‑ratios, the scale of \(\beta\) controls the curvature (how strongly the KL penalty is respected) but does not introduce an arbitrary offset. Hence, the optimiser cannot drift to a reward that would produce numerical instability; the reward is always tied to probabilities that are already bounded between \(0\) and \(1\).

---

**Bottom line:**  
DPO sidesteps the issue of picking an arbitrary reward from an equivalence class by *parameterising* rewards as log‑ratios of policy probabilities. This parameterisation chooses the canonical, normalised member of each equivalence class, exactly the same way that explicit reward models are normalised in standard RLHF. As a result, DPO neither suffers from numerical instability nor needs extra hyper‑parameter tuning to avoid pathological rewards.
