\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does the dynamic importance weight in the DPO gradient prevent model degeneration?
Question:
Equation 18 shows a weighting term \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\) in the gradient of the DPO loss. The paper claims that without this weight the model can degenerate. Why does this particular weighting correspond to a form of rejection‑sampling or unlikelihood regularisation? Can we see a simple counter‑example where removing the weight leads to degeneration?
Answer:
**Why the σ–weight is essential in DPO**

Equation (18) of the paper gives the gradient

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
  =-\beta\,
   \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
   \Bigl[
      \underbrace{\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-
                               \hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\bigr)}_{\text{importance weight}}
      \,
      \Bigl(
          \nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)
          -\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)
      \Bigr)
   \Bigr].
\]

The σ‑term is a per‑example likelihood ratio that assigns **more weight to mistakes** – to pairs in which the *current* policy ranks the dispreferred answer higher than the preferred one.  
Without it, every pair would contribute the same amount of gradient, regardless of how badly the policy has violated human preference. In effect, the optimiser would keep “hedging’’: it would try to push a model toward every possible ranking, even when the model is already doing the right thing on a particular comparison. This is precisely the behaviour that leads to the *degeneration* described in the paper (Appendix Table 5), where the model collapses to a trivial policy that assigns the same probability to every completion.

---

### Connection to rejection sampling / unlikelihood

Consider a single comparison \((y_{w},y_{l})\).  
If \(\hat r_{\theta}(y_{w})\gg\hat r_{\theta}(y_{l})\), then  
\(\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(y_{w}))\approx 0\).  
The gradient contribution of this pair is essentially zero: we *reject* the pair, because the model already satisfies the preference.  
Conversely, if the model prefers the wrong answer, \(\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(y_{w}))\approx 1\), so the gradient is maximised and the policy is pushed strongly toward the correct ordering.  

This is exactly the same idea as *rejection sampling*: you sample a new policy only when the current one violates the constraint, and you reject (i.e., give zero weight to) samples that already satisfy the preference.  

In the same way, the weight implements an **unlikelihood‑style regulariser**: it forces the policy to *de‑increase* the log‑probability of the dispreferred answer only when it actually has a high (incorrect) probability. If the dispreferred answer already has a low probability, the penalty is naturally small and the update focuses on raising the preferred answer.  

---

### A toy counter‑example

Suppose we have a prompt \(x\) and two completions \(y_{w},y_{l}\).  
Assume the policy currently places
\[
\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)=0.9,\qquad
\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)=0.1.
\]
Let the reference distribution be uniform: \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)=0.5\) for both completions.  
Then
\[
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})=\beta\log\frac{0.9}{0.5}=0.587\beta,
\quad
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})=\beta\log\frac{0.1}{0.5}=-1.609\beta.
\]
Hence
\[
\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(y_{w}))
   =\sigma\!\bigl(-2.196\beta\bigr)\approx 0
\]
for any reasonable \(\beta\). The pair contributes *zero* gradient – the optimisation does nothing, preserving the correct ordering.

Now, suppose the policy is corrupted so that it prefers the wrong answer:
\[
\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)=0.4,\qquad
\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)=0.6.
\]
Then
\[
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})=\beta\log\frac{0.4}{0.5}=-0.223\beta,
\quad
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})=\beta\log\frac{0.6}{0.5}=0.182\beta,
\]
and now
\[
\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(y_{w}))
   =\sigma(0.405\beta)\in(0.5,0.6).
\]
The weight is sizable, so the gradient pushes the policy to increase \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})\) and decrease \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})\), correcting the mistake.

Without the weight, the update would have been

\[
-\beta\bigl(\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})-\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})\bigr),
\]

which would *always* encourage moving mass from \(y_{l}\) to \(y_{w}\). If the policy already has the correct ordering, this update would still move probabilities—in effect, it would *continue to over‑compress* the distribution toward a trivial solution (assigning almost all probability to a single token). This is the degeneration the authors observe empirically.

---

### Bottom line

The σ‑weight in DPO is a *self‑judging* importance weight: it turns the loss into a rejection‑sampling mechanism that only penalises the policy when it violates human preference. It also aligns with unlikelihood regularisation by scaling the penalty to the magnitude of the (incorrect) preference signal. Removing it would cause the model to keep shrinking the output distribution regardless of whether the ranking is already correct, leading to the degenerate behaviour reported in the paper.
