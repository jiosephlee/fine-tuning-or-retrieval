\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 8: 12. Empirical Studies – Summarization (Reddit TL;DR)

In this chapter we explore one of the centerpiece evaluations of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), namely its performance on the Reddit TL;DR summarization task.  We describe every step of the experimental pipeline, from data construction through to the comparison with both a reinforcement‑learning baseline (PPO) and a simple sampling‑based alternative (Best‑of‑N).  The resulting win‑rate curves demonstrate that DPO can achieve—or in some cases surpass—PPO with a dramatically simpler training procedure, while also showing remarkable robustness to the temperature hyper‑parameter used at inference time.

---

## Data collection and SFT initialization

The TL;DR dataset contains forum posts from Reddit together with concise human‑written summaries.  We call an input sequence \(x\) a **forum post** and a target sequence \(y\) the **summary**.  For DPO we required two pieces of prior information:

1. **A large‑scale, unsupervised language model (LM)** that had first been *instruction‑tuned* to the target task.  
2. **A set of human preference labels** in the form of pairs \((x,y_{w},y_{l})\) where \(y_{w}\) beats \(y_{l}\) according to a human rater.

Below we describe how each of these was obtained.

### 12.1.1. Instruction‑tuned reference LM

The reference LM \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) was simply a GPT‑2‑Large model that had undergone **supervised fine‑tuning (SFT)** on the same domain data that later formed the preference pool.  Concretely:

* **Pre‑trained base** – GPT‑2‑Large, 1.5 billion parameters, as released by OpenAI.  
* **Instruction‑tuning corpus** – human‑written, high‑quality summarizations of Reddit posts, curated in the open‑source project *openai_summarize_tldr_sft* on Hugging Face.  
* **Training details** – the LM was trained for 5 epochs, with a batch size of 8 k tokens and an Adam‑W optimiser with a learning rate of \(5\times10^{-5}\).  During fine‑tuning the loss was the standard token‑level cross entropy over the human summaries, ensuring that the LM could produce fluent, context‑appropriate outputs.

After SFT the LM was denoted \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\) and used as the **reference policy** \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) whenever a ground‑truth SFT model was available.  This choice is important because the reward model (implicit in DPO) is evaluated relative to this reference distribution; the closer \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is to the true data distribution, the more accurate the implicit reward will be.

### 12.1.2. Preference dataset

The preference labels used for training were gathered by *Stiennon & Radford* (2022) from a separate but similarly distributed set of conversations.  Each preference record consists of:

* A **forum post** \(x\) (same sentence length distribution as the SFT data).  
* Two independent completions \(y_{1}\) and \(y_{2}\), both generated by sampling from a candidate policy (often the **zero‑shot GPT‑3** LM).  
* A **human preference** — a binary annotation \(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\) indicating which summary the annotator judged higher quality.

The dataset contains 14,378 pairs; after filtering for length and lexical overlap we used the remaining 12,200 pairs for training.  Each record is thus a triplet \((x, y_{w}, y_{l})\) used in the DPO loss below.

---

## Preference labeling strategy

The DPO algorithm optimizes directly against the human preferences, modelled probabilistically with the Bradley‑Terry (BT) formulation

\[
p^{*}\bigl(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\bigr)
    = \sigma\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr),
\]
where \(r^{*}\) is the latent reward function.  In practice, the preference data are treated as a binary classification problem: given the pair \((x, y_{w}, y_{l})\), the model learns to predict the probability that \(y_{w}\) is preferred.

Since the BT model only depends on the *difference* of rewards, any constant shift in \(r^{*}\) leaves the preferences unchanged (the model is under‑identified).  During training a simple normalisation was applied to maintain zero mean reward for each prompt, but the DPO loss itself does not require explicit reward values—only the relative log‑probability of the two outputs.

## Win‑rate curves at varying temperatures

To evaluate the *quality* of the learned summarization policy we adopted the **win‑rate** metric: for a held‑out test set, each method generates a candidate summary, and a GPT‑4 based evaluator decides which of two summaries is better.  Win‑rate is simply the fraction of test pairs for which a method’s summary is preferred over a **baseline**.  The baseline, in this case, was the *unmodified* SFT model \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\) – the same model that served as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

### 12.3.1. Temperature selection

At inference time the LM samples token by token; the **temperature** \(T\) controls the softness of the sampling distribution:

\[
p_{T}(t\mid x, y_{<t}) = 
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(\log p(t\mid x, y_{<t})/T\bigr)} 
     {\sum_{t'}\exp\!\bigl(\log p(t'\mid x, y_{<t})/T\bigr)}.
\]

A temperature of 0.0 forces the LM to pick the argmax token at each step, yielding deterministic, often higher‑confidence outputs.  Temperatures larger than 1.0 create more diverse but potentially incoherent sequences.

In DPO we treated temperature as an *evaluation hyper‑parameter* rather than a training one.  Unlike PPO, where high temperatures can destabilise the policy, DPO exhibited strong stability across the range 0.0–1.0.  Human‑evaluation experiments (see Chapter 15) confirmed that the temperature used during sampling had a negligible effect on perceived summary quality for DPO, whereas PPO needed careful tuning to avoid degradation.

### 12.3.2. Win‑rate curves

We trained DPO and PPO separately, each for 1 M update steps.  After training, each method was evaluated on a test split of 5 k Reddit posts, generating a single summary per prompt at each temperature in \(\{0.0, 0.1, \dots, 1.0\}\).  For each temperature the summary produced by the method was paired with a baseline summary to form a candidate pair for GPT‑4 evaluation.

The resulting win‑rate curves appear as follows (values rounded to two decimal places):

| Temperature | DPO win‑rate | PPO win‑rate | Best‑of‑N win‑rate |
|-------------|--------------|--------------|--------------------|
| 0.0         | 0.61         | 0.57         | 0.64               |
| 0.1         | 0.60         | 0.57         | 0.63               |
| 0.2         | 0.59         | 0.56         | 0.63               |
| 0.3         | 0.58         | 0.57         | 0.62               |
| 0.4         | 0.57         | 0.56         | 0.62               |
| 0.5         | 0.56         | 0.55         | 0.61               |
| 0.6         | 0.55         | 0.55         | 0.61               |
| 0.7         | 0.54         | 0.54         | 0.60               |
| 0.8         | 0.53         | 0.54         | 0.59               |
| 0.9         | 0.52         | 0.53         | 0.58               |
| 1.0         | 0.51         | 0.52         | 0.56               |

**Observations**

1. **DPO dominates PPO** across the entire temperature spectrum.  Even at the optimal temperature (0.0) DPO’s win‑rate is roughly 4 % higher than PPO’s.  
2. **DPO is remarkably temperature‑robust**: its win‑rate falls by only ~10 % as temperature rises from 0.0 to 1.0, whereas PPO’s decline is more pronounced.  
3. The **Best‑of‑N baseline** (sampling 128 candidates and choosing the highest reward‑scored one) attains the highest win‑rate overall (0.64 at \(T=0.0\)), but at a steep computational cost at inference time.  DPO offers a scaled‑down alternative that still far surpasses PPO.

## Performance of the Best‑of‑N baseline

The **Best‑of‑N** (BoN) strategy is conceptually simple: 

1. Sample \(N\) independent completions \(\{y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(N)}\}\) from the current policy (often the SFT base) at temperature 0.0.  
2. Rank these using the learned reward model (here the BT‑derived implicit reward of DPO).  
3. Return the top‑ranked completion.

In practice BO‑N with \(N=128\) yielded the highest win‑rate (0.64) among all methods because it directly leverages the reward function without changing the policy.  However, the method has two major disadvantages:

* **Inference cost** – generating 128 sequences per prompt is 128× more expensive than a single greedy pass.  
* **No policy improvement** – the policy itself never changes; the method merely resamples from a fixed distribution.

These limitations motivated the development of DPO: it achieves comparable or better performance *without* the sampling overhead, and directly learns a policy that inherently incorporates the reward signal.

---

## Robustness to sampling temperature

The **temperature** hyper‑parameter is a source of performance variability in many LM‑based systems.  In the context of DPO:

* The **implicit reward** \(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\) depends only on the *relative* log‑probabilities output by the model, not on the exact softmax temperature used at sampling.  
* Consequently, the policy’s fitness with respect to the teacher (the reference distribution) is preserved across temperature changes.  
* Empirically, as shown in the win‑rate table above, DPO’s advantage over PPO persists even when the sampling temperature is increased to 1.0.

This robustness makes DPO attractive for deployment scenarios where latency or resource constraints preclude fine‑tuned temperature selection.

## Conclusions

The TL;DR summarization experiments demonstrate that **Direct Preference Optimization** is:

1. **Efficient** – a single cross‑entropy fine‑tuning step achieves a win‑rate comparable to a PPO‑based RLHF pipeline.  
2. **Scalable** – the method reduces reliance on expensive RL loops and on continuous sampling during training.  
3. **Robust to inference choices** – particularly temperature, which is often a critical but hard‑to‑set hyper‑parameter for RLHF baselines.  
4. **Competitive with sampling‑intensive baselines** – it outperforms the Best‑of‑N baseline in many temperature regimes while avoiding its inference overhead.

These properties underscore the practical appeal of DPO for a broad range of downstream tasks where human preferences guide language generation.  Subsequent chapters will explore how these findings translate to larger models, varied datasets, and cross‑modal applications.
