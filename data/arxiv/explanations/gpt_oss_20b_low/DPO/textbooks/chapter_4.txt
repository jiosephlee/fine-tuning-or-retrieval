\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 4: 5. Re‑parameterizing Reward with the Policy  

After establishing the standard reinforcement‑learning‑from‑human‑feedback (RLHF) pipeline, we now turn to the core mathematical innovation that underpins Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). The key insight is that the **reward function**—which is usually learned in a separate step—can be expressed *exactly* in terms of the *optimal policy* that maximises that reward under the familiar KL constraint. This section takes the reader through the derivation, shows how the partition function disappears from the Bradley–Terry preference model, and explains why the resulting maximum‑likelihood objective can be evaluated with a simple binary cross‑entropy loss that directly trains a language‑model policy.  

---

## 5.1  Re‑parameterizing the Reward  

Recall the reward‑maximisation problem that underlies RLHF. We wish to find a policy \(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\) that maximises expected reward while staying close to a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\), penalised by a KL‑divergence term:

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[
r(x,y)\bigr]
-\beta \,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\bigl(
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\;\|\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)
\bigr)
\Bigr].
\tag{5.1}
\]

The inner term is the standard entropy‑regularised reward maximisation familiar from policy‑gradient literature.  
A classical result (see e.g. Appendix A of the paper) tells us that the *optimal solution* to this constrained problem takes a closed‑form Boltzmann form:

\[
\pi^{*}_{r}(y\mid x)=
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\;
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad
Z(x)=\!\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y')\Bigr).
\tag{5.2}
\]

Equation (5.2) shows that the optimal policy is simply a re‑weighting of the reference policy by an exponential of the reward.  

Now we *invert* this relationship. Taking a logarithm on both sides of (5.2) gives:

\[
\log \pi^{*}_{r}(y\mid x)
=
\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)
+\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)
-\log Z(x).
\]

Rearranging for the reward, we obtain the **re‑parameterisation**:

\[
\boxed{
r(x,y)
=
\beta\,\log \frac{\pi^{*}_{r}(y\mid x)}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\;+\;
\beta\,\log Z(x).
}
\tag{5.3}
\]

The first term is a *log‑ratio of probabilities*, and the second term is simply a normalising constant that depends only on the prefix \(x\). Importantly, the partition function \(\log Z(x)\) is the same for *all* completions under a given \(x\); hence, as we shall see, it will cancel out when we compute preference probabilities.  

---

## 5.2  Substituting into the Bradley–Terry Model  

Human preference data are typically collected as **pairwise comparisons**: for a given prompt \(x\), a labeler decides whether completion \((y_{1})\) is preferred to completion \((y_{2})\). If we assume that preferences arise from some latent reward function \(r^{*}(x,y)\), a common statistical model is the Bradley–Terry (BT) model. The BT model posits that the probability of preferring \(y_{1}\) over \(y_{2}\) is

\[
p^{*}\!\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\bigr)
=
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)
+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}.
\tag{5.4}
\]

The only thing the BT model cares about is *the difference* \(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})\). We now substitute our re‑parameterisation (5.3) for each reward term. For any two completions \(y_{a}\) and \(y_{b}\),

\[
r^{*}(x,y_{a})-r^{*}(x,y_{b})
=
\beta\,\log \frac{\pi^{*}_{r}(y_{a}\mid x)}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{a}\mid x)}
-
\beta\,\log \frac{\pi^{*}_{r}(y_{b}\mid x)}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{b}\mid x)}.
\]

The \(\beta\,\log Z(x)\) term cancels, because it appears with a plus sign for both completions. Plugging this into (5.4) yields:

\[
p^{*}\!\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\bigr)
=
\frac{1}
{1+
\exp\!\Bigl(
\beta\,\log \frac{\pi^{*}_{r}(y_{2}\mid x)}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}
-
\beta\,\log \frac{\pi^{*}_{r}(y_{1}\mid x)}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}
\Bigr)}.
\tag{5.5}
\]

Equation (5.5) is remarkable: **the entire preference probability now depends solely on the optimal policy \(\pi^{*}_{r}\) and the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)**. The partition function has disappeared, and the only *unknown* is the policy we ultimately wish to learn.  

---

## 5.3  The Preference Likelihood and Its Simplification  

With (5.5) in hand, we can write the likelihood of the entire preference dataset \(\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}_{w}, y^{(i)}_{l})\}_{i=1}^{N}\) as a product over comparisons:

\[
\mathcal{L}(\pi^{*}_{r})\;=\;
\prod_{i=1}^{N}
p^{*}\!\bigl(y^{(i)}_{w}\succ y^{(i)}_{l}\mid x^{(i)}\bigr).
\]

Taking the negative log‑likelihood gives the objective we wish to minimise:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
=
-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\log
\Bigl[
\sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\,\log \frac{\pi^{*}_{r}(y^{(i)}_{w}\mid x^{(i)})}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{(i)}_{w}\mid x^{(i)})}
-
\beta\,\log \frac{\pi^{*}_{r}(y^{(i)}_{l}\mid x^{(i)})}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{(i)}_{l}\mid x^{(i)})}
\Bigl)
\Bigr],
\tag{5.6}
\]

where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) is the logistic function.  

Equation (5.6) has two important properties:

1. **No explicit reward model is needed.** The terms inside the logistic function are *log‑ratios of probabilities* computed directly by the policy \(\pi^{*}_{r}\) and the fixed reference policy.

2. **The loss is a simple binary cross‑entropy.** If we view each comparison as a binary classification problem (preferred vs dispreferred), the expression above is precisely the log‑likelihood of a *logistic regression* that predicts the preference label from the log‑ratio of policy probabilities.

Thus, by re‑parameterising the reward in terms of the policy, we *collapse* the two‑stage RLHF pipeline (reward‑learning + RL) into a **single‑stage supervised‑learning problem**.  

---

## 5.4  From Loss to Update: Intuition Behind the Gradient  

The gradient of \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}\) with respect to the policy parameters \(\theta\) has an intuitive form. After a short calculation (see Appendix B of the paper), we obtain:

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
=
-\beta\,
\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_{w}, y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[
\sigma\!\bigl(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\bigr)\;
\bigl(
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)
-
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)
\bigr)
\Bigr],
\tag{5.7}
\]

where \(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\,\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\) is the *implicit reward* induced by the policy.  

Each comparison contributes a term that *increases* the log‑probability of the preferred completion \(y_{w}\) and *decreases* that of the dispreferred one \(y_{l}\). The weighting factor \(\sigma(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))\) is large when the policy currently thinks the dispreferred completion is better than the preferred one, and small otherwise. In other words, **DPO pays most attention to the “hard” examples where the model’s current ordering of the pair is wrong**. This dynamical importance weighting is precisely what prevents the policy from collapsing to trivial (e.g., degenerate) solutions that arise when we simply push the probabilities of the preferred tokens up and down without any constraint.  

---

## 5.5  Summary  

* The central idea of Direct Preference Optimization is to **parameterise the reward directly in terms of the optimal policy**: \(r(x,y)=\beta\log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\).  
* Plugging this form into the Bradley–Terry preference model **eliminates the partition function** and yields a preference probability that depends only on the policy.  
* Maximising the likelihood of the observed preference data under this model leads to a **binary cross‑entropy loss** that can be optimised with standard supervised‑learning tools, *without any reinforcement learning* or explicit reward‑model training.  
* The resulting gradient updates have an intuitive interpretation: increase the probability of the preferred answers, decrease that of the dispreferred ones, and weight each comparison by how wrong the current implicit reward ordering is.  

These observations explain why DPO is *stable*, *computationally cheap*, and *competitive* with existing PPO‑based RLHF pipelines, while requiring far fewer engineering decisions and hyperparameter tunings. The remainder of the book will show how DPO behaves in practice across a variety of tasks and give guidance on its practical implementation.
