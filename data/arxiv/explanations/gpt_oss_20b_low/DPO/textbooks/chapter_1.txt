# Foundations of Language Model Alignment

Large‑scale language models (LMs) trained purely in an unsupervised manner on the vastness of the public internet can acquire remarkable factual knowledge, linguistic fluency, and even rudimentary reasoning skills. Yet, because the data that inspire their learning contains an almost immeasurable mix of intentions, biases, and errors, the raw capabilities of an LLM are not inherently aligned with the goals or safety standards that users—or the society at large—require. This chapter introduces the core challenges of steering these expansive neural systems toward desired behavior and surveys the conventional approach that has been adopted to bridge the gap between the wide‑spread, noisy knowledge stored in the model weights and the narrow, user‑specific objectives that we ultimately wish to instantiate.

---

## Role of Unsupervised Pre‑training in Acquiring Knowledge

The foundational step that gives LLMs their power is self‑supervised pre‑training. A model such as GPT or PaLM processes an enormous, diverse corpus of web text. Its objective is to predict the next token given a preceding context—formally, it maximizes the likelihood

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{unsup}}(\theta)=\sum_{(x,y)}\log p_\theta(y\mid x),
\]

where \(x\) denotes a partial sequence and \(y\) the following token(s). Because the training data spans many topics and styles, the resulting model captures broad world knowledge, syntactic patterns, and basic reasoning pathways. Empirical evidence shows that such pre‑trained models can zero‑shot or few‑shot perform a wide spectrum of tasks, from translation to arithmetic reasoning.

However, the very unsupervised nature that endows LMs with versatility also instills a problem: the dataset from which they learn has an implicit objective that reflects the aggregate intentions of all the data creators. These include professional writers, casual posters, automated bots, and countless technology platforms, each with distinct biases and worldviews. The model consequently learns to generate text that is likely to be found in the training distribution, regardless of whether such text is safe, helpful, or desired by a particular end‑user. The pre‑training objective does not distinguish between “good” and “bad” generations; it merely optimizes for statistical fidelity to the source.

---

## Safety and Controllability Concerns

Because LMs do not inherently discriminate between correct and harmful content, several safety and controllability issues arise:

1. **Amplification of Misinformation and Bias**  
   The model can reproduce or even magnify biases present in the data, including gender, racial, or ideological bias, and it can produce misleading or false statements that the model “intrinsically believes” to be true.

2. **Unwanted Behavior**  
   In the absence of explicit constraints, an LM might generate content that violates policies such as harassment, hate speech, or privacy violations—even if the user never explicitly requests it.

3. **Lack of Purpose‑driven Control**  
   Users may wish to steer the model toward generating responses that are more factual, more concise, more cheerful, more empathetic, or consistently aligned with a particular code of conduct. This selective control over the output distribution, without modifying the entire model’s internal knowledge, is not possible with raw pre‑trained weights.

Collectively, these concerns motivate a second phase of modeling—one that explicitly grounds the model’s outputs in human preferences and safety criteria.

---

## The RLHF Pipeline

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as the dominant paradigm for infusing safety and controllability into LLMs. The RLHF pipeline unfolds across three substantive stages:

1. **Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT)**  
   A pre‑trained LM is fine‑tuned on a curated dataset of high‑quality demonstrations. This step serves two purposes: (a) it infuses the model with task‑specific knowledge (e.g., instruction following, summarization), and (b) it pushes the distribution of generated outputs closer to the distribution of labeled examples. Formally, SFT minimizes a loss such as

   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^*)}\bigl[\log p_\theta(y^* \mid x)\bigr].
   \]

2. **Preference Collection and Reward Modeling**  
   To capture *relative* judgments that capture nuances of safety and helpfulness, human annotators are presented with *pairs* of completions \((y_1, y_2)\) generated by the SFT model for the same prompt \(x\) and asked to indicate which completion is preferable, denoted as \(y_w \succ y_l \mid x\). These *preference pairs* \(\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N\) implicitly provide samples from a latent reward function \(r^*(x,y)\). A flexible choice for modeling preferences is the Bradley‑Terry (BT) model:

   \[
   p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)}.
   \]

   A neural reward model \(r_\phi(x,y)\) is learned by maximizing the likelihood over \(\mathcal{D}\), yielding the loss

   \[
   \mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi,\mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
   \bigl[\log\sigma\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\bigr)\bigr],
   \]
   where \(\sigma\) is the logistic function.

3. **RL Fine‑Tuning**  
   The final stage treats the reward \(r_\phi(x,y)\) as a signal to adjust the policy \(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\) so as to maximize expected reward while limiting deviation from the reference policy (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi_{\text{SFT}}\)). The objective is

   \[
   \max_{\pi_\theta}
   \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}
   r_\phi(x,y)
   \;-\;
   \beta\,
   \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
   \]

   Because the reward is a discrete scalar obtained only after a complete sequence is sampled, this objective is not differentiable. Practitioners therefore employ policy gradient methods, most notably Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). PPO repeatedly samples sequences from the current policy, evaluates them with the learned reward, and updates the parameters \(\theta\) using a clipped surrogate objective that attempts to stay close to the reference distribution.

---

## Limitations of Standard RLHF

Despite its empirical successes, the standard RLHF pipeline carries several pragmatic and theoretical burdens that hamper rapid and robust deployment:

1. **Complexity of the RL Loop**  
   The RL step introduces an additional training loop that involves repeatedly sampling many sequences, estimating rewards for each, and applying policy gradient updates. Implementing, debugging, and tuning an RL system is far more intricate than a single supervised objective. Researchers must manage learning rates, clipping parameters, batch sizes, and reward shaping—all hyperparameters that can dramatically influence stability and final performance.

2. **Sampling Burden and Computational Cost**  
   Each iteration of PPO must draw a large number of trajectories from the policy to estimate gradients. For large LMs with billions of parameters, generating a single completion may take several seconds of GPU time. Thus, a full RLHF fine‑tuning job can require the equivalent of hours or days of compute, especially if the target horizon is a high‑quality generation with thousands of tokens.

3. **Instability and Reward Over‑Optimization**  
   The combination of high‑variance policy gradients and the reward reward-shaping loop can lead to training collapse. RLHF models are notorious for exhibiting stagnation, mode collapse (all outputs become identical high‑reward sequences), and unintended reward hacking (the model finds short‑loops or degenerate outputs that fool the reward network).

4. **Loss of Transparency and Theoretical Guarantees**  
   By learning a separate reward model \(r_\phi\) and then optimizing a policy to match that reward through RL, we introduce two layers of abstraction. Any mis‑specification or under‑fitting in the reward network propagates into the policy. Moreover, the practical RL algorithms are approximate; they do not guarantee convergence to the global optimum of the underlying constrained optimization problem.

These constraints motivate the search for “RL‑free” approaches that directly surface a policy that satisfies human preferences, thereby simplifying the pipeline, reducing computational burden, and improving stability.

---

In the ensuing chapters we will explore *Direct Preference Optimization*, an elegant framework that bypasses explicit reward modeling and RL altogether. By reparameterizing the optimal policy in terms of a latent reward, we arrive at a single‐step maximum‑likelihood training objective that retains the formal fidelity of the RLHF objective while dispensing with sampling and policy‑gradient noise. This contrast sets the stage for a deeper dive into the mathematics that enable a direct pathway from human preference data to aligned language outputs.

# Chapter 2  
## Human Preference Data and Ranking Models  

Language models trained online or offline respond to humans in a variety of ways, and it is often easier to ask a user to rank two completions than to provide a single “correct” answer.  This chapter explains how we formalise this relative judgment, why it is an attractive supervision signal, and how we translate the data into mathematically tractable models – notably the Bradley–Terry model and its extensions to the Plackett–Luce family.  We also describe practical workflow for collecting large offline preference datasets and pre‑processing these data before they are fed to a reward‑learning pipeline.  

---

## 2.1  From Human Rankings to Preference Pairs  

### 2.1.1  The Building Block: a Preference Pair

When a user is posed with a prompt \(x\) (for instance, “Generate a summary of the following Reddit post”), the language model generates two candidate completions, \(\;y_1\) and \(\;y_2\).  A human labeler is asked to choose the preferable one.  The order supplied by the labeler is formalised as the preference pair  

\[
y_w \succ y_l \mid x \quad\text{or}\quad \bigl(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x\bigr)\;\text{or}\;\bigl(y_2 \succ y_1 \mid x\bigr),
\]

where \(y_w\) denotes the *winning* or *preferred* completion and \(y_l\) denotes the *losing* or *dispreferred* completion.  The horizontal bar \(\mid x\) reminds us that the preference is conditioned on the prompt – the same two completions might be ranked differently depending on the context.  

A single preference pair captures relative quality: it tells the model *how* the two candidates compare, but it does not supply an absolute target score.  This relative signal is extremely valuable in training, especially when absolute positive examples are sparse or costly to obtain.  Unfortunately, relative reasoning does not provide an absolute scale, so we need statistical models that turn these pairwise judgments into a form usable for learning reward functions.  

### 2.1.2  Why pairwise preferences are stronger than single labels  

In many NLP tasks, obtaining a *high‑quality* demonstration that is both correct and concise is difficult.  A practitioner can, of course, provide a demonstration (e.g. a correct summary), but can they guarantee that it is the *best* possible?  Moreover, different annotators might diverge on a dialectic preference.  By collecting a *pairwise* comparison between two real completions the model can learn the *relative* ordering that humans care about, even when they disagree on absolute quality.  This compares favourably against *supervised demonstrations*, which are vulnerable to biases inherent in the annotator’s style, or to *single‑label* human‑written objectives that can be noisy or inconsistent.  

---

## 2.2  The Bradley–Terry Model  

### 2.2.1  Classical formulation  

The Bradley–Terry (BT) model was introduced in 1952 to analyse paired comparisons in psychometrics.  It posits that each object (in our case, each completion \(y\)) has an intrinsic *score* or *ability* \(\theta_y\).  The probability that \(y_1\) beats \(y_2\) is then  

\[
\mathbb{P}(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) 
   = \frac{e^{\theta_{y_1}}}{e^{\theta_{y_1}} + e^{\theta_{y_2}}}\!,
\]

where the exponentials guarantee positivity, and the denominator ensures the probabilities sum to one.  In the context of language models, however, we do not have a static \(\theta_y\) but a *reward function* \(r(x,y)\) that depends on the prompt \(x\).  Replacing \(\theta_y\) by \(r(x,y)\) yields the probabilistic expression  

\[
p_{\text{BT}}\!\bigl(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x\bigr)
  = \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_1)\bigr)}
         {\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_1)\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_2)\bigr)} ,
\qquad\text{(1)}
\]

which is the formula used in most RLHF pipelines to relate a latent reward function to observed human comparisons.  Note that the denominator depends on both completions, so the model implicitly accounts for *pairwise context*.  

### 2.2.2  Intuition: magnitude versus comparison  

The BT model cares only about the *difference* between the two rewards:

\[
p_{\text{BT}}\!\bigl(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x\bigr)
  = \sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\bigr) ,
\qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\!.
\]

If the reward function assigns \(r(x,y_1) = 3\) and \(r(x,y_2)=1\), the probability that \(y_1\) wins is \(\sigma(2) \approx 0.88\).  Conversely, swapping the values flips the probability to \(1-\sigma(2)\).  Thus a latent reward model determines the odds of a human preferring one completion over another, letting us *fit* the model using standard logistic regression in a maximum‑likelihood framework.  

---

## 2.3  Extensions: Plackett–Luce Models  

### 2.3.1  From pairwise to full rankings  

Sometimes human labelers may provide *more than two* candidates per prompt.  A natural extension is the Plackett–Luce (PL) family, which generalises BT to arbitrary rankings of more than two items.  The PL probability that an ordering \(\bigl(y_{(1)},y_{(2)},\dots,y_{(k)}\bigr)\) occurs is  

\[
p_{\text{PL}}\!\bigl(y_{(1)} \succ y_{(2)} \succ \dots \succ y_{(k)} \mid x\bigr)
  = \prod_{i=1}^{k}
      \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_{(i)})\bigr)}
           {\sum_{j=i}^{k}\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_{(j)})\bigr)} .
\]

When \(k=2\), the PL model collapses to BT, because the denominator in the first fraction becomes \(\exp(r(x,y_1))+\exp(r(x,y_2))\).  

### 2.3.2  Practical reasons for using BT  

In many RLHF workloads, labelers are asked to compare only two completions at a time, because it is faster, cheaper, and less error‑prone.  Consequently, most public preference datasets (e.g., from GPT‑4 prompts or human‑annotated dialogue) consist of *pairwise* labels only.  The BT model is therefore the standard because it provides a direct, closed‑form relationship between the latent reward and the observed data.  PL models are employed when datasets include *rank lists* (e.g., all responses collected from a fixed prompt, sorted by a rubric).  In that case we use the full PL likelihood, which can be optimized in the same way as BT but with an additional summation over the remaining items.  

---

## 2.4  Collecting Large Offline Preference Datasets  

### 2.4.1  Generating candidate completions  

A typical pipeline starts from an unsupervised or instruction‑tuned base language model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  For every prompt \(x\) in a curated set, we draw two samples \(\;y_1, y_2 \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\).  Sampling may be controlled: temperature, top‑k, or nucleus sampling is often tuned to generate diverse but high‑quality candidates.  The two sampled completions become the *candidates* for comparison.  

### 2.4.2  Human annotation workflow  

Human annotators (or paid crowd workers) are presented with the prompt and the two completions side‑by‑side.  The annotator selects the *better* one (or may choose “tie” if they are indistinguishable; this is often allowed as a third class).  Because the labeler is simply making a *relative* decision, the process is faster than writing an entire response and tends to require fewer annotations per prompt.  Moreover, annotators can be instructed to consider specific properties (e.g., factuality, politeness) to bias the preference toward the desired quality dimension.  

Once the annotations are collected, we obtain a large list  

\[
\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\,\bigl|\,i = 1,\dots,N\bigr\}
\]

of *offline* preference pairs.  Crucially, the data are offline: once collected, they can be reused for multiple training runs, hyper‑parameter sweeps, or even for evaluating newer models.  

### 2.4.3  Balancing diversity and difficulty  

Because we sample from the reference policy, the difficulty of the preferences depends on the diversity of \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  If \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is over‑confident, many pairs will have a clear winner; if it is highly stochastic, the annotator may often say “tie”.  It is common practice to enforce a *minimum* reward gap (e.g., requiring that one sample's log‑probability be at least \(0.5\) lower than the other) so that each comparison is informative and the labeler’s decision is not arbitrary.  

---

## 2.5  Pre‑processing and Normalising Reward Signals  

### 2.5.1  From completions to discounted rewards  

During RLHF, we typically treat the latent reward as a scalar function \(r(x,y)\).  When fitting a reward model via a neural network, an extra convention is to normalise the predicted reward values to zero mean and unit variance over the validation set.  This stabilises optimisation because the scale of \(r\) directly effects the gradients of the policy.  Concretely, after initial training we compute  

\[
\mu_x = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim \mathcal{D}}[\,r_\phi(x,y)\,], \qquad
\sigma_x^2 = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim \mathcal{D}}\!\Bigl[\,\bigl(r_\phi(x,y)-\mu_x\bigr)^2\Bigr] .
\]

The normalised reward is  

\[
\hat r_\phi(x,y) = \frac{r_\phi(x,y)-\mu_x}{\sigma_x} .
\]

All computations that involve \(r\) – such as the Bradley–Terry likelihood (1) or the RL objective – can then use \(\hat r_\phi\).  The normalisation step can be performed iteratively during training, re‑computing \(\mu_x\) and \(\sigma_x\) after a certain number of epochs.  

### 2.5.2  Avoiding bias from reference model variance  

A frequent source of bias arises if the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) has very high entropy for certain prompts.  Since the reward is trained by comparing two samples from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), the noise level in the preference data is higher when \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is uncertain.  A simple mitigation is to *re‑sample* prompts or *filter* prompts with extremely high log‑probability variance.  Some research works even capture *confidence scores* from the grader (e.g., “I was sure”, “I was unsure”) and weight the likelihood contributions accordingly.  

---

## 2.6  Example: Fashioning a Reward from Human Preference Data  

Suppose we have a preference pair \((x,y_w,y_l)\) and a reward network \(r_\phi(x,y)\).  The Bradley–Terry likelihood for this pair is  

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BT}}(\phi;\,x,y_w,y_l) 
   = -\log \sigma\!\Bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\Bigr)\!.
\]

Aggregating over the dataset gives the total reward‑loss

\[
\mathcal{L}_R(\phi;\mathcal{D}) = 
  \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N
   \mathcal{L}_{\text{BT}}\bigl(\phi;\,x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)}\bigr).
\]

By minimising \(\mathcal{L}_R\), we train \(r_\phi\) so that its induced preferences match human judgments.  Later, when we train a policy using reinforcement learning (or Direct Preference Optimization, DPO), we simply use \(\hat r_\phi(x,y)\) as the reward signal.  

---

## 2.7  Closing Remarks  

The Bradley–Terry model and its Plackett–Luce extensions offer a principled, mathematically tractable bridge between noisy human judgments and a continuous reward function.  Because they only require *relative* preferences, we can collect large, diverse datasets cheaply and reuse them repeatedly.  Coupled with careful pre‑processing such as reward normalisation and prompt filtering, these models form the backbone of contemporary preference‑based fine‑tuning pipelines for language models.  In the next chapter we will see how these reward signals are harnessed to train policies that truly reflect human intent—including the novel Direct Preference Optimization technique that eliminates the need for a separate reinforcement‑learning loop.
