\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 7: 11. Empirical Studies – Controlled Sentiment Generation  

In this chapter we investigate one of the most transparent settings in which a language‑model can be steered toward a desired behavior: the generation of movie‑review snippets with a preferred sentiment.  
Because the task is entirely synthetic—human preferences are produced automatically by a trusted sentiment classifier—the ground‑truth reward function is known.  This setting lets us reason quantitatively about the *reward–KL frontier* that is usually considered in the reinforcement‑learning‑from‑human‑feedback (RLHF) pipeline, and it provides a clean testbed to compare the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) algorithm against competing methods such as proximal policy optimization (PPO) and an oracle variant that has access to the true reward (RLHF‑GT).  

The chapter is organized into the following sub‑sections: (i) the formal definition of the task and the construction of the preference dataset; (ii) the hyper‑parameter regimes explored for each method; (iii) an empirical analysis of the reward–KL frontier; and (iv) an in‑depth discussion of why DPO shows a markedly superior efficiency. Throughout we emphasize intuition and derivation, but keep the exposition at a level suitable for an introductory textbook on large‑scale language‑model alignment.  

---

## Task Setup  

### 1.1 Data Source and Prompt Construction  

The movie‑review corpus comes from the open‑access IMDb dataset \citep{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}.  
For each review we extract a *prefix* \(x\) consisting of the first 60 tokens (approximately 300–400 characters).  The language‑model policy must then produce a *completion* \(y\) that completes the review to a coherent, sentiment‑appropriate ending.  The generated completion should either continue a positive review or shift to a negative one; the objective is to keep the sentiment *positive* in proportion to the human preference labels we supply.  

Mathematically, we define the generation problem as the policy distribution  
\[
\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \frac{\exp \bigl( \theta^\top h(x,y) \bigr)}{Z_{\pi_\theta}(x)}\,,
\]
where \(\theta\) are the transformer parameters and \(h(x,y)\) stacks the contextual hidden state embeddings.  In practice \(\pi_\theta\) is the soft‑max output of the model at every decoding token.  

### 1.2 Ground‑Truth Reward via Sentiment Classifier  

A key advantage of the controlled setting is that we can treat a pretrained sentiment classifier \(\mathcal{S}\) as the ground‑truth reward function \(r^*(x,y)\).  \(\mathcal{S}\) is a fine‑tuned BERT encoder that outputs a probability \(p_{\text{pos}}(x,y) = \mathbb{P}[\text{positive sentiment}\mid x,y]\).  We set  
\[
r^*(x,y) \;=\; \log \frac{p_{\text{pos}}(x,y)}{1-p_{\text{pos}}(x,y)} ,
\]
the log‑odds of positive sentiment, so that larger values correspond to *more* positive reviews.  

Because the ground‑truth reward is known, we can compute the *expected reward* of any policy exactly:
\[
J(\pi_\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}}\Bigl[ \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\bigl[ r^*(x,y) \bigr] \Bigr] .
\]
The KL divergence term measures distance from a *reference policy* \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), which is the unsupervised GPT‑2‑large model after a supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) step on positive reviews:
\[
\operatorname{KL}\bigl(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr) \;=\;
 \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}} \Bigl[\, \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\!
   \bigl[ \log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \bigr] \Bigr].
\]

The constrained RLHF objective can now be written exactly:
\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; J(\pi_\theta) \;-\; \beta \; \operatorname{KL}\bigl(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr),
\]
where \(\beta>0\) controls how much the policy is allowed to drift away from the source distribution.  

### 1.3 Preference Dataset Construction  

The DPO, PPO, and RLHF‑GT methods all require *pairwise preference labels*.  Since we can evaluate sentiment exactly, we generate them programmatically:  

1. For each prompt \(x\), sample two completions \((y_1, y_2)\) from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  
2. Evaluate each with \(\mathcal{S}\) to obtain \(r^*(x,y_1)\) and \(r^*(x,y_2)\).  
3. If \(r^*(x,y_1) > r^*(x,y_2)\), label \(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x\); otherwise replace the sign.  

The resulting dataset
\[
\mathcal{D} \;=\; \{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}_w, y^{(i)}_l)\}_{i=1}^{N}
\]
contains \(N=50{,}000\) examples, balanced between positive and negative completions, and is *offline* (batch) rather than online.

## Hyper‑parameter Sweeps  

Because the reward–KL trade‑off is the central quantity of interest, each algorithm is tested over a grid of hyper‑parameters that adjust how conservative the learned policy is.  The tables below summarize the values explored.  

| Algorithm | Hyper‑parameter | Values |
|-----------|------------------|--------|
| DPO | \(\beta\) | 0.05, 0.1, 1, 5 |
| PPO | target KL | 3, 6, 9, 12 |
| RLHF‑GT | target KL | 3, 6, 9, 12 |
| Unlikelihood | \(\alpha\) (weight on negative log‑likelihood) | 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0 |
| Preferred‑FT | none (supervised) | baseline |

All models were trained for 100 gradient steps on the same dataset, using a learning rate of \(5\times 10^{-5}\) and the Adam optimizer with \(\beta_1=0.9, \beta_2=0.999\).  For PPO, the standard clipped surrogate objective was used with a batch size of 64, a discount factor \(\gamma=1.0\) (since each completion is treated as a single episode), and an entropy bonus of 0.01.

## Frontier Plots: Reward vs. KL  

For each hyper‑parameter setting, after training we evaluate the policy on a held‑out set of 5,000 prompts.  We compute two statistics:

1. **Average reward** \( \bar{R} = J(\pi_\theta) \).  
2. **Average KL divergence** \( \bar{K} = \operatorname{KL}\bigl(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr) \).

Plotting \((\bar{K},\, \bar{R})\) for all runs gives a *reward–KL frontier*.  Points that lie far to the right correspond to policies that have drifted substantially from the reference model; points that are higher have achieved higher reward.  Because the objective is linear in \(-\beta\,K\), the optimal frontier is expected to be a convex curve approximating the Pareto frontier of the constrained optimization problem.

### 11.4.1 Frontier Results  

Figure 11.1 (in the text) shows the frontier plots.  The salient observations are:

- **DPO** produces the *tightest* frontier: for any given reward it achieves a lower KL divergence than the other methods.  Equivalently, for any given KL budget it obtains the highest reward.  
- **PPO** trails behind DPO across the board.  Even when we give PPO a target KL equal to the *largest* KL achieved by a DPO configuration, PPO still underperforms in reward.  
- **RLHF‑GT** (PPO with ground‑truth reward) is slightly better than standard PPO, but still far from the DPO frontier.  
- **Unlikelihood** and **Preferred‑FT** produce very shallow frontiers: rewarding reward alone (unlikelihood) causes catastrophic mode collapse, while supervised fine‑tuning on preferred completions (Preferred‑FT) halts at a low reward plateau.

Quantitatively, at \(\bar{K}=4.0\) the DPO optimum achieves \(\bar{R}=0.88\), whereas PPO (with the same KL) reaches only \(\bar{R}=0.75\).  At \(\bar{R}=0.9\), DPO keeps \(\bar{K}=3.2\) while PPO requires \(\bar{K}>6.5\).  The RLHF‑GT method is more conservative; it requires a KL budget of roughly 8 to reach \(\bar{R}=0.89\).  

These results corroborate the theoretical claim that DPO directly maximizes the same objective that PPO tries to approximate, but without needing an explicit policy gradient estimate.  The DPO loss
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\,
  \log\sigma\!\left(
    \beta\,\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} \;-\;
    \beta\,\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
  \right)
\]
is simply a weighted binary cross‑entropy that scales the weight of each example by how much the implicit reward \(\hat{r}_\theta\) misorders the pair.  Thus each gradient step moves the policy in a direction that *exactly*\ inner‑product‑aligns with the optimal policy w.r.t. the discounted objective.

## Discussion of DPO’s Superior Efficiency  

### 12.1 Ratio of Learned vs. Reference Probabilities  

The core of DPO is the observation that the optimal policy for a KL‑constrained reward maximization problem can be expressed directly in terms of a *ratio* of the policy probabilities:
\[
\boxed{
   r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\,\log\frac{\pi(x,y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x,y)} \;+\; \beta\,\log Z(x) \, .
}
\]
Because the partition function \(\log Z(x)\) is *constant* w.r.t. the choice of action \(y\), it vanishes when computing preferences between two completions.  Consequently the Bradley‑Terry choice probability depends only on the ratio \(\pi(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\).  The consequence is that the *implicit reward* is automatically *calibrated* to the reference policy; it does not need any external baseline or value function.  In PPO, by contrast, the critic estimates the value of a policy configuration, a task that can be highly noisy in the discrete language generation setting.  The DPO loss sidesteps this entirely.

### 12.2 No Sampling Required  

PPO (and most policy‑gradient methods) must generate *roll‑outs* of complete sequences from the current policy in order to estimate expected reward and advantage.  Each rollout is expensive: even a single-pass generation of a 200‑token completion on a 6‑B parameter model requires millions of FLOPs.  DPO, however, uses the current *pre‑computed* probabilities from the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and the policy being updated \(\pi_\theta\).  The ratio \(\pi_\theta(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\) can be obtained in a single forward pass for each token, after which the per‑example cross‑entropy loss is computed exactly.  Thus DPO’s *single‑step*, *batch‑wise* gradient updates are far more efficient computationally and require no simulation loop.

### 12.3 Stability via Implicit Normalization  

The log‑ratio reward automatically incorporates a *soft* normalization term \(\beta\,\log Z(x)\) that ensures the policy distribution stays well‑behaved.  If the numerator becomes too large (policy over‑confident) or too small (policy too flat), the ratio will adjust so that the log‑ratio reward does not diverge.  In PPO, a similar effect must be achieved by clipping the surrogate objective or by adding a large KL penalty; but setting these hyper‑parameters correctly is delicate and problem‑dependent.  DPO obviates this tuning: the only free hyper‑parameter is \(\beta\), which directly trades reward gain against KL penalty in a transparent way.

### 12.4 Empirical Sensitivity to \(\beta\)  

Although DPO makes \(\beta\) the *only* crucial hyper‑parameter for the trade‑off, it is also remarkably robust.  Table 11.2 (in the text) reports reward–KL curves for \(\beta=0.05,0.1,1,5\).  Across all values, DPO remains within 5% of the optimum frontier; the largest difference occurs at \(\beta=5\), where the policy becomes very conservative and rewards drop modestly.  PPO, by contrast, is *highly* sensitive to the target KL: a single change in the KL budget can drastically swing the reward.  This empirical resilience mirrors the analytic nature of the DPO objective.

## Conclusion  

In the controlled sentiment generation setting, Direct Preference Optimization demonstrates a *principled* and *efficient* alternative to reinforcement‑learning‑based policy optimization.  The reward–KL frontier clearly shows that DPO consistently achieves higher reward for a given KL divergence than PPO or RLHF‑GT.  This superiority stems from three intertwined properties:

1. **Exact recovery of the optimal policy** via a closed‑form expression that eliminates the need for a separate reward estimator.  
2. **Elimination of sampling and the associated variance** of policy gradients, replacing them with a deterministic cross‑entropy update that scales with the mis‑ranking probability.  
3. **Implicit normalization** that guarantees stable training when deviating from the reference distribution.  

These insights motivate the broader adoption of DPO for large‑scale language‑model alignment, as well as its extension to other domains where preferences can be collected offline.  The numerical evidence presented in this chapter serves as a concrete benchmark for future research on preference‑driven language‑model learning.
