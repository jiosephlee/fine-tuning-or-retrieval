\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 3: 4. Theoretical Insight: Constrained RL has a Closed‑Form Optimal Policy  

In this chapter we reconstruct from first principles the analytic solution of the KL‑regularized reward‑maximization problem that underlies all iterations of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).  By understanding this optimal policy we can appreciate why DPO can compute gradient updates directly from preference data, without the need for an explicit reinforcement‑learning loop.  The exposition below proceeds in four parts: first the problem statement, then a formal derivation of the **soft‑max policy**, a discussion of the **partition function** that normalises it, and finally a commentary on the practical consequences of having an exact policy expression.

---

## Statement of the KL‑constrained RL problem  

Consider an environment in which a language model implements a conditional probability distribution
\[
  \pi_\theta(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})\,,
\]
over completions \(\mathbf{y}\) given a prompt \(\mathbf{x}\).  A *reward function* \(r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\) assigns a real‑valued score to each completion in the context of the prompt.  The task is to find a policy \(\pi\) that both *maximises expected reward* and *does not deviate far* from a trusted reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (typically the unsupervised or instruction‑tuned model).  This is expressed as the constrained optimisation problem

\[
\begin{aligned}
  \max_{\pi}\;\; & \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim \mathcal{D}}\;
        \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim \pi(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x})}\bigl[\,r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\bigr]  \\
  \text{s.t. } \;\;& 
  \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x}) \;\|\; \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x})\bigr] \;\leq\;\epsilon
  \quad \forall\,\mathbf{x}\,.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.1}
\]

The **KL‑divergence constraint** guarantees that the learned policy does not collapse into a narrow set of high‑reward behaviours and, more importantly for inference efficiency, prevents the model from wandering into regions of the response space where the reward signal is unreliable.

It is convenient to transform (4.1) into an unconstrained problem by adding a penalty term with a Lagrange multiplier \(\beta>0\):

\[
\max_{\pi}\;
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim \mathcal{D}}\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim \pi(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x})}\!
\Bigl[\, r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) - \beta \bigl(
    \log \pi(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x}) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x}) \bigr)
\Bigr].
\tag{4.2}
\]

For any fixed \(\beta\), the solution of (4.2) yields the *optimal policy* for the corresponding KL‑threshold \(\epsilon\).

---

## Derivation of the soft‑max policy form  

The optimisation in (4.2) is a *convex* problem over probability distributions because it is a linear objective in \(\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})\) plus an entropy‑like term that imposes strict convexity.  The Lagrangian for a single prompt \(\mathbf{x}\) is

\[
\mathcal{L}_\mathbf{x}(\pi,\lambda)
=
  \sum_{\mathbf{y}} \pi(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})\,r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
  \;-\;
  \beta \sum_{\mathbf{y}} \pi(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})\,
      \bigl( \log \pi(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x}) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x}) \bigr)
  \;+\;
  \lambda\!\Bigl(\sum_{\mathbf{y}}\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})-1\Bigr),
\]
where \(\lambda\) enforces normalisation.

Differentiating with respect to \(\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})\) and setting the derivative to zero gives

\[
r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) - \beta \bigl( \log \pi(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x}) -
                                 \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x}) + 1 \bigr)
+ \lambda = 0 .
\]

Solving for \(\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})\),

\[
\log \pi(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})
= \frac{1}{\beta} r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) + \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x}) + C,
\]
where \(C = -\,\frac{1}{\beta}(\lambda + \beta)\) is a constant that does **not** depend on \(\mathbf{y}\).  
Exponentiating both sides yields

\[
\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})
= e^{C}\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})\;
  \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\Bigr).
\tag{4.3}
\]

The constant \(e^{C}\) is fixed by the normalisation requirement \(\sum_{\mathbf{y}}\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})=1\).  Thus the closed‑form *soft‑max* policy is

\[
  \boxed{
  \pi_{r}(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})
  =
  \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})\;
        \exp\!\bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\bigr)}
       {Z(\mathbf{x})}
  }
\tag{4.4}
\]

with the *partition function*

\[
\displaystyle
Z(\mathbf{x})
=
  \sum_{\mathbf{y}}\pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{x})\;
           \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\bigr).
\tag{4.5}
\]

Equation (4.4) is the central analytic result that connects the reward function, the reference policy, and the optimal policy that balances exploitation with the KL‑restriction.

---

## Definition of the partition function \(Z(\mathbf{x})\)  

The partition function (4.5) acts as a normalising constant that guarantees \(\pi_{r}\) is a proper probability distribution.  It is analogous to the soft‑max denominator in a standard categorical distribution but with \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) weighting each term.  For a concrete example, suppose the reference policy assigns probabilities to a handful of completions \(\{\mathbf{y}_1,\mathbf{y}_2,\dots\}\).  Then

\[
Z(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k}
    \pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}_k\mid \mathbf{x})\,
    \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_k)\bigr).
\]

Because \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is a *known* quantity (e.g. a pretrained GPT model), all terms in \(Z(\mathbf{x})\) are evaluable.  In principle, computing the exact sum is intractable for language models because the set of all possible completions is infinite.  In practice, DPO sidesteps explicit evaluation of \(Z(\mathbf{x})\) by re‑expressing the objective in terms of *reward differences* (see Section 3.2 of the paper).  This means that while the partition function is conceptually essential for the analytic derivation, it does not need to be calculated during training.

---

## Implications of the analytic form for policy evaluation  

Having an explicit expression for \(\pi_{r}\) notable consequences:

1. **No sampling during training:**  Classical RLHF requires generating trajectories from the current policy, measuring returns, and performing policy gradients.  With (4.4) we can compute gradients directly with respect to \(\pi_{\theta}\) by re‑parameterising the reward.  This eliminates the need for a reinforcement‑learning optimiser (e.g. PPO) and removes the stochasticity associated with sampling.

2. **Closed‑form relation between reward and policy:**  Equation (4.4) shows that any reward function \(r\) gives rise to a unique optimal policy.  DPO exploits this fact by *parameterising the reward in terms of the policy itself* (\(\hat{r}_\theta = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\)).  Consequently, the policy learning objective can be written entirely in terms of policy logits and the preferences; the reward is an implicit by‑product.

3. **Clear conservation of the reference policy:**  Because the reference policy appears multiplicatively inside the numerator, the optimal policy smoothly trades off staying close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (high KL term) versus pushing the probability mass toward high‑reward completions (exponential reward term).  This explains why different values of \(\beta\) produce drastically different behaviours: a small \(\beta\) encourages exploration near the reference policy, while a large \(\beta\) aggressively aligns the policy with high‑reward samples.

4. **Theoretical guarantees:**  The derivation shows that the maximised value is *exact* — no approximation is required.  This underpins the proof that the policy obtained by DPO is *optimal* with respect to the underlying preference model (Bradley‑Terry or Plackett‑Luce), provided the reward is correctly expressed in terms of the policy.

In essence, recognising the soft‑max closed‑form of the constrained RL problem allows DPO to bypass the entire RL loop, leading to a clean, single‑step optimisation that is both mathematically justified and empirically powerful.  The rest of the book will use this analytic insight to explain how DPO transforms human preference data into an implicit reward and why it performs competitively with, or better than, classic PPO‑based reinforcement learning from human feedback.
