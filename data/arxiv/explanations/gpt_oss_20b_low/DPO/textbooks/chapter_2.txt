\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 2: 3. From Preferences to Reward Functions  

In the reinforcement‑learning‑from‑human‑feedback (RLHF) pipeline, the first key ingredient after the supervised‐fine‑tuned (SFT) language model is a **reward model** that can map a context–completion pair \((x,y)\) to a scalar reflecting how desirable the completion is for the human.  This section explains how such a reward model is learned from a collection of human pairwise preferences, how it fits into the larger RL objective, and the practical engineering choices that enable stable training.

## 3.1 Formulating Reward Learning as a Binary Classification  

Human annotators rarely provide numerical scores for each completion; instead, they are asked to compare two completions and indicate which one they prefer.  The data thus consist of triplets \((x,y_{\!w},y_{\!l})\), where \(x\) is the prompt, \(y_{\!w}\) the *won* (preferred) completion, and \(y_{\!l}\) the *losing* one.  The Bradley–Terry (BT) model gives a parametric form for the probability that a human prefers \(y_{\!w}\) over \(y_{\!l}\) given \(x\):

\[
p^*(y_{\!w}\succ y_{\!l}\mid x)=
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_{\!w})\bigr)}
     {\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_{\!w})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_{\!l})\bigr)}.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Here \(r^*(x,y)\) is the *latent* reward function we wish to recover.  Importantly, (3.1) depends only on the **difference** \(r^*(x,y_{\!w})-r^*(x,y_{\!l})\); adding the same constant to all rewards for a given context leaves the preference probabilities unchanged.  This under‑specification is a deliberate feature—it permits many reward functions to explain the same preference data.  

The above probability can be rewritten using the logistic (sigmoid) function \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\):

\[
p^*(y_{\!w}\succ y_{\!l}\mid x)=\sigma\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_{\!w})-r^*(x,y_{\!l})\bigr).
\tag{3.2}
\]

Thus learning the reward function is equivalent to **binary classification**: for each preference, we label the tuple \((c=(x,y_{\!w}),t=(x,y_{\!l}))\) as a training example where the target is 1 (human prefers the first).  The cross‑entropy loss for a parameterised reward model \(r_{\phi}\) is

\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi},\mathcal{D})=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{\!w},y_{\!l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
\!\Bigl[
\log\sigma\!\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_{\!w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{\!l})\bigr)
\Bigr].
\tag{3.3}
\]

Maximising the likelihood (or equivalently minimising (3.3)) yields a reward network that best explains the observed preferences under the BT model.

## 3.2 Reward Head Design (Linear Layer over LM Logits)  

A reward model must consume an input pair \((x,y)\) and produce a scalar.  The most common design in practice is a **linear head** attached to the final hidden state of a pre‑trained transformer.  Concretely:

1. The LM processes the concatenated context–completion sequence and produces token‑level logits \(h_t\) at the final transformer layer.
2. These logits are pooled (e.g. by a learned weighted average, or by taking the last‑token representation).
3. A single linear transformation downsizes the pooled vector to a scalar:
   \[
   r_{\phi}(x,y)= \mathbf{w}^{\top}\,\mathbf{z} + b,
   \]
   where \(\mathbf{z}\) is the pooled LM representation, \(\mathbf{w}\) and \(b\) are the reward head parameters.

The reward head is **initialized from the SFT policy**: we reuse the final LM representation (e.g. the last‑layer hidden vector) as the input to the head, ensuring that the rewards are in the same representational space as the language model’s logits.  This practice helps the optimisation converge quickly and reduces the amount of extra training data needed.

Because the BT model cares only about reward differences, any constant shift applied to all rewards in a given context is irrelevant.  However, for numerical stability during subsequent RL stages, it is common to **normalize** the rewards so that they have zero mean and unit variance across the preference dataset:

\[
\tilde r_{\phi}(x,y) = \frac{r_{\phi}(x,y)-\mu_x}{\sigma_x},
\quad
\mu_x=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_x|}\sum_{(x',y')\in\mathcal{D}_x} r_{\phi}(x',y'),
\quad
\sigma_x=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_x|}\sum_{(x',y')\in\mathcal{D}_x}(r_{\phi}(x',y')-\mu_x)^2}.
\tag{3.4}
\]

Here \(\mathcal{D}_x\) denotes all preference pairs involving context \(x\).  Zero‑mean rewards ensure that the reward model does not favour any context purely because of a shift in its output scale, which would otherwise bias the RL policy toward a subset of prompts.

## 3.3 Normalizing Rewards (Mean Zero per Context)  

Empirically, **mean‑zero normalisation** improves the stability of both the reward model and the subsequent RL phase.  The rationale is twofold:

1. **Anchor for Probabilistic Modeling.**  The RL objective we discuss next contains a KL term that penalises deviations from a reference policy.  The scale of the reward directly translates into the magnitude of the policy update.  If different contexts produce rewards with wildly different magnitudes, the gradient direction can become dominated by a few high‑variance contexts, leading to unstable updates.
   
2. **Policy Regularisation.**  During RL, the reward is subtracted by a baseline (often the reward of the reference policy).  If the baseline is roughly centred at zero, the optimisation does not inadvertently drift the policy in a systematic direction.  Normalising per‑context rewards keeps the baseline distribution tight and prevents the policy from over‑optimising for a small set of high‑reward contexts.

In practice, the normalisation statistics \((\mu_x,\sigma_x)\) are computed online: after each epoch of reward‑model training the reporter averages across all pairs seen that include a given context.  As the reward model improves, these statistics are updated, ensuring that the normalisation follows the slowly evolving reward scale.

## 3.4 The KL‑Regularised RL Objective  

Once a reward model \(r_{\phi}\) is in hand, the language model policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) is fine‑tuned to maximise the **expected reward** while remaining close to a *reference* policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (usually the original SFT model).  The KL‑regularised objective is

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\Bigl[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr]
- \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)
\Bigr].
\tag{3.5}
\]

The first term rewards the policy for generating high‑reward completions; the second term penalises divergence from the reference policy, with \(\beta>0\) controlling how conservatively the policy should deviate.  A large \(\beta\) forces the policy to stay near the baseline, limiting the risk of *mode collapse* or unintended behaviour, while a small \(\beta\) allows the policy to explore richer responses.

The KL term is typically instantiated at the token level and summed over the generation:

\[
\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{:t})\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x_{:t})\bigr),
\tag{3.6}
\]
where \(x_{:t}\) denotes the partial sequence up to token \(t\).  This token‑wise decomposition facilitates both efficient gradient computation and the use of the **proximal policy optimisation (PPO)** algorithm to approximate the expectation over \(\pi_{\theta}\).

Although (3.5) cannot be differentiated directly because the expectation over \(\pi_{\theta}\) involves sampling from the discrete language model, the standard practice is to express the reward in *scaled‑log‑probability* form:

\[
r(x, y) \;\;=\;\; r_{\phi}(x, y) \;-\; \beta\!\bigl[\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)-\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr]
\tag{3.7}
\]

and then plug this reward into a policy gradient algorithm such as REINFORCE or PPO.  This manipulation is a consequence of the *control‑as‑inference* view: the optimal policy for a given reward is obtained by exponentiating the reward and normalising, exactly as in Eq. (3.5).

## 3.5 Typical Policy Parameterisation and Initialization  

The *policy* \(\pi_{\theta}\) is simply the language model itself.  Usually the policy is an **autoregressive transformer** that outputs a token‑wise probability distribution:
\[
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x_{:t-1}),
\tag{3.8}
\]
where \(\theta\) denotes all LM weights.  Because the reward optimisation occurs after an initial SFT stage, the policy is **initialised** to the SFT weights \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\).  This warm start has multiple benefits:

1. **Faster Convergence.**  The SFT policy already captures general language structure and the distribution of valid completions, so the RL updates only need to nudge the probabilities toward higher reward, not learn syntax from scratch.
2. **Stable Reward Normalisation.**  The reference policy is used in the KL penalty; if both \(\pi_{\theta}\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) start from the same parameters, early updates result in small KL values, preventing the RL loss from exploding.
3. **Controlled Exploration.**  The KL term is easier to satisfy initially because \(\pi_{\theta}\) is already close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  As training progresses, the KL penalty forces the policy to move gradually, mitigating catastrophic forgetting of the base LM behaviour.

During RL fine‑tuning, the policy parameters are updated by an **actor‑critic** algorithm (commonly PPO).  The algorithm unfolds as follows:

- **Collect trajectories** from \(\pi_{\theta}\) on a subset of prompts sampled from \(\mathcal{D}\).
- **Compute the reward** for each trajectory using (3.7).
- **Update the critic** (value head) to fit the discounted return targets.
- **Update the actor** \(\pi_{\theta}\) by maximizing a clipped surrogate objective that incorporates the KL penalty.

Because the reward depends on the current policy (via the log‑ratio term in (3.7)), each optimisation step implicitly balances exploitation (follow the reward) and conservation (stay close to the base policy).  The final policy \(\pi_{\theta}^{*}\) is the one that satisfies the trade‑off defined in (3.5).

---

In summary, the reward‑learning stage in RLHF converts raw human pairwise preferences into a neural reward function via a simple binary cross‑entropy objective.  The reward head is a lightweight linear layer attached to the final hidden state of the transformer, with per‑context normalisation to guarantee zero‑mean outputs.  This reward model feeds into a KL‑regularised RL objective that fine‑tunes the language‑model policy, carefully balancing reward maximisation against divergence from a trusted reference policy.  The resulting pipeline is robust, but its reliance on costly policy sampling and delicate hyperparameter tuning motivates the exploration of alternative, RL‑free optimisation methods—precisely the motivation for Direct Preference Optimization introduced in the following chapter.
