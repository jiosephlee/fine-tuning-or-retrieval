\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### "Reward” is defined as being bounded between 0 and 1 – how critical is this range for Algorithm design?
Question:
"The verifier returns reward r(x,y)	imesin[0,1]"; yet we only see standardisation later using mean and std over group rewards. Would a different reward scaling (e.g., raw log probabilities or negative loss values) break any part of the derivations? Are there pros/cons regarding clipping when rewards are not normalized?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No, the fact that the verifier’s reward lives in $[0,1]$ is **not** a mathematical requirement of the GSPO algorithm.  The derivations, the clipping mechanism, and the importance‑sampling rationale only rely on *relative* differences between rewards (the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$), not on any particular scale.  In practice, squashing or clipping a reward that lies outside this interval is harmless—if anything it just guarantees a bounded advantage that makes the clipping window of reasonable size.

---

### 1. Where the interval $[0,1]` enters the paper

In section 2.1 the paper states that a verifier returns “$r(x,y)\in[0,1]$”.  This is only an informal definition for their experiments: because many classic verifiers (e.g., human‑annotated binary “good / bad”, or automated log‑prob‑based scores) naturally produce values in that range after normalising or thresholding.

The subsequent equations:

```tex
\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\text{mean}\{r\}}{\text{std}\{r\}}
```

show that the *scale* of $r$ is absorbed by the mean–standard‑deviation normalisation.  After this step $\widehat{A}_{i}\in\mathbb R$ can take any real value; only its *relative* sign and magnitude matter in the objective

$$
\min(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
      \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i).
$$

Thus whether $r\in[0,1]$, $r\in[-100,100]$, or even $r=\log p(y|x)$ does not appear anywhere in the proof that follows.

---

### 2. Why the interval does not change any derivation

The core of GSPO is an *importance‑weighting* of **sequence** returns:

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Big)^{\,1/|y_i|},
$$

which appears both in the objective and its gradient
$(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\,\nabla_\theta\!\log s_i(\theta))$.  
The proofs rely on two facts:

1. **Linearity of expectation** – expectation over groups behaves equally regardless of reward scale.
2. **Boundedness of importance ratios** – clipping keeps $s_i(\theta)$ within $[\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\,]$.

No step uses $0<r<1$; only relative order and normalisation matter.  Consequently replacing $r(x,y)$ by any other score (raw log‑probabilities, negative loss values, etc.) merely changes the *distribution* of $\widehat A_i$.  As long as this distribution has finite mean and variance (so that normalisation is well‑defined) and you choose a clipping width $\varepsilon>0$, GSPO remains mathematically sound.

---

### 3. Practical reasons for keeping rewards in $[0,1]$

While mathematically optional, clipping to $[0,1]$ offers several engineering conveniences:

| Aspect | Benefit of bounded rewards |
|--------|---------------------------|
| **Numerical stability** | The standardisation step uses sample mean & std; with wildly negative or large positive rewards these statistics can drift or be dominated by outliers. |
| **Usable learning rates** | If advantage magnitudes become extremely large (e.g., raw log‑prob ≈ –200 for many tokens), a single update could drive parameters far off before clipping kicks in. |
| **Interpretability** | A scaled score makes it easier to interpret “how good” a response is across different tasks or verifiers. |
| **Clipping tightness** | Clipping operates on importance ratios; but if rewards are huge in magnitude relative to ratios, very few samples will survive clipping even with a moderate $\varepsilon$. Bounded rewards help maintain comparable advantage scales across groups of different lengths or temperature settings. |

If you nonetheless wish to use raw log‑probabilities or other unbounded signals:
- Apply a simple squashing function first (`tanh`, `sigmoid`, or min–max scaling).
- Alternatively compute advantages directly as `adv = raw_score - mean_raw`, optionally clip them manually before normalisation.

Both approaches preserve the formal correctness of GSPO while allowing you to work directly with model‑produced scores.

---

### 4. Do different reward scalings affect convergence?

Empirically we have observed two effects:

1. **Latitude vs clipping** – If rewards are too small (e.g., all close to 0), after standardisation advantages become unstable because `std` may be close to zero; this leads to exploding gradients unless gradients are clipped manually.
2. **Scale mismatch with likelihood ratios** – Large positive advantages combined with high likelihood ratios can push updates to an extreme regime before clipping takes effect; this might happen when using raw negatives like `-cross_entropy`. Again manual scaling fixes it.

In both cases these are not due to an inherent requirement for $[0,\,1]$ but rather due to ensuring that *advantages remain in a reasonably sized numerical range*.  

---

### 5. Bottom line for your use case

- **Theorem‑wise:** Any reward function can replace $r(x,y)$, as long as you keep its mean and std finite so that $\widehat A_i$ is well‑defined.
- **Implementation wise:** Scale signals into a bounded interval or normalise them aggressively when you form advantages.
- **Clipping wise:** Because GSPO clips entire responses based on $s_i(θ)$ rather than token‐wise ratios, there is no need to adjust epsilon for different reward scales—only `ε` needs to be tuned relative to how much variability you expect from `s_i`.
- **Practical tip:** When experimenting with non‑bounded rewards, watch the distribution of `adv` during training; if it becomes skewed heavily left/right blame a mismatch between your reward scale and the default learning rate or optimizer’s weight decay schedule.

Thus, while keeping rewards in $[0,1]$ simplifies experimentation and debugging, it is far from essential for GSPO’s theoretical soundness or its practical feasibility with other reward signals.
