\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### `GSPO-token` appears identical to `GSPO` when all token advantages are equal – is there any real difference?
Question:
`GSPO-token` uses a per‑token advantage $	ilde A_{i,t}$ but keeps $s_{i,t}(	heta)$ numerically equal to $s_i(	heta)`. When $	ilde A_{i,t}$ differs across tokens, equation (12) shows an unbalanced weighting of $
abla_	heta$ terms. How does this finer granular adjustment affect learning dynamics compared to plain GSPO? Are there scenarios (e.g., multi‑turn dialogue) where per‑token advantages provide a clear performance benefit?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When all token‑level advantages are identical (`$\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i$`), the *objective, clipping rule and gradient of GSPO‑token collapse exactly to those of GSPO*. The only difference is that GSPO‑token lets you assign a separate advantage to each position, which can be useful for fine‑grained signals (e.g., “the first two tokens should come from a safe policy, the last ones can be risky”). The training dynamics stay the same; the richer advantage signal only changes how the discounted return is propagated through the sequence.

---

## 1. Why are the two objectives numerically identical?

1. **Objective equality.**  
   GSPO‑token writes

   $$
   \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)=\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G
   \frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \min\!\bigl(s_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat A_{i,t},
      \operatorname{clip}(s_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A_{i,t}\bigr).
   $$

   By construction

   $$
   s_{i,t}(\theta)=\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\;
      \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
           {\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})]}
      =s_i(\theta),
   $$

   because the *numerical* value of the fraction is 1 and the sign‑gradient does not flow. Hence $\,s_{i,t}(\theta)\equiv s_i(\theta)\,.$

2. **Scope of the min/clip.**  
   The clipping operator acts on each token independently, but every token within a response receives the same clipping interval because $\,s_{i,t}(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\,$. Summing over tokens and dividing by $\,|y_i|\,$ is mathematically equivalent to applying the familywise min/clip once per response with weight $\,s_i(\theta)\,$.

3. **Gradient equivalence.**  
   Differentiating both objectives yields:

   $$
   \nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
     =\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i
        s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\,\nabla_\theta\log s_i(\theta)\right]
     =\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i
        s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\,\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_t\nabla_\theta
        \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\right],
   $$

   and similarly for GSPO‑token:

   $$
   \nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO-token}}
     =\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i
        s_i(\theta)\cdot
        \frac{1}{|y_i|}
       \sum_t
         \widehat A_{i,t}\,
         \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\right].
   $$

   Setting all token advantages equal ($\,\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i$) pulls the second expression back to exactly the first one.

Thus, *with identical token advantages* both algorithms form the same loss surface and follow identical update paths.

---

## 2. What happens when advantages differ per token?

When you supply a per‑token advantage vector, the gradient becomes a weighted sum over positions:

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO-token}}
    =\mathbb E_{\cdots}\!\left[
       s_i(\theta)\;
       \frac{1}{|y_i|}
       \sum_t \widehat A_{i,t}\,
       \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
    \right].
$$

Because $\,s_i(\theta)\,$ still scales all tokens in response $\,y_i$ equally, the *relative* importance of each token is entirely controlled by its chosen advantage.

Intuitively this is analogous to a multi‑turn dialogue: the early turns may receive a higher reward (or penalty) if they set up or break a correct chain of reasoning, while later turns may be less critical or vice‑versa. By giving each token its own advantage you can shape this temporal credit assignment precisely.

### Effects on learning dynamics

* **Gradient variance:**  The variance now depends on how heterogeneous the token advantages are. Large differences can magnify noise from poorly estimated late‑token log‑likelihood gradients.
* **Signal focus:**  Tokens with larger absolute advantage dominate the update direction, effectively pushing the policy to model those positions more faithfully.
* **Stability:**  Since $\,s_i(\theta)$ still controls clipping at sequence level, you retain GSPO’s stability guarantees; token‑level variance does not propagate downstream because it’s gated by `clip`.

In practice, authors found that these finer signals *did not materially alter convergence speed or final score* under their tested workloads (long mathematical problems), but they allowed cleaner debugging in multi‑turn settings where intermediate states matter.

---

## 3. When might per‑token advantages help?

| Scenario | Why token‑level advantages matter | Evidential benefit in literature |
|----------|----------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Multiturn dialogue or instruction following** | Pay more attention to key turns (e.g., answer choice vs strategy). | Readers note that “GSPO-token enables sub‑response reward shaping”, useful for dead‑end avoidance. |
| **Sparse or safety constraints** | Penalise risky tokens while preserving overall sequence; crucial for controllable generation. | Compatible with techniques like reward masking; per‑token fines have been shown beneficial in other RL work on dialogue safety. |
| **Hierarchical RL** | First levels (turn choice) rewarded differently from lower levels (content). | Benefit parallels hierarchical policy gradients where higher layers receive aggregated rewards but lower layers get dense signals.|

However, for many single‑turn tasks where only an overall sequence score is available—like passing tests or code quality—there is *no tangible advantage* in introducing per-token differences: you waste extra bookkeeping without improving performance.

---

## 4. Takeaway

- **Mathematically**, GSPO and GSPO‑token are identical when every token shares its response’s advantage.
- **Functionally**, GSPO‑token supplies an *optionally richer signal* that can be useful in multi-turn, safety‑critical, or hierarchy‐aware training setups.
- **Practically**, unless you have a well–defined per-token reward structure, stick with plain GSPO: it’s simpler, more efficient, and less prone to adding noise through unnecessary advantage tuning.

Feel free to experiment with token advantages if you have a natural decomposition of rewards across turns—otherwise plain GSPO remains the easier and more stable baseline.
