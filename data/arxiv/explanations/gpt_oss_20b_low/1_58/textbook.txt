# 1. Introduction to 1‑bit Large Language Models

Large language models (LLMs) have quickly become the cornerstone of modern natural‑language processing, powering everything from conversational agents to content‑creation tools. However, as the scale of these models increases—from hundreds of millions to hundreds of billions of parameters—their computational, memory, and energy footprints grow proportionally. Even with state‑of‑the‑art hardware and optimized software stacks, deploying a full‑precision (FP16 or BF16) LLM on a typical GPU or edge device can still require several gigabytes of memory and consume orders of magnitude more power than a re‑engineered, low‑precision alternative.

The drive toward extreme quantization—compressing each weight to a handful of bits—is therefore attractive on multiple fronts. Lower precision cuts down model size, shrinks the bandwidth needed to stream weights from off‑chip memory, and can drastically reduce the arithmetic workload if the underlying computation shifts from floating‑point multiplies to simpler integer operations. In theory, a properly engineered 1‑bit LLM should match or even surpass a full‑precision counterpart while being markedly cheaper to run.

The paper *“The Era of 1‑bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits”* presents such an engineering breakthrough: **BitNet b1.58**. In this chapter we trace the lineage that led to this model, compare it against older quantization strategies, and describe how BitNet b1.58 pivots from traditional post‑training quantization to an architectural redesign that leverages ternary weights for maximal efficiency.

---

## Historical Evolution of LLM Computational Cost

Early generations of transformer‑based language models such as GPT‑2 or BERT were trained and served exclusively in floating‐point precision (FP32), largely because hardware libraries at the time were optimized for that format. As model sizes grew—GPT‑3 introduced 175 B parameters—researchers quickly realized that the compute budget was stretching beyond what could be managed safely in a single data center.

Around 2020 a confluence of pressures emerged:

1. **Cost**: Training and inference clusters using high‐performance GPUs drive up electricity bills and hardware depreciation.
2. **Latency**: Real‐time applications (e.g., serverless chatbots) demand sub‐100 ms inference times.
3. **Deployment Constraints**: Even cloud providers with powerful GPUs balk at hosting naive 16‑bit LLMs due to memory saturation.

These challenges spurred an explosion of work in model compression and efficiency, ranging from sparsification techniques, parameter sharing across layers, to various forms of quantization. The computational cost became a central metric alongside perplexity or downstream accuracy.

---

## Early Work in Post–Training Quantization

Post–training quantization (PTQ) refers to the practice of compressing a fully trained high‑precision model without any further weight updates. PTQ is attractive because it requires no expensive re-training pipeline—only a few tens or thousands of calibration samples are needed to establish scaling factors.

Prominent PTQ methods include:
- **SmoothQuant** [Ref] which introduces per‐layer scaling tricks for better Slimmable-Quantization results.
- **GPTQ** [Ref], which performs per-column top‑k rounding guided by second‐moment statistics.
- **SQuant** variants that consider zero-point shifts for activation quantization.
- **QuIP** [Ref], which adapts both weights and activations online during inference.

These approaches have demonstrated that 4‑bit precision can sometimes approximate FP16 performance when combined with careful calibration and regularisation tricks. Nonetheless PTQ has inherent limitations:

- **Fixed Quantization Granularity**: Post training rounds correct errors only at fine granularity after the fact; large errors can still accumulate despite scaling.
- **Bias Introduction**: As weights are clamped or rounded arbitrarily post hoc, fine-grained dynamics are lost.
- **Incompatibility With Nonlinear Operations**: Traditional transformers rely heavily on normalized attention maps and feed-forward networks that are sensitive to bit-widths below 8 bits.

As hardware support for integer arithmetic grows—particularly for packed bit operations—the Stage was set for going beyond PTQ.

---

## Limitations of 4-bit and 8-bit Models

While the community achieved noteworthy success pushing LLMs toward lower precision using PTQ, several roadblocks remained visible in practice:

| Precision | Memory Footprint | Dynamic Range | Arithmetic Overhead | Typical Performance |
|-----------|------------------|---------------|--------------------|---------------------|
| FP16      | Full          | Unbounded   | Multiply + Add     | Baseline (≈100 %)   |
| BF16      | Full          | Reduced    | Multiply + Add     | Base (≈95–97 %)     |
| 8-bit Int | ~⅛ RAM        | Narrow       | I/O + Multiply   | Slight drop         |
| 4-bit Int | ~¼ RAM        | Very narrow | I/O + Multiply   | Nontrivial drop     |

In particular:

- **Dynamic Range Collapse**: Packing many tokens into fewer bits reduces numeric resolution dramatically; even small variations can lead to catastrophic loss in softmax distributions inside self-attention heads.
- **Calibration Overhead**: Achieving accurate per-tensor scaling necessitates large calibration sets; otherwise end-task accuracy degrades significantly.
- **Mixed Complexity**: Many optimiser kernels still must perform dummy float multiplications due to library constraints or Quality-of-Service guarantees.

Consequently, simply cutting the bit-width below 4 did not scale in real deployments; most production systems stayed somewhere between BF16/FP16 and 8-bit PTQs at best.

---

## Quantization Versus Architectural Redesign

Two complementary approaches appeared as ruminations over how best to tackle low-bit representation:

1. **Pure Quantization** (PTQ / Quantization Aware Training): The transformer architecture remains unchanged; only weights and activations undergo rounding after being trained in full precision or simulated during training via fake quantisation layers.
2. **Architectural Redesign**: Replace fundamental building blocks (e.g., `nn.Linear`) with integer-aware analogues (e.g., `BitLinear`). The network topology is altered so that multiplication by an integer weight becomes a simple sign flip or zero-expansion operation that can be implemented by add-and-shift patterns rather than costly multipliers.

BitNet selected the latter path underpinned by two insights:

- **Squarely Integer Ops by Design**: By enforcing ternary weights (\{-1,0,+1\}), each matrix multiplication boils down purely to signed additions and subtractions—no multiplications whatsoever—and each element's contribution can be gated out directly (weight = 0).
- **Feature Filtering via Zero Weight**: The inclusion of zero among allowed values grants an explicit sparsity mechanism that conventional post–training compressions cannot claim—not only does it save memory but it also permits dropout-like gating within computation flow natively during training.

This shift essentially rewrites what *feasible* question means for transformer computation on future low-power accelerators.

---

## Defining the “1‑bit era”

With architecture like BitNet demonstrating superior trade-off curves between perplexity / downstream accuracy versus energy/latency/memory costs, researchers coalesced around what they colloquially term the "**1-bit era**." In contrast to aggressive quantisation regimes that retain substantial RF control flow overhead on GPUs, BitNet’s 1 bit paradigm is oriented toward specialized hardware where addition units dominate compute budgets:

- **Power Efficiency Dictated by Sampling Cosine Function**: Multiplication’s power dissipation scales quadratically with operand width; converting all matmul ops into additions yields energy reductions scalable across all future process nodes.
- **Simplified Memory Hierarchy Communication**: Less data movement per token permits higher effective bandwidth on devices limited by off-chip memory traffic.
- **Long Sequence Support Comes Naturally**: Each token now needs only base-of-two storage—allowing straightforward doubling or halving token span without changing arithmetic paths—a key requirement for interactive AI systems dealing with pages-long contexts on mobile CPUs.

Thus “the era” refers not only to software but also signalling a waveform shift where new chip families may design specialized accelerators around this paradigm rather than readjusting existing GPU pipelines.

# 2. Background: Transformers, LLaMA and Quantization Primer

Large language models (LLMs) have grown to involve billions or even trillions of parameters, yet the underlying computation graph is still built upon a small set of well‑studied primitives: multi‑head self‑attention and feed‑forward trans­formations placed inside residual blocks. One influential instantiation of these primitives is the LLaMA family of models, which has become the de‑facto backbone for many open‑source LLMs. Concurrently, the energy and memory demands of full‑precision floating‑point inference have motivated systematic research into quantization—the act of compressing the numerical precision of model tensors without sacrificing too much predictive acuity. This chapter dissects each of these strands: we review the Transformer architecture with an emphasis on its residual structure and attention mechanism; we survey the particular design choices that define LLaMA, such as bias removal, RMS normalization and SwiGLU gating; and we lay out a concise primer on quantization theory and practice, describing both uniform and non‑uniform schemes as well as the most popular post‑training approaches (SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ). The aim is to provide a solid foundation upon which later chapters may delve into 1‑bit LLM variants.

## Residual Networks and Self‑Attention

The Transformer was introduced to replace recurrent architectures in sequence modelling by using attention mechanisms that allow each token to interact directly with all others in a single layer. A typical Transformer block consists of two sub‑modules:

1. **Multi‑Head Self‑Attention (MHSA)**  
   Each token \(x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is mapped to three representations—query \(Q_t\), key \(K_t\), and value \(V_t\)—by applying learned linear projections \(W_Q, W_K, W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\). The attention weight between tokens \(t\) and \(s\) is computed as
   \[
   \alpha_{t,s} = \frac{\exp(Q_t^\top K_s / \sqrt{d_\text{head}})}{\sum_{u}\exp(Q_t^\top K_u / \sqrt{d_\text{head}})}.
   \]
   The output vector for token \(t\) is then
   \[
   h_t = \sum_s \alpha_{t,s} V_s,
   \]
   which captures contextualized relationships across the entire sequence.

2. **Feed‑Forward (FFN) Subnetwork**  
   The transformed token enters a typically two‑layer MLP:
   \[
   h_t' = \sigma(W_2\,\rho(W_1\,h_t + b_1) + b_2),
   \]
   where \(W_1, W_2\) are weight matrices, \(b_1, b_2\) biases, and \(\sigma,\rho\) are activation functions such as ReLU or GLU.

Both submodules are wrapped inside *residual connections*:
\[
x_{t}^{(\ell+1)} = x_{t}^{(\ell)} + h_t',
\]
where superscript \(\ell\) denotes layer index. Residual links mitigate vanishing gradients by providing direct paths for gradient flow to lower layers. In practice, these connections are often followed by normalization layers (LayerNorm or RMSNorm), further stabilising training dynamics.

In the original Transformer paper, LayerNorm was applied *after* each submodule; however subsequent variants—including those that preface attention with normalization—have been introduced to improve convergence speed. Regardless of configuration, the core idea remains: stack several residual blocks of MHSA plus MLPs to build deep sequence models.

## LLaMA Architecture Choices

The LLaMA family bridges this generic Transformer blueprint with specific engineering decisions that reduce memory usage while retaining expressivity. Table 1 summarises the salient components that define a canonical LLaMA staff model.

| Component | Typical Parameter | Effect |
|-----------|-------------------|--------|
| **Bias Terms** | Removed from weight matrices | Reduces parameters; empirical evidence shows minimal performance loss |
| **Normalization** | RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm | RMSNorm normalises only through root‐mean‐square; faster backpropagation & fewer memory stalls |
| **Gate Activation** | SwiGLU (Swish + Gated Linear Unit) | Provides higher capacity than ReLU while remaining lightweight |
| **Embeddings** | Token & positional embeddings stored in FP16 | Keeps embedding matrix energy low; serves as backbone for decoding |
| **Rotary Embedding** | Implemented via Q-Key rotation per head | Enables position awareness without extra parameters |

The removal of biases partly owes to work demonstrating that weight matrices can absorb constant shifts through subsequent layers or embedding adjustments. RMSNorm omits centering operations entirely; it computes
\[
x_{\text{norm}} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[x^2] + \epsilon}},
\]
achieving similar stabilisation while reducing computational overhead.

SwiGLU expands upon standard GLU by replacing its linear gating with a swish activation:
\[
\sigma_{\text{swish}}(z)=z\,\mathrm{sigmoid}(z),
\]
thereby letting the gate learn smoother nonlinearity patterns while keeping parameter count low.

Rotary positional embeddings encode relative positions by rotating query and key vectors in complex plane:
\[
Q_{\text{rot}} = Q\,R,\quad K_{\text{rot}} = K\,R,
\]
where \(R\) is a block‑diagonal rotation matrix derived from sinusoidal frequencies. This facilitates extrapolation to longer sequences without additional learnable position tokens.

Collectively these design choices make LLaMA computationally efficient—in terms of FLOPs and memory consumption—without sacrificing language modelling performance compared to larger baselines such as GPT-3 or GPT-4.

## Quantization Basics

Quantization is formally defined as mapping a high‑precision tensor \(X = [x_i]_{i=1}^N\subseteq \mathbb R^N\) onto a discrete set
\[
X_q = [q_i]_{i=1}^N,\quad q_i \in S,
\]
where \(S=\{-Q_Q+1,\dots,Q_Q-1,Q_Q\}\subseteq \mathbb Z\). The integer scale factor correlates each quantized value back to real domain:
\(x_i' = q_i s_x + z_x.\)

### Uniform vs Non‑Uniform

*Uniform* quantizers use fixed step sizes: 
\(s_x = (\max(X)-\min(X)) / (2^{b}-1)\),
with \(b=\log_2(|S|)\). They are straightforward to implement but can poorly represent activation distributions exhibiting heavy tails or bimodal shapes.

*Non‑uniform* quanti­sation adapts bin widths via clustering methods (e.g., k‑means), non-linear transforms (logarithms), or learned scaling factors per channel/weight group. This approach can approximate arbitrary distributions better but at increased implementation cost.

### Symmetric vs Asymmetric Schemes

In *symmetric* quantization (\(z_x=0\)), negative values map to negative integers symmetrically around zero; this aligns well with weight tensors that are centred around zero due to weight decay regularisers. Symmetry simplifies arithmetic because zero remains exactly zero after quantisation.

In *asymmetric* schemes (\(z_x≠0\)), activations may have non-zero mean; mapping them directly would shift numeric ranges unnecessarily. Asymmetric quantisation introduces an offset term handling this shift but complicates hardware multiplication because two offsets must be considered at runtime.

Most modern text generation inference engines employ symmetric uniform quantisation for weights (int8 or int4), while using asymmetric schemes for activations only when required to capture nuanced dynamic ranges without clipping saturation early.

## Common Post‑Training Quantization Methods

Post‐training quantisation (PTQ) refers to techniques that compress pretrained full‑precision weights/activations without retraining from scratch. Three leading PTQ algorithms dominate research discussions today: SmoothQuant, GPTQ, and AWQ. Each addresses specific challenges—bias mismatch during fine-tuning, per-channel scaling precision, or efficient per-head calibration—which together reduce accuracy loss in practical deployments.

### SmoothQuant

SmoothQuant introduces an intermediate “soft” scaling operation before applying quantisation:
Given a weight matrix \(W^{(l)}_k_j(t)\), it first normalises via channel means:
\(W^{(l)}_{k_j,\text{norm}} = W^{(l)}_{k_j}/m_k,\)
where \(m_k=\frac{1}{n}\sum_j |W^{(l)}_{k_j}|.\)
The normalised weights are then clipped and rounded within controlled ranges:
\(W'^{(l)}=ClippedRound(W_{\text{norm}}, Q_b).\)
During inference a per-colour “cancellation” factor rescues dynamic range lost during rounding. SmoothQuant traditionally operates in 4‐bit domains but can be extended downwards; its main advantage lies in effective bias expansion leading to end-to-end improvements when transferring pre-trained models into lower bit precision.

### GPTQ (Gaussian Posterior Fine-Tuning Quantisation)

GPTQ views weights as random variables following Gaussian posteriors informed by gradient updates during training. It computes optimal per-channel scaling factors analytically:
Letting
\(a_k=\mathbb E[W^{(l)}_{k}] , b_k=\operatorname{var}(W^{(l)}_{k}) , c_k=\operatorname{cov}(W^{(l)}_{k}, Y)\)
(where Y denotes activations), GPTQ selects scale factors
\(s_k= f(a_k,b_k,c_k)\)
by solving a minimum-squared error objective across all channels simultaneously.
The resulting integer weights preserve covariance relationships between layers more faithfully than naive symmetric PTQ.
GPTQ’s key novelty lies in capturing higher order statistics beyond means—essential for plateauing performance on very large language models where weight magnitudes vary wildly across layers.

### AWQ (Adaptive Weight Quantisation)

AWQ proposes an iterative strategy where each layer’s stupentical lambda distribution is adapted last-to-first (“online” quantisation). For every channel k it solves
minimize \(||Y X - Y R s||_2^2,\)
subject to discrete constraints on R∈ℤ^n,
over an auxiliary residual vector r that collects previously layered errors.
This yields near‐optimal scales with minimal error propagation across subsequent layers.
It uses simple polynomial approximation instead of solving complex convex program at every step thus remaining computationally cheap for very large models (<10 min PTQ).

---

Across these methods practitioners commonly choose:

- **SmoothQuant** when high-intensity biases dominate model performance.
- **GPTQ** if low bias but high variance weights are prevalent—as often seen in larger backbone models.
- **AWQ** when fast PTQ turnaround is critical—as it scales efficiently beyond 7B parameter threshold.

All three variants share key shared traits: they precompute canonical integer shifts once per layer; they honor shape partitioning so that GPUs can batch integer GEMM kernels efficiently; they preserve *activations* in INT8 unless explicitly cast further down their pipelines (`8-bit`, `FP16`).  

Understanding how each histogram aligns with particular architectural contexts provides intuition for why 0–±1 weights can be used without harming perplexity: when activations vary slowly but over many tokens—typical for autoregressive LLM inference—the row-level scaling informed by $\gamma$ (median absolute value) captures exact magnitude information necessary for the ternary weight mapping ($-1$, $0$, $+1$). 

Hence this background material equips readers not only with knowledge about how standard Transformers locate their power and bottlenecks but also explains the sophisticated toolsets already present in the community – necessary prerequisites before venturing into true 1-bit model designs such as BitNet b¹·⁵⁸.

# 3. Designing BitNet b1.58 Architecture

Large‑scale language models have long relied on the Transformer backbone, where each self‑attention or feed‑forward head is normally implemented with dense linear layers and floating‑point parameters. BitNet b1.58 abandons the conventional 32‑bit storage and computation in favour of a ternary weight set \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) while preserving an 8‑bit integer representation of activations. This chapter dissects how the original BitNet design is morphed into the new 1.58‑bit variant, explaining every architectural choice and its practical impact.

---

## Replacing nn.Linear with BitLinear

At the core of any transformer lies a collection of linear projections: query, key, value matrices for attention, and the two intermediate projections in the feed‑forward network (FFN). In vanilla PyTorch these are expressed as `nn.Linear`, which is a wrapper around an 16‑bit or 32‑bit weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) and an optional bias vector \(b \in \mathbb{R}^{m}\). The forward operation reduces to a matrix multiplication followed by addition of the bias.

BitNet redefines this building block through a custom operator called `BitLinear`. Its essential responsibilities are:

1. **Ternary Weight Storage**  
   Every element of \(W\) is forced into \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) by a quantization function that first rescales the matrix by its average absolute value  

   \[
   \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, +1\right),
   \quad
   \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|.
   \]

   The invariant `RoundClip` functions as a clipped rounding operator; it drives values to the nearest integer among … and truncates extreme outliers.

2. **Elimination of Multiplication**  
   Since every weight is either –1, 0 or +1, the dot product of an input feature vector \(x\) and the weight column reduces to

   \[
   y = W^{\top}x = \sum_{k} w_k x_k,
   \quad w_k \in \{-1,0,+1\}.
   \]

   Thus the inner product becomes a sequence of signed integer adder operations; multiplication units are not required in hardware, dramatically reducing power consumption.

3. **Preservation of Additive Scaling**  
   Although `BitLinear` removes multiplication inside the core tensor operation, it still supports scaling by its bit‑32 or bit‑16 proxy during training. A learnable scaling per neuron's output can be introduced via *weight normalisation* or *batch scaling*, which is effectively a separate multiplier applied after addition—an inexpensive operation compared to floating‑point multiplications at scale.

In practice all occurrences of `nn.Linear` inside both attention heads and FFN layers were replaced wholesale with `BitLinear`. The forward pass thus becomes only integer addition plus tiny scaling factors that can be fused directly into integer accumulators or handled with one multiply per neuron in very low precision hardware—a perfect match for custom accelerators.

---

## Zeroing Biases – Impact on Expressivity

A bias vector in standard transformer layers enables every neuron to shift its activation independently before applying non‑linearity. Removing biases might sound counterintuitive: it appears to reduce model capacity.

However, several observations justify their elimination in BitNet b1.58:

- **Parameter Count Reduction**  
  Each layer’s bias consumes one float per output dimension—already an insignificant fraction (~0.01 %) compared with millions of weights when scaling to billions of parameters—but in integer form this still consumes memory and adds transmission cost between on‑chip rings or SRAM banks.

- **Implicit Feature Filtering via Zero Weight**  
  Since one weight value is exactly zero, neurons can be selectively deactivated simply by assigning zero to relevant connections during quantisation. This gives a richer *feature selection* capability than a mere scalar bias might provide: if all incoming edges are set to zero for an input token, that neuron will output zero regardless of activation state.

- **Normalization Layers Compensate**  
  With RMSNorm replacing LayerNorm—an explicitly scale‑free normalisation—the network relies less on learned offsets to align distributions across layers. RMSNorm normalises by root mean square magnitude without adding learnable offsets, making bias terms redundant.

Empirical studies from Table 2 in the paper show that removing biases does not degrade perplexity or zero‑shot task scores; on the contrary, it eases regularisation and leads to better generalisation on longer sequences where additive noise might accumulate across layers.

---

## Retention of RMSNorm and SwiGLU Gates

Despite replacing linear modules with their ternary counterparts, BitNet b1.58 preserves two pivotal components from contemporary open source LLMs:

### RMSNorm
The original LLaMA employed RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm because RMSSN avoids computing per‐channel means—a costlier operation for int8 matrices—and provides stability when operating close to machine precision limits. Its formulation for an input vector \(x\) is:

\[
\operatorname{RMSNorm}(x) = 
\frac{x}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d} x_i^2}}
\times g,
\]

where \(g\) is a learnable scaling vector shared across all neurons within a layer but kept at FP32 precision during inference for numerical stability (the paper does not clarify if \(g\) remains full‐precision; it can be quantised similarly if resources allow). Because no subtraction from mean occurs, the variance estimation remains entirely multiplicative (`sqrt(mul)`), which aligns naturally with integer circuits (product plus square‐root approximations).

### SwiGLU Gates
The feedforward subnetwork uses a SwiGLU gate: two linear projections producing vectors \(a,b\), followed by elementwise multiplication after applying GELU on one branch:

\[
f(x)=\,(\mathrm{GELU}(a)\odot b).
\]

In BitNet b1.58 both projections employ `BitLinear`. Multiplication inside SwiGLU is preserved because gating expresses critical “feature mixing”: even though weights are ternary in each projection separately, their product forms rich combinatorial possibilities (e.g., \(+1\times-1=-?+?\)). Crucially these operations require only integer multiplications at low bitwidth (`int8 × int8 → int16`) which modern hardware can perform efficiently via barrel shifters or DSPs enabled for small operands.

The retention of RMSNorm and SwiGLU gates ensures that higher-layer interaction patterns—normally learned through complex residue additions—remain largely unaltered while enabling near exhaustive parameter compression down into ternary space.

---

## Integration of Rotary Positional Embeddings

Transformer attention mechanisms rely on positional cues; LLaMA introduced rotary embeddings (RoPE) that encode positions as complex rotations applied directly to query and key vectors:

\[
q^{(pos)}_t = q_t \cdot R(t), 
\quad
k^{(pos)}_t = k_t \cdot R(t),
\]

where \(R(t)\) implements circular shifts determined by sinusoidal functions elevated to high frequencies (RS [romance]. 

In BitNet b1.58 this scheme remains unchanged: all positional embeddings continue as low‑rank multiplicative shifts before feeding into `BitLinear`. Because RoPE does not require dynamic parameters—it only modulates via predetermined sine/cosine tables—its cost footprint stays negligible even after quantisation: we can precompute the rotations once as floating‐point coefficients applied outside grid transfer boundaries.

Thus rotary embeddings preserve relative position information without adding any learnable weights expandable beyond ternarisation limits; they simply turn linear combinations with set coefficients into signed additions which pair well with `BitLinear`.

---

## Compatibility with HuggingFace / vLLM / llama.cpp Ecosystems

Deploying an exotic architecture like BitNet b1.58 requires software tools familiar with open-source libraries otherwise massively used by practitioners:

- **HuggingFace Transformers**  
  The library ships model classes such as `LlamaForCausalLM` that expose their module hierarchy (token embedding tables, transformer blocks). By subclassing these classes you can monkey-patch every occurrence of `nn.Linear` into `BitLinear` instances during model loading without altering downstream inference pipelines (`forward()`, `generate()`). Standard model loading utilities (`from_pretrained`) seamlessly recognise quantised tensors because they are stored using PyTorch's optional quantised tensors (`torch.float16` → custom-coded custom format).  

- **vLLM**  
  vLLM uses ahead‑of-time compilation for inference parallelism; its kernel generation logic can detect whether parameters are binary/ternary through token introspection (`tensor.dtype`) and pick specialized kernels accordingly (e.g., INT8 addition kernel vs FP16 multiply-add). The paper explicitly mentions using FasterTransformer + Ladder 2‑bit kernels: these can be reused under vLLM's GPU batching framework once bitwidth-check logic is added.

- **llama.cpp**  
  This lightweight C++ baseline supports int8 weights via basic static-integer quantisation and binary multiplication skipping tricks; expanding it to support ternary values only requires modifying weight lookup logic (`w[k]∈{-1,+/−0}`) while preserving existing int8 activation flow and fusing sign detection early at each step—a straightforward modification because llama.cpp already has bit-level arithmetic loops accessible from C.

By ensuring that each interchangeable module retains the same public interface (`nn.Module.forward()` signature plus parameter naming conventions), hierarchical interactions – such as pipeline parallelism – remain untouched. Consequently developers can drop a pre-trained BitNet b1.58 checkpoint downstream after converting every other layer into its ternary counterpart right away without reimplementing whole inference pipelines.

---
 
In summary, constructing BitNet b1.58 involves swapping dense floating‐point linear functions for `BitLinear` that store ternary weights and perform exclusively signed additions; pruning biases whilst counterbalancing expressive power via zero-valued connections; retaining advanced normalisers (RMSNorm) and gating mechanisms (SwiGLU); adopting existing efficient positional encodings (RoPE); then carefully mirroring PyTorch's APIs so that off-the-shelf frameworks such as HuggingFace Transformers, vLLM, or llama.cpp seamlessly recognise and exploit this new architecture without code rewrites beyond monkey patching linear modules. This editorial design yields a compact yet performant transformer able to compete texture-for-perplexity against full-precision LLMs while offering significant gains in compute latency,
memory usage, throughput,
and energy efficiency—all within familiar software ecosystems.

# 4. Quantization Scheme for Weights and Activations

Large‑language models (LLMs) are traditionally trained with 16‑bit floating‑point parameters and activations. In *BitNet b1.58* the entire model is reduced to ternary weights \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) while keeping activations in 8 bits. The paper proposes a carefully designed quantization procedure that preserves the statistical properties of the original full‑precision weights, guarantees an unbiased expectation during training, and simplifies the handling of activations at inference time. This chapter explains each part of that scheme in depth, exposing the motivation, the precise equations, and the implications for training dynamics.

---

## Absmean Weight Scaling \(\gamma\)

The first step in converting a full‑precision weight matrix \(W\) into a ternary matrix \(\widetilde{W}\) is a data‑dependent scaling that preserves the overall magnitude of the weights. Because each weight in \(\widetilde{W}\) can only take three discrete values, we need to map the diverse values of \(W\) onto this restricted set without disproportionately distorting large or small weights.

The paper uses an *absmean* scaling factor defined as
\[
\gamma \;=\; \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m} |W_{ij}|,
\]
where \(n\) and \(m\) are the dimensions of \(W\). In other words, \(\gamma\) is simply the average absolute value of all entries in the matrix. By dividing every weight by this scalar we nondimensionalize them:
\[
Z_{ij} = \frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma + \epsilon},
\]
where \(\epsilon = 10^{-7}\) (a tiny constant to avoid division by zero). The result is that typical entries of \(Z\) lie close to \([-1, 1]\), while preserving their relative magnitudes.

Why *absmean* instead of *max* or *standard deviation*?  
- **Max scaling** would aggressively compress values near zero, causing many distinct weight magnitudes to collapse into zero after rounding.  
- **Standard deviation** would ignore heavy outliers that carry significant model capacity.  
- *Absmean* strikes a balance: it is sensitive to both small and large values but remains robust to outliers because it averages absolute values rather than squaring them.

---

## RoundClip Operation

After scaling, each element must be projected onto the ternary set \(\{-1,0,+1\}\). Direct rounding would lead to many entries mapping to zero when \(Z_{ij}\in[-0.5,0.5]\). The paper introduces a two‑step operation called **RoundClip**:

1. **Rounding**:  
   \[
   R_{ij} = \mathrm{round}(Z_{ij}),
   \]
   where `round` returns the nearest integer (-1, 0, or +1).  
2. **Clipping**:  
   \[
   \widetilde{W}_{ij} = \max(-1,\;\min(+1,\;R_{ij})).
   \]
   
Combining both steps yields:
\[
\widetilde{W}_{ij}
= 
\mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(
    Z_{ij},-\,1,+\,1
\bigr)
=
\mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(
    \frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma+\epsilon},-\,1,+\,1
\bigr).
\]

The clipping is redundant mathematically in this context because `round` already produces values in \{-1,0,+1\}. However, including it guarantees robustness if implementation details change—e.g., if a custom rounding policy is introduced later.

---

## Ensuring Unbiased Expectation after Quantization

Quantizing weights can introduce bias: if we simply clamp most small‐magnitude weights to zero, the expected value of a unit’s output changes. To avoid systematic drift during training, BitNet maintains an expectationally unbiased mapping from full precision to ternary by applying *BatchNorm*-style scaling on top of quantized weights.

Specifically:

- Let \(X_t\) denote an input vector feeding into a linear layer.
- The quantized output before any bias terms (which are removed in BitNet for simplicity) is
  \[
  Y_t = X_t^\top \widetilde{W}.
  \]
- Since each entry in \(\widetilde{W}\) is symmetric around zero (\(-p\,L + q\,L_0 + r\,L_+ = 0\) statistically for large matrices), the expected value of \(Y_t\) equals zero when \(X_t\)'s mean is zero.
- In practice we enforce a *zero‑centered* initialization for each block of weights before training. During updates we keep track of running averages that preserve symmetry: any non‑zero update must be countered by an equal but opposite update elsewhere (or by massaging gradients through Stein’s unbiased risk estimator).

Moreover, stochastic rounding could be adopted during forward passes to keep the expected value equal to that of full precision—an idea discussed in related literature—but BitNet opts for deterministic RoundClip because it facilitates hardware acceleration: deterministic integer arithmetic aligns easily with SIMD pipelines on specialized chips.

## Impact on Gradient Flow During Training

Training requires backpropagation through both the quantized weight function and activation scaling function. Because RoundClip and per-token scaling are non‑differentiable operations (they involve discrete rounding), BitNet adopts straight‑through estimators (STEs):

### Weight Gradient Estimator

During backward pass for weight updates we treat RoundClip as identity for gradient flow:

For any loss term \(L,\)
\[
\nabla_W L 
= 
\nabla_{\widetilde W} L,
\\
\nabla_{\widetilde W} L,
= 
\nabla_{\text{real output}}\!L.
%
% In practice STE: dx/dw' ~ dx/dw basically ignoring nondiff.
%

In other words,
if normally we would need derivative of sign or round (\(\partial{\rm Round}/\partial x=0)\),
the STE sets it equal to one within valid range and clamps otherwise.
Thus gradients flow as if linear between adjacent ternary thresholds.

### Activation Gradient Estimator

The per-token scale factor depends on \(M_t=\max_i |A^{(f)}_{t,i}|\). Computing gradient w.r.t floating activations involves Rule-of-Beta: treat scale as constant during backward pass (i.e., assume gradient flows only through `round` output). This is standard practice when using per-channel quantization in training.

Even though quantized values are integer clamped between -127 and +127,
the STE lets gradients pass unchanged from real-valued activations during backpropagation until training settles on stable ternary weights.

Because BitNet removes biases entirely from all layers (simplifying weight updates further), there are no additive terms introduced after linear layers that could break symmetry; thus maintaining unbiased expectation becomes easier under STE rules.

## Summary

BitNet b1.58 achieves ternary weight representation through a two‐step pipeline:

1. **Absmean scaling** normalizes weight magnitudes relative to their average absolute value.
2. **RoundClip** projects normalized weights onto a fixed ternary set deterministically.

To maintain statistical stability during training:

- An unbiased expectation is enforced via symmetrical initializations and removal of biases.
- Straight–through estimators allow gradients to flow through non‑differentiable rounding operations seamlessly.

Activations are scaled per token into an integer interval centered at zero (\([-Q_b,Q_b]\)), avoiding any zero‑point offsets while keeping computational simplicity for inference accelerators that rely on pure integer arithmetic plus occasional multiply–accumulate simulation via shifts and adds.

These design choices together enable BitNet b1.58 to match full‑precision baselines on perplexity and downstream tasks while dramatically saving memory bandwidth and arithmetic energy—a key ingredient for bringing large language models onto next‑generation low‑power hardware platforms.
