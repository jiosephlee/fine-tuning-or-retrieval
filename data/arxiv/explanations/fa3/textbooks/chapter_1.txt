Chapter 1: Why Attention Is the Bottleneck and What FlashAttention-3 Delivers

# Quadratic cost of attention with sequence length; implications for long-context LLMs and multimodal tasks
At the core of the Transformer architecture is the self-attention operation. For a single attention head with query, key, and value matrices $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ (sequence length $N$, head dimension $d$) the forward pass computes
\[
\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\qquad
\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\qquad
\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d},
\]
where typically $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ and the softmax is applied row-wise. The matrix $\mathbf{S}$ contains the attention scores for every query–key pair, so its size grows as $N^2$. This quadratic dependence on $N$ is the fundamental source of the computational and memory bottleneck in attention.

Because both the arithmetic work and the intermediate storage scale like $N^2$, attention becomes the dominant cost when models are applied to long contexts. Increasing context length from, say, 1k to 8k or 16k tokens multiplies the work and memory by factors of 64 or 256. This imposes practical limits on model designs and on the size of inputs that can be handled for training or inference. The bottleneck matters beyond text: long-context capability is needed for modeling long documents, high-resolution images, audio and video sequences, and applications that retain long user histories or long agent workflows. Any aim to scale Transformers into these long-context or high-resolution domains must therefore confront the quadratic attention cost.

From an algorithmic perspective, this quadratic cost motivates two complementary directions: reducing the asymptotic cost (approximation methods, sparse / linearized attention variants) and reducing the constant factors or hidden costs through better implementation (software that fully exploits hardware). The remainder of this chapter focuses on the latter — how to make exact attention much faster on modern GPUs while preserving numerical fidelity.

# Paths to acceleration: approximation, software optimization, alternative architectures
When practitioners and researchers confront the $O(N^2)$ attention bottleneck, they typically follow one of three broad strategies.

One route is approximation: replace the dense $N\times N$ attention with an approximation that reduces arithmetic or memory. Examples include low-rank factorization, kernel-based linearizations, and sparsity patterns that avoid computing all pairwise scores. These methods can reduce asymptotic cost, but they change the mathematical behavior of attention and may affect model quality, especially for tasks that need precise long-range interactions.

A second route — the one taken by FlashAttention and its successors — keeps exact attention but accelerates it by making the implementation IO-aware and hardware-conscious. This means designing tiling, data movement, and computation patterns that avoid slow accesses to global memory (HBM), keep intermediates on-chip when possible, and fuse operations to amortize overhead. Software optimization aims to exploit the GPU’s memory hierarchy, specialized units, and instruction-level features so that the same mathematically exact attention is executed much faster.

A third route is architectural change: design new neural layers that provide comparable modeling power with cheaper computation (for example, recurrence-inspired or stateful mechanisms). These alternatives can be compelling, but they require rethinking modeling assumptions and re-validating performance across tasks. FlashAttention-3 operates within the exact-attention family: it keeps the mathematical semantics of attention and attacks the bottleneck by exploiting modern GPU hardware features to accelerate the exact computation.

# From FlashAttention to FlashAttention-2: IO-aware tiling and sequence-parallel restructuring
The first FlashAttention paper introduced the key idea of fusing the attention computation into a single kernel and using a tiling strategy that avoids staging full $N\times N$ intermediates in global memory. The main trick is a local softmax reduction that accumulates contributions block-by-block, so only small blocks of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ need to be resident on-chip (shared memory or registers) at any time. This eliminates expensive reads and writes of $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to and from HBM and thereby substantially reduces memory traffic and latency.

FlashAttention-2 extended that idea by restructuring the parallelization to also tile over the sequence length. Concretely, instead of assigning the entire sequence to a single unit of work, FlashAttention-2 divides $\mathbf{Q}$ into query blocks and iterates over blocks of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$, performing the inner loop of the forward pass over those key/value blocks. That sequence-parallel tiling improves occupancy and distributes work more evenly across threadblocks and SMs, which is important for throughput on GPUs. FlashAttention-2 thus combined algorithmic fusion (local softmax) with a tiling schedule that better matches the GPU execution model.

Despite these gains, FlashAttention-2 leaves room for improvement on the newest hardware. In particular, on NVIDIA’s Hopper H100 GPUs FlashAttention-2 achieves substantially lower Tensor Core utilization than high-performance GEMM kernels: typical reported utilization of FlashAttention-2 is around 35% on H100, whereas optimized GEMM kernels can reach 80–90%. This utilization gap motivates rethinking how attention can take advantage of newer hardware capabilities that FlashAttention-2 did not exploit.

# Hardware evolution: why Hopper H100 changes the game (asynchrony and FP8)
Modern GPU generations introduce new hardware features that change the best way to organize computations. Two Hopper-specific capabilities are central to why a new algorithmic design is valuable.

First, Hopper exposes more robust asynchrony between specialized hardware units. Hopper has the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) for asynchronous bulk copies between global memory and shared memory, and it also exposes an asynchronous warpgroup-wide Tensor Core instruction (WGMMA) for matrix-multiply-accumulate work. Because these units can operate independently and overlap with other instruction streams, software can be structured so that some warps emphasize data movement (producer warps issuing TMAs) while other warps emphasize compute (consumer warps issuing WGMMA). This hardware asynchrony enables warp-specialization and software pipelining that hide latency by overlapping communication and compute in ways that were not possible or beneficial on prior architectures.

Second, Hopper provides fast low-precision arithmetic units (FP8 Tensor Cores). These can roughly double performance (FLOPs/s) compared to FP16 for matmul-heavy workloads, but they come with constraints and pitfalls. FP8 accumulators and operand layouts have specific alignment and “major” constraints: FP8 WGMMA requires operands in the k-major format (contiguous in the inner dimension $K$), while FP16 WGMMA accepts both mn-major and k-major operands when sourcing from shared memory. Moreover, using FP8 increases quantization error, a significant concern for attention because outlier activations in large models can make per-tensor quantization inaccurate.

Together, these hardware advances (asynchronous TMA and WGMMA, plus FP8 support) open an opportunity: design an attention algorithm that explicitly uses asynchrony to overlap independent work and that carefully adapts layouts and quantization to benefit from FP8’s throughput while keeping numerical error acceptable.

# FlashAttention-3 contributions: warp-specialized producer–consumer asynchrony; overlapping GEMMs and softmax; FP8 with block quantization and incoherent processing
FlashAttention-3 is designed exactly to exploit the new hardware properties while preserving exact (or closely accurate FP8) attention semantics. It brings three principal ideas.

The first idea is warp-specialized producer–consumer asynchrony. Instead of each warp doing both loads and arithmetic, the warps of a CTA are partitioned into producers (responsible for issuing TMA loads from global memory into a circular shared-memory buffer) and consumers (responsible for computing on the data using WGMMA and other instructions). Producers and consumers synchronize through a small circular buffer staged across shared memory. Because TMA and WGMMA are asynchronous, the producer warps can keep streaming blocks of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ into shared memory while consumer warps schedule WGMMA on previously loaded blocks, improving the ability to hide memory latencies and instruction-issue stalls. The implementation uses Hopper features such as \texttt{setmaxnreg} to reassign registers between warp roles and TMA for overlapable large loads.

The second idea is to overlap softmax with GEMMs by breaking the iteration dependencies in the inner loop and pipelining across iterations. In a naive inner loop, computing a block of scores $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ (a GEMM) must complete before the softmax-based rescaling can be applied and before using $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ to multiply with $\mathbf{V}_j$ (another GEMM). FlashAttention-3 introduces a two-stage (and optionally three-stage) pipeline: while one stage executes a WGMMA to produce the next block of scores, another stage executes the WGMMA to multiply the previously computed (and already-softmaxed/rescaled) probabilities by the previous $\mathbf{V}$ block. The softmax work (which relies on exponentials and other scalar ops) is scheduled to run in the shadow of the high-throughput WGMMA operations from other warpgroups or iterations. The paper describes a “pingpong” scheduling: two warpgroups alternate roles so that the comparatively slow special-function units (exponential) are busy while the Tensor Cores perform dense matmul. This overlapping is critical because the GPU’s multi-function unit (for exponentials) has far lower peak throughput than the Tensor Cores — for example, the H100’s FP16 matmul peak is quoted as 989 TFLOPS while special functions like exponential are only about 3.9 TFLOPS. Without overlap, the softmax-related work would become a visible bottleneck; with overlap, the low-throughput softmax is hidden under the matmul high-throughput pipeline and the overall utilization increases substantially.

The third idea is to make FP8 practical and fast for attention. This requires several coordinated actions. First, layout transformations are needed: FP8 WGMMA accepts only k-major operands, but typical attention data is contiguous in the head dimension (mn-major for $\mathbf{V}$ tiles). FlashAttention-3 performs an in-kernel tile transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ tiles after loading them into shared memory using LDSM/STSM instructions (which can transpose at tile granularity efficiently and with low register use). Second, FP8 imposes different register-accumulator layouts; FlashAttention-3 uses byte-permute and register rearrangements to transform the FP32 accumulators produced by one WGMMA into the layout expected by the next FP8 WGMMA, coordinating these permutations with the in-kernel transpose so that the chain of FP8 WGMMAs computes correct results while remaining register- and memory-efficient.

On the accuracy side, FP8 requires careful quantization to avoid large errors due to outliers. FlashAttention-3 adopts two techniques. Block quantization replaces per-tensor scaling with per-block scaling: each block (the natural block sizes used by the algorithm, e.g., $B_r\times d$ or $B_c\times d$) gets its own FP8 scale factor. Because the algorithm already operates block-by-block, this per-block quantization can be fused into preceding operations (such as rotary embeddings) at essentially no extra cost. Second, incoherent processing multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by the same random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ before quantization. Because $\mathbf{M}$ is orthogonal, $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top = (\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top$, so the attention math is unchanged. Multiplying by $\mathbf{M}$ spreads out outlier magnitudes across coordinates, reducing worst-case quantization error. Practically, $\mathbf{M}$ is implemented as a product of diagonal sign matrices and a Hadamard transform, which can be applied in $O(d\log d)$ time and fused with other pre-attention operations at no extra memory bandwidth cost.

Together, these three contributions — warp-specialized asynchronous producer/consumer scheduling, GEMM–softmax pipelining (pingpong and intra-warpgroup overlapping), and FP8-aware layout plus quantization strategies — allow FlashAttention-3 to exploit Hopper’s hardware more fully than previous exact-attention kernels.

# Headline results: 1.5–2.0× speedup in FP16, up to 740 TFLOPs/s (75%); FP8 near 1.2 PFLOPs/s; 2.6× lower FP8 error than baseline
The empirical results reported for FlashAttention-3 on an H100 80GB SXM5 GPU validate the design goals.

In FP16, FlashAttention-3 achieves a forward-pass speedup of roughly 1.5–2.0$\times$ compared to FlashAttention-2 across a variety of heads, sequence lengths, and masking modes. Peak measured throughput reaches up to 740 TFLOPs/s, which corresponds to about 75% of the device’s theoretical matmul peak in the paper’s accounting. Backward-pass speedups are also substantial (roughly 1.5–1.75$\times$), because the backward computation still benefits from the same producer/consumer and pipelining ideas.

In FP8, FlashAttention-3 attains even higher matmul throughput (because FP8 Tensor Cores have roughly double the matmul throughput of FP16 on Hopper). The paper reports FP8 performance close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s in favorable regimes. FP8 performance does require careful engineering trade-offs (for instance, persistent kernels and load balancing used for FP16 were not yet fully integrated for FP8 in the paper’s implementation), so for small sequence lengths and causal masking FP8 kernels did not always beat vendor libraries. For medium-to-long sequences, however, FP8 FlashAttention-3 is competitive or superior.

On the numerical-accuracy front, FP16 FlashAttention-3 matches FlashAttention-2 (and both improve upon naive standard attention in many cases) because intermediate reductions in softmax are kept in FP32, reducing rounding artifacts. For FP8, the combination of block quantization and incoherent processing yields a significant reduction in quantization error compared to a baseline FP8 attention that uses per-tensor scaling: up to 2.6$\times$ lower root-mean-squared error in the synthetic outlier experiments reported in the paper. These experiments were designed to stress outliers by adding occasional large-magnitude components to entries of $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$; block quantization and incoherent processing mitigate their deleterious effect on quantization.

Finally, the benchmarks show that for moderate and long sequences FlashAttention-3 can even outperform vendor-optimized libraries (cuDNN) that target H100, demonstrating that algorithmic redesign to exploit asynchrony and low-precision can beat carefully tuned library implementations that do not exploit the same combination of techniques.

Taken together, these results indicate that exact attention can be made significantly faster and (in the FP8 case) acceptably accurate on modern hardware — provided software explicitly exploits the asynchronous, specialized units and deals carefully with layout and quantization constraints. FlashAttention-3 is a concrete implementation of that insight: warp-specialization and pipelining to hide latency, and block-level FP8 strategies to harness higher FLOPs/s without sacrificing numerical reliability.
