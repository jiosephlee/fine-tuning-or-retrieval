\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: fa3}

### How should I choose the number of stages $s$ in the circular SMEM buffer, and what are the trade-offs?
Question:
Algorithm 1 uses an $s$-stage circular buffer in shared memory to pipeline TMA loads of $\{\mathbf{K}_j, \mathbf{V}_j\}$. How should $s$ be chosen on H100 in practice? What are the trade-offs in terms of: (1) SMEM footprint vs. occupancy, (2) latency hiding for TMA, and (3) added synchronization overhead? Is there a rule of thumb relating $s$ to the time of one WGMMA tile vs the TMA tile load time, or to the number of consumer warpgroups?
Answer:
Short answer: choose $s$ small (typ. $2$–$4$) so that the producer can enqueue enough TMA loads to keep all consumer WGMMA work busy, but not so large that the per-stage SMEM allocation kills occupancy or adds excessive sync bookkeeping. A practical rule of thumb is

$s \approx \operatorname{clamp}\!\left(1 + \left\lceil \frac{\text{TMA\_tile\_load\_time}}{\text{WGMMA\_tile\_time}} \right\rceil, 2, 4\right)$

subject to the hard SMEM-per-CTA constraint: $s \cdot \text{stage\_size} + \text{other\_SMEM} \le \text{SMEM\_per\_SM} / \text{CTAs\_per\_SM}$. Measure and tune if you need to push for extreme throughput.

Why this, and the trade-offs (intuitive + a few technical details grounded in the paper)

SMEM footprint vs. occupancy.
Each circular-buffer stage stores one $(K, V)$ tile (and any metadata). The per-stage cost is roughly $B_c \cdot d \cdot \mathrm{sizeof(element)}$ for $K$ plus $B_c \cdot d \cdot \mathrm{sizeof(element)}$ for $V$ (plus a few words of control). Increasing $s$ multiplies that cost, so SMEM per-CTA grows linearly with $s$. On Hopper the SMEM is limited and the scheduler will reduce CTA occupancy if a CTA’s SMEM demand is too large. Lower occupancy means fewer concurrent CTAs per SM and thus lower ability to hide other latencies (and lower aggregate throughput). So the practical constraint is: pick the largest $s$ that still lets you have enough CTAs per SM (or threads/registers) to achieve good occupancy. In practice the paper’s implementation uses small $s$ so register/SMEM pressure stays manageable.

Latency hiding for TMA.
TMA loads are asynchronous but have non-negligible latency. The circular buffer exists so the producer can issue TMA for future $(K,V)$ tiles ahead of time and the consumers can find data ready when they need it. To avoid producer stalls you want enough stages so that while the consumers and their WGMMA tiles are executing, the producer can load the next tiles. Conceptually you want $s$ large enough that the pipeline depth covers the TMA latency divided by the time between consumer consumption events. A clean formulation is to compare the time to perform one WGMMA tile (the consumer work that consumes one buffer stage) to the time to issue and complete one TMA tile load:

$\text{if }\ \text{TMA\_tile\_load\_time} \le \text{WGMMA\_tile\_time},\ s = 1$ (producer can keep up); otherwise you need roughly $\left\lceil \dfrac{\text{TMA\_tile\_load\_time}}{\text{WGMMA\_tile\_time}} \right\rceil$ stages so producer can issue loads far enough ahead. The paper exploits this by having the producer not wait during the first $s$ iterations while the buffer fills; that is the exact role of $s$ in hiding TMA latency.

Added synchronization overhead.
Each buffer stage introduces producer/consumer coordination: commit notifications, waits on $(j \bmod s)$ stages, and barrier / release operations. More stages mean slightly more bookkeeping and more potential for stalling if synchronization is not perfectly overlapped. In practice the cost of these extra syncs is small relative to WGMMA cost, but it is not zero: it adds instructions and increases the number of conditional waits and branch points in the producer/consumer loops. Also larger $s$ can exacerbate compiler/inspector complexity (and interact with register allocation), as noted in the paper’s discussion of register pressure and compiler reordering. So there is diminishing return: after you reach enough stages to hide TMA latency, increasing $s$ further produces little benefit and more overhead.

Relation to number of consumer warpgroups and WGMMA vs TMA times (rule of thumb).
Think of the system as having $P$ producer warpgroups and $C$ consumer warpgroups in the CTA (the paper uses warp-specialization and warpgroups to split producers and consumers). The producer must issue enough TMA loads to feed all $C$ consumers. A simple practical rule of thumb derived from the paper’s description is:

Make $s$ at least the number of consumer “tickets” required to keep all consumers busy while one TMA completes. Concretely set

$s \ge 1 + \left\lceil \dfrac{\text{TMA\_tile\_load\_time}}{\text{WGMMA\_tile\_time} \cdot \text{effective\_consumers\_per\_tile}} \right\rceil$

where $\text{effective\_consumers\_per\_tile}$ is the average number of WGMMA tiles that can be executed (in parallel or in pipeline interleaving) while a TMA finishes. For single-producer, multiple-consumer warp-specialization, a simpler and robust rule used in the paper is

$s \approx 1 + \left\lceil \dfrac{\text{TMA\_time}}{\text{WGMMA\_time}} \right\rceil$

and then clamp to small integers ($2$–$4$). The paper’s experiments show big wins from overlapping even with small $s$ (the 2-stage pipelining and ping-pong scheduling produced a large TFLOPs jump), so you usually do not need a large $s$.

Practical recommended procedure on H100 (step-by-step)
1) Compute per-stage SMEM: $\text{stage\_size} \approx 2 \cdot B_c \cdot d \cdot \mathrm{sizeof}(\text{float or fp8 packed}) + \text{small control words}$. Ensure $s \cdot \text{stage\_size}$ plus other SMEM fits your SM budget while leaving enough CTAs/SM to keep occupancy healthy. If it doesn’t, reduce $s$ or reduce $B_c$. 2) Microbenchmark or estimate: measure (or model from CUTLASS/SASS) the time to (a) do one WGMMA tile ($\text{WGMMA\_tile\_time}$) and (b) complete one TMA tile load into SMEM ($\text{TMA\_tile\_load\_time}$). 3) Set $s = 1 + \left\lceil \dfrac{\text{TMA\_tile\_load\_time}}{\text{WGMMA\_tile\_time}} \right\rceil$ and clamp to a small max (the paper’s practical designs use $2$ or $3$; a clamp of $4$ is conservative). 4) Validate: run the kernel and check whether the producer stalls (look at profiler timeline or SM stalls). If the producer stalls, increase $s$ (if SMEM allows) or adjust $B_c$. If occupancy drops too much, decrease $s$ and try to improve overlap by other means (e.g., ping-pong scheduling across warpgroups or the 2-stage GEMM-softmax).

Why small $s$ often suffices
The paper demonstrates that a combination of warp-specialization, ping-pong scheduling between warpgroups, and a 2-stage intra-warpgroup GEMM-softmax pipeline already captures most of the benefits on H100. Because WGMMA is extremely fast on H100 (and softmax is relatively slow), you only need a few buffer stages to mask TMA latency; making $s$ very large wastes precious SMEM and reduces occupancy for little additional latency hiding.

Summary (concise)
- $s$ trades SMEM footprint (linear in $s$) vs. ability to hide TMA latency. - Use a small $s$ ($2$–$4$) in practice on H100; start with $2$ (matches the 2-stage pipelining in the paper) and raise if producer stalls. - Rule of thumb: $s \approx 1 + \left\lceil \dfrac{\text{TMA\_tile\_load\_time}}{\text{WGMMA\_tile\_time}} \right\rceil$ but clamp to small integers and always check SMEM/occupancy constraints. - Monitor producer stalls and SMEM-induced occupancy drops; tune $B_c$, $s$, or consumer warpgroup split accordingly.
