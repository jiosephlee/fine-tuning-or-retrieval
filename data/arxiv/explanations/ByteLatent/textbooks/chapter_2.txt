\title{A Textbook about the Paper: ByteLatent}

Chapter 2: Background: From Tokens to Bytes and Information-Theoretic Metrics

# Tokenization basics: BPE/subword vocabularies and their trade-offs

Modern large language models (LLMs) almost always begin by converting raw character sequences into discrete units called tokens. A common family of tokenizers is subword tokenization, such as byte-pair encoding (BPE). Subword tokenizers are trained on a corpus to produce a finite vocabulary of multibyte units that balance frequency (common words or subwords become single tokens) and coverage (rare words are split). The result is that the model operates over a sequence of tokens rather than raw bytes; metric quantities such as sequence length, model input embeddings, and output softmax size are therefore all defined with respect to that token vocabulary.

This design has practical advantages. By grouping frequently co-occurring bytes into tokens, average sequence length (in tokens) is reduced compared to raw bytes. Shorter token sequences mean fewer autoregressive steps for the main Transformer, which can reduce some parts of computational cost and, historically, allowed models to reach strong performance with feasible compute. At the same time, tokenizers impose a fixed vocabulary determined prior to model training. That fixed vocabulary both constrains how the model represents inputs and introduces systematic biases: tokenization choices affect how strings are compressed into model inputs, and those choices can interact poorly with different domains, noisy text, or scripts the tokenizer was not optimized for. The paper points out concrete pitfalls: sensitivity to input noise, lack of orthographic knowledge (difficulty reasoning about spellings and character manipulations), and multilingual inequity arising from tokenizers optimized for some languages at the expense of others.

A key architectural consequence of a finite vocabulary is the presence of large embedding tables and a correspondingly large final output projection (the matrix that maps model hidden states to logits over the vocabulary). That matrix grows with vocabulary size and is a significant parameter and flop cost. Thus, when designing tokenizers and model-vocabulary pairs there is an unavoidable trade-off: larger vocabularies yield fewer tokens per input (shorter sequences) but increase embedding and output-projection dimensions and costs.

# Vocabulary size vs. sequence length vs. output projection cost

Tokenizers introduce a trade-off between three linked quantities: vocabulary size, average token length (i.e., how many bytes each token represents on average), and the cost of the final projection / embedding layers. Increasing vocabulary size typically allows tokens to represent larger byte-granular chunks on average, reducing the number of autoregressive steps for the core Transformer. But this comes at the cost of growing the embedding table and the output projection (both scale with vocabulary size), and these layers become large memory and compute sinks.

The paper gives an illustrative example from Llama versions: increasing average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes led to the embedding table increasing by roughly 4× between Llama 2 and Llama 3. This highlights the sharp, non-linear resource effect of increasing vocabulary: small increases in average token size (and therefore vocabulary complexity) can drastically inflate the size and cost of the embedding and output-projection matrices. Because those matrices are tied to a fixed vocabulary, tokenizers leave little practical room to vary token size without incurring large parameter and flop penalties. In other words, tokenizers entangle sequence length reduction with a directly proportional increase in final-layer cost.

Contrast this with alternatives that do not commit to a fixed vocabulary: if the model maps byte groups to learned latent patch representations (not discrete tokens tied to an output-vocabulary), then you can change the average input grouping (patch size) without inflating the output projection. That is an essential motivation behind patch-based and tokenizer-free approaches: break the strict coupling between short sequences and large vocabulary cost.

# Byte-level modeling: pros and cons compared to tokens

Modeling raw bytes directly avoids the pre-processing step of tokenization and the fixed-vocabulary constraint. The advantages, as emphasized in the paper, are multiple and intuitive.

First, byte-level models have direct access to underlying character and orthographic information. This makes them inherently more robust to character-level noise (misspellings, case changes, inserted or deleted characters) and better at tasks that require manipulation or understanding of character sequences, such as grapheme-to-phoneme mapping, fine-grained orthographic reasoning, or low-resource language variants where tokenizers poorly cover the script. Byte-level modeling also avoids heuristics baked into tokenizers that can favor or disadvantage certain languages or domains.

Second, because bytes are the atomic unit, byte-level models do not rely on a fixed vocabulary. This eliminates the large embedding and final-projection matrices tied to vocabulary size, and it opens the possibility of dynamic grouping strategies that allocate compute adaptively where it is needed.

The primary drawback is sequence length: raw byte sequences are much longer than token sequences, so a naive byte-level Transformer must run its compute-heavy layers (especially feed-forward networks) at every byte position. Historically, this made byte-level models far more expensive to train and to run, and previous byte-level Transformer attempts either lagged behind token-based models at scale or required specialized architectures focused on more efficient attention or alternative sequence mechanisms.

Prior work attempted to mitigate the cost by making attention cheaper (local/efficient attention, strided downsampling, or attention-free architectures) or by designing encoder–decoder approaches that compress byte sequences. Those approaches often brought gains at small scales but still struggled at larger scales because, as the paper stresses, at scale the dominant cost in Transformers is the large feed-forward network (FFN) layers that are applied per sequence position. Therefore any approach that reduces the number of positions processed by the heavy FFNs is particularly valuable for efficiency at scale.

# Perplexity vs. Bits-Per-Byte (BPB): tokenizer-invariant evaluation

Perplexity is the classic evaluation metric for autoregressive language models, but it depends on a fixed tokenization: perplexity measures the average exponential of cross-entropy per token. When comparing models that use different tokenizers—or models that operate on bytes rather than tokens—perplexity is not directly comparable.

To obtain a tokenizer-invariant measure of uncertainty over text, the paper uses Bits-Per-Byte (BPB). BPB expresses the model’s average information-theoretic uncertainty per raw byte. Given a byte sequence \(\mathbf{x}\) of length \(n_{\text{bytes}}\) and a cross-entropy loss \(\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})\) measured in nats, BPB is defined as

\[
\text{BPB}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}.
\]

Dividing by \(\ln(2)\) converts nats to bits, and normalizing by the number of bytes yields bits per byte. BPB lets us compare byte-level and token-level models on a common footing, because it measures how many bits of uncertainty remain per underlying byte regardless of how a tokenizer would have grouped them. In practice, studies comparing byte and token models report BPB when they need tokenizer-invariant comparisons.

# Where computation goes in LLMs: attention vs. FFNs at scale

Understanding where Transformer compute is spent is essential to designing efficient byte-level architectures. A Transformer block has two dominant computational components: the self-attention mechanism and the feed-forward network (FFN). Attention costs scale with the square of the sequence length for dense attention, while FFN costs scale linearly with sequence length but can dominate because FFN layers have large hidden dimensions and are applied to every sequence position.

The paper emphasizes a crucial point for scaling: at large model sizes the computational budget is dominated by the FFN layers operating per position. That means reducing the number of positions fed into the heavy FFNs yields disproportionate savings at scale. Efficient attention mechanisms (sparse/local) help but do not address the per-position FFN cost if the model still processes every byte. Therefore, approaches that compress or group bytes into fewer higher-level units before applying the large global model can yield significant flop reductions without resorting to fundamentally different attention mechanisms.

This insight motivates the BLT design: a lightweight local encoder and decoder operate at the byte level to map between bytes and latent patch representations, while a large latent (global) Transformer operates on those patches. Because the global Transformer (where most FFN flops lie) is invoked at patch granularity rather than byte granularity, the number of heavy FFN applications is reduced in proportion to the average patch size. Thus, patching is an effective way to control and reallocate the dominant source of compute in large-scale Transformer models.

# Patching formalism and cost implications

Formally, a patching function \(f_p\) segments a byte sequence \(\mathbf{x} = \{x_i \mid i=1,\ldots,n\}\) into a shorter sequence of \(m < n\) patches \(\mathbf{p} = \{p_j \mid j=1,\ldots,m\}\). In implementation terms, this can be represented by a mapping that assigns to each byte \(x_i\) a flag in \(\{0,1\}\) where 1 denotes the start of a new patch. The number of patches \(m\) determines the number of steps executed by the main (global) Transformer, so average patch size \(n/m\) becomes the principal factor controlling the heavy-flop cost during training and inference.

Different patching strategies trade off consistency, adaptivity, and implementation simplicity. The simplest is strided patching that groups every fixed number \(k\) of bytes into a patch. Strided patching is easy to use for controlling compute (average patch size is exactly \(k\)), but it lacks adaptivity: it treats low- and high-information regions the same and can split the same linguistic unit inconsistently across contexts.

Space-based patching creates new patches at whitespace-like bytes (space, punctuation, etc.). This better aligns patches with linguistic units (commonly words) and makes patch boundaries consistent across contexts. It therefore often allocates more compute at word boundaries, which often correspond to higher-uncertainty predictions (for example, predicting the first byte of a word is typically harder than the remaining bytes of that word). The downside is that space-based patching cannot adapt patch size continuously or account for information-density variations within words or non-linguistic domains.

Entropy-based (dynamic) patching, introduced in the paper, uses a small byte-level language model to estimate the next-byte entropy at each position,

\[
H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}),
\]

where \(p_{e}\) is the entropy model’s predictive distribution over the byte vocabulary \(\mathcal{V}\). Patch boundaries are chosen where the entropy is relatively high, for example using a global threshold \(H(x_t) > \theta_g\) or by detecting points where entropy increases significantly relative to the previous byte \(H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1})>\theta_r\). Entropy patching is adaptive to contextual uncertainty and produces patches with more uniform information density, enabling the model to allocate heavy compute (the global Transformer) to genuinely difficult predictions.

One practical constraint for generation is that patching decisions must be incremental: the patching rule must be computable from the already-generated prefix, because generation cannot rely on future bytes. Formally, an incremental patching scheme satisfies

\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}.
\]

Importantly, BPE tokenization in its standard form does not satisfy this property, because the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on the continuation. This distinction matters when implementing autoregressive generation without peeking ahead.

# Prior byte-level approaches: Byte/Char Transformers, ByT5, CANINE, MambaByte, MegaByte, SpaceByte

The BLT paper situates its contributions in the context of prior byte- and character-level modeling literature. Several prior efforts aimed to bridge the gap between tokenized and byte-level modeling, with different architectural trade-offs.

Early character-level models and character RNNs demonstrated that operating on subword-free inputs can work and can be especially beneficial for morphologically rich languages, but these models commonly needed specialized architectures or auxiliary objectives to reach competitive performance. With the rise of Transformers, several attempts adapted Transformers to bytes/characters. Some approaches focused on architectural changes to downsample or compress character sequences before feeding them to a larger model; others modified attention to be more efficient over long byte sequences.

ByT5 explored encoder–decoder byte-level modeling and found improved robustness to noise, but without patching the cost of processing every byte created heavy compute overhead. CANINE applied deep Transformer stacks with specialized local downsampling to build word representations from characters and matched or outperformed comparable token-level encoder-only models in some multilingual settings. MambaByte explored attention-free or memory-centric designs to support very large byte contexts; such approaches decrease certain costs and showed good results at smaller model scales. MegaByte introduced a fixed static patching and concatenation approach to downsample bytes into patches; it obtained competitive results at 1B parameter scale but, according to the paper, static patching lags behind tokenizer-based models at large scale unless combined with additional architectural improvements. SpaceByte improves on static strided patching by using space-like bytes as natural patch boundaries and demonstrated practical gains over static patching in some domains.

The common theme is that many prior byte-level approaches reduce cost by either (a) changing attention mechanisms, (b) applying fixed downsampling/patching, or (c) using different memory/architectural primitives. These approaches showed promising results—especially at small and medium scales—but they faced difficulty when scaling to the large-model regime where FFN costs per position dominate. The BLT approach combines a dynamic patching scheme (entropy-based) with a three-part model (small byte-level local encoder and decoder around a large global latent Transformer) so that the large FFNs operate on far fewer, information-weighted patch positions. This combination is designed to retain the benefits of byte-level representations (robustness, character awareness, tokenizer-freedom) without suffering the prohibitive per-byte compute cost that hindered prior pure byte-level Transformers.

# Summary: conceptual takeaways

Tokenizers trade off sequence length against vocabulary-driven parameter costs; they are practical but introduce biases and rigidity. Byte-level modeling gives direct access to character structure and robustness advantages but traditionally incurs prohibitive compute because the heavy FFNs run at every byte. Patching—especially dynamic, entropy-informed patching—offers a principled middle path: group bytes into variable-sized patches so that the large global Transformer runs only at patch granularity, while lightweight byte-level encoders and decoders handle mapping to and from bytes. Evaluating such models fairly requires tokenizer-invariant metrics like Bits-Per-Byte (BPB), and careful accounting of where flops are spent (local modules vs global FFNs) guides architectural choices. The BLT architecture implements these ideas by combining entropy-based patching with light local models and a large latent Transformer to obtain both the robustness of byte-level modeling and the scaling efficiency needed for large LLMs.
