### Is the entropy definition missing a minus sign in BLT’s patching criterion?
Question:
In Section 2.3 (Entropy Patching), the paper defines the next-byte entropy as

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})$$

This is typically negative unless there is an explicit minus sign or a specified log base with negation. Later, the method thresholds entropy with conditions like $H(x_t) > \theta_g$. Should the definition actually be $H(x_i) = -\sum_v p_e \log p_e$? Also, what log base is used and how are the threshold values (e.g., $\theta_g=0.6$ vs $0.1$ during inference) calibrated under that convention?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the paper’s entropy formula as printed is missing the usual leading minus sign. They use the standard (non-negative) Shannon entropy in nats (natural log), and the numeric thresholds (e.g. $0.6$ at training, $0.1$ at inference) were chosen empirically to produce the desired average patch size on the pretraining data (and thus the desired flop budget). Below I explain why, how to interpret the thresholds, and how you would reproduce/calibrate them.

Why the minus sign must be there
The paper writes
\[
H(x_i)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal V} p_e(x_i=v\mid\mathbf{x}_{<i})\log p_e(x_i=v\mid\mathbf{x}_{<i}).
\]
That expression is the negative of the usual Shannon entropy (it equals $-H$ and is non-positive). Later they use comparisons like $H(x_t)>\theta_g$ with positive thresholds ($\theta_g=0.6$ or $\theta_g=0.1$), which only makes sense if $H$ is non-negative. Therefore the intended definition is the standard one:
\[
H(x_i)=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal V} p_e(x_i=v\mid\mathbf{x}_{<i})\log p_e(x_i=v\mid\mathbf{x}_{<i}).
\]

Log base (units)
The paper’s other notation (e.g. BPB uses natural log in the denominator: $\mathrm{BPB}=\mathcal{L}_{CE}/(\ln 2\cdot n)$) implies they work in natural logarithms. So entropy is in nats (not bits). Thus the thresholds $0.6$ and $0.1$ are in nats.

What the numeric thresholds mean and how they were chosen
Those threshold values are not theoretical constants; they are hyperparameters tuned to achieve a target average patch size (and therefore a target inference flop budget). Practically the authors describe choosing a patching threshold that yields the desired average patch size on the pretraining data mix. Concretely you would:

1. Run your trained entropy model across a representative corpus (same mix used for pretraining).
2. For each byte position compute the entropy in nats: $H=-\sum p\log p$.
3. Sweep candidate global thresholds $\theta_g$ and compute the resulting segmentation (mark boundary where $H>\theta_g$) and from that the average patch size (mean bytes per patch).
4. Pick the $\theta_g$ that gives the average patch size you want (or that matches the flop budget you must meet).

The same reasoning applies to the approximate-monotonicity rule $H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1})>\theta_r$: that $\theta_r$ is in nats and is similarly tuned (it measures a positive upward jump in entropy).

Why they use different thresholds at training vs inference
The paper explicitly says they set a threshold during preprocessing for training to hit a target average patch size. For the large-scale BLT-Entropy run they then lowered the inference threshold from $0.6$ to $0.1$ to produce more patches at inference time (improves task performance at the cost of additional steps). In other words, lowering $\theta_g$ makes more positions qualify as “high entropy” boundaries, so you get shorter patches / more latent steps and (usually) better accuracy.

Practical caveats mentioned in the paper
The entropy values depend on the entropy model architecture and its context (they train a $100\mathrm{M}$ byte LM, sliding window $512$ by default). Changes to the entropy model size, receptive field, or any context resets (they sometimes reset the entropy context at newlines to avoid “entropy drift”) change the distribution of $H$ and therefore shift the threshold you must pick to get the same patch size. So reproduce the thresholds only when using the same entropy model and context; otherwise re-calibrate with the sweep above.

Summary
The intended entropy is the usual non-negative entropy,
\[
H=-\sum p\log p,
\]
computed with natural logs (nats). The thresholds ($0.6$, $0.1$) are empirical values chosen to yield particular average patch sizes / inference flop trade-offs for the entropy model and data mix used in the paper; to reproduce or change them you should compute entropies over your corpus and pick the threshold that gives your desired patch statistics.
