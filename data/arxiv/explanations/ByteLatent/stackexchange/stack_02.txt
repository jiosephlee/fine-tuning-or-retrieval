### How does entropy-based patching remain incremental at generation time?
Question:
The paper states that patching must be incremental for generation, i.e., it must decide a boundary at position $i$ without seeing future bytes and must satisfy $f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}$. For entropy-based patching, the boundary decision depends on $H(x_i)$ computed by a small byte LM. During generation, is this entropy computed autoregressively using only the generated prefix? Does this require running a separate LM in parallel at every step, and how are the extra FLOPs accounted for? If not, what approximation is used to make the boundary decision online?
Answer:
Short answer: the entropy used for entropy-patching at generation time is computed autoregressively from the prefix (you only use information available up to position $i$). In practice this means you do need a next‑byte predictor at generation time, but the paper uses a deliberately small, cacheable entropy model and a few practical approximations (small receptive field → precomputed/look‑up option, cached states, and an approx‑monotonic test) so the per‑step extra cost is small and either accounted for or absorbed into the local-model cost in their FLOP accounting.

Why this is necessary and how it is done (intuition + technical grounding in the paper)
- The paper enforces incremental patching: the decision at byte position $i$ must depend only on the prefix $x_{<i}$ (they require $f_p(x_{<i}) = f_p(x)_{<i}$). Entropy‑based patching therefore uses an estimate $H(x_i \mid x_{<i})$ computed from a next‑byte distribution $p_e(\cdot \mid x_{<i})$. That distribution must be produced from an autoregressive byte LM evaluated on the prefix, not from any future bytes. This is explicitly stated in Section 2.3 where they define $H(x_i)$ using $p_e(x_i \mid x_{<i})$.
- The entropy model is small by design (Section 3.2: "we use a transformer with $100\text{M}$ parameters, 14 layers, hidden 512, sliding window 512 bytes" as default; ablations explore much smaller sizes). Because it is small it is cheap relative to the large global latent transformer invoked at patch boundaries.
- During training the paper computes patch boundaries in a "lightweight preprocessing step executed during dataloading" (Section 2.3). That avoids running the entropy model repeatedly during training iterations. For generation (inference), however, the model must decide online, so the entropy must be available incrementally from only the prefix.

How the extra work is handled / FLOP accounting
- The paper explicitly discusses FLOP accounting (Section 3.6). Their per‑byte FLOP estimate for BLT sums the costs of the local encoder, local decoder, global latent transformer and cross‑attention blocks. The entropy model cost is not part of the big latent transformer cost, and the paper treats the entropy model as a separate lightweight component: they ablate entropy model size and context (Section 6.1) and remark that when the entropy model has a sufficiently small receptive field it can be encoded into an efficient lookup table (Section 3.2 and 3.6). In practice the entropy model’s extra FLOPs are either (a) negligible compared to the global model, or (b) explicitly considered in ablations that explore different entropy model sizes and contexts.
- Practically, the paper uses several measures to keep the entropy overhead small: the entropy model is small; it has a limited sliding window (e.g. 512 bytes) so its computation is cheap and cacheable; its hidden states can be incrementally cached during generation so you do not recompute from scratch at every byte; and in the extreme small‑context case you can replace the model by a lookup table of conditional entropies.

Online/approximate decision rules used to avoid full per‑step cost
- Approximate monotonicity test. One of the two boundary rules they consider is an approximate monotonic constraint: mark a boundary when $H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$. That only needs the current and previous entropy estimates, so with cached entropy states you can make the decision using a tiny incremental computation. They also note (Section 3.5) that they use this approx‑monotonicity rule and reset the entropy model context on new lines in some large runs to avoid "entropy drift", which is an engineering tweak that reduces pathological long‑patch behavior without looking ahead.
- Threshold tuning at inference. The paper mentions they adjusted the entropy threshold at inference (e.g. from $0.6$ to $0.1$ for BLT‑Entropy) to trade off more/fewer patch boundaries. That shows a practical knob: you can change how aggressively you trigger the big latent transformer without changing the online logic.
- Caching and small receptive field / lookup table. With a limited sliding window you can maintain cached hidden states and update them in $O(1)$ per new byte for the entropy model's forward pass, or in the extreme case precompute entropy values for short contexts into a table (Section 3.2). Both strategies remove the need to run a full transformer from scratch at each byte.

Summary
Entropy patching is incremental: the entropy is computed from the prefix with a separate small autoregressive byte LM. Yes, generation requires evaluating that entropy model online, but the paper mitigates the cost by using a small, cacheable model (or a lookup table when receptive field is tiny), an approximate monotonic test that only needs local entropy differences, and thresholding tricks. The entropy model’s cost is intentionally small relative to the global latent transformer and is evaluated in their ablations and design choices; when quoting FLOPs the paper treats these choices explicitly (they explore entropy model sizes/contexts and note when it can be folded into an efficient lookup).
