### How can a small-context entropy model be turned into a ‘lookup table’?
Question:
Section 3.2 claims that when the receptive field of the entropy model is small enough, it can be encoded into an efficient lookup table. Given the byte alphabet size 256, even a context of length 6 yields $256^6$ states, which is infeasible. What exactly is being looked up? Are contexts hashed into a much smaller table (with collisions), or are you memoizing model outputs over a fixed training corpus? Some detail on the representation and collision/approximation behavior would clarify this claim.
Answer:
Short answer: the paper is not claiming they literally build a full $256^R$ exact table for $R$ bytes; “encode in an efficient lookup table” means that when the entropy LM’s receptive field ($R$) is small enough in practice, the learned mapping from recent context → next-byte distribution (or its entropy) can be implemented as a compact, fast table-like structure rather than running the full transformer each time. In practice you get that compactness by (a) hashing / bucketizing contexts, (b) storing only observed contexts (memoization / cache), and/or (c) quantizing the model outputs (entropy values or class-probability summaries). All of those trade off exactness for huge speed/memory wins, and are exactly the engineering move the paper alludes to.

Why you cannot (and do not) use a raw $256^R$ table
A literal table indexed by the last $R$ bytes would have $256^R$ entries; even $R=6$ is astronomically large ($\approx 2^{48} \approx 281\ \text{trillion}$ entries) so that’s infeasible. The sentence in the paper should be read as “if the model’s receptive field is small enough that the effective context space is practically compressible, you can implement the model as a lookup.” In other words, the feasibility depends on how you compress / coarsen the input-to-entropy map, not on $256^R$ itself.

Practical, commonly used ways to make a lookup-table implementation feasible and what they mean for correctness
1) Hash / bucket contexts (lossy compression). Apply a fast rolling hash (the paper mentions RollPolyHash for $n$-gram embeddings) to the $R$-byte context, modulo the size of a manageable table (for example $100\text{k}$–$500\text{k}$ entries). Store either the entropy or a small conditional distribution per bucket. Collisions merge multiple distinct contexts into the same bucket; the stored value will be the average (or aggregated) entropy/probability for all contexts that fell in that bucket. This is cheap and memory-bounded; the approximation error depends on table size and collision rate. Because patch boundaries only need to separate “low” vs “high” entropy bytes, the thresholding tends to be tolerant of modest hash noise.

2) Cache only seen contexts (memoization). During preprocessing over the training corpus you can cache the entropy for each observed $R$-byte suffix and store only those entries (e.g., a hash map keyed by the raw bytes seen). The number of unique $R$-byte suffixes in real corpora is far smaller than $256^R$, especially for small $R$ and for natural text, so this can be surprisingly compact. For unseen contexts at inference you either (i) run the small transformer on that context, or (ii) fall back to a default/approximate entropy. This memoization gives exact values for observed contexts and only approximates unseen ones.

3) Quantize outputs / store low-dimensional summaries. Instead of storing the full $256$-way distribution, you can store a scalar entropy or a few summary statistics (top-$k$ probabilities, a small softmax centroid, etc.). Scalar entropy is what BLT needs for patching thresholds, so this is extremely compact and robust to approximation. Quantizing entropy (e.g., into $256$ bins) further reduces memory and often suffices for threshold comparisons.

4) Combine techniques. In realistic systems you often combine a hashed/quantized table for the bulk of contexts with a small neural fallback for rare/unseen or high-ambiguity contexts. This hybrid yields fast average-case throughput while preserving accuracy where it matters.

Why these approximations are acceptable for entropy patching
Entropy patching only uses the entropy (or a thresholded decision based on it) to decide patch boundaries. That is a $1$-bit-ish decision per byte (is entropy > threshold?). Small errors in the entropy estimate from hashing/quantization tend to have limited impact: most bytes are clearly low-entropy (very predictable) or clearly high-entropy (word starts, punctuation), and thresholding is robust to small perturbations. Also, the BLT training procedure tunes the entropy threshold to achieve a desired average patch size on the training mix; that tuning absorbs some systematic bias introduced by approximation.

Clues from the paper about what they likely implemented
The paper explicitly says they use a small byte LM with a sliding window (e.g., up to $512$ bytes) and RollPolyHash for $n$-gram embeddings. It also mentions “when the receptive field of the model is small enough, the trained entropy model can be encoded in an efficient lookup table.” That phrasing plus the RollPolyHash and the $n$‑gram hash infrastructure suggests they used hash-based / table-based implementations (and/or a cache over training contexts) rather than a literal $256^R$ table. Also, because BLT only needs entropies for patching (and sometimes relative increases in entropy), storing scalars is enough and very compact.

Check the BLT codebase the authors released. Takeaway intuition
You cannot index every $R$-byte raw context exactly, but entropy patching only needs a compact, robust signal (entropy scalar and threshold decision). Hashing, caching, and quantization let you approximate the small LM with a fast, memory-bounded lookup structure that is good enough to decide patch boundaries in practice.
