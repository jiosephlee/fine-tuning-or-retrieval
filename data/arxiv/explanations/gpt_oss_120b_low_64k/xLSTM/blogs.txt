# From LSTM to xLSTM: Why Reviving Recurrent Networks Matters for Today's Large Language Models  

Large language models (LLMs) have become synonymous with Transformers, but the humble recurrent neural network (RNN) that powers the classic Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) still hides a lot of untapped potential. A recent paper, **xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory**, shows that by addressing three core weaknesses of the original LSTM, we can scale recurrent models to billions of parameters and compete head‑to‑head with state‑of‑the‑art Transformers and State‑Space Models. In this post we walk through the original LSTM, its historical impact, the three fundamental limitations that have kept it from the limelight, and how the new xLSTM architecture tackles each problem.

---

## A Quick Refresher: What Is an LSTM?  

The LSTM was introduced in the early 1990s to solve the *vanishing gradient* problem that plagued vanilla RNNs. Its key ideas are:

* **Constant error carousel** – an additive update to a cell state $c_t$ that preserves gradients over long time horizons.  
* **Gating mechanisms** – three sigmoid‑controlled gates (input $i_t$, forget $f_t$, output $o_t$) that regulate what information enters, stays in, or leaves the memory.

Mathematically, a single LSTM cell works as follows:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t, \\
h_t &= o_t \,\psi(c_t),
\end{aligned}
\]

where $z_t$ is the transformed input, $\psi$ is typically a $\tanh$ squashing function, and each gate is a sigmoid of an affine transformation of the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$.

Because gradients can flow through many time steps unchanged by the multiplicative forget gate, LSTMs quickly became the go‑to architecture for any sequence problem: language modeling, machine translation, speech synthesis, video captioning, and even reinforcement‑learning agents such as AlphaStar. For a decade LSTMs were **the** deep learning workhorse for sequential data.

---

## Why LSTMs Fell Out of Favor  

When Transformers arrived in 2017 with their fully parallel self‑attention, they eclipsed RNNs on large‑scale language tasks. The main reasons were practical rather than theoretical:  

1. **Irreversible storage decisions** – Once an LSTM writes a value into its cell state it cannot later “undo” that decision without overwriting everything else. In tasks like nearest‑neighbor search where newer evidence should replace older memory, classic LSTMs struggle.  
2. **Scalar memory bottleneck** – Each cell holds a single scalar (or a small vector) that must compress all information seen so far. Rare tokens or fine‑grained details get lost because there isn’t enough capacity to store them separately.  
3. **Sequential bottleneck** – The hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence forces strict step‑by‑step processing; you cannot parallelize across time steps. This makes training on long sequences slower and less hardware‑efficient compared to the parallel self‑attention of Transformers.

These three limitations are precisely why the community shifted toward attention‑based models and later toward linear attention or State‑Space Models (SSMs). However, they also suggest *opportunities*: if we could fix these issues while keeping the linear time complexity of recurrent updates, RNNs might make a comeback.

---

## Introducing xLSTM: Extending What Already Works  

The xLSTM paper proposes two orthogonal extensions that directly target the three problems above:

### 1️⃣ Exponential Gating + Normalization  

Instead of restricting gates to sigmoid outputs (which lie in $[0,1]$), xLSTM allows **exponential activations** ($\exp(\cdot)$). This lets the model amplify or attenuate signals much more aggressively:

\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t) \;\text{or}\; \exp(\tilde{f}_t).
\]

Because exponential values can explode, the authors introduce a *normalizer state* $n_t$ that accumulates the product of past forget gates and current input gates:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,\\
n_t &= f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,
\end{aligned}
\qquad
h_t = o_t\, \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
\]

A stabilizer state $m_t$ further prevents overflow by re‑centering the logarithms of $i_t$ and $f_t$. The result is **revisable storage**: later inputs can effectively “overwrite” earlier memories without destroying everything else—a direct fix for the irreversible storage problem.

### 2️⃣ New Memory Structures  

#### • sLSTM (scalar memory with mixing)  
Keeps the classic scalar cell but adds *memory mixing* across multiple heads/cells. The exponential gates make mixing more expressive while preserving the ability to revise stored values.

#### • mLSTM (matrix memory)  
Boosts capacity dramatically by replacing the scalar $c_t$ with a full matrix $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. Updates follow a **covariance rule** reminiscent of associative memories:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\]

where $\mathbf{k}_t$ (key) and $\mathbf{v}_t$ (value) are linear projections of $\mathbf{x}_t$. Retrieval uses a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$, giving

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t}{\max(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\,1)}.
\]

Because $\mathbf{C}_t$ is updated additively and does not involve any learned parameters beyond the key/value/query projections, it acts like an *infinite* high‑dimensional buffer—solving the limited capacity issue while still being trainable end‑to‑end.

Crucially, mLSTM discards hidden‑to‑hidden connections (“memory mixing”), making its recurrence *fully parallelizable*: all time steps can be computed in one large matrix operation akin to FlashAttention for Transformers.

### 3️⃣ Residual xLSTM Blocks  

Both sLSTM and mLSTM are wrapped inside residual blocks that perform either *post* or *pre* up‑projection (similar to Transformer or SSM backbones). Stacking these blocks with pre‑LayerNorm yields deep **xLSTM architectures** that resemble modern LLM stacks but retain linear time/constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length.

---

## How Do These Changes Pay Off?  

Empirical results from the paper paint a clear picture:

| Setting | Model | Validation Perplexity (15 B tokens) |
|---------|-------|------------------------------------|
| Baseline LSTM | – | ∼73 |
| Transformer (GPT‑3 sized) | – | 49 |
| State‑Space Model (Mamba) | – | 45 |
| **xLSTM[7:1]** (mostly mLSTM blocks) | – | **38** |
| **xLSTM[1:0]** (pure sLSTM) | – | **39** |

* The exponential gating alone shrinks perplexity by ≈ 10 points; adding matrix memory gives another ≈ 5–6 point gain.  
* On synthetic tasks requiring *state tracking* (e.g., parity languages), only models with memory mixing—i.e., sLSTM—solve them perfectly, confirming that exponential gating truly enables revisable storage.  
* On associative recall benchmarks with up to 256 key/value pairs, mLSTM matches or outperforms Transformers despite using far fewer parameters for explicit attention.  
* Scaling experiments up to 300 B tokens show that xLSTMs maintain low perplexity even when extrapolating context length from 2 048 to 16 384 tokens—something Transformers struggle with due to quadratic attention cost.

In short, xLSTM delivers **Transformer‑level performance**, **linear scaling**, and **far better long‐context handling**, all while keeping memory usage constant in sequence length.

---

## Why Reviving Recurrent Networks Matters Today  

1. **Hardware Efficiency** – Linear time complexity means less GPU memory pressure for very long sequences; this is especially valuable on edge devices or when serving ultra‐long prompts.  
2. **Better Long‑Range Reasoning** – Because hidden states are updated incrementally rather than recomputed via attention over all previous tokens, xLSTMs naturally retain information across tens of thousands of steps without exploding compute cost.  
3. **New Algorithmic Capabilities** – Exponential gating gives RNNs true *revision* abilities—think of a dynamic knowledge base that can overwrite outdated facts on the fly.  
4. **Complementary Design Space** – Researchers now have three viable large‑scale families—Transformers, SSMs, and recurrent models—with distinct tradeoffs; future systems may combine them for specialized tasks (e.g., RNN cores for streaming data plus attention heads for global reasoning).

---

## Takeaway  

The classic LSTM was revolutionary because it let gradients flow through time; decades later its core ideas still hold immense promise. By equipping LSTMs with exponential gates, a normalizer state, and richer memory structures (scalar mixing or matrix covariance), xLSTM revives recurrent networks as first‑class citizens in the era of massive language models. If you’re building next‑generation LLMs—or simply curious about alternatives to self‑attention—giving xLSTMs a look could be well worth your time.

*Code is open source at* <https://github.com/NX-AI/xlstm>, *so feel free to experiment!*



# Exponential Gating Explained: How xLSTM Learns to Forget and Remember Better  

## Introduction  

Long Short‑Term Memory networks (LSTMs) have been the workhorse of sequence modeling for decades. Their signature “gates” – input, forget, and output – are all sigmoid functions that decide how much new information to write, what to keep, and what to expose at each time step. While this design solved the classic vanishing‑gradient problem, it also introduced a subtle limitation: once a piece of information is stored behind a sigmoid‑scaled gate, revising that decision later becomes difficult.  

The recent **xLSTM** paper tackles this head‑on by replacing (or augmenting) the traditional sigmoid gates with **exponential gates**. In this post we unpack what exponential gating means, why it helps a model *revise* its memories, and how it shines on a concrete task – the nearest‑neighbor search problem.  

---  

## 1. From Sigmoid to Exponential – the math in plain words  

### 1.1 The classic LSTM update  

At its core an LSTM updates its cell state \(c_t\) as  

\[
c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t,
\]

where  

* \(i_t\) (input gate) and \(f_t\) (forget gate) are **sigmoid** values in \([0,1]\).  
* \(z_t\) is the transformed input at time \(t\).  

Because both gates are bounded by 1, the contribution of any past cell value can only be **scaled down**, never amplified. If a piece of information was stored with a modest forget gate value early on, later timesteps can only *decay* it – they cannot “bring it back” with higher strength.

### 1.2 What exponential gating changes  

xLSTM proposes to let one (or both) of these gates be **exponential** instead of sigmoid:

\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad  
f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t) \text{ or } \sigma(\tilde{f}_t).
\]

Here \(\tilde{i}_t\) and \(\tilde{f}_t\) are unrestricted pre‑activations (real numbers). The exponential transforms them into **positive numbers that can grow arbitrarily large**, rather than being capped at 1.  

To keep training stable we also introduce a *normalizer state* \(n_t\):

\[
n_t = f_t \, n_{t-1} + i_t,
\]
\[
\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}, \qquad h_t = o_t\,\tilde{h}_t .
\]

The normalizer accumulates the same gate products that affect the cell state, acting like a dynamic scaling factor that prevents the hidden state from blowing up.

### 1.3 Why “forgetting” now becomes “revising”  

With an exponential forget gate we can set \(f_t > 1\). In the update equation this *increases* the magnitude of the previous cell value:

\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t.
\]

If during processing we discover that an earlier stored value should be emphasized (e.g., a more similar neighbor appears in a search), we simply let the forget gate at that moment be larger than 1. The model can **amplify** previously written information rather than merely letting it decay. The complementary exponential input gate can also write large values when needed.

The normalizer \(n_t\) guarantees that the hidden output remains well‑scaled even when gates explode temporarily, so training stays numerically safe.

---  

## 2. Visualizing the effect: Nearest‑Neighbor Search  

### 2.1 The task  

Imagine a stream of vectors \(\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_T\) arriving one after another. Each vector is associated with a label (its “value”). At the end of the sequence we are asked: *What is the label of the vector most similar to a given query vector?*  

A vanilla LSTM processes the stream sequentially and stores a running “best match” in its cell state using sigmoid gates. Once it has committed to a particular vector, later vectors can only *overwrite* it if they happen to have a higher sigmoid‑scaled write signal; otherwise the old decision sticks.

### 2.2 What goes wrong with sigmoids  

Because sigmoid values never exceed 1, an early high‑confidence write cannot be easily boosted later; if a later vector is *slightly* more similar but receives a smaller gate activation (say 0.7 vs 0.8), the previous decision dominates. Empirically this shows up as higher mean‑squared error for LSTMs on this benchmark (see Figure 1 in the paper).

### 2.3 Exponential gating fixes it  

With exponential gating, when a more similar vector arrives the model can set its forget gate to say \(f_t = e^{2}\approx7.4\). This multiplies the stored cell value by more than seven, effectively “re‑remembering” the newer vector with stronger weight than any prior one. The normalizer simultaneously records this boost, so hidden outputs stay bounded.

In practice xLSTM’s error curve drops dramatically compared to vanilla LSTM: it quickly learns to revise its internal memory whenever a better candidate appears, achieving near‑optimal performance on the nearest‑neighbor task.

---  

## 3. Stabilizing Exponential Gates – The “m” State  

Exponentials can explode, so xLSTM adds an auxiliary stabilizer:

\[
m_t = \max\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\, \log i_t\bigr).
\]

We then rescale gates:

\[
i'_t = \exp(\log i_t - m_t), \qquad
f'_t = \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t).
\]

These transformed gates \(i'_t,f'_t\) are mathematically equivalent to the raw ones (they produce identical forward passes and gradients) but stay within a safe numeric range during back‑propagation. Think of \(m_t\) as tracking the log‑magnitude of everything seen so far and subtracting it off – akin to layer‑norm but applied directly on gate magnitudes.

---  

## 4. Putting Exponential Gating Inside xLSTM Blocks  

xLSTM integrates exponential gating into two families of recurrent cells:

| Variant | Memory shape | Parallelizable? | Main gating change |
|--------|--------------|----------------|--------------------|
| **sLSTM** | scalar cell (\(c_t\)) | No (memory mixing via hidden‑hidden connections) | Exponential input + optional exponential forget |
| **mLSTM** | matrix memory (\(\mathbf{C}_t\)) | Yes (no hidden‑hidden links) | Exponential input + exponential forget; matrix update follows a covariance rule |

Both variants use the normalizer \(n_t\) (scalar for sLSTM, vector for mLSTM) to keep hidden activations stable. When stacked inside residual blocks (the backbone used by modern LLMs), these cells become **xLSTM blocks**, which can be layered deep enough to reach billions of parameters.

---  

## 5. Takeaways for Practitioners  

* **Revision matters** – tasks that require updating an earlier decision (nearest neighbor lookup, dynamic programming over sequences, code execution tracing) benefit from gates that can *grow* beyond 1.  
* **Exponential gating is cheap** – replacing sigmoid with `exp` adds negligible compute; most overhead comes from maintaining the normalizer and stabilizer states, which are simple elementwise ops.  
* **Stability tricks are essential** – without the \(m_t\) stabilizer or normalizer you’ll quickly encounter overflow/underflow in training; these mechanisms preserve gradient flow just like LayerNorm does for activations.  
* **Parallelism trade‑off** – sLSTM offers richer memory mixing but must run sequentially; mLSTM trades mixing for full parallelization across time steps using matrix operations reminiscent of fast weight programmers.

---  

## Conclusion  

Exponential gating gives LSTMs an ability they lacked for three decades: **the power to amplify past memories when new evidence justifies it**. By coupling this with a dynamic normalizer and careful stabilization, xLSTM demonstrates dramatically better revision behavior on synthetic tasks like nearest‑neighbor search and scales up to compete with Transformers on massive language modeling benchmarks.

If you’re building models where *revisiting* earlier decisions is crucial—think retrieval‑augmented generation, long‑range reasoning over code or scientific data—consider swapping in an xLSTM block with exponential gates. The mathematics is simple, the implementation only adds a few extra tensors, and the empirical gains speak for themselves. Happy gating!



# Memory Matters: Scalar vs. Matrix Memories in sLSTM and mLSTM  

*In the original LSTM, each cell stores a single scalar value. The new xLSTM family expands that idea in two directions: a scalar‑based sLSTM that adds exponential gating and a matrix‑based mLSTM that behaves like a fast‑weight associative memory. Below we break down the differences, why the matrix memory helps with rare‑token prediction, and what practical trade‑offs you should keep in mind when choosing between them.*

---  

## Why revisit LSTM memory at all?  

The classic LSTM update  
\[
c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t ,\qquad   
h_t = o_t \,\psi(c_t)
\]  
stores information in a **single scalar** \(c_t\). This design solved the vanishing‑gradient problem but it also imposed three well‑known limits:

1. **Irreversible storage decisions** – once a value is written, the model can’t easily “overwrite” it later.  
2. **Limited capacity** – everything must be compressed into one number per cell, which hurts rare‑token prediction where subtle cues get lost.  
3. **Sequential bottleneck** – hidden‑to‑hidden connections force strictly serial processing.

The xLSTM paper addresses each of these points with two new building blocks:

| Variant | Memory shape | Key idea | Primary benefit |
|---------|--------------|----------|-----------------|
| **sLSTM** | Scalar \(c_t\) (still one number per cell) | Exponential gating + a *normalizer state* that lets the network revise past writes | Enables *memory mixing* and state tracking that vanilla LSTMs lack |
| **mLSTM** | Matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) | Covariance (outer‑product) update → *fast‑weight associative memory* | Vast storage capacity; dramatically improves rare‑token prediction and long‑range recall |

Below we dive into how each works.

---  

## sLSTM: Scalar Memory with Exponential Gating  

### The core change  
Instead of the usual sigmoid gates, sLSTM lets the **input gate** be an exponential function:
\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad 
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{x}_t + r_i h_{t-1}+b_i .
\]
The forget gate can stay sigmoid or also become exponential. Because exponentials can explode, the authors introduce a *stabilizer state* \(m_t\) that rescales \(i_t\) and \(f_t\) on the fly, preserving numerical stability without changing the gradients.

### Normalizer state & revisable storage  
A new auxiliary variable
\[
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t
\]
tracks the cumulative “strength” of past gates. The hidden output is then normalized by this tally:
\[
h_t = o_t \,\frac{c_t}{n_t}.
\]
If a later input is more relevant, its larger \(i_t\) will increase both the numerator (new content) and denominator (total gate mass), effectively *re‑weighting* earlier information. This solves the “cannot revise storage decisions” problem that standard LSTMs suffer from—see the Nearest Neighbour Search experiment in the paper.

### Memory mixing (the hidden‑to‑hidden connection)  
When multiple scalar cells are stacked, sLSTM allows **memory mixing** via learned recurrent matrices \(\mathbf{R}\) that connect hidden states back into each cell’s input and gates. Although this preserves sequential dependence (no full parallelism), it gives the network extra expressive power for tasks that require tracking complex state (e.g., parity or nested brackets).  

---  

## mLSTM: Matrix Memory as a Fast‑Weight Associative Store  

### From key–value pairs to an outer product  

In mLSTM each time step produces a *key* \(\mathbf{k}_t\) and a *value* \(\mathbf{v}_t\). The memory matrix updates by adding their outer product:
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\, \mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^\top .
\]
If we ignore gating (\(f_t=1,i_t=1\)) this is exactly the classic **Bidirectional Associative Memory** rule introduced in the 1970s. The forget gate acts as a decay term; the input gate controls how much of the new outer product is written—mirroring learning‑rate and weight‑decay dynamics in fast‑weight programmers.

### Retrieval via matrix–vector multiplication  

A query vector \(\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + b_q\) reads from memory:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = 
\frac{\mathbf{C}_t \,\mathbf{q}_t}
{\max\bigl(| \mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\bigr)},
\qquad
h_t = o_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t,
\]
where the normalizer state
\[
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t\, \mathbf{k}_t
\]
plays the same role as in sLSTM: it rescales the dot product to avoid division by near‑zero values.

### Why does this help rare tokens?  

Rare tokens often appear only a handful of times during training, so a model needs to *remember* them with high fidelity across long gaps. A scalar cell can only keep a compressed summary; if many different tokens compete for that single slot, information gets blended and rare signals fade.  

A matrix memory, however, stores **pairs of vectors** directly. Each key/value pair occupies its own rank‑one subspace inside \(\mathbf{C}\). When a query aligns with a stored key, the corresponding value pops out with minimal interference from unrelated keys—a property that translates into **higher signal‑to‑noise ratio** for infrequent items. The paper’s Wikitext‑103 experiments confirm this: mLSTM dramatically reduces perplexity on low‑frequency token bins compared with both vanilla LSTM and sLSTM.

### Parallelism advantage  

Because there is no hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence (no mixing), all updates in mLSTM can be expressed as batched matrix multiplications:
- Compute keys/values/queries for every time step in parallel.
- Perform a cumulative scan of \(f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^\top\) using prefix sums.
- Apply one final matrix–vector product for retrieval.

In practice this yields GPU-friendly kernels similar to FlashAttention’s approach—linear time in sequence length and constant memory usage per token.

---  

## Capacity vs. Computational Cost: When to Use Which?  

| Aspect | sLSTM (scalar) | mLSTM (matrix) |
|--------|----------------|----------------|
| **Memory capacity** | Limited to one number per cell; good for short‑term dependencies. | Up to \(d^2\) stored dimensions (outer products); excels at long‑range associative recall. |
| **Rare‑token handling** | Modest improvement via exponential gating; still compresses heavily. | Significant boost; each rare token can retain its own key/value subspace. |
| **Parallelizability** | Requires sequential hidden‑to‐hidden passes → slower on GPUs (≈2× slower than mLSTM). | Fully parallelizable across time steps; overhead dominated by dense matrix ops (≈4× slower than FlashAttention but still linear). |
| **Compute cost per step** | Small (few vector ops). | Higher: \(O(d^2)\) for outer product update & matrix–vector retrieval. |
| **Best suited for** | Tasks needing explicit state tracking or fine‑grained mixing (e.g., formal language grammars, parity). | Tasks where sheer storage matters—large vocabularies, associative recall, rare token prediction, long context language modeling. |

In practice most large‐scale xLSTM models blend both: an architecture like **xLSTM[7:1]** stacks many mLSTM blocks for raw capacity while sprinkling occasional sLSTM blocks to give the network explicit state tracking abilities.

---  

## Takeaway  

The move from a scalar cell to a matrix memory isn’t just a cosmetic change; it fundamentally alters how an RNN stores and retrieves information:

* **Scalar sLSTM** keeps the classic LSTM’s simplicity but augments it with exponential gates and a normalizer so that past decisions can be revised on the fly.
* **Matrix mLSTM** brings fast‐weight associative memory into modern deep learning, allowing billions of parameters to act like an external key–value store while staying fully recurrent and linear in sequence length.

If your application struggles with rare words, long‐range dependencies, or needs an “infinite” associative cache, consider leaning on mLSTM’s matrix memory despite its heavier compute footprint. When you need tight control over state transitions—say for program execution or formal grammar parsing—sprinkle in some sLSTM blocks for their superior mixing capabilities.

The xLSTM paper shows that combining both yields models that match or outperform Transformers on large language modeling benchmarks while retaining linear time/space scaling. So yes, *memory truly matters*, and choosing between scalar and matrix memories gives you another powerful lever to push RNNs back into the spotlight.



# Building xLSTM Blocks: Residual Connections, Up‑Projection, and Scaling to Billions of Parameters  

*In this post we walk through the core building unit of the new **xLSTM** family – the xLSTM block. We explain why residual stacking is crucial, compare the two up‑projection strategies (pre‑ vs. post‑), and show how these design choices let us train recurrent models with **linear‑time** complexity while keeping the expressive power of deep RNNs. All details are grounded in the “Extended Long Short‑Term Memory” paper.*  

---  

## 1. Why a New Block for LSTMs?  

The classic LSTM introduced in the early 1990s solved the vanishing‑gradient problem with a **constant error carousel** and three gates (input, forget, output). Despite decades of success, vanilla LSTMs still suffer from three practical limitations:  

1. **Irreversible storage decisions** – once a value is written to the scalar cell it cannot be easily overwritten later.  
2. **Limited capacity** – a single scalar per cell forces heavy compression, which hurts rare‑token prediction.  
3. **Sequential bottleneck** – hidden‑to‑hidden connections (the “memory mixing” step) prevent parallel computation across timesteps.  

xLSTM answers these problems by (i) using **exponential gating** (which lets gates grow or shrink multiplicatively without saturating), (ii) introducing richer memory structures (a scalar sLSTM and a matrix mLSTM), and (iii) embedding these cells inside **residual blocks with up‑projection**. The result: a recurrent architecture that scales like modern Transformers but retains the state‑tracking capabilities of RNNs.

---  

## 2. The Anatomy of an xLSTM Block  

An xLSTM block is a modular wrapper around either an **sLSTM** or an **mLSTM** cell. The block performs three high‑level operations:

1. **Non‑linear aggregation of past information** – the recurrent cell summarizes everything seen so far.  
2. **Projection to a higher dimension** – we lift the hidden representation into a space where linear separation of histories becomes easier (Cover’s theorem).  
3. **Residual shortcut** – the original hidden state bypasses the non‑linear path and is added back, stabilizing training for very deep stacks.

Visually, an xLSTM block looks like this:

```
input → [Recurrent cell] → Linear ↑ → Nonlinearity → Linear ↓ → + (residual) → output
```

The key design choice lies in **where** we apply the up‑projection (`↑`) and down‑projection (`↓`).  

### 2.1 Post Up‑Projection (sLSTM)  

- The recurrent cell first works **in its native dimensionality** (e.g., 1024).  
- After producing its hidden output `h_t`, we *project up* to a higher dimension `d_h` (`Linear ↑`), apply an activation (e.g., GeLU), then *project down* back to the original size before adding the residual connection.  

Why this order for sLSTM?  
- sLSTM retains **memory mixing** across cells, which is inherently sequential; keeping this mixing in the low‑dimensional space keeps the matrix multiplications cheap.  
- The subsequent high‑dimensional embedding only needs to separate *already mixed* histories, so a post‑up projection suffices.

### 2.2 Pre Up‑Projection (mLSTM)  

- For mLSTM we first *project up* the input before feeding it into the recurrent cell (`Linear ↑ → mLSTM`).  
- The matrix memory (`C_t ∈ ℝ^{d×d}`) lives in this expanded space; after the cell produces its output we *project down* (`Linear ↓`) and add the residual shortcut.  

Why pre‑up for mLSTM?  
- mLSTM’s memory is a full matrix; its capacity grows quadratically with the hidden size `d`. By moving to a larger space we give the covariance update rule more “room” to store distinct key–value pairs without hitting saturation early.  
- Because mLSTM has **no hidden‑to‑hidden mixing**, it can be parallelised across timesteps (the recurrence reduces to simple matrix scans). Placing the up‑projection before the recurrence preserves this parallelism.

---  

## 3. Residual Stacking – From One Block to Billions of Parameters  

A single xLSTM block is powerful, but deep language models need dozens to hundreds of layers. The solution is straightforward: **stack blocks using pre‑LayerNorm residual connections**, exactly as modern Transformers do.

### 3.1 Pre‑LayerNorm Residual Path  

Each block follows the pattern

```
y = LayerNorm(x)
z = xLSTMBlock(y)
output = x + z
```

*Why LayerNorm first?*  
- Normalising before any non‑linear operation stabilises gradients across very deep stacks, preventing the explosion or vanishing that plagued early deep RNNs.  
- It also makes it easy to share hyperparameters (learning rate, weight decay) across all layers.

### 3.2 Mixing sLSTM and mLSTM Blocks  

The paper introduces a notation `xLSTM[a:b]` meaning “a parts mLSTM, b parts sLSTM”. For example, `xLSTM[7:1]` uses seven mLSTM blocks and one sLSTM block per 8‐layer segment. Empirically:

- **mLSTM dominates** because its matrix memory gives huge capacity while being parallelizable.
- A few **sLSTM blocks** improve state tracking by allowing memory mixing—useful for tasks that require explicit sequence state (e.g., code evaluation).

When scaling to billions of parameters, you typically keep a constant ratio (e.g., 7:1) and simply increase:
- Hidden dimension `d` (which enlarges both scalar and matrix memories),  
- Number of blocks `N` (deepening the stack), or  
- Model width via multi‑head configurations.

All three knobs increase parameter count roughly linearly while preserving *linear* training time with respect to sequence length, because each timestep still involves only one pass through the recurrent update plus two cheap linear projections.

---  

## 4. Linear‑Time Training Meets Expressive Recurrence  

Traditional RNNs already have linear time complexity (`O(T)` for sequence length `T`). What changes with xLSTM?  

| Component | Complexity w.r.t `T` | Comments |
|-----------|----------------------|----------|
| Recurrent update (`c_t`, `C_t`) | `O(T·d)` for sLSTM; `O(T·d²)` for mLSTM | Matrix ops are highly parallel on GPUs, keeping wall time low |
| Up/Down projection | `O(T·d·d_h)` | Done once per layer; `d_h` is chosen moderately larger than `d` |
| Residual addition & LayerNorm | `O(T·d)` | Negligible overhead |

Because every operation is a *scan* or a *batched matrix multiply*, GPUs can pipeline many timesteps together, yielding throughput comparable to linear Transformers such as GLA or RetNet while retaining true recurrence (no cache of past key/value pairs needed).  

Furthermore, exponential gating eliminates sigmoid saturation, so gradients flow without attenuation even over very long sequences—critical when training on billions of tokens.

---  

## 5. Putting It All Together – A Minimal Code Sketch  

Below is a PyTorch‑style pseudo implementation that captures the core ideas:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class XLSTMSBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model, d_hidden, kind='m'):   # kind='s' or 'm'
        super().__init__()
        self.norm = nn.LayerNorm(d_model)
        self.kind = kind

        if kind == 's':                     # scalar sLSTM
            self.rnn = ScalarSLSTMCell(d_model)
            self.up   = nn.Linear(d_model, d_hidden)
            self.down = nn.Linear(d_hidden, d_model)
        else:                               # matrix mLSTM
            self.up   = nn.Linear(d_model, d_hidden)
            self.rnn = MatrixMLSTMCell(d_hidden)   # operates in high dim
            self.down = nn.Linear(d_hidden, d_model)

    def forward(self, x):
        y = self.norm(x)                     # pre‑LayerNorm
        y = self.up(y)                       # pre or post proj handled by ctor
        h = self.rnn(y)                      # recurrent step (scan internally)
        h = self.down(h)
        return x + h                         # residual add
```

A full model stacks many such blocks:

```python
class XLSTMLanguageModel(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vocab_sz, d_model=1024,
                 n_blocks=48,
                 ratio_m=7):                # e.g., 7:1 m:s ratio
        super().__init__()
        self.embed = nn.Embedding(vocab_sz, d_model)
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList()
        for i in range(n_blocks):
            kind = 'm' if i % (ratio_m+1) != ratio_m else 's'
            self.blocks.append(XLSTMSBlock(d_model,
                                           d_hidden=2048,
                                           kind=kind))
        self.out = nn.Linear(d_model, vocab_sz)

    def forward(self, ids):
        h = self.embed(ids)
        for blk in self.blocks:
            h = blk(h)
        return self.out(h)
```

Training proceeds exactly like any autoregressive model: feed token IDs, compute cross‑entropy loss on next token predictions, and use AdamW with learning‑rate schedules common to LLM training.

## 6. Scaling Outlook – From Hundreds of Millions to Billions  

Empirical results from the xLSTM paper show:

| Tokens | Params | Validation PPL |
|--------|--------|----------------|
| 15 B   | 350 M | ~8.9 (best among Transformers/SSMs) |
| 300 B  | 1.3 B| ~6.3 (outperforms Llama 13 B and Mamba 1 B) |

Key observations:

- **Exponential gating + matrix memory** give consistent PPL gains across all scales.
- Even with modest hardware (single A100 GPU), training remains feasible because memory usage grows *constant* with sequence length.
- For inference, xLSTM’s linear scaling means generation time grows linearly with context size—unlike quadratic growth in vanilla Transformers—making it especially attractive for long prompts (>8k tokens).

---  

## 7. Takeaways  

1️⃣ **Residual stacking + up/down projection** gives deep recurrent networks stable gradients and high expressive power—mirroring what made Transformers so successful.

2️⃣ **Pre vs. post up‑projection** aligns with each variant’s strengths: keep sLSTM’s mixing cheap by projecting after recurrence; give mLSTM’s matrix memory room by projecting before recurrence.

3️⃣ **Linear-time training & inference** are retained despite adding sophisticated gating and memory structures; modern GPUs can handle the extra matrix operations efficiently.

4️⃣ When scaled to billions of parameters, xLSTM matches or exceeds state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models on language modeling benchmarks while preserving true recurrence—opening doors for tasks that demand explicit state tracking such as code execution or long‐range reasoning.

# Benchmarks & Beyond: How xLSTM Stacks Up Against Transformers, SSMs, and Modern RNNs

In the past two decades, Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) networks have been the workhorse of sequence modeling. Yet, since 2017 Transformers have dominated language modeling, and more recently State‑Space Models (SSMs) and linear‑attention RNNs have entered the arena. The new **xLSTM** family—built on exponential gating, scalar‑mixing sLSTM, and matrix‑memory mLSTM—asks a simple question: *What happens if we bring LSTMs up to the scale of today’s large language models (LLMs) while fixing their classic shortcomings?*  

Below we walk through the extensive empirical suite presented in the xLSTM paper, translating perplexities, accuracy numbers, and scaling‑law plots into practical advice for practitioners who are deciding which architecture to try next.

---

## 1. From Toy Problems to Real‑World Text

The authors evaluate xLSTM on four increasingly realistic benchmarks:

| Benchmark | What it tests | Why it matters |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| **Synthetic formal‑language tasks** | State tracking / memory revision (e.g., parity, nested parentheses) | Highlights the ability to *revise* stored information—something vanilla LSTMs struggle with. |
| **Multi‑Query Associative Recall** | Storing and retrieving many key–value pairs (up to 256 pairs, sequence length 2048) | Directly probes *storage capacity* of the model’s memory. |
| **Long‑Range Arena (LRA)** | Diverse long‑sequence tasks (list ops, image classification from pixels, etc.) | Measures how well a model scales with context length. |
| **Large‑scale language modeling** | Autoregressive training on 15 B and 300 B tokens from SlimPajama; evaluation on validation perplexity, downstream reasoning tasks, and the PALOMA benchmark (571 domains). | The ultimate test: does the model compete with state‑of‑the‑art LLMs? |

---

## 2. Synthetic Formal Languages – Do Exponential Gates Help?

### The problem
Classic LSTMs have a hard time *revising* a decision once a token is stored (think of a nearest‑neighbor search where a later vector is more similar). The paper uses a suite of formal languages that require such revisions.

### Results at a glance
- **xLSTM[0:1]** (pure sLSTM) and **xLSTM[1:0]** (pure mLSTM) both achieve near‑perfect accuracy (> 0.95) on parity and copy tasks.
- Competing Transformers, SSMs (e.g., Mamba), and even vanilla RNNs hover around 0.6–0.7 accuracy—far from solving the task.
- Adding *memory mixing* (the hidden‑to‑hidden connections in sLSTM) is crucial; models without it cannot track state.

### Takeaway
If your application needs to constantly overwrite or refine internal state—think online recommendation systems or incremental code analysis—xLSTM’s exponential gating gives you a solid advantage over both Transformer‑style attention and traditional RNNs.

---

## 3. Associative Recall – How Much Can You Remember?

### The setup
Each sequence contains many random key–value pairs; later a query asks for the value belonging to a specific key. The task is scaled up to 256 pairs with sequences up to 2048 tokens.

### Head‑to‑head numbers  
(Accuracy reported for a 2‑block architecture; higher is better)

| Model                | 64 pairs | 128 pairs | 256 pairs |
|----------------------|----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Transformer (Llama)** | 0.98     | 0.95      | 0.88 |
| **xLSTM[1:1]** (mixed)   | **0.99** | **0.97**   | **0.93** |
| **xLSTM[1:0]** (mLSTM only) | 0.98     | 0.96      | 0.91 |
| RWKV‑5 / RWKV‑6      | 0.94     | 0.89      | 0.80 |
| Mamba                | 0.93     | 0.86      | 0.74 |

### What’s happening?
- The matrix memory of mLSTM implements a *covariance update rule* (essentially an outer‑product fast weight), which dramatically expands storage capacity without blowing up parameters.
- Surprisingly, adding an sLSTM block on top (**xLSTM[1:1]**) improves performance even on the hardest setting, indicating that scalar mixing helps organize the matrix memory.

### Takeaway
When you need high‐capacity associative memory—e.g., retrieval‑augmented generation or large knowledge bases—xLSTM’s matrix memory beats most modern RNNs and even rivals Transformers while keeping linear time complexity in sequence length.

---

## 4. Long‑Range Arena – Scaling to Long Contexts

The authors evaluate xLSTM on all six LRA tasks (ListOps, byte-level text classification, etc.). Across the board:

- **xLSTM** matches or slightly exceeds the best SSM baseline.
- Performance degrades gracefully as context length grows to 4096 tokens.
- Unlike vanilla Transformers whose quadratic cost forces severe truncation, xLSTM maintains constant memory usage.

**Practical implication:** For workloads where you cannot afford to truncate inputs—long documents, DNA sequences, or video streams—xLSTM provides linear‐time inference while staying competitive in accuracy.

---

## 5. Large‑Scale Language Modeling – Real LLM Competition

### Training regimes
- **15 B token run:** Models roughly comparable in size to GPT‑3 “small” (≈ 350 M parameters).  
- **300 B token run:** Four scales (125 M → 1.3 B parameters), matching recent LLM training budgets.

All models share the same pre‑LayerNorm residual backbone; only the recurrent core varies.

### Validation perplexity (15 B tokens)

| Model                     | Validation ppl |
|---------------------------|----------------|
| GPT‑3 (Transformer)       | 13.4 |
| LLaMA                     | 12.9 |
| Mamba (SSM)               | 12.7 |

| **xLSTM[7:1]**            | **12.3** |
| **xLSTM[1:0]**            | **12.2** |

*Lower perplexity = better predictive power.*

### Scaling law curves
When plotting loss vs. compute (`tokens × parameters`), all families follow a clear power law,
\[
\text{Loss} = A \cdot (\text{Compute})^{-\alpha} + B,
\]
with:
- Similar exponent \(\alpha \approx 0.07\) across models,
- **xLSTM’s offset \(A\)** being ~10–15 % lower than Transformers and ~5–8 % lower than SSMs.

Thus for larger models (10+ B parameters), xLSTM is expected to stay ahead by roughly the same margin.

### Sequence length extrapolation (1.3 B models)
All models trained at context = 2048 tokens:
- Transformers’ perplexity climbs sharply beyond ≤ 4096 tokens.
- Mamba shows moderate increase.
- **xLSTM retains near‑training perplexity up to ≈ 16k tokens**, confirming linear scaling of compute and memory.

### Downstream reasoning tasks
Across five common-sense benchmarks (ARC, BoolQ, HellaSwag, etc.):

- xLSTM wins on *four* of five tasks for every model size.
- Only ARC sometimes favors Mamba at the largest scale.

### PALOMA benchmark (571 domains)
For each domain we compute per-token perplexity:

- xLSTM[1:0] beats Mamba in **568/571** domains (~99.5 %).
- Beats LLaMA in **486/571** domains (~85 %).
- Beats RWKV‑4 in **570/571** domains (~99.8 %).

The improvement is especially pronounced on low‑resource or highly specialized domains where long context matters.

### Inference speed & throughput
Because xLSTM’s recurrence is linear in sequence length:

- Generation time grows linearly like Mamba/RWKV but *much slower* than quadratic Transformers.
- At batch size 1 and prefill = 16 tokens, xLSTM achieves higher throughput than LLaMA even when LLaMA uses an optimized KV cache.
- Larger batch sizes are feasible without exhausting GPU memory—a key advantage for serving many concurrent requests.

## 6. What Should Practitioners Take Away?

1️⃣ **If you need long context + constant memory**, choose xLSTM over vanilla Transformers or SSMs—especially for tasks such as legal document analysis or scientific paper summarization.

2️⃣ **When high storage capacity is critical**, rely on the mLSTM variant with its matrix memory; it outperforms fast-weight baselines like RWKV while remaining linear in time.

3️⃣ **For state tracking / revision problems**, incorporate sLSTM blocks with exponential gating; they solve formal languages that other modern architectures cannot.

4️⃣ **Scaling confidence:** The scaling-law analysis shows that xLSTM’s advantage persists as you increase data and parameters; you can expect similar relative gains at multi‑billion parameter scales.

5️⃣ **Implementation notes:**  
   - sLSTM cannot be fully parallelized due to hidden–hidden mixing; however, a custom CUDA kernel makes it only ~2× slower than fully parallel mLSTM.  
   - mLSTM’s matrix ops are still heavier than FlashAttention but can be accelerated with optimized kernels; current overhead is modest compared to overall training time.

## 7. Bottom Line

The experimental suite paints a consistent picture: ***xLSTM matches or exceeds Transformer and State‑Space baselines across synthetic reasoning tasks, associative recall, long-range benchmarks, and full-scale language modeling***—all while preserving linear time/memory complexity.

For anyone building next-generation LLMs—or even domain-specific models where context length and memory revision are decisive—xLSTM offers a compelling alternative that blends the classic strengths of recurrent networks with modern scaling tricks borrowed from Transformers.

# Practical Tips for Training xLSTM Models on Real Data

In the past year the **xLSTM** family (sLSTM + mLSTM) has emerged as a strong alternative to Transformers and State‑Space Models for large‑scale language modeling. The paper *xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory* shows that, once equipped with exponential gating, matrix memories and residual backbones, xLSTMs match or beat state‑of‑the‑art baselines even at the 1 B‑parameter scale.  

However, moving from a research prototype to a production‑grade model that processes billions of tokens uncovers a set of engineering hurdles. Below we distill the most useful lessons from the paper’s “Limitations” section, organize them into concrete recommendations, and add a few practical tricks that have proven helpful when training xLSTMs beyond 1 B parameters.

---

## 1. CUDA Kernels – Speed vs. Parallelism

### 1.1 mLSTM: Parallel but Heavy

* **What hurts speed?**  
  mLSTM stores a full matrix $\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and updates it with a rank‑1 outer product $\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top$ each timestep. Although the operation can be expressed as standard matrix multiplications (hence parallelizable), the naïve kernels used in the reference implementation are roughly **4× slower** than highly tuned attention kernels such as FlashAttention.

* **How to mitigate?**  
  - **Fuse the update and decay:** Compute $f_t\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top$ in a single GEMM+AXPY kernel rather than two separate calls.  
  - **Leverage cuBLASLt / cuTensorCore:** When $d$ is a multiple of 8 or 16, enable tensor‑core math (`torch.cuda.amp.autocast`) to accelerate the $d\times d$ multiply.  
  - **Batch multiple timesteps:** For long sequences you can process small windows (e.g., 8–16 steps) with a custom scan kernel; this reduces kernel launch overhead while preserving the linear recurrence.

### 1.2 sLSTM: Memory Mixing Costs

* **Why is sLSTM slower?**  
  The hidden‑hidden connections required for memory mixing prevent straightforward parallelization; every step depends on the previous hidden state.

* **Practical workaround:**  
  The authors provide a fast CUDA implementation that keeps most data in registers and uses an in‑place scan (`torch.cumsum`). In practice this implementation is **≤ 2×** slower than mLSTM, which is acceptable for models where the scalar memory is crucial (e.g., tasks that need explicit state tracking).  
  - Profile with NVIDIA Nsight Systems to ensure the kernel stays compute‑bound rather than memory‑bound.  
  - If your application can tolerate losing explicit mixing, consider swapping sLSTM blocks for mLSTM blocks after a certain depth (the paper’s xLSTM[7:1] configuration does exactly that).

---

## 2. Gate Initialization – Stabilizing Exponential Gates

Exponential gating (`i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)`, `f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)`) dramatically increases expressivity but also introduces risk of overflow or vanishing gradients.

### Recommended Initialization Scheme

| Gate | Bias Initialization | Reason |
|------|--------------------|--------|
| Input gate $i_t$ | $\log(0.1)$ (≈ −2.3) | Starts with a small learning rate on new information; the stabilizer state `m_t` will quickly adapt if stronger updates are needed. |
| Forget gate $f_t$ | $\log(0.9)$ (≈ −0.1) if using exponential forget; otherwise sigmoid bias = +1 (≈ 0.73) | Guarantees gentle decay at the beginning of training, preventing runaway growth of $\mathbf{C}$ or scalar memory. |
| Output gate $o_t$ | $0$ (sigmoid bias = 0 → $o_t\approx0.5$) | Keeps hidden activations moderate until later layers learn useful gating patterns.

### Stabilizer State (`m_t`)

The paper introduces a stabilizer:
```python
m_t = max(log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, log(i_t))
i'_t = exp(log(i_t) - m_t)
f'_t = exp(log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)
```
Implement this directly in your forward pass (no extra parameters). It rescales both gates so that their product never exceeds numerical limits, while preserving exact gradients.

---

## 3. Managing Matrix Memory Size

The matrix memory dimension `d` determines both capacity and computational cost:

* **Memory capacity vs. compute:**  
  For language modeling, `d = √(hidden_dim)` works well (e.g., hidden size = 2048 ⇒ `d≈45`). This keeps $\mathbf{C}$ manageable (< 2 kB per layer) while still enabling rich key/value associations.

* **Scaling beyond 1 B parameters:**  
  - **Chunked memory:** Split $\mathbf{C}$ into several smaller blocks (e.g., four $d/2 \times d/2$ matrices) and treat each as an independent head. This reduces per‑kernel load and improves cache locality.
  - **Sparse updates:** If your hardware supports sparse matrix ops, apply dropout to outer‑product updates (`v_t k_t^T`) so only a fraction of entries are written each step; this leaves the “signal” intact while cutting FLOPs roughly proportionally to dropout rate.

* **Preventing overflow for long contexts:**  
  When processing sequences longer than those seen during training (e.g., > 16k tokens), monitor `||\mathbf{C}_t||_F`. If it grows beyond a preset bound (e.g., `10^4`), rescale both $\mathbf{C}_t$ and the normalizer $\mathbf{n}_t$ by a constant factor (`*0.5`) to keep retrieval numerically stable.

---

## 4. Mixed‑Precision Pitfalls

The authors trained all models in mixed precision but warned about instabilities for certain RNN variants:

* **Critical sections:**  
  - The stabilizer state `m_t` must be kept in **float32**; otherwise rounding errors can destroy the max operation and break gate scaling.
  - The matrix memory $\mathbf{C}$ can safely be stored in FP16 because it is only used inside GEMM kernels that internally up‑cast to FP32 on NVIDIA GPUs.

* **Loss scaling strategy:**  
  Use PyTorch’s native `torch.cuda.amp.autocast` combined with a static loss scale of `2^{15}` for early epochs, then switch to dynamic scaling once gradients stabilize.

* **Gradient clipping:**  
  RNNs are notorious for exploding gradients despite exponential gating’s stabilizer, so apply global norm clipping at `0.5`–`1.0`. This works well with AMP because clipping happens after loss scaling is removed.

---

## 5. Hyperparameter Tuning at Scale

Below are empirically validated defaults for models ≥ 1 B parameters; start from them before embarking on grid searches.

| Hyperparameter | Suggested Value | Why |
|----------------|-----------------|-----|
| Learning rate (AdamW) | $3\times10^{-4}$ → cosine decay | Larger LSTMs benefit from an initially higher LR because gating adapts quickly; cosine decay prevents overshooting later stages |
| Weight decay | $0.01$ | Regularizes both linear layers and projection matrices |
| Warmup steps | `$0.01 \times \text{total steps}` | Gives gates time to settle before aggressive updates |
| Dropout (attention & residual) | $0.1$–$0.15$ | Controls overfitting on long‑range dependencies |
| Number of heads (mLSTM) | `$d // head_dim`` where `head_dim = 64` | Keeps each head’s key/value vectors within a manageable size for efficient GEMM |
| Block composition (`xLSTM[a:b]`) | `7:1` for deep models (> 12 layers), `3:1` for shallow prototypes | More mLSTM blocks bring parallelism; a few sLSTM blocks preserve state‑tracking abilities |

**Tuning tip:** Increase the proportion of sLSTM blocks only when you observe systematic failures on “state tracking” synthetic tasks (e.g., parity or nearest‑neighbor search). Otherwise keep sLSTM to ≤ 10 % of total blocks to retain high throughput.

---

## 6️⃣ End‑to‑End Checklist

Before launching a multi‑billion token run:

1️⃣ Verify CUDA kernels: run a short benchmark (`torch.backends.cuda.enable_cudnn = True`) and confirm that mLSTM throughput is within ~75 % of FlashAttention on an equivalent tensor shape.

2️⃣ Initialize gates according to Table 2 and enable stabilizer logic.

3️⃣ Set matrix memory dimension `d` so that `$d^2 \leq$ ~10⁶` elements per layer (≈4 MiB at FP16).  

4️⃣ Enable AMP with FP32 stabilizer & FP16 memory; add gradient clipping (`max_norm=0.75`).  

5️⃣ Choose block composition `xLSTM[7:1]` for > 12 layers, otherwise start with `xLSTM[3:1]`.

6️⃣ Run a sanity check on a synthetic “Nearest Neighbor Search” task – make sure MSE drops below the baseline LSTM after ~10k steps.

7️⃣ Launch full‐scale training with cosine LR schedule, monitor both validation perplexity and the Frobenius norm of each $\mathbf{C}$ matrix; apply rescaling if norms exceed thresholds.

---

Training xLSTMs at scale is no longer an academic curiosity; with careful engineering they achieve competitive perplexities while retaining linear time/memory cost—an attractive proposition for edge deployment or any scenario where GPU memory is at a premium. By following these practical guidelines—optimizing CUDA kernels, initializing exponential gates safely, handling matrix memories efficiently, leveraging mixed precision wisely, and tuning hyperparameters deliberately—you can tame the complexity of xLSTMs and unlock their expressive power in real‑world language modeling projects.
