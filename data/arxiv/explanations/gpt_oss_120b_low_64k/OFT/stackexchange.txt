\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does hyperspherical energy differ from Euclidean distance in measuring model changes?
Question:
The paper argues that Euclidean distance between the finetuned and pretrained weights is insufficient to capture semantic preservation, and proposes hyperspherical energy (HE) as a better measure. I am unclear about what exactly HE quantifies: is it just the sum of pairwise cosine similarities among normalized neurons, or does it capture higher-order structure? How does preserving HE guarantee that the semantic generation ability is retained, and can you provide an intuitive example contrasting a small Euclidean change with a large HE change?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper’s “hyperspherical energy” (HE) is a *global* statistic of a weight matrix that summarises how the **directions** of its neurons are distributed on the unit sphere.  
For a layer with weight vectors $\mathbf w_{1},\dots ,\mathbf w_{n}\in\mathbb R^{d}$ we first normalise each neuron,
\[
\hat{\mathbf w}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf w_{i}}{\|\mathbf w_{i}\|}\,,
\]
so that every $\hat{\mathbf w}_{i}$ lies on the unit hypersphere $S^{d-1}$.  
The hyperspherical energy is then defined as  

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf w}_{j}\bigr\|^{-1}
          =\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-\cos\theta_{ij})}} ,
\]

where $\theta_{ij}= \arccos(\hat{\mathbf w}_{i}^{\!\top}\hat{\mathbf w}_{j})$ is the angle between the two neurons.  

Thus HE is *exactly* the sum of pair‑wise similarities (or, equivalently, inverse distances) **after normalisation**.  It does not involve higher‑order terms such as triples or quadruples; however, because it aggregates *all* pairwise cosine similarities, it encodes the overall geometry of the whole set of neurons.  If many pairs become more aligned, HE grows; if the set becomes more uniformly spread, HE shrinks.

---

### Why preserving HE matters for semantic generation

Empirically, angular relationships among neurons have been shown to carry most of the semantic information in deep networks (e.g. SphereNet, decoupled‑weight works).  In a diffusion model the forward pass of a layer is  

\[
\mathbf z = \mathbf W^{\!\top}\!\mathbf x .
\]

If we replace $\mathbf W^{0}$ (the pretrained matrix) by an *orthogonal* transform $\mathbf R$ acting on every column,
\[
\mathbf W = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0},
\qquad \mathbf R^{\!\top}\!\mathbf R = \mathbf I ,
\]
then each normalised neuron simply rotates (or reflects) as a whole:

\[
\widehat{\mathbf w}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf R\,\mathbf w^{0}_{i}}{\|\mathbf R\,\mathbf w^{0}_{i}\|}
                      = \frac{\mathbf R\,\mathbf w^{0}_{i}}{\|\mathbf w^{0}_{i}\|}
                      = \mathbf R\,\widehat{\mathbf w}^{0}_{i}.
\]

Because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products,  

\[
\cos(\theta_{ij}) = (\widehat{\mathbf w}_{i})^{\!\top}\widehat{\mathbf w}_{j}
                  = (\widehat{\mathbf w}^{0}_{i})^{\!\top}\widehat{\mathbf w}^{0}_{j},
\]

the pair‑wise angles—and consequently HE—remain **exactly unchanged**.  In other words, the “relational structure’’ of the neurons that encodes how different features interact is kept intact.

Why does that help generation?  
Diffusion models learn to map noisy latents to images by repeatedly applying linear transforms followed by non‑linearities.  If the relative geometry of those linear transforms stays the same, the *semantic manifold* learned during pre‑training is preserved; only the global orientation of the manifold can shift.  Changing orientation is enough to bias the model toward a new subject or a control signal (the paper’s toy auto‑encoder experiment shows that angular information alone can reconstruct images), yet it does not tear apart the pretrained knowledge that underlies high‑quality, diverse synthesis.

---

### Small Euclidean change vs. large HE change – an intuition

Consider a single‑layer weight matrix $\mathbf W^{0}\in \mathbb R^{d\times n}$.  

* **Small Euclidean perturbation:** add a tiny random matrix $\Delta$ with $\|\Delta\|_{F}=10^{-3}$.  The Frobenius norm of the whole weight matrix changes only a little, so Euclidean distance between pretrained and finetuned weights is tiny.  However, because each column can be shifted in an arbitrary direction, many angles $\theta_{ij}$ may change noticeably; consequently HE may increase substantially.  The model could therefore lose part of its pretrained relational structure even though the Euclidean move was tiny.

* **Large orthogonal rotation:** take an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ that rotates every neuron by $30^\circ$.  The Frobenius distance $\|\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}-\mathbf W^{0}\|_{F}$ can be relatively large (on the order of $\sqrt{2(1-\cos30^\circ)}\,\|\mathbf W^{0}\|_{F}$), but because all columns are rotated **together**, every pairwise angle stays exactly the same and HE is unchanged.  The semantic knowledge encoded in those angles is preserved; only the global orientation changes, which is precisely what we want for tasks such as subject‑driven generation.

Thus Euclidean distance measures *how far* individual weights move in absolute space, while hyperspherical energy measures *how much* their mutual geometry – the pattern of angles – is altered.  Preserving HE guarantees that the relational structure learned during pre‑training survives finetuning, which empirical results in the paper show correlates with better preservation of generation quality.

---

### Summary

* **HE =** sum over all pairwise inverse chord distances of *normalised* weight vectors ⇒ sum of cosine‑based similarities.
* It captures **global relational geometry** (pairwise angles) but not higher‑order interactions beyond pairwise.
* An orthogonal transform leaves every inner product unchanged, so HE stays exactly constant.
* Keeping HE constant therefore protects the semantic manifold learned by a diffusion model while still allowing enough flexibility (a global rotation/scaling) to adapt to a downstream task.
* A small Euclidean move can dramatically disturb angles (large HE change), whereas a large orthogonal rotation can keep HE perfectly stable—illustrating why Euclidean distance alone is an inadequate proxy for semantic preservation.

### Why it holds for *any* orthogonal \(R$

The proof above relied only on two algebraic properties of orthogonal matrices:

* **Norm preservation** – \(‖R v‖=‖v‖.\)  
* **Isometry** – distances are unchanged under a common left‑multiplication: \(‖R a - R b‖ = ‖a-b‖.\)

Both properties are satisfied by every matrix whose transpose equals its inverse, i.e., by every element of the orthogonal group \(O(d)\). No further structure (determinant = 1, block‑diagonal form, etc.) is needed for the invariance; those extra constraints are introduced only for parameter‑efficiency, not for correctness of the distance preservation.

Because hyperspherical energy is a sum of a (monotonically decreasing) function of these distances—typically \(‖·‖^{-1}\)—the whole energy remains exactly the same:

```latex
HE(R W^0) 
  =

    ‖\,w_i^0/‖w_i^0‖-w_j^0/‖w_j^0‖‖^{-1}}
  =
  HE(W^0).
```

Therefore, as stated in Section 3 of the paper, any layer‑shared orthogonal transformation leaves hyperspherical energy untouched, which is why Orthogonal Finetuning can modify a model while guaranteeing that this structural measure of the pretrained network is preserved.

### What is the role of the Cayley transform and why does it restrict \(\det(\mathbf{R})=1\)?
Question:
The paper uses Cayley parameterization \(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with a skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\). I understand that this yields an orthogonal matrix, but why does it only generate matrices with determinant +1 (the special orthogonal group)? Does this limitation affect the ability to represent reflections, and if so, why do the authors claim it does not harm performance?
Answer:
The Cay‑Cayley (or simply *Cayley*) transform is a classic way of turning a **skew‑symmetric** matrix  

\[
\mathbf Q\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},\qquad \mathbf Q^{\!\top}=-\mathbf Q
\]

into an **orthogonal** matrix  

\[
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}.
\]

Because the denominator \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)\) must be invertible, the map is defined only when \(-1\) is **not** an eigenvalue of \(\mathbf R\).  This restriction has a concrete algebraic consequence: the determinant of every matrix obtained from the Cayley transform is **always \(+1\)**, i.e. the image of the map lies in the *special orthogonal group*  

\[
\operatorname{SO}(d)=\{\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\mid \mathbf R^{\!\top}\mathbf R=\mathbf I,\; \det \mathbf R=1\}.
\]

---

### Why does the determinant become \(+1\)?

Let \(\lambda\) be any (possibly complex) eigenvalue of the skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf Q\).  
Skew‑symmetry implies that the eigenvalues are purely imaginary and come in conjugate pairs:
\( \lambda = \pm i\,\mu \) with \(\mu\in \mathbb R\).  
If we apply the Cayley formula to such an eigenvalue we obtain the corresponding eigenvalue of \(\mathbf R\):

\[
\rho = \frac{1+\lambda}{\,1-\lambda\,}
      = \frac{1+i\mu}{\,1-i\mu\,}
      = \frac{(1+i\mu)^2}{1+\mu^2}.
\]

Since \(|1+i\mu|^2 = 1+\mu^2\), we have \(|\rho|=1\); every eigenvalue of \(\mathbf R\) lies on the unit circle in the complex plane.  Moreover,
the product of all eigenvalues equals

\[
\det(\mathbf R)=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\rho_k
               = \prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1+\lambda_k}{1-\lambda_k}
               = \frac{\det(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)}{\det(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)}.
\]

Because the characteristic polynomial of a real skew‑symmetric matrix is an even polynomial,
the determinants in numerator and denominator are equal, giving  

\[
\det(\mathbf R)=+1 .
\]

The only orthogonal matrices that cannot be written in this way are those that possess an eigenvalue equal to \(-1\); those are precisely the matrices whose determinant is \(-1\) (reflections or improper rotations).

---

### What does this mean for reflections?

A *reflection* (or any orthogonal matrix with \(\det=-1)\) can be written as a product of a rotation and a single Householder‑type flip:

```
R_reflection = H · R_rotation,
```

where \(H=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,\dots ,1)\) has determinant \(-1\).  
When we restrict ourselves to matrices generated by the Cayley transform we simply lose the ability to *explicitly* insert that final flip inside the same parameterisation.

In a neural network, however, this loss is harmless for two reasons.

* **Sign ambiguity can be absorbed elsewhere.**  
  The output of a linear layer is
  \( \mathbf z = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}\!\mathbf x
                = (\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}\!\! (\mathbf R^{\!\top}\!\mathbf x)\).
  Multiplying a column of `\(\mathbf W^{0}\)` by ‑1 (or equivalently flipping one coordinate of `\(\mathbf x``) does not change any downstream non‑linearities that depend only on inner products or cosine similarities, because those operations are invariant under a global sign change of one neuron.  Hence a missing reflection can be “absorbed’’ by re‑scaling a single neuron or by an equivalent sign change in a subsequent layer.

* **The training objective does not distinguish proper from improper rotations.**  
  The paper’s loss functions are based on *hyperspherical energy*, i.e. on pairwise cosine similarities between normalized weight vectors.  Cosine similarity depends only on the angle between two vectors, not on their absolute orientation in space.  Flipping all vectors in one subspace (the effect of a reflection) leaves all pairwise angles unchanged, so the hyperspherical energy stays exactly the same.  Consequently the optimisation landscape treats a reflection and its corresponding rotation as equivalent solutions.

Because OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning) merely seeks an orthogonal transformation that preserves these angles, it never needs to venture outside `\(\operatorname{SO}(d)\)` to achieve its goal.  Empirically, the authors report that using Cayley‑parameterised matrices does **not** hurt generation quality or control performance; they simply obtain a subset of all possible orthogonal maps that is sufficient for preserving hyperspherical energy while still providing enough flexibility to adapt to downstream tasks.

---

### Summary

* The Cayley transform maps any skew‑symmetric matrix `\(\mathbf Q` to an orthogonal matrix `\(\mathbf R` whose determinant is forced to be `+1`.  
* This happens because eigenvalues of `\(\mathbf Q` are purely imaginary, which makes every eigenvalue of `\(\mathbf R` lie on the unit circle but never equal to `-1`.  
* Matrices with determinant `-1` (reflections) cannot be produced directly, but in deep networks their effect can be absorbed by sign changes in other layers without altering pairwise angles.  
* Since OFT’s objective is invariant under such sign flips, restricting to `SO(d)` does not impair performance, which explains why the authors observe no degradation despite the theoretical limitation.

Thus, the Cayley parameterisation provides an efficient way to enforce orthogonality while remaining expressive enough for the tasks considered in the paper.

### How is the block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix parameterized and why does it improve parameter efficiency?
Question:
Section 3.2 describes splitting \(\mathbf{R}\) into \(r\) blocks of size \(d/r \times d/r\). I am confused about how many learnable parameters this introduces compared to a full orthogonal matrix, and how sharing blocks further reduces parameters. Could someone compute the exact parameter count for a concrete example, e.g., \(d=256\) with \(r=4\) and with/without block sharing?
Answer:
In the paper the orthogonal transformation $\mathbf R$ is introduced as a way to rotate all neurons of a layer while keeping the pair‑wise angles (and therefore the hyperspherical energy) unchanged.  
If we were to store an arbitrary orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ we would need  

$$
\#\text{params}_\text{full}= \frac{d(d-1)}{2},
$$

because an orthogonal matrix is completely described by a skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf Q$ ($\mathbf Q^\top=-\mathbf Q$), which has exactly $d(d-1)/2$ independent entries.

---

### Block‑diagonal parameterisation

The paper reduces the number of degrees of freedom by forcing $\mathbf R$ to be block‑diagonal with $r$ identical (or different) square blocks

$$
\mathbf R=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf R_{1},\dots,\mathbf R_{r}\bigr), \qquad 
\mathbf R_{i}\in O\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right).
$$

Each block has size $b=d/r$.  A single $b\times b$ orthogonal block needs  

$$
\frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{(d/r)\bigl(d/r-1\bigr)}{2}
$$

free parameters.  If we keep all $r$ blocks independent, the total count becomes  

$$
\#\text{params}_{\text{block}} 
= r \times \frac{(d/r)\bigl(d/r-1\bigr)}{2}
= \frac{d\,(\,d/r-1\,)}{2}.
$$

When we **share** the same block across all positions ($\mathbf R_{1}=\dots=\mathbf R_{r}$), only one block has to be stored, so the parameter count drops further to  

$$
\#\text{params}_{\text{shared}}
= \frac{(d/r)\bigl(d/r-1\bigr)}{2}.
$$

---

### Concrete numbers for $d=256,\; r=4$

* **Full orthogonal matrix**  
  $$
  \#_{\text{full}} = \frac{256\times255}{2}=32\,640.
  $$

* **Block‑diagonal with four *different* blocks** ($b=256/4=64$)  
  $$
  \#_{\text{block}} = \frac{256\times(64-1)}{2}
                  = \frac{256\times63}{2}
                  = 8\,064.
  $$

* **Block‑diagonal with *shared* blocks** (only one $64\times64$ orthogonal matrix)  
  $$
  \#_{\text{shared}} = \frac{64\times63}{2}=2\,016.
  $$

Thus, compared with a generic orthogonal matrix, the block‑diagonal trick reduces the number of learnable parameters by a factor of about 4 (no sharing) or about 16 (with sharing). The reduction is exactly what the authors call “parameter‑efficient”: we retain the desirable property that every row of $\mathbf R$ is still an orthonormal basis (hence hyperspherical energy is preserved), but we limit the degrees of freedom, making finetuning both cheaper and more stable.

In practice these three settings correspond to:

| Setting | Trainable parameters | Relative cost vs. full |
|---------|----------------------|------------------------|
| Full orthogonal ($r=1$) | 32 640 | 100 % |
| Block‑diagonal, $r=4$, no sharing | 8 064 | ≈ 25 % |
| Block‑diagonal, $r=4$, shared | 2 016 | ≈ 6 % |

The paper’s experiments use values of $r$ that give a similar order of magnitude reduction, which explains why OFT can be applied to large diffusion models while still keeping the parameter budget comparable to—or even smaller than—other low‑rank adapters such as LoRA.

### How is the \(\epsilon\)-constraint enforced in Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)?
Question:
COFT adds a deviation constraint \(‖\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}‖ \leq \epsilon\. The paper suggests moving this constraint to the skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) via a Neumann series approximation. How exactly is the projection onto the feasible set performed during training? Is it simply clipping the Frobenius norm of \(\mathbf{Q}\), and does this guarantee that the resulting \(\mathbf{R}\) satisfies the original bound?
Answer:
In COFT the optimisation variable is the orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) that is applied to the pretrained weight \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  
The two constraints that must hold for every update are  

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I},\qquad 
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}\le \epsilon .
\]

Because we parametrise \(\mathbf{R}\) by a Cayley transform  

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad \mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q},
\]

the orthogonality is automatically satisfied for any skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\) (the Cayley map sends the Lie algebra \(\mathfrak{so}(d)\) to the special‑orthogonal group).  
The remaining deviation constraint can therefore be expressed directly in terms of \(\mathbf{Q}\).
Expanding the Cayley transform as a Neumann series (which converges when \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{2}<1\)) gives  

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
        = \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+O(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{2}),
\]

so that, up to higher‑order terms,  

\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}\;\approx\;2\,\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}.
\]

Hence enforcing  

\[
\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}\le \epsilon' := \frac{\epsilon}{2}
\]

is sufficient to guarantee that the original bound on \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}\) holds (the neglected \(O(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{2})\) term becomes negligible when \(\epsilon'\) is small, which is exactly the regime used in COFT).

During training we therefore perform **projected gradient descent** on the skew‑symmetric parameters:

1. **Gradient step** – compute the usual stochastic gradient of the loss w.r.t. the unconstrained entries of \(\mathbf{Q}\).  
2. **Skew‑symmetrisation** – enforce \(\mathbf{Q}\leftarrow (\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}^{\top})/2\); this guarantees that the update stays in the Lie algebra.  
3. **Norm projection** – if the Frobenius norm exceeds \(\epsilon'\), we rescale:
   \[
   \mathbf{Q}\leftarrow \epsilon'\,\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}} .
   \]
   This operation is exactly a Euclidean projection onto the ball
   \(\{\mathbf{Q}:\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}\le \epsilon'\}\).

Because the projection step is a true Euclidean projection, it does not alter the direction of the gradient beyond what is necessary to stay inside the feasible set; consequently training remains stable.

After each update, the orthogonal matrix used in the forward pass is recomputed by applying the Cayley formula with the projected \(\mathbf{Q}\). By construction, every such matrix satisfies both constraints:

* orthogonality follows from the Cayley map,
* deviation from identity is bounded because
  \[
  \|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}
  = \bigl\| (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} - \mathbf{I} \bigr\|_{F}
  = 2\,\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F} + O(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{3})
  \le 2\,\epsilon' = \epsilon .
  \]

Thus **clipping the Frobenius norm of \(\mathbf{Q}\)** (after symmetrisation) is exactly how COFT enforces the “radius” constraint. The approximation introduced by truncating the Neumann series does not break the guarantee as long as \(\epsilon'\) is chosen small enough—this is precisely why COFT reports good stability for modest values of \(ε\) (e.g., \(ε≈0.1–0.3\) in their experiments). In practice the higher‑order terms are negligible, and empirical results show that the resulting \(R\) always respects the original bound.

### Why does re‑scaled OFT (with a diagonal scaling matrix \(\mathbf{D}\)) not change hyperspherical energy?
Question:
The authors claim that multiplying each neuron by a positive scalar (via \(\mathbf{D}=diag(s_1,…,s_n)\)) leaves hyperspherical energy unchanged because HE uses normalized neurons. However, during forward propagation the scaling interacts with the input vector. Does this scaling affect the effective learning dynamics or only act as an additional degree of freedom after normalization? Clarify how gradients flow through \(s_i\) and whether any regularization is needed.
Answer:
**Answer**

In the paper the authors introduce *re‑scaled* Orthogonal Finetuning (re‑scaled OFT) as a straightforward extension of the basic OFT formulation.  
The forward pass of a linear layer becomes  

$$
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}\,\mathbf{D})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
\qquad 
\mathbf{D}= \operatorname{diag}(s_{1},\dots ,s_{n}),\; s_{i}>0,
$$

where  

* $\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ are the pretrained weights,  
* $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is a layer‑shared orthogonal matrix ($\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$), and  
* $\mathbf{D}$ rescales each *output* neuron independently.

The key claim in the paper is that this extra diagonal scaling **does not change the hyperspherical energy (HE)** of the layer, because HE is defined only in terms of *directions* of the columns of $\mathbf{W}$.  Formally, let  

$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|},
\qquad
\mathbf{w}_{i}= \bigl(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}\,\mathbf{D}\bigr)_{:,i}.
$$

Since $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal it preserves Euclidean norms,
$\|\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{u}\|=\|\mathbf{u}\|$, and the diagonal matrix $\mathbf{D}$ only multiplies each column by a positive scalar $s_{i}$.  Consequently  

$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}
= \frac{\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}s_{i}}{\| \mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}s_{i} \|}
= \frac{\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|}
= \mathbf{R} \, \hat{\mathbf{w}}^{0}_{i},
$$

so all normalized neurons are simply rotated (or reflected) by the *same* orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$.  The pairwise cosine similarities  

$$
\langle \hat{\mathbf w}_{i},\hat{\mathbf w}_{j}\rangle
= \langle \hat{\mathbf w}^{0}_{i},\hat{\mathbf w}^{0}_{j}\rangle ,
$$

and therefore the hyperspherical energy  

$$
\text {HE}(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\neq j}
    \|\hat{\mathbf w}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf w}_{j}\|^{-1}
$$

remains exactly equal to $\text {HE}(\mathbf W^{0})$.  This proves the authors’ statement: **the diagonal scaling does not affect HE because it disappears after normalisation**.

---

### Does the scaling affect learning dynamics?

Even though HE is invariant, the scaling matrix does intervene in the *raw* forward computation:

$$
\tilde{\mathbf z}= (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top} (\mathbf D^{\!\top} \!\! \,\boldsymbol{x}),
$$

or, equivalently,

$$
z_{i}= s_{i}\; (\mathbf w^{0}_{i})^{\!\top} (\mathbf R^{\!\top} \boldsymbol{x}),
$$

so each output channel is multiplied by a learnable gain $s_{i}$.  This has two practical consequences:

1. **Effective learning rate per neuron** – gradients with respect to the loss are scaled by $s_{i}$.  If during training a certain neuron becomes useful for a downstream task, increasing its $s_{i}$ amplifies its contribution without having to rotate the whole basis.  Conversely, decreasing $s_{i}$ can suppress noisy directions.  Thus $s_{i}$ provides an *additional degree of freedom* that can be exploited more cheaply than rotating all weights.

2. **Interaction with the input** – because $\boldsymbol{x}$ is multiplied by nothing in this formulation (the scaling sits on the *output* side), there is no coupling between $s_{i}$ and the input distribution beyond what already exists for the original weights.  The optimisation landscape therefore remains smooth: gradients w.r.t. $s_{i}$ are simply  

   $$
   \frac{\partial L}{\partial s_{i}}
   = (\partial L / \partial z_{i})\, (\boldsymbol{x}^{\!\top} \,\! \bigl(\! (\!\! \ldots )^\top\bigr)),
   $$

   i.e. the usual chain rule applied to a scalar gain.

Because $s_i>0$ is enforced (the paper uses a softplus or exponentiation to keep them positive), there is no risk of flipping signs that would break the angular relationships; only magnitudes change.

---

### Gradient flow through $s_i$

During back‑propagation we have

```
z = (R·W0·D)^T x
L = loss(z,…)
```

Let $g = \partial L / \partial z \in \mathbb R^{n}$.  
The gradient w.r.t. each scale parameter is

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_i}
 = g_i\, (\mathbf w^{0}_i)^{\!\top} (\mathbf R^{\!\top} x),
$$

and the gradient w.r.t. the orthogonal matrix is identical to ordinary OFT,

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf R}
   = (\,W^{0} D\,)\, x\, g^{\!\top}.
$$

Hence training re‑scaled OFT amounts to **simultaneously** learning an orthogonal transformation (which preserves pairwise angles) and a set of per‑neuron gains (which modulate magnitudes). The optimiser treats them as independent parameter blocks; no special coupling term is required.

---

### Do we need extra regularisation for $s_i$?

From the theoretical standpoint, no regulariser is required to keep HE unchanged—scaling cannot affect HE.  In practice, however, unbounded growth of some $s_i$ can lead to numerical instability (very large activations may saturate downstream nonlinearities or cause exploding gradients). The authors therefore follow two simple engineering tricks that appear in most implementations:

* **Positive parametrisation** – they optimise an unconstrained variable $v_i$ and define $s_i = \exp(v_i)$ or $s_i = {\rm softplus}(v_i)$. This guarantees positivity while keeping gradients well‑behaved.
* **Weight decay / small‑scale prior** – applying an $L_2$ penalty on $v_i$ (or directly on $s_i-1$) gently encourages the scales to stay near 1, which matches the “no‑scaling” initialisation used for OFT. This does not alter HE but prevents pathological magnitudes.

The paper’s experiments show that with these minimal measures re‑scaled OFT converges faster than plain OFT (see Appendix B), confirming that the extra degrees of freedom are beneficial but do not require a dedicated hyperspherical‑energy regulariser.

---

### Summary

* Multiplying each neuron by a positive scalar via a diagonal matrix $D=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots ,s_n)$ leaves hyperspherical energy unchanged because HE depends only on *directions*, which are invariant under independent positive scaling and common orthogonal rotation.
* The scaling does influence forward activations and thus provides an extra, cheap way for the optimiser to adjust each channel’s magnitude.
* Gradients flow through both $R$ (as in ordinary OFT) and through each $s_i$; they are computed exactly as with any linear layer containing per‑output gains.
* No special regularisation is needed to preserve HE, but modest regularisers (positivity enforcement and mild weight decay) are advisable to keep numerical stability.
* Consequently, re‑scaled OFT adds only a negligible number of parameters while giving more flexibility in finetuning, which explains why it typically improves convergence speed without violating the hyperspherical‑energy invariance that underpins OFT’s stability.

### What is the ‘control consistency metric’ introduced for controllable generation?
Question:
In Section 5, a new metric is proposed that computes IoU/F1 for Canny edges, mean IoU/accuracy for segmentation, and L2 distance for facial landmarks. How exactly are these metrics computed from generated images—do they run an external detector on the output and compare its prediction to the original control signal? Also, are these metrics comparable across methods that may produce slightly different image styles?
Answer:
The “control‑consistency metric’’ that the paper introduces is nothing more exotic than a **two‑step evaluation**:

1. **Recover the control signal from the generated image** – after a model has produced an image from a text prompt **and** an external condition (Canny edge map, semantic segmentation map, or facial‑landmark layout), the authors run an off‑the‑shelf perception module that is specialised for that kind of signal.  
   * For Canny‑edge control they run a standard Canny edge detector on the synthesis output.  
   * For segmentation‑map control they feed the synthesis output to a pretrained semantic‑segmentation network (e.g. a DeepLab‑v3+ model) and obtain a per‑pixel class label map.  
   * For landmark‑to‑face control they apply a pretrained facial‑landmark detector (such as the 68‑point dlib/Face‑Alignment network) to obtain a set of 2‑D landmark coordinates.

2. **Compare the recovered signal with the original input condition** – the comparison uses conventional similarity measures appropriate to each signal type:  

   * **Canny edges** – compute the binary edge maps for both the ground‑truth condition and the recovered map, then calculate Intersection‑over‑Union (IoU) and the F1 score (the harmonic mean of precision and recall on the pixel level).  
   * **Segmentation maps** – treat each pixel as a class label; compute per‑class IoU, then average over classes (mean IoU).  In addition, overall pixel accuracy (the fraction of correctly labelled pixels) and mean accuracy (average of per‑class accuracies) are reported.  
   * **Facial landmarks** – after obtaining two sets of $(x,y)$ coordinates, take the Euclidean distance for each corresponding landmark, average them, and finally report the mean $\ell_2$ distance.

Thus, the metric does **not** compare the generated image directly to a reference image; instead it measures how well a downstream detector can reconstruct the *control* that was fed to the model. The better the reconstruction, the higher the score, indicating that the diffusion model respects the conditioning signal.

---

### Why this makes sense

The detectors used in step 1 are themselves trained on large, diverse datasets, so their outputs are relatively stable across modest variations in style or texture. By measuring how faithfully the conditioning information survives through generation and subsequent detection, we get an objective proxy for “controllability’’ that is independent of subjective visual quality. In other words, if two methods produce images with very different artistic styles but both allow a reliable edge/segmentation/landmark recovery, they will obtain comparable scores.

### Comparability across methods

Because each method is evaluated with **the same external detector**, scores are directly comparable even when the visual appearance (colour palette, shading, artistic style) differs. The only caveat is that detector performance can be biased toward certain visual domains—for example, a segmentation network trained mainly on natural scenes might struggle on highly stylised renderings. The authors acknowledge this limitation in Appendix B (settings) and mitigate it by using robust detectors that have been validated on varied benchmarks (DeepLab‑v3+ on COCO/ADE20K, dlib landmark detector on CelebA‑HQ).

Consequently:

* If method A yields higher IoU/F1 than method B on Canny edges, we can conclude that A preserves edge geometry more faithfully—regardless of whether A’s images look more cartoonish or more photorealistic.  
* Similarly for segmentation and landmarks: higher mean IoU or lower $\ell_2$ distance means better adherence to spatial structure.

In practice, researchers report both these control‑consistency numbers **and** standard image quality metrics (FID, CLIP similarity) because an algorithm could trivially copy the input control (giving perfect IoU) while producing low‑quality images. The joint reporting therefore provides a balanced view of *how well* a model follows its control versus *how good* its output looks.

---

### Summary

The control consistency metric works by:

1. Running an appropriate off‑the‑shelf detector (Canny edge detector, semantic segmentation net, or facial landmark predictor) on each generated image.
2. Comparing the detector’s output to the original conditioning signal with IoU/F1 (edges), mean IoU & accuracy (segmentation), or mean $\ell_2$ distance (landmarks).

Since every method is evaluated with exactly the same detectors, scores are comparable across approaches even when their visual styles differ; any systematic bias originates only from the detectors themselves, which are chosen to be robust across styles. This metric therefore quantifies how faithfully a diffusion model respects its additional control inputs—a core requirement for controllable generation.

### How does OFT compare to LoRA in terms of effective rank of the weight update?
Question:
(a) The paper rewrites OFT’s update as \((
        \mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^0\). If \(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\) is low‑rank because of block‑diagonal structure, what rank can we expect? (b) LoRA explicitly parametrizes a low‑rank matrix \(\Delta=W_aW_b^T\). How do these two approaches differ in capturing expressive updates, and can we theoretically bound one’s expressivity relative to the other?
Answer:
**Answer**

Both OFT and LoRA modify a pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf W^{0}$ by adding a *structured* perturbation, but they do so in very different ways.  
In OFT the perturbation is written as  

$$
\Delta_{\text{OFT}}=(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)\,\mathbf W^{0},
$$

where $\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix that is forced to be **block‑diagonal** (Section 3.2).  
In LoRA the perturbation has the familiar low‑rank factorisation  

$$
\Delta_{\text{LoRA}}=\mathbf A\,\mathbf B^{\!\top},\qquad
\mathbf A\in\mathbb R^{d\times r'},\;\mathbf B\in\mathbb R^{n\times r'} ,
$$

with $r'\ll \min(d,n)$.

Below we analyse the effective rank of $\Delta_{\text{OFT}}$ and compare the expressive power of the two updates.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### (a) Expected rank of $(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)\mathbf W^{0}$

Because $\mathbf R$ is block‑diagonal with $r$ blocks, each block has size $d/r\times d/r$.  
Write  

$$
\mathbf R=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_{1},\dots,\mathbf R_{r}), \qquad
\mathbf I=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf I_{1},\dots,\mathbf I_{r}),
$$

so that  

$$
\mathbf R-\mathbf I=
\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{\mathbf R_{1}-\mathbf I_{1}}_{\text{size }d/r},
\dots,
\underbrace{\mathbf R_{r}-\mathbf I_{r}}_{\text{size }d/r}).
$$

Each block $\mathbf R_{k}-\mathbf I_{k}$ is a **difference of two orthogonal matrices**. For a generic orthogonal matrix the eigenvalues lie on the unit circle; subtracting the identity moves them away from zero, and unless the block happens to be exactly $\mathbf I_{k}$ the block will be full rank. Consequently each block contributes at most $d/r$ to the rank, and in practice—because we start from $\mathbf R=\mathbf I$ and move only a small amount during training—most blocks are *approximately* low‑rank.  

If we denote by $\rho=\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)$, then a simple bound follows from basic linear‑algebra:

$$
\rho \le \frac{d}{r}\; r = d,
$$

but because of the block structure we can tighten this to  

$$
\rho \le r\,\, \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf R_{k}-\mathbf I_{k}) 
      \;\approx\, r\,\,c,
$$

where $c<d/r$ is the typical numerical rank of a single block (often on the order of a few dozen when $d=1024, r=8$).  
Thus **the effective rank of the OFT update is proportional to the number of blocks**, not to the full dimension $d$. In practice the authors report using $r=8,16,\dots$; with a 1024‑dimensional layer this yields an update whose numerical rank is roughly $8-16\times c\simeq 50-200$, i.e. far lower than $d=1024$.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### (b) Expressivity comparison with LoRA

Both methods can be seen as low‑rank modifications of the original weights, but their *source* of low‑rankness is different.

|                     | Source of low‑rankness                                   | Typical parameter count                                   |
|---------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| **LoRA**            | Explicit factorisation $\Delta = \mathbf A \mathbf B^{T}$ with rank $r'$. | $r'(d+n)$.                                               |
| **OFT**             | Implicit low‑rankness because $(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)$ is block‑diagonal; each block contributes at most its size. | With sharing: $\frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}$ (or $\frac{d(d/r-1)}{2r}$ if blocks are shared). |

*Expressivity* can be interpreted as the set of matrices that can be realised as an update.

* **LoRA** can represent *any* matrix of rank at most $r'$. Its span is exactly all matrices whose column space lies in the span of the columns of $\mathbf A$ (or rows of $\mathbf B^{T}$). This gives a *dense* subspace in the space of low‑rank matrices.

* **OFT** can represent any matrix that can be written as a product of a *block‑diagonal orthogonal perturbation* with the full‑rank pretrained matrix. Because $\mathbf W^{0}$ is usually full rank, the rank of $\Delta_{\text{OFT}}$ cannot exceed the rank of $(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)$. Hence every OFT update lives in a subspace that is *restricted by the block pattern*: only directions that are linear combinations of columns belonging to the same block can be altered together.

Consequently, **LoRA is strictly more expressive for a given numerical rank**: any OFT update with effective rank $k$ can be reproduced by LoRA with $r'\ge k$, simply by setting $\mathbf A=\Delta_{\text{OFT}}$ and $\mathbf B^{T}=I_{n}$. The converse does **not** hold in general, because LoRA can mix columns across arbitrary rows whereas OFT mixes columns only within each block.

However, this extra freedom comes at a cost. The paper argues (Section 3.4) that preserving pair‑wise neuron angles—i.e. enforcing orthogonality—prevents destructive drift of hyperspherical energy and yields far better finetuning stability. In other words, *the restriction imposed by OFT is a useful inductive bias*: it discards many directions that would otherwise corrupt the pretrained relational structure while still providing enough degrees of freedom to rotate neuron directions efficiently.

From a theoretical standpoint we can state:

*Let $r_{\text{eff}} = \operatorname{rank}((\mathbf R-\mathbf I)) = O(r)$. Then for any downstream task that can be solved by an additive low‑rank update of rank at most $r_{\text{eff}}$, there exists an OFT solution that achieves comparable training loss.*  
Conversely, there exist tasks whose optimal additive update has rank larger than $r_{\text{eff}}$; those tasks would require either increasing the number of blocks (larger $r$) or resorting to a less constrained method such as LoRA.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Take‑away

* The **effective rank** of OFT’s update equals roughly *(number of blocks × numerical rank of a single block)*; with typical settings it lies in the low‑hundreds even for thousand‑dimensional layers.
* **LoRA** offers an unrestricted low‑rank space; any OFT update can be mimicked by LoRA with equal or larger rank, but LoRA can also produce updates that break hyperspherical energy.
* The *expressivity gap* therefore reflects a trade‑off: OFT sacrifices some degrees of freedom in exchange for preserving angular relationships among neurons, which empirically yields faster convergence and better stability on diffusion finetuning tasks.

In practice you should pick OFT when you need strong preservation of pretrained semantics and are happy to tune only a modest number of blocks; pick LoRA when you suspect that a richer, possibly non‑block‑structured low‑rank modification is required.

### Why does preserving pairwise neuron angles lead to faster convergence?
Question:
The authors suggest that changing neuron directions is the most effective way to modify semantics, citing a toy autoencoder experiment. Can someone explain why optimization on the hypersphere manifold (as induced by orthogonal constraints) yields a smoother loss landscape? Is there any theoretical result linking manifold geometry to convergence speed in diffusion model finetuning?
Answer:
In the paper *Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning* the authors observe experimentally that the orthogonal‑finetuned (OFT/COFT) models reach a good solution in far fewer epochs than a naïve fine‑tuning or LoRA baseline.  
The underlying reason can be understood from two complementary viewpoints that the authors discuss:

1. **Semantic relevance of neuron directions**  
   The toy auto‑encoder experiment (Figure 1 in the paper) shows that, after a network has been trained with ordinary inner‑product activations, the *angular* component of a convolutional filter contains almost all of the information needed to reconstruct the input, whereas the magnitude of the filter contributes little. This mirrors earlier work on *angular embeddings* (e.g., SphereNet, decoupled networks) which demonstrates that normalising neurons onto a unit hypersphere does not hurt capacity and often improves generalisation because the direction encodes the semantic feature while the norm is mostly a scaling factor. Consequently, when we want to modify the semantics of a diffusion model (e.g., make it render a new subject or obey a control signal) we should chiefly change **directions** of the weight vectors rather than their lengths.

2. **Optimization on a smooth manifold**  
   By constraining each layer’s weight matrix $\mathbf W$ to be an orthogonal transformation of the pretrained weights,
   $$
   \mathbf W = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0},\qquad \mathbf R^{\top}\mathbf R = \mathbf I,
   $$
   we are restricting the parameters to lie on the **special orthogonal group** $SO(d)$, i.e. a compact Riemannian manifold (the hypersphere for each row/column). On such a manifold two important things happen:

   * **Preservation of pair‑wise angles** – For any two neurons $\mathbf w_i,\mathbf w_j$ in the same layer,
     $$
     \cos\bigl(\angle (\mathbf R\mathbf w_i),(\mathbf R\mathbf w_j)\bigr)
     = \cos\bigl(\angle \mathbf w_i,\mathbf w_j\bigr),
     $$
     so the hyperspherical energy,
     $\text{HE}(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1}$,
     stays exactly equal to that of the pretrained model.  In other words, the *relative geometry* of the representation space is unchanged.

   * **Reduced curvature of the loss landscape** – When optimisation is performed in Euclidean weight space with no constraints, every parameter can move independently; many directions correspond to changes that only affect norms or rotate individual neurons arbitrarily, and these directions typically generate high‑frequency, highly non‑convex variations in the loss. By projecting all updates onto $SO(d)$ we eliminate those redundant degrees of freedom. The remaining degrees of freedom correspond to *global rotations* that preserve all pairwise angles; such rotations act almost like a change of coordinate system and therefore cause only a smooth, slowly varying change in the output distribution. The paper cites Liu et al. 2021, which empirically demonstrated that training networks whose parameters evolve on orthogonal manifolds yields a much flatter loss surface and consequently allows larger effective learning rates and faster convergence.

   Because every admissible update is an orthogonal matrix, gradient descent (or Adam) does not have to “search” through flat directions where the loss does not change perceptibly; it can concentrate on directions that genuinely improve the task‑specific loss while keeping the pretrained semantic structure intact. This explains why OFT reaches comparable or better quality after only 8–10 epochs whereas LoRA needs $20\,+$ epochs.

3. **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)** adds an explicit bound
   $\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|\le\varepsilon$.  Geometrically this restricts optimisation to a small geodesic ball around the identity on $SO(d)$.  The ball’s radius directly controls how far we are allowed to rotate the pretrained basis.  A smaller radius yields an even smoother landscape (the loss becomes locally quadratic), which further stabilises training and reduces sensitivity to learning‑rate choices—hence another source of faster convergence observed in Figure 4 of the paper.

Putting these pieces together, preserving pairwise neuron angles does two things simultaneously: it keeps the *semantic geometry* learned during pretraining untouched, and it forces optimisation onto a low‑dimensional, well‑behaved manifold where gradients point more consistently toward minima.  The combination produces precisely what you asked for—a smoother loss surface and consequently faster, more stable convergence when fine‑tuning large diffusion models.

### Can multiple OFT matrices be composed for sequential downstream tasks?
Question:
The conclusion mentions compositionality: if we fine‑tune on task A obtaining \(\mathbf{R}_A\) and later on task B obtaining \(\mathbf{R}_B\), their product remains orthogonal. Does applying both transforms sequentially preserve both tasks’ knowledge? Are there any experimental results or theoretical insights on catastrophic forgetting when stacking orthogonal finetunes?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper shows that an orthogonal fine‑tuning (OFT) step can be written as  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{(t)} = \mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{W}^{0},
\qquad \mathbf{R}^{(t)\!\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}=\mathbf{I},
\]

where \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) are the pretrained weights and \(\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\) is the layer‑shared orthogonal matrix learned for downstream task \(t\).  
If we later fine‑tune the same model on a second task \(B\) we obtain another orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\) and the final weights become  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{AB}= \mathbf{R}^{(B)}\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\mathbf{W}^{0}.
\]

Because the product of two orthogonal matrices is again orthogonal, \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\in\mathcal O(d)\) and the hyperspherical energy of the network is still exactly preserved. Hence the **compositionality property** that the authors mention holds mathematically: stacking OFT steps never destroys the pair‑wise angular relationships that the method is designed to protect.

---

### Does stacking preserve both tasks’ knowledge?

Preserving hyperspherical energy guarantees that **the geometric structure of the weight space** (i.e. all pair‑wise cosine similarities between neurons) stays unchanged up to a global rotation/reflection. This is a *necessary* condition for retaining the pretrained semantics, but it is **not sufficient** to guarantee that the specialised knowledge acquired for each downstream task survives unchanged.

Think of an orthogonal transform as rotating a coordinate system. After task A, we have rotated the network by \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\); after task B we rotate it again by \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\). The final representation space is therefore different from both intermediate spaces unless \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\) happens to commute with \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\) (i.e. they are rotations about the same axis or one is the identity). In general, two arbitrary rotations do **not** commute, so the second fine‑tuning can move representations away from those that were optimal for task A.

Consequently, stacking OFT matrices can cause **interference** between tasks:

1. **If tasks are compatible** – e.g., both require only a small adjustment of neuron directions in roughly the same subspace – then \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\) will be close to identity in the basis defined by \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\), and performance on A will degrade only slightly.
2. **If tasks are conflicting** – e.g., subject‑driven generation (which learns a subject‑specific rotation) followed by a control‑signal task that needs a very different rotation of the same layers – then \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\) may undo or overwrite part of the transformation introduced by \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\), leading to catastrophic forgetting of A.

The paper itself lists this compositionality question as an **open problem** (Section “Concluding Remarks and Open Problems”) and does not provide empirical evidence that stacking OFT steps works without forgetting. The authors only prove that the product remains orthogonal; they do **not** experimentally verify whether knowledge from multiple sequential fine‑tunings is retained.

---

### Theoretical intuition

- **Preserved pairwise angles:** For any two neurons \(i,j\),
  \[
  \cos\!\bigl(\langle \mathbf{R}^{AB}\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0},
               \mathbf{R}^{AB}\mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}\rangle\bigr)
  = \cos\!\bigl(\langle \mathbf{w}_{i}^{0},
               \mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}\rangle\bigr),
  \]
  because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products. This guarantees that *the distribution of angles* across a layer stays identical to the pretrained model.

- **Task‑specific loss landscapes:** Each downstream loss \(L_{A}(\mathbf{R})\) or \(L_{B}(\mathbf{R})\) induces a different optimum on the orthogonal manifold. The manifold contains many points (all possible rotations). Optimising for A moves us to a point \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}_{\star}\); optimising subsequently for B moves us to another point \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}_{\star}\). Unless those optima coincide, reaching \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}_{\star}\) will move us away from \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}_{\star}\).

- **Absence of an explicit multi‑task regulariser:** In multi‑task learning with OFT one would have to add a regularisation term such as
  \[
  \lambda\,\|\,\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{(A)}_{\star}\|_{F}^{2}
  \]
  during training on task B to keep the new rotation close to the previous one. The current formulation does not contain such a term.

---

### Practical take‑aways

1. **You can multiply OFT matrices safely from a numerical standpoint** – the result is still orthogonal and will not break the hyperspherical‑energy guarantee.
2. **Do not expect perfect knowledge retention without additional constraints.** Empirically you may observe degradation on earlier tasks, especially when tasks are dissimilar.
3. **If you need true compositionality**, consider one of these strategies (all of them are consistent with the paper’s framework):
   - *Joint optimisation*: fine‑tune on all tasks simultaneously, learning a single orthogonal matrix that balances all losses.
   - *Regularised sequential training*: when fine‑tuning on B, add a penalty that keeps the new matrix close to the previously learned one.
   - *Separate adapters*: keep each task’s OFT matrix separate and switch between them at inference time (exactly analogous to LoRA adapters), at the cost of storing multiple matrices.

4. **Future work** cited by the authors includes systematic experiments on stacking OFT matrices and measuring forgetting; until such results appear, any claim about “catastrophic forgetting does not happen” would be speculative.

In short, stacking orthogonal fine‑tuning matrices preserves the mathematical property of orthogonality, but it does not automatically guarantee preservation of each downstream task’s performance. Empirical validation and possibly additional multi‑task regularisation are needed to avoid forgetting when composing OFT transforms.
