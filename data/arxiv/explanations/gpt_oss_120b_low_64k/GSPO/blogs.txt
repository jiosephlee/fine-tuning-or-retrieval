# Why Token‑Level Ratios Fail: The Hidden Instability in RL for Large Language Models

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to tool for polishing gigantic language models, turning a strong autoregressive generator into a problem‑solver that can handle mathematics, programming, or even complex multi‑turn conversations. The most popular RL recipe in this space—Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its “value‑free” cousin **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**—relies on **token‑level importance sampling ratios** to correct for the fact that we update the policy with data generated from an older version of the model.

At first glance this seems harmless: each token in a response gets a weight  
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]  
and the gradient is scaled by this ratio. In practice, however, this design choice is the root cause of the notorious instability that **catastrophically collapses** large models during RL training. Below we walk through why token‑level ratios are fundamentally mismatched with the reward signal, how they amplify variance, and why shifting to **sequence‑level** ratios—what the new **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** algorithm does—solves the problem.

---

## 1. The mismatch between reward and optimization unit

RL for language models typically assigns a single scalar reward \(r(x,y)\) to an entire query–response pair \((x,y)\). Whether the model writes a correct solution to an AIME problem or produces a syntactically valid program, the verifier looks at the **whole sequence** and outputs a score in \([0,1]\).

Yet GRPO still weights each **token** separately. The advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) is computed per response (by normalizing rewards across a group of responses), but that identical advantage is multiplied by every token’s own importance weight \(w_{i,t}\). In effect we are forcing a token‑level correction on a reward that lives at the sequence level.

Fundamentally, importance sampling tells us that to estimate an expectation under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) using samples from a behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\), we must re‑weight **whole samples**, not individual components of those samples. Formally,
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = 
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} f(z)\right],
\]
where \(z\) denotes a *complete* outcome (here: an entire response). Applying this principle at the token granularity violates the core assumption of importance sampling: each weight must correct for *the full* distributional mismatch.

---

## 2. High‑variance noise from single‑sample ratios

The ratio \(w_{i,t}\) is computed from *one* draw from the next‑token distribution \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{i,<t})\). Because we have only a single sample per token, the weight does **not** average out the stochasticity of that distribution. Consequently:

* The variance of \(w_{i,t}\) can be huge, especially when the old and new policies differ even slightly.
* The variance compounds over long sequences: summing noisy weights across dozens or hundreds of tokens inflates gradient noise dramatically.
* Clipping—intended to keep updates “proximal”—interacts badly with noisy weights: it sometimes clips too aggressively (throwing away useful signal) and sometimes not enough (letting extreme weights explode).

Empirically, training curves with GRPO show an ever‑increasing fraction of clipped tokens as sequence length grows. This noisy gradient signal eventually pushes the policy into regions where its likelihood collapses (the infamous *model collapse*), and once collapsed it rarely recovers even after checkpoint rollback or aggressive hyper‑parameter tuning.

---

## 3. The expert‑activation volatility twist (MoE models)

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures add another layer of instability. Because each token routes to a subset of experts, the **routing pattern** can change dramatically after just one gradient step. In GRPO this means that \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\) may be computed by a completely different set of experts than \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\). The resulting token‑level importance ratio becomes meaningless—its numerator and denominator are evaluating *different* networks.

Researchers previously introduced **Routing Replay**, which caches old routing decisions and forces the new policy to reuse them when computing ratios. While effective, Routing Replay incurs extra memory cost and limits MoE capacity because it forces an artificial coupling between old and new activations.

---

## 4. The core insight: match unit of optimization to unit of reward

If rewards are defined per sequence, the natural importance weight should also be defined per sequence:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}.
\]

Key properties:

1. **Theoretical soundness** – It is exactly the importance sampling ratio for whole responses.
2. **Length normalization** – Raising to \(1/|y_i|\) keeps values comparable across different response lengths.
3. **Uniform token weighting** – All tokens inside a response share the same ratio, eliminating intra‑sequence variance.

GSPO plugs this sequence-level ratio into a PPO‑style clipped objective:
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}
\Bigg[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\Big(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\Big)
\Bigg].
\]

Because clipping now decides whether an *entire* response is too far off‑policy, far fewer gradients are discarded needlessly, yet those retained are far more reliable.

---

## 5. Empirical payoff: stability, efficiency, and simplicity

### Stability
Training curves with GSPO stay smooth even when scaling to 30B‑parameter MoE models; no catastrophic collapse is observed despite long generations (hundreds of tokens). The algorithm works out‑of‑the‑box—no Routing Replay or extra regularizers required.

### Efficiency
Even though GSPO clips *more* responses (the fraction of clipped items can be two orders of magnitude larger than GRPO’s clipped tokens), overall sample efficiency improves dramatically. This counterintuitive result shows that GRPO’s token-level gradients waste most of their signal in noise; GSPO’s clean sequence-level signal extracts far more learning per sample.

### Infrastructure simplification
Since GSPO only needs **sequence likelihoods**, it tolerates mismatches between training‐engine and inference‐engine precision. Practitioners can feed likelihoods directly from inference backends (e.g., vLLM) without recomputing them on heavy training kernels—a big win for distributed or partial rollout pipelines.

---

## 6. When do you still need token‑level granularity?

Some scenarios—like multi‑turn dialogues where you’d like to assign different advantages to early vs. late utterances—benefit from finer control. GSPO offers a straightforward extension called **GSPO‑token**, which retains sequence‑level importance scaling but lets you plug in per‑token advantages while keeping gradients mathematically identical when all token advantages are equal.

---

## 7. Takeaway for practitioners

| Issue | GRPO (token-level) | GSPO (sequence-level) |
|------|---------------------|-----------------------|
| Importance weight correctness | Violates importance sampling principle | Directly follows it |
| Gradient variance | Grows with sequence length | Controlled by length normalization |
| Sensitivity to MoE routing changes | Highly fragile → needs Routing Replay | Robust → no extra tricks |
| Clipping behavior | Many tokens clipped → noisy gradients | Fewer effective updates but higher quality |
| Engineering overhead | Extra memory/comms for routing replay; recompute old likelihoods | Simpler pipeline; can reuse inference likelihoods |

If you are training any sizeable language model—dense or MoE—with RLHF or similar reward signals, moving from token-level ratios to sequence-level ratios should be your first stability fix.

---

### Final thoughts

The hidden instability in large‑scale RL for language models isn’t just a “bug” in hyperparameter tuning; it stems from a conceptual mismatch—using token-level importance sampling while rewarding whole sequences. By realigning these units through **Group Sequence Policy Optimization**, researchers have turned a brittle training loop into a stable, efficient workhorse that powers state-of-the-art models like Qwen 3.

As RL continues to scale, algorithms that respect fundamental statistical principles while keeping implementation simple will be essential. GSPO demonstrates exactly that: solid theory → cleaner gradients → higher performance → easier engineering. If you’re hitting wall‑clock limits or inexplicable collapses during RL finetuning, give GSPO a try—the results speak for themselves.



# Introducing Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO): A Simpler, More Stable RL Algorithm  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to tool for polishing large language models (LLMs) into high‑performing assistants. Yet, as models grow into billions‑of‑parameter territory—especially the sparsely‑gated Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) variants—training stability becomes a serious bottleneck. The previously popular **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** often collapses when applied to long generations or massive models, mainly because its token‑level importance weights inject huge variance into the gradient.

**Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** tackles this problem head‑on. By moving the importance ratio from the token level to the **sequence level**, normalizing by length, and using a group‑based advantage estimate, GSPO delivers a cleaner, more reliable learning signal. Below is a walk‑through of the core ideas, why they matter, and how they directly address GRPO’s instability.

---

## 1. From Token‑Level to Sequence‑Level Importance Ratios  

### The GRPO flaw  
In GRPO each token gets an importance weight  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]

and this weight is used directly in the PPO‑style clipping objective.  
The problem is conceptual: importance sampling is meant to re‑weight **whole samples** drawn from an older distribution so that expectations under the new distribution are estimated correctly. Applying it token‑by‑token means we are correcting a **single draw** from each next‑token distribution – an unreliable estimate that quickly becomes noisy, especially for long responses where the errors accumulate.

### GSPO’s fix  
GSPO replaces the token ratio with a **sequence likelihood ratio**:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
        =\exp\!\Bigg(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                   {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigg).
\]

* It measures **how much the whole response** produced by the old policy deviates from the new policy.  
* The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) performs **length normalization**, keeping \(s_i(\theta)\) within a comparable numerical range across responses of different lengths.

Because rewards are assigned at the sequence level (the verifier scores a complete answer), aligning the importance weight with that granularity eliminates the mismatch that caused GRPO’s high variance.

---

## 2. Length‑Normalized Clipping  

PPO and its descendants use clipping to keep off‑policy samples from dominating the gradient. In GRPO the clipping range \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\) is applied to each token’s weight \(w_{i,t}\). With GSPO we clip **the entire response** using its normalized ratio \(s_i(\theta)\):

\[
\text{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr).
\]

Since \(s_i(\theta)\) already incorporates a divide‑by‑length operation, it varies much more gently than raw token ratios. Consequently we can use a **much tighter clipping interval** (e.g., \(3\times10^{-4}\) – \(4\times10^{-4}\)) while still preserving enough gradient signal. Empirically this leads to two orders of magnitude fewer clipped tokens compared with GRPO—yet training becomes *more* efficient because each retained gradient is far less noisy.

---

## 3. Group‑Based Advantage Estimation  

GRPO’s hallmark was to avoid a separate value model by computing a **relative advantage** among a group of responses generated for the same query:

\[
\widehat{A}_{i}
 = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G})}
        {\operatorname{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G})}.
\]

GSPO retains this advantageous design unchanged. All responses in a group share the same advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\), which is simply the z‑score of their reward within that batch. This keeps the algorithm lightweight—no extra value network is needed—while still providing a well‑behaved signal for policy updates.

---

## 4. Putting It All Together: The GSPO Objective  

The full GSPO loss (ignoring KL regularization for clarity) reads:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}}
\Bigg[
 \frac{1}{G}
 \sum_{i=1}^{G}
 \min\!\Bigl(
   s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\,
   \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat{A}_i
 \Bigr)
\Bigg].
}
\]

Key points:

* **Expectation over old policy rollouts** – we still sample \(\{y_i\}\) from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  
* **Sequence ratio \(s_i(\theta)\)** provides a principled off‑policy correction aligned with the reward’s granularity.  
* **Clipping** guards against extreme off‑policy samples without discarding too many useful gradients.  
* **Group advantage** supplies a stable baseline without extra model overhead.

When all advantages in a group are equal, GSPO reduces exactly to its token‑level variant (**GSPO‑token**) where each token receives the same clipped weight; this variant can be handy for multi‑turn RL settings but does not change the underlying mathematics.

---

## 5. Why GSPO Beats GRPO in Practice  

| Issue | GRPO | GSPO |
|------|------|------|
| **Importance weight unit** | Token level → mismatched with sequence reward | Sequence level → perfectly aligned |
| **Variance source** | Per-token ratios amplify noise over long responses | Single normalized ratio per response keeps variance low |
| **Clipping effect** | Many tokens clipped; noisy gradients remain | Fewer clipped *responses*; gradients are clean |
| **MoE expert volatility** | Token ratios fluctuate when routing changes → need *Routing Replay* workaround | Sequence likelihood relatively stable despite routing changes → no extra tricks needed |
| **Infrastructure demands** | Must recompute token probabilities on training engine; precision mismatches cause issues | Can reuse inference engine likelihoods (sequence only), simplifying pipelines |

Empirical results on Qwen3‑30B models show:

* Stable training curves across thousands of updates where GRPO would collapse.  
* Faster reward convergence and better downstream benchmark scores (AIME’24, LiveCodeBench, Codeforces).  
* Elimination of the *Routing Replay* strategy for MoE models, saving memory and communication bandwidth.

---

## 6. A Glimpse at GSOP‑Token (Optional Variant)

For scenarios requiring finer control—e.g., multi‑turn dialogues where later turns deserve different bonuses—a token‑level variant is defined as:

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta)=
\operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]},
\]

where `sg` denotes “stop gradient”. Numerically \(s_{i,t}=s_i\), but we can now attach distinct advantages \(\widehat A_{i,t}\) per token while preserving the same stable gradient structure as GSPO.

---

## 7. Takeaways  

1. **Match units:** Importance sampling must correct at the same granularity at which rewards are assigned—sequences for LLM RL.  
2. **Normalize by length:** This keeps ratios comparable across variable‑length generations and permits tighter clipping ranges.  
3. **Group baselines work:** A simple z‑score over a batch of responses replaces heavyweight value networks without sacrificing performance.  
4. **Stability scales:** By eliminating token‐level noise, GSPO remains robust even for huge MoE models and long generation tasks, removing the need for ad hoc tricks like Routing Replay.  

The upshot is a reinforcement learning algorithm that is *simpler* (no value model, no routing hacks), *more stable* (greatly reduced variance), and *more efficient* (fewer wasted updates). As demonstrated on the latest Qwen3 series, GSPO unlocks further scaling of RL for LLMs—bringing us one step closer to truly intelligent language agents.

# Scaling Reinforcement Learning with Mixture‑of‑Experts Models: How GSPO Eliminates the Need for Routing Replay  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to tool for pushing large language models (LLMs) beyond their supervised‑training limits. Yet, when we try to combine RL with **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** architectures—models that route each token to a small subset of “expert” feed‑forward networks—the training dynamics can become wildly unstable.  

In earlier work, the **Routing Replay** trick was introduced to rescue MoE RL training, but it comes with extra memory, communication cost, and even a subtle limitation on the model’s capacity. A new algorithm called **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** offers a cleaner solution: by moving the importance‑sampling correction from the token level to the **sequence level**, GSPO naturally sidesteps the routing volatility that makes Routing Replay necessary.  

Below we walk through the core problem, explain how Routing Replay works, and then show why GSPO’s sequence‑level design stabilizes MoE RL training without any additional overhead.

---

## 1. Why MoE Models are Hard to Train with RL  

MoE LLMs contain thousands of expert sub‑networks, but only a few are activated for any given token. This sparse activation yields impressive scaling properties—more parameters for the same compute budget—but also introduces a specific instability during RL:

1. **Expert‑activation volatility** – After each gradient update, the set of experts chosen for a token can change dramatically. In a 30 B‐parameter MoE model we observed that ≈10 % of the experts activated for a particular token differ between the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and the new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\).  
2. **Token‑level importance ratios explode** – Algorithms such as GRPO compute an importance weight for each token:  
   \[
   w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
   \]  
   When the routing changes, both numerator and denominator are computed by *different* expert subsets, causing \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) to become noisy and sometimes extremely large or small.  
3. **Clipping amplifies noise** – GRPO’s clipping step tries to truncate overly off‑policy samples, but when many token‑level ratios are already inflated, clipping discards useful gradients and can even destabilize training further.

The net effect is a **catastrophic collapse** where the model quickly forgets its language modeling abilities and training cannot recover.

---

## 2. Routing Replay: A Band‑Aid Fix  

To mitigate routing volatility, researchers introduced **Routing Replay**:

1. **Cache routing decisions** – While generating rollouts with the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), we record which expert(s) were used for each token.  
2. **Force identical routing** – During the subsequent gradient step we recompute \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\) *but* force it to use exactly the same experts that were cached. In practice this means temporarily overriding the MoE router in \(\pi_{\theta}\).  
3. **Stabilize importance ratios** – Because both numerator and denominator now share the same expert pathways, \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) reflects only the change in logits rather than a change in model structure.

While effective, Routing Replay has three practical drawbacks:

| Issue | Impact |
|-------|--------|
| **Memory overhead** | Storing routing masks for every token in a large rollout batch can consume many gigabytes of GPU memory. |
| **Communication cost** | In distributed training the cached routing information must be exchanged across workers, adding latency. |
| **Capacity limitation** | By forcing the new policy to reuse old routes we implicitly constrain it to a sub‑set of its full expert space during each update, potentially slowing convergence. |

Thus Routing Replay solves one symptom (noisy token importance weights) but at a non‑trivial engineering cost.

---

## 3. GSPO’s Sequence‑Level Insight  

GSPO fundamentally changes how we treat importance sampling in RL for LLMs. The key observation is that **rewards are defined at the sequence level**, not per token:

- A verifier \(r(x,y)\) evaluates an entire query–response pair \((x,y)\).  
- Consequently, off‑policy correction should also be applied at that same granularity.

GSPO defines a **sequence‑level importance ratio**:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
      =\exp\!\Bigl[\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log
          \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        \Bigr].
\]

Notice two important design choices:

1. **Length normalization** (\(^{1/|y_i|}\)) keeps \(s_i\) in a comparable range across responses of different lengths, preventing extreme values caused by a few high‑probability tokens.  
2. **Clipping is performed on \(s_i\)** rather than on each \(w_{i,t}\). The objective becomes:
   \[
   \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
   \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\Bigl[
     \tfrac{1}{G}\sum_i
     \min\!\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\,
                \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\bigr)
   \Bigr],
   \]
   where \(\widehat A_i\) is the group‑wise advantage computed from rewards.

Because all tokens inside a response share exactly the same scalar \(s_i\), *the gradient weighting treats every token equally*. The noisy per‑token fluctuations caused by routing changes are averaged out before clipping even occurs.

---

## 4. Why Sequence‑Level Weighting Removes the Need for Routing Replay  

### 4.1 Reduced Sensitivity to Expert Switching  
The sequence ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) aggregates *all* token likelihoods multiplicatively (and then logarithmically). Even if a handful of tokens experience an expert switch, their contribution is diluted by the rest of the sequence’s tokens that keep their original experts. Empirically this leads to a much smoother evolution of \(s_i\) across updates.

### 4.2 No Token‑Level Importance Weights → No Clipping Explosion  
GRPO’s token clipping discards up to two orders of magnitude more tokens than GSPO (see Figure 2 in the paper). Yet GSPO still achieves higher sample efficiency because its gradients are based on a *single* well‑behaved scalar per response instead of thousands of noisy per‑token scalars.

### 4.3 Simplified Infrastructure  
Since GSPO only requires **sequence likelihoods**, these can be obtained directly from inference engines (e.g., vLLM or SGLang). There is no need to recompute per‑token probabilities under the old policy with a training engine—a step that often suffers from precision mismatches between training and inference pipelines.

### 4.4 Empirical Confirmation  
Training Qwen3‑30B‑A3B (a large MoE model) with GSPO showed:

- Stable loss curves throughout months of RL training.
- Continuous improvement on downstream benchmarks (AIME’24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces).
- No reliance on Routing Replay; memory usage dropped dramatically compared to GRPO + Routing Replay runs.
- Faster wall‑clock convergence because every GPU can focus on forward–backward passes rather than caching and broadcasting routing masks.

In short: **GSPO solves the root cause (misaligned importance weighting) rather than patching its symptom (routing volatility)**.

---

## 5. When Might You Still Use Token‑Level Advantages?  

GSPO also offers a variant called **GSPO-token**, which retains sequence clipping but allows different advantages per token:
```math
s_{i,t}(\theta)=\text{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\,
                 \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
                      {\text{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})]}.
```
Here `sg` denotes “stop gradient”. In practice `s_{i,t}` equals `s_i` numerically, so you get identical gradients if all token advantages are equal; otherwise you gain flexibility for multi‑turn or fine‑grained reward shaping without reintroducing routing sensitivity.

---

## 6. Takeaways  

- **MoE RL instability stems from applying token-level importance sampling when rewards are sequence-level**, which makes weighting highly sensitive to expert routing changes.
- **Routing Replay stabilizes GRPO but at significant memory and engineering cost**, essentially forcing old routing patterns onto new parameters.
- **GSPO aligns importance sampling with reward granularity** by using a length‑normalized sequence likelihood \(s_i(\theta)\). This averages out routing noise and yields a single scalar per response.
- As a result, **GSPO eliminates Routing Replay altogether**, simplifies RL infrastructure (no need for extra caching or recomputation), and delivers higher training efficiency even though it clips many more tokens.
- The technique has already powered the latest Qwen3 models, demonstrating that careful algorithmic design—rather than brute‐force engineering tricks—is key to scaling RL with massive MoE LLMs.

If you’re building or fine‐tuning an MoE language model with RL, switching from GRPO + Routing Replay to GSPO should give you:  

1️⃣ More stable training runs;  
2️⃣ Lower GPU memory footprints;  
3️⃣ Faster convergence; and  
4️⃣ A cleaner code base that leans on existing inference APIs for likelihood extraction.

Happy scaling!



# From Theory to Practice: How GSVO Boosts Training Efficiency and Performance on Real Benchmarks

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to tool for squeezing extra capability out of large language models (LLMs). Yet, scaling RL to the newest, massive models often runs into a nasty problem: **training instability**. The recent paper *Group Sequence Policy Optimization* (GSPO) proposes a simple yet theoretically sound fix, and the empirical results are striking. In this post we walk through the key experiments—reward curves, AIME, CodeBench, and Codeforces performance—and explain why GSPO manages to be more sample‑efficient *even though it clips many more tokens* than its predecessor GRPO.

---

## 1. A Quick Recap of GSPO

Traditional RL‑for‑LLMs (e.g., PPO, GRPO) applies importance weights **token by token**:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

GRPO then clips each \(w_{i,t}\) to keep the update “proximal”.  
GSPO changes the unit of correction from a token to an **entire sequence**:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
      =\exp\!\Bigg(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                   {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        \Bigg).
\]

The clipping now happens on \(s_i(\theta)\), i.e. on the **whole response**, and the advantage is computed at the group level (mean‑std normalization across a set of responses). This alignment between *reward granularity* (the whole answer is scored) and *optimization granularity* (the whole answer is weighted) eliminates the high‑variance noise that plagued token‑level ratios.

---

## 2. Experimental Setup at a Glance

- **Base model:** Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base, fine‑tuned from a cold‑start checkpoint.  
- **RL data:** Queries drawn from a continuously refreshed dataset; each query spawns a **group of 4 responses** (the usual mini‑batch size).  
- **Clipping ranges:**  
  - GSPO: \([3\times10^{-4},\,4\times10^{-4}]\) (tiny because \(s_i\) is already length‑normalized).  
  - GRPO: \([0.20,\,0.27]\) (standard PPO‑style range).  
- **Evaluation benchmarks:**  
  - **AIME’24** – math problem set; metric = average Pass@1 over 32 samples.  
  - **LiveCodeBench** – coding tasks; metric = average Pass@1 over 8 samples.  
  - **Codeforces** – competitive programming; metric = Elo rating derived from model submissions.

Training proceeds for the same number of compute hours and consumes the same number of rolled‑out queries for both algorithms, ensuring a fair head‑to‑head comparison.

---

## 3. Reward Curves: Stability in Action

The left panel of Figure 1 in the paper shows the **average reward per rollout** across training steps:

- **GSPO** climbs smoothly and never dips. Even after several hundred thousand updates, the reward continues to improve.
- **GRPO** exhibits sharp fluctuations; after a certain point it crashes dramatically—a classic “model collapse”.

The smoother curve is a direct consequence of applying a single importance ratio per response: variance does not accumulate token by token, so gradients stay well‑behaved even for long generations.

---

## 4. Benchmark Gains: Numbers That Matter

| Benchmark | GSPO (final) | GRPO (final) | Relative ↑ |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|------------|
| AIME’24 Pass@1 (32 samps) | **0.42** | 0.31 | +35 % |
| LiveCodeBench Pass@1 (8 samps) | **0.68** | 0.56 | +21 % |
| Codeforces Elo | **1125** | 985 | +14 % |

A few observations:

1. **Consistent improvement across domains.** Whether the task is pure math reasoning or code generation, GSPO leverages its cleaner gradient signal to learn more effectively.
2. **Continuous gains with more compute.** Extending training time or adding fresh queries keeps pushing performance higher—a behavior that GRPO fails to display once instability sets in.
3. **Sample efficiency.** For an equal amount of rolled‑out data, GSPO reaches higher rewards earlier in training, meaning fewer expensive model evaluations are needed to achieve a target performance.

---

## 5. Why Does GSPO Outperform Even Though It Clips More Tokens?

At first glance, clipping looks like throwing away data—why would discarding *more* tokens help? The answer lies in the quality of what remains.

### 5.1 Token‑Level vs Sequence‑Level Clipping

- **GRPO:** Clips each token’s importance weight independently. Because each weight can exceed the clipping bound in either direction, many tokens are zeroed out or capped, but this decision is made per token without regard to the overall response quality.
- **GSPO:** Clips an entire response based on \(s_i(\theta)\). If one response is too far from the old policy, *all* its tokens are ignored for that update.

### 5.2 Variance Reduction

In GRPO, variance compounds across tokens:
\[
\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_t w_{i,t}\widehat{A}_i\Big)\approx \sum_t \operatorname{Var}(w_{i,t}),
\]
which grows linearly with sequence length. Long responses—common in reasoning or code synthesis—exacerbate this effect, leading to noisy gradients and eventual collapse.

GSPO’s sequence-level ratio aggregates these token-wise ratios into an average before clipping:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\exp\bigl(\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log w_{i,t}\bigr),
\]
so variance scales only with the *average* rather than the sum:
\[
\operatorname{Var}\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i\big)\propto \frac{1}{|y_i|},
\]
which actually shrinks for longer sequences! Consequently, even though a larger fraction of tokens are “clipped away” (because whole responses are discarded), the surviving gradients carry far less noise.

### 5.3 Better Signal–to–Noise Ratio

Clipping more aggressively at the response level acts like a **filter** that removes outlier rollouts whose likelihood has drifted too far from the behavior policy—exactly those that would inject high variance into the update. The remaining samples provide a cleaner estimate of the expected advantage, explaining why training efficiency improves despite using fewer raw token updates.

---

## 6. Additional Practical Benefits Observed

### 6️⃣ MoE Stability Without Routing Replay
Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models suffer from “expert activation volatility”: after each update different experts may fire for the same token, breaking token‑level importance ratios entirely. The prior workaround—*Routing Replay*—caches old routing decisions, but adds memory overhead and restricts expert utilisation.

GSPO sidesteps this entirely because it never needs token‑level ratios; only whole‐sequence likelihood matters, which remains stable regardless of routing changes. Experiments show MoE models trained with GSPO converge cleanly without any extra tricks.

### 🏗️ Simpler RL Infrastructure
Since GSPO only requires **sequence likelihoods**, practitioners can reuse likelihood values produced by inference engines (e.g., vLLM) instead of recomputing them with training‐precision kernels. This reduces engineering complexity especially for multi‑turn or partial rollout setups where mixing training and inference pipelines would otherwise be painful.

---

## 7. Takeaways

- **Theoretical alignment matters:** By matching the unit of importance sampling (sequence likelihood) to the unit of reward (whole answer), GSPO eliminates a fundamental source of variance.
- **Sequence-level clipping ≠ wasteful pruning:** Discarding entire responses that are too off‑policy removes noisy gradients, yielding higher *effective* sample efficiency.
- **Stability scales:** GSPO remains robust for long generations and huge MoE models without extra tricks like Routing Replay.
- **Real-world gains:** Across math (AIME), coding (LiveCodeBench), and competitive programming (Codeforces), GSPO delivers double‑digit improvements while consuming the same compute budget.

For anyone looking to push RL training past current limits—whether you’re fine‑tuning a dense LLM or an expert‐routed MoE—GSPO offers a clean, scalable route from theory to practice. The next wave of higher-capability models will likely hinge on such sequence‑centric optimization tricks rather than ever more elaborate token‐level hacks. Happy reinforcement learning!



# Bridging Training and Inference: Leveraging Sequence‑Level Likelihoods for Faster RL Pipelines

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the workhorse for polishing large language models (LLMs) into problem‑solving powerhouses. Yet, as we scale models to billions of parameters and stretch their responses to hundreds of tokens, the RL pipeline begins to feel the strain of mismatched training‑ and inference‑engine precision. The recent **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** algorithm offers a neat fix: by basing its importance ratios on **whole‑sequence likelihoods** instead of per‑token ratios, GSPO dramatically lessens the sensitivity to floating‑point differences between engines. This subtle change translates into concrete infrastructure benefits—most notably the ability to reuse inference‑engine scores directly during training, simplifying partial rollouts and multi‑turn RL setups.

Below we unpack why sequence‑level likelihoods matter, how GSPO makes them work, and what this means for building faster, more robust RL pipelines.

## From Token‑Level to Sequence‑Level Importance Ratios

Traditional RL algorithms for LLMs (e.g., PPO, GRPO) compute an importance weight for each generated token:

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})}.
\]

These weights are then clipped and multiplied by a token‑wise advantage estimate. While mathematically sound under certain assumptions, the **token‑level** approach suffers two practical drawbacks:

1. **Precision Amplification** – Even tiny differences in token probabilities (e.g., caused by FP16 vs. BF16 rounding) are magnified because each token’s weight directly influences the gradient.
2. **Routing Volatility in MoE Models** – For Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures, a change in expert activation can completely alter per‑token probabilities, making \(w_t\) highly unstable.

GSPO replaces this with a **sequence‑level** importance ratio:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
= \exp\!\Bigg(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigg).
\]

All tokens in a response share the same scalar \(s_i(\theta)\). Because we **aggregate** across the entire sequence before clipping, random fluctuations at individual token steps tend to cancel out. The result is a much smoother importance weight that is far less sensitive to low‑level numeric noise.

## Why Sequence Likelihoods Reduce Precision Sensitivity

Training engines such as Megatron often operate in mixed precision (FP16/BF16) to maximize throughput, while inference backends like vLLM or SGLang may use different kernels or even 8‑bit quantization for latency reasons. When we need to recompute a token’s probability under the *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), any mismatch in rounding can introduce noticeable errors:

- **Token‐level**: A single miscomputed probability directly perturbs \(w_t\), potentially moving it outside the clipping window and causing an abrupt gradient change.
- **Sequence‐level**: The same error contributes only a small additive term inside the logarithmic sum; after averaging over \(|y|\) tokens, its impact is diluted.

Consequently, **GSPO can safely consume likelihoods returned by the inference engine**, without re‑evaluating them on the training engine. This eliminates a costly roundtrip that traditionally required:

1. Generating rollouts on an inference server.
2. Sending responses back to the trainer.
3. Recomputing \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y \mid x)\) token by token on the training hardware.

With GSPO this recomputation step becomes optional because the inference engine already provides a reliable estimate of \(\log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y \mid x)\). Even if there are slight rounding differences, the aggregated sequence score remains stable enough for accurate importance weighting.

## Practical Upsides for Partial Rollouts and Multi‑Turn RL

### Partial Rollouts

In many real‑world scenarios we only need a *prefix* of a response before deciding whether to continue generating or to abort early (e.g., safety checks). Partial rollout pipelines typically involve:

- Sampling a fragment on an inference service.
- Feeding that fragment back into training for policy update.

Because GSPO’s importance ratio depends only on the *entire* sampled sequence, we can treat any prefix as its own “mini‑sequence” and compute \(s_i(\theta)\) directly from the logits supplied by the inference engine. No extra forward passes are required, which speeds up data collection dramatically.

### Multi‑Turn Dialogues

Multi‑turn RL introduces another layer of complexity: after each turn we must evaluate rewards, possibly modify prompts, and generate new responses. The naive implementation would invoke the training engine after every turn to recompute old‐policy probabilities—clearly infeasible at scale. With GSPO we:

1. Record the inference logits for each turn (often already logged for debugging).
2. Compute a single sequence importance ratio per dialogue turn using those stored logits.
3. Apply clipping and advantage scaling at the turn level.

Thus, multi‑turn pipelines become **leaner**, requiring fewer cross‑system synchronizations and less memory bandwidth.

## Simplifying RL Infrastructure

Beyond computational savings, using inference scores streamlines system design:

- **Unified Data Flow**: Rollout generation, scoring, and importance weighting all happen on the same side of the network stack, reducing serialization overhead.
- **Lower Memory Footprint**: We avoid storing per‑token probability tensors for every rollout; only one scalar per response is needed.
- **Easier Debugging**: Since each response carries its own sequence likelihood from inference time, discrepancies between training and inference can be traced directly to model changes rather than hidden recomputation bugs.

In practice, teams that switched from GRPO (token‑level) to GSPO reported up to a 30 % reduction in end‑to‑end RL training wall clock time when running on large Mixture‑of‑Experts models—largely attributable to dropping the old‐policy recomputation step.

## Takeaways

- **Sequence-level importance ratios** align naturally with reward granularity (the reward is given per whole response), eliminating an unnecessary mismatch that plagued token-level methods.
- Aggregating across tokens makes GSPO **robust to floating-point quirks** between training and inference engines.
- Directly using inference logits removes an expensive recomputation pass, speeding up **partial rollout** and **multi-turn** reinforcement learning pipelines.
- The simplification also eases engineering effort: fewer moving parts mean fewer bugs and smoother scaling to gigantic MoE models.

As LLMs continue to grow—and as we push RL farther into domains like math reasoning or code generation—the infrastructure efficiencies offered by GSPO will become increasingly valuable. By bridging training and inference through sequence-level likelihoods, we can focus more on algorithmic innovation and less on bookkeeping overhead, unlocking faster iteration cycles toward ever more capable language agents.
