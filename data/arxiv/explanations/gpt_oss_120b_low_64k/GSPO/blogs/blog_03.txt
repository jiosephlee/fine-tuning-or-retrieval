# Scaling Reinforcement Learning with Mixture‑of‑Experts Models: How GSPO Eliminates the Need for Routing Replay  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to tool for pushing large language models (LLMs) beyond their supervised‑training limits. Yet, when we try to combine RL with **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** architectures—models that route each token to a small subset of “expert” feed‑forward networks—the training dynamics can become wildly unstable.  

In earlier work, the **Routing Replay** trick was introduced to rescue MoE RL training, but it comes with extra memory, communication cost, and even a subtle limitation on the model’s capacity. A new algorithm called **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** offers a cleaner solution: by moving the importance‑sampling correction from the token level to the **sequence level**, GSPO naturally sidesteps the routing volatility that makes Routing Replay necessary.  

Below we walk through the core problem, explain how Routing Replay works, and then show why GSPO’s sequence‑level design stabilizes MoE RL training without any additional overhead.

---

## 1. Why MoE Models are Hard to Train with RL  

MoE LLMs contain thousands of expert sub‑networks, but only a few are activated for any given token. This sparse activation yields impressive scaling properties—more parameters for the same compute budget—but also introduces a specific instability during RL:

1. **Expert‑activation volatility** – After each gradient update, the set of experts chosen for a token can change dramatically. In a 30 B‐parameter MoE model we observed that ≈10 % of the experts activated for a particular token differ between the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and the new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\).  
2. **Token‑level importance ratios explode** – Algorithms such as GRPO compute an importance weight for each token:  
   \[
   w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
   \]  
   When the routing changes, both numerator and denominator are computed by *different* expert subsets, causing \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) to become noisy and sometimes extremely large or small.  
3. **Clipping amplifies noise** – GRPO’s clipping step tries to truncate overly off‑policy samples, but when many token‑level ratios are already inflated, clipping discards useful gradients and can even destabilize training further.

The net effect is a **catastrophic collapse** where the model quickly forgets its language modeling abilities and training cannot recover.

---

## 2. Routing Replay: A Band‑Aid Fix  

To mitigate routing volatility, researchers introduced **Routing Replay**:

1. **Cache routing decisions** – While generating rollouts with the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), we record which expert(s) were used for each token.  
2. **Force identical routing** – During the subsequent gradient step we recompute \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\) *but* force it to use exactly the same experts that were cached. In practice this means temporarily overriding the MoE router in \(\pi_{\theta}\).  
3. **Stabilize importance ratios** – Because both numerator and denominator now share the same expert pathways, \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) reflects only the change in logits rather than a change in model structure.

While effective, Routing Replay has three practical drawbacks:

| Issue | Impact |
|-------|--------|
| **Memory overhead** | Storing routing masks for every token in a large rollout batch can consume many gigabytes of GPU memory. |
| **Communication cost** | In distributed training the cached routing information must be exchanged across workers, adding latency. |
| **Capacity limitation** | By forcing the new policy to reuse old routes we implicitly constrain it to a sub‑set of its full expert space during each update, potentially slowing convergence. |

Thus Routing Replay solves one symptom (noisy token importance weights) but at a non‑trivial engineering cost.

---

## 3. GSPO’s Sequence‑Level Insight  

GSPO fundamentally changes how we treat importance sampling in RL for LLMs. The key observation is that **rewards are defined at the sequence level**, not per token:

- A verifier \(r(x,y)\) evaluates an entire query–response pair \((x,y)\).  
- Consequently, off‑policy correction should also be applied at that same granularity.

GSPO defines a **sequence‑level importance ratio**:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
      =\exp\!\Bigl[\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log
          \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        \Bigr].
\]

Notice two important design choices:

1. **Length normalization** (\(^{1/|y_i|}\)) keeps \(s_i\) in a comparable range across responses of different lengths, preventing extreme values caused by a few high‑probability tokens.  
2. **Clipping is performed on \(s_i\)** rather than on each \(w_{i,t}\). The objective becomes:
   \[
   \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
   \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\Bigl[
     \tfrac{1}{G}\sum_i
     \min\!\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\,
                \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\bigr)
   \Bigr],
   \]
   where \(\widehat A_i\) is the group‑wise advantage computed from rewards.

Because all tokens inside a response share exactly the same scalar \(s_i\), *the gradient weighting treats every token equally*. The noisy per‑token fluctuations caused by routing changes are averaged out before clipping even occurs.

---

## 4. Why Sequence‑Level Weighting Removes the Need for Routing Replay  

### 4.1 Reduced Sensitivity to Expert Switching  
The sequence ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) aggregates *all* token likelihoods multiplicatively (and then logarithmically). Even if a handful of tokens experience an expert switch, their contribution is diluted by the rest of the sequence’s tokens that keep their original experts. Empirically this leads to a much smoother evolution of \(s_i\) across updates.

### 4.2 No Token‑Level Importance Weights → No Clipping Explosion  
GRPO’s token clipping discards up to two orders of magnitude more tokens than GSPO (see Figure 2 in the paper). Yet GSPO still achieves higher sample efficiency because its gradients are based on a *single* well‑behaved scalar per response instead of thousands of noisy per‑token scalars.

### 4.3 Simplified Infrastructure  
Since GSPO only requires **sequence likelihoods**, these can be obtained directly from inference engines (e.g., vLLM or SGLang). There is no need to recompute per‑token probabilities under the old policy with a training engine—a step that often suffers from precision mismatches between training and inference pipelines.

### 4.4 Empirical Confirmation  
Training Qwen3‑30B‑A3B (a large MoE model) with GSPO showed:

- Stable loss curves throughout months of RL training.
- Continuous improvement on downstream benchmarks (AIME’24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces).
- No reliance on Routing Replay; memory usage dropped dramatically compared to GRPO + Routing Replay runs.
- Faster wall‑clock convergence because every GPU can focus on forward–backward passes rather than caching and broadcasting routing masks.

In short: **GSPO solves the root cause (misaligned importance weighting) rather than patching its symptom (routing volatility)**.

---

## 5. When Might You Still Use Token‑Level Advantages?  

GSPO also offers a variant called **GSPO-token**, which retains sequence clipping but allows different advantages per token:
```math
s_{i,t}(\theta)=\text{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\,
                 \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
                      {\text{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})]}.
```
Here `sg` denotes “stop gradient”. In practice `s_{i,t}` equals `s_i` numerically, so you get identical gradients if all token advantages are equal; otherwise you gain flexibility for multi‑turn or fine‑grained reward shaping without reintroducing routing sensitivity.

---

## 6. Takeaways  

- **MoE RL instability stems from applying token-level importance sampling when rewards are sequence-level**, which makes weighting highly sensitive to expert routing changes.
- **Routing Replay stabilizes GRPO but at significant memory and engineering cost**, essentially forcing old routing patterns onto new parameters.
- **GSPO aligns importance sampling with reward granularity** by using a length‑normalized sequence likelihood \(s_i(\theta)\). This averages out routing noise and yields a single scalar per response.
- As a result, **GSPO eliminates Routing Replay altogether**, simplifies RL infrastructure (no need for extra caching or recomputation), and delivers higher training efficiency even though it clips many more tokens.
- The technique has already powered the latest Qwen3 models, demonstrating that careful algorithmic design—rather than brute‐force engineering tricks—is key to scaling RL with massive MoE LLMs.

If you’re building or fine‐tuning an MoE language model with RL, switching from GRPO + Routing Replay to GSPO should give you:  

1️⃣ More stable training runs;  
2️⃣ Lower GPU memory footprints;  
3️⃣ Faster convergence; and  
4️⃣ A cleaner code base that leans on existing inference APIs for likelihood extraction.

Happy scaling!
