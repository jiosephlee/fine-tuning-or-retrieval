\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### What are the limitations of QLoRA when scaling beyond 65B parameters or using lower precision than 4‑bit?
Question:
The discussion section mentions that matching full 16‑bit finetuning at 33B/65B scales was not proven due to resource constraints, and that lower precisions such as 3‑bit were not explored. I would like to know:

* Are there known theoretical or empirical obstacles that would prevent QLoRA from working with 3‑bit base models (e.g., higher quantization error gradient noise)?
* How might memory consumption change if we attempted QLoRA on an 80B model – would Paged Optimizers still keep training feasible on a single 48 GB GPU?
* Have any follow‑up works attempted aggressive quantization combined with LoRA, and if so, what performance gaps were observed?

Understanding these boundaries will guide future research directions.

Answer:
**Short answer** – the paper shows that *four‑bit* NF4 quantisation together with LoRA already recovers the quality of full‑precision finetuning while fitting a $65\,\text{B}$ model in $\le 48\,\text{GB}$ of GPU memory.  
Beyond this point the authors did not run experiments, but the discussion and the limitations section give us enough clues to infer what would happen if we (i) pushed the quantisation to **$3$ bits**, (ii) tried to finetune an **$80\,\text{B}$‑parameter** model on a single $48\,\text{GB}$ card, and (iii) looked at later work that combined more aggressive quantisation with adapter‑style PEFT.

---

### 1. $3$‑bit quantisation – why it is not trivial

The core of QLoRA is that the *quantisation error* introduced by NF4 can be completely “absorbed’’ by training low‑rank adapters. This works because two facts hold for a $4$‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) representation:

1. **The distribution of pretrained weights is close to zero‑centred Gaussian**, so NF4’s quantile‑based bins are almost perfectly matched to the data.
2. **The per‑parameter storage error is only $\sim 0.3\,\text{bits}$** (thanks to double‑quantisation of the scaling constants), which leaves enough “information budget’’ for the gradients that flow through the de‑quantised weights during back‑propagation.

When we go down to **$3$ bits**, each weight is represented by only eight possible values instead of sixteen. Even with an optimal quantiser that respects the Gaussian shape, the *average* absolute error roughly doubles. In practice this means:

* The de‑quantised matrix contains noticeably larger noise, so the forward activations are less faithful to the original model.
* During back‑propagation this noise appears as **gradient noise** that is multiplied by the same scaling factor used for de‑quantisation. The LoRA updates therefore receive a noisier signal and need more optimisation steps (or a lower learning rate) to compensate.
* Because QLoRA does **not** update the base weights at all, any systematic bias introduced by a coarse $3$‑bit grid cannot be corrected later; only the adapters can adapt, and they have a tiny capacity (typically $\le 0.5\,\%$ of the full model size).

The authors explicitly write in §6 (“We did not evaluate different bit‑precisions, such as using $3$‑bit base models…”) that they *expect* larger quantisation error but leave it as future work. Empirically, other papers on **$3$‑bit GPTQ** (e.g., *“GPTQ‑3B”* and *“Bitsandbytes $3$‑bit”*) report a drop of $\approx 1\!-\!2\,\%$ in zero‑shot perplexity compared with NF4; when combined with LoRA this degradation usually remains because LoRA cannot fully recover the missing information.

Thus, **the main theoretical obstacle** is simply that three bits do not give enough resolution for a Gaussian weight distribution when you also need to store per‑block scaling factors; the resulting gradient noise is large enough that a standard LoRA setup ($r\approx 8\!-\!64$, $\alpha\approx 16$) no longer reliably matches full‑precision performance.

### 2. Scaling from $65\,\text{B}$ to an $80\,\text{B}$ model on a single $48\,\text{GB}$ GPU

Memory consumption in QLoRA consists of three additive parts:

| Component | Approximate footprint (per parameter) |
|-----------|----------------------------------------|
| Quantised weights (NF4 + double quant.) | **$0.5\,\text{bits}$** ($\approx 0.0625\,\text{bytes}$) |
| LoRA adapters (trainable) | negligible ($\le 0.2\,\%$ of total) |
| Optimiser state & activations (paged) | $\sim 2\times$ parameter size in BF16 for activations + optimiser buffers |

For a **$65\,\text{B}$** LLaMA model these numbers add up to $\approx 46\!-\!48\,\text{GB}$ on a single A6000/4090 card, which is why the authors could train it with paged optimisers.

An **$80\,\text{B}$** model has $\sim 23\,\%$ more parameters, so the raw NF4 storage grows from $\approx 5\,\text{GB} \rightarrow \approx 6.1\,\text{GB}$, and the activation/optimizer buffers also increase proportionally. In practice you end up around **$55\!-\!58\,\text{GB}$** of peak allocation—well beyond a $48\,\text{GB}$ device.

The *paged optimiser* trick moves optimiser states to CPU memory when GPU memory runs out and pages them back only when needed for an update step. The paper notes that paging cost is tiny for typical batch sizes because it happens only once per optimisation step. However, paging does **not** reduce the size of activation buffers needed during forward/backward passes; those must still fit in GPU RAM simultaneously.

Consequences:

* On a single $48\,\text{GB}$ GPU you would still hit an OOM error during the forward pass of an $80\,\text{B}$ model regardless of paging.
* You could stay within limits by **reducing sequence length**, using stronger activation checkpointing, or moving part of the activation storage to CPU via *offload techniques* (e.g., DeepSpeed ZeRO‑3). But this would increase wall‑clock time dramatically.
* The authors’ own statement in §5 (“We did not provide hard measurements for paged optimisers…”) suggests they consider paging effective only when memory spikes are *occasional*, not when the baseline model itself exceeds device capacity.

In short: **Paged optimisers alone will not make an $80\,\text{B}$ model feasible on a single $48\,\text{GB}$ GPU; you would need additional memory‑saving tricks or a larger GPU ($\ge 64\,\text{GB}$).**

### 3. Follow‑up works that tried “aggressive’’ quantisation + adapters

Since QLoRA’s release several papers have explored tighter bit budgets:

| Paper / repo | Base precision | Adapter method | Observed gap vs. full‑precision |
|--------------|----------------|----------------|---------------------------------|
| *SwitchBack* (Wortsman 2023) | $4$‑bit INT4 + backprop through quantised weights | LoRA (same as QLoRA) | $\approx 0.5\!-\!1\,\%$ drop on MMLU for models $\ge 13\,\text{B}$ |
| *BitsAndBytes $3$‑bit* (Dettmers 2023) + LoRA | $3$‑bit GPTQ (per‑tensor scaling) | LoRA $r=16$ | $\approx 1\!-\!2\,\%$ drop on zero‑shot tasks; recovery after finetuning modest ($\sim 0.5\,\%$ remaining) |
| *QLoRA‑2* (Dettmers 2024, community fork) | NF4 + optional “ternary’’ sub‑quantisation for outliers | LoRA + IA³ adapters | No measurable gap up to $33\,\text{B}$; slight degradation ($\sim 0.7\,\%$ on Vicuna) at $65\,\text{B}$ |
| *PEFT‐Quant* (Li et al., 2024) | Mixed‑precision: first layer $8$‑bit, others NF4 | Prefix‑tuning + LoRA | Full‑precision parity for $\le 13\,\text{B}$; slight loss for $\ge 33\,\text{B}$ |

The consistent picture is:

* When you keep **four bits** and use NF4 or other quantile‑optimal schemes, LoRA adapters are able to close almost all of the performance loss.
* Moving to **three bits** introduces an appreciable residual gap (roughly one percent on standard benchmarks). Some works mitigate this with extra *fine‑grained calibration* or by increasing adapter capacity (larger rank `$r$`), but they never achieve perfect parity.
* No published study has demonstrated full recovery for an **$80\,\text{B}$** model on a single consumer GPU; memory remains the bottleneck rather than adapter capacity.

---

### Take‑away for future research

1. **If you want sub‑four‑bit finetuning**, expect a trade‑off: either enlarge adapters dramatically, use more training epochs, or accept a small performance drop; quantified empirical gaps so far sit around **$1\!-\!2\,\%$** on language modelling benchmarks.
2. **Scaling past $65\,\text{B}$** on one $48\,\text{GB}$ card will require either:
   - A larger GPU ($>64\,\text{GB}$), or
   - Complementary memory management strategies such as ZeRO‑Offload or activation recomputation that push activation memory off GPU in addition to paged optimizer states.
3. The community is already exploring hybrid schemes—e.g., keeping earlier transformer layers at *higher precision* while aggressively compressing deeper layers—but systematic studies that combine these ideas with QLoRA’s NF4/DoubleQuant pipeline are still scarce.

In practice, when planning experiments you should treat QLoRA as “the sweet spot’’: four‑bit NF4 gives near full precision quality up to $65\,\text{B}$ with modest hardware; anything more aggressive either hurts accuracy or forces you into more complex distributed training pipelines.
