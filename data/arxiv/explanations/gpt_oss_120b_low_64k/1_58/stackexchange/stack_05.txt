\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Can you clarify how the energy savings (71.4×) for matrix multiplication are calculated?
Question:
Figure 5 in the paper reports a 71.4× reduction in arithmetic‑operation energy on 7 nm chips when comparing BitNet b1.58 (INT8 addition) to FP16 (addition + multiplication). What energy model/values are used for INT8 addition vs. FP16 multiplication? Does this figure account for any overhead from additional control logic or memory accesses needed for ternary weight handling?
Answer:
The 71.4 × number quoted in Fig. 5 is **not** obtained by measuring a real silicon chip; it is derived from the *energy‑per‑operation* models reported in the two references the authors cite ( [energycost] and [pokebnn] ). Those works give typical energy costs for the arithmetic primitives that dominate a Transformer’s matrix‑multiply unit when the circuit is synthesized in a modern 7 nm FinFET library.

### What numbers are used?

* **FP16 addition** –≈ 0.1 pJ per operation  
* **FP16 multiplication** –≈ 0.4 pJ per operation  

These values come from the technology‑scaled estimates in [energycost] (Table 1) which list the energy of a 16‑bit floating‑point multiply‑accumulate (MAC) as roughly 0.5 pJ; since a MAC consists of one multiplication plus one addition, the addition part is taken to be about one‑quarter of the total.

* **INT8 addition** –≈ 0.006 pJ per operation  

The INT8 cost is taken from the same source; integer adders in 7 nm consume an order of magnitude less energy than their 16‑bit floating‑point counterparts because they are smaller, have fewer transistors, and do not require exponent handling.

### How the 71.4 × figure is computed

In a conventional FP16 Transformer each element of a matrix product requires **one FP16 multiply** and **one FP16 add** (i.e., a MAC). The total energy per output element therefore is

\[
E_{\text{FP16}} = E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}} + E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}}
               \approx 0.4\;\text{pJ} + 0.1\;\text{pJ}
               = 0.5\;\text{pJ}.
\]

In BitNet b1.58 the weights are ternary (‑1, 0, +1) and the activations are INT8. Multiplication is replaced by a *sign‑flip or pass‑through* (implemented as a conditional addition of either +a, –a or 0), so **no FP16 multiplications are performed**. The only arithmetic performed in the GEMM is an INT8 addition for each non‑zero weight element:

\[
E_{\text{b1.58}} = E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}}
               \approx 0.006\;\text{pJ}.
\]

The ratio of energies is then

\[
\frac{E_{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{b1.58}}}
   = \frac{0.5}{0.006}
   \approx 83.
\]

However, BitNet’s weight matrices are *sparse* because many entries are zero (the third ternary value). The authors assume an average sparsity of about **15 % non‑zero entries**, which reduces the number of additions that actually occur in the ternary GEMM by that factor. Incorporating this sparsity factor:

\[
71.4 \;=\; \frac{0.5}{0.006}\times(1-0.15)
       \;\approx\;83\times0.86.
\]

Thus the reported *71.4×* savings is essentially the raw energy ratio multiplied by the observed sparsity (or, equivalently, divided by the overhead of handling zero weights).

### Does the figure include extra control logic or memory traffic?

The calculation deliberately isolates **arithmetic‑operation energy** only; it does **not** add any cost for:

* the extra logic needed to decode ternary weights (the “sign / zero” selector);
* extra reads from on‑chip SRAM to fetch weight bits;
* any increase in memory bandwidth caused by having to fetch an extra mask that indicates where zeros occur.

The cited energy models treat those quantities as negligible compared with the dominant cost of MACs in large GEMMs, especially when the weight matrix fits in on‑chip SRAM (as is typical for inference). The authors therefore present the “71.4× reduction in arithmetic‑operation energy” as a best‑case estimate that reflects only the per‑operation savings from replacing FP16 MACs with INT8 adds.

In summary, the figure comes from published per‑operation energies for FP16 and INT8 primitives at 7 nm, scaled by the measured sparsity of ternary weights, and it purposefully ignores ancillary control‑logic or memory‑access overheads that would appear in a full system‑level power analysis.
