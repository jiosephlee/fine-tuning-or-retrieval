\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 4: **Quality Evaluation of the DeepSeekMath Corpus**

The DeepSeekMath project places a strong emphasis on establishing that the massive 120 B‑token mathematical corpus harvested from Common Crawl is indeed of high quality and, consequently, useful for improving the mathematical reasoning abilities of language models.  To make this claim concrete, the authors adopt a “learn‑by‑doing’’ methodology: they train a modest 1.3 B‑parameter language model on several publicly available math‑oriented corpora—including the newly constructed DeepSeekMath Corpus—as well as on three competing corpora that have been used in prior work.  By measuring how each model performs on a diverse set of downstream mathematics benchmarks, the authors can directly compare the relative usefulness of the underlying pre‑training data.  

The following sections describe (1) the competing corpora that serve as baselines, (2) the experimental configuration used for all training runs, (3) the suite of few‑shot chain‑of‑thought (CoT) evaluations employed, (4) the learning‑curve analysis that reveals how performance scales with token exposure, and (5) multilingual considerations and ablation findings that highlight the broader impact of the DeepSeekMath data.

---

### Competing Corpora: MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof‑Pile‑2  

Before introducing the DeepSeekMath Corpus, three alternative math‑focused datasets are examined:

1. **MathPile** – a multi‑source collection aggregating textbooks, Wikipedia articles, ProofWiki entries, Common Crawl extracts, StackExchange discussions, and a dominant fraction of arXiv papers.  The total size is roughly 8.9 B tokens, with more than 85 % originating from arXiv.  MathPile represents an approach that attempts to capture a broad spectrum of mathematical text but relies heavily on scientific publications.

2. **OpenWebMath** – a curated subset of Common Crawl filtered specifically for mathematical material.  It contains about 13.6 B tokens and is derived using a fastText classifier trained on high‑quality web pages (the seed being OpenWebMath itself).  This corpus is English‑centric and has been used as a benchmark for web‑mined math data.

3. **Proof‑Pile‑2** – an amalgam that merges OpenWebMath with “AlgebraicStack” (≈10.3 B tokens of mathematical code) and arXiv papers (≈28.0 B tokens).  The authors follow a prescribed ratio of `arXiv : Web : Code = 2 : 4 : 1` when mixing these sources, thereby blending formal research articles with code snippets that encode mathematical algorithms.

These three baselines differ not only in size but also in composition (e.g., proportion of code vs. natural language vs. formal LaTeX).  By training identical models on each corpus under the same regimen, any performance differences can be attributed primarily to data quality and domain relevance rather than architectural or optimization factors.

---

### Experimental Setup (1.3 B Model, 150 B Tokens)

All comparative experiments share a common training scaffold:

* **Model Architecture** – The base model is a decoder‑only transformer with roughly 1.3 B parameters (`DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B`).  This size mirrors many publicly released open‑source LLMs and is small enough to permit multiple full‑epoch trainings while still being expressive enough to learn sophisticated reasoning patterns.

* **Training Budget** – Each model processes exactly **150 B tokens** from its respective corpus.  For corpora larger than this budget (e.g., Proof‑Pile‑2), the training proceeds over multiple passes; for smaller corpora the data are simply repeated until the token count is met.

* **Optimizer & Schedule** – Training employs AdamW with β₁=0.9, β₂=0.95, weight decay=0.1.  A multi‑step learning‑rate schedule raises the peak LR to **5.3 × 10⁻⁴** after 2 k warmup steps; thereafter it decays to 31.6 % of the peak at 80 % of total steps and finally to 10 % at the final 10 % of training.

* **Batching & Context Length** – Mini‑batches contain **4 M tokens**, and each sequence is truncated/padded to a context window of **4 k** tokens.  These settings mirror those used in larger DeepSeek models, ensuring comparability.

* **Implementation Framework** – Training runs are executed with HAI‑LLM, an efficient lightweight training system designed for large language models; this guarantees that memory usage and compute efficiency remain comparable across all experiments.

By standardizing every hyperparameter except for the source corpus, any observed differences in downstream performance can be confidently interpreted as reflecting intrinsic differences in data quality or relevance.

---

### Few‑Shot CoT Evaluation on Eight Benchmarks  

To probe mathematical reasoning capabilities after pre‑training, each model is evaluated with **few‑shot chain‑of‑thought prompting**, a technique wherein several exemplars containing step‑by‑step reasoning are supplied before asking the model to solve a new problem.  The evaluation suite comprises eight well‑established benchmarks spanning both English and Chinese contexts:

| Benchmark | Language | Task Type | Typical Difficulty |
|-----------|----------|----------|-------------------|
| GSM8K | English | Grade‑school quantitative reasoning | Elementary / middle school |
| MATH | English | Competition‐level problem solving | High school / early college |
| SAT Math | English | Multiple‐choice standardized test | High school |
| OCW Courses | English | University‐level quantitative problems | College |
| MMLU‐STEM | English | Multiple choice across STEM subjects | Broad |
| MGSM‑zh | Chinese | Translated GSM8K style problems | Elementary / middle school |
| CMATH | Chinese | Chinese competition‐level reasoning | High school / early college |
| Gaokao‐MathCloze / MathQA | Chinese | Chinese national exam style questions | High school |

For each benchmark, the model receives **four exemplars** drawn from its training split (or from published examples when no official split exists), then generates an answer for each test item without any external tools or code execution.  Accuracy is computed as the proportion of exact matches between model output and gold answer.

Because all models share identical prompting templates and exemplar selections, performance differences arise solely from their pre‑training experience.

### “Learning Curve” Analysis Across Token Scales  

Beyond a single static comparison at 150 B tokens, the authors also examine how performance evolves as more tokens from each corpus are consumed:

* For each corpus they train intermediate checkpoints after ingesting roughly **40 B**, **80 B**, **120 B**, and **160 B** tokens.
* These checkpoints are evaluated on all eight benchmarks using the same few‑shot CoT protocol.
* Plotting accuracy versus token count yields *learning curves* that reveal two key phenomena:
  
  1. **Steeper Gains for DeepSeekMath** – The DeepSeekMath Corpus produces markedly higher slopes early on; even at only **40 B tokens**, its model already surpasses models trained on Proof‑Pile‑2 at **120 B tokens**.  
     
     This suggests that each token from DeepSeekMath carries more “useful” information for solving math problems than tokens from competitors.
  
  2. **Plateauing for Smaller Corpora** – Models trained on MathPile or OpenWebMath quickly reach a performance ceiling: after about **80–100 B tokens**, gains become marginal despite continued exposure to additional data.
     
     The plateau aligns with the intuition that these corpora contain considerable redundancy (e.g., repeated arXiv abstracts) or noisy content that does not further enrich reasoning abilities.

Overall, these curves reinforce the claim that *quality outweighs sheer quantity*: even though the DeepSeekMath Corpus is roughly seven times larger than Minerva’s web math subset and nine times larger than OpenWebMath, its superior curation makes each token more valuable for downstream mathematics.

---

### Multilingual Impact and Ablation Results  

A distinctive property of the DeepSeekMath Corpus is its **multilingual composition**, dominated by English but also containing substantial Chinese web material harvested via domain‐level classification (e.g., mathoverflow.net.cn, zhihu.com topics).  

The evaluation results confirm two complementary findings:

1. **Cross‑Language Gains** – On English benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH) models trained on DeepSeekMath achieve higher absolute accuracies than those trained on any English‐only corpus (e.g., MathPile).  
   Moreover, when evaluated on Chinese benchmarks (MGSM‑zh, CMATH), DeepSeekMath consistently outperforms competing corpora whose training data are almost exclusively English.  
   This indicates that exposure to high‐quality non‑English mathematical text benefits reasoning even when solving problems posed in another language—likely because many underlying concepts (definitions, theorem statements) share universal symbolic structures.

2. **Ablation Insights** – The authors perform an ablation where they restrict training to only the *English* portion of DeepSeekMath while keeping token count constant.  
   The resulting model exhibits a measurable drop in Chinese benchmark performance but only a modest change on English tasks, confirming that multilingual data directly contributes to multilingual competence rather than merely acting as generic noise.

Together these observations underscore that building a mathematically rich corpus should not neglect non‑English sources; even modest amounts of high-quality foreign-language math content can boost overall reasoning robustness.

---

## Synthesis

By systematically training an identical 1.3 B parametric model on four distinct corpora—MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof‑Pile‑2, and the newly assembled DeepSeekMath Corpus—the authors demonstrate empirically that:

* The **DeepSeekMath Corpus** yields superior downstream performance across all eight benchmarks.
* Its advantage is evident early in training and persists as token exposure grows, yielding *steeper learning curves*.
* Multilingual coverage further enhances performance on non-English tasks without hurting English results.
* Competing corpora either suffer from excessive reliance on arXiv (which proved ineffective for reasoning gain) or lack sufficient diversity/size to sustain progress beyond an early plateau.

Consequently, these experiments constitute a rigorous validation pipeline: choosing high‐quality web pages via fastText classification, iteratively refining seed data through domain analysis and human annotation, and finally confirming effectiveness through downstream few-shot CoT evaluation and learning‐curve analysis.  

The methodology described here offers a blueprint for future researchers who wish to assess new domain-specific pre‑training corpora—particularly in fields where structured reasoning is crucial—by coupling *controlled pre-training experiments* with *broad multilingual benchmark suites*.
