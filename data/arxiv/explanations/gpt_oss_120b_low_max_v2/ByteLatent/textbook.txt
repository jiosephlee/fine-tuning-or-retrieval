# Introduction to Byte‑Level Language Modeling  

Large language models (LLMs) have become the dominant paradigm for natural‑language processing, yet virtually every modern LLM still depends on a **fixed‑vocabulary tokenizer** as a preprocessing step.  A tokenizer groups raw bytes (or Unicode code points) into a static set of sub‑word tokens that the model then consumes.  Although this heuristic has enabled impressive scaling, it also introduces systematic shortcomings that become more apparent as models are applied to diverse domains, noisy inputs, or low‑resource languages.  The **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)** demonstrates that it is possible to train a byte‑level LLM at scales comparable to token‑based models while gaining efficiency and robustness.  This chapter explains why traditional tokenizers are limiting, surveys earlier byte‑level approaches and their scaling problems, and presents the core ideas behind BLT: *dynamic patches* that adaptively allocate compute according to the information density of the data.

---

## Limitations of Traditional Tokenizers  

### Domain and Modality Bias  
Tokenizers are trained on a specific corpus using compression heuristics (e.g., byte‑pair encoding, BPE).  The resulting vocabulary reflects the statistical regularities of that corpus, so tokens tend to align with frequent words or morphemes in the training domain.  When the model later encounters data from a different domain (code, scientific text, or social media) the fixed token set can fragment common strings into many rare tokens, forcing the model to allocate the same amount of computation to each token regardless of its predictive difficulty.  This mismatch can degrade performance on specialized domains.

### Sensitivity to Input Noise  
Because tokens are deterministic mappings from raw bytes, any perturbation that alters byte boundaries—such as misspellings, random character insertions/deletions, or encoding errors—typically changes the token sequence dramatically.  A single corrupted character can cause an entire downstream token to be mis‑identified, causing a cascade of errors in the representation.  Empirically, token‑based LLMs show large drops in accuracy when evaluated on *noisy* variants of standard benchmarks (e.g., HellaSwag with random character drops).

### Multilingual Inequity and Orthographic Blindness  
A sub‑word vocabulary is inevitably biased toward languages that dominate the pretraining data.  Languages with larger character sets or different scripts receive fewer dedicated tokens; they are forced to be represented as longer sequences of uncommon sub‑words.  Moreover, tokenizers hide *orthographic* information: the model never sees individual characters or diacritics directly, which hampers tasks that require fine‑grained phonological or morphological reasoning (e.g., grapheme‑to‑phoneme conversion).  Studies have shown that tokenizers lack explicit knowledge of spelling and phonology that would otherwise be easy for a model operating on raw bytes.

---

## Historical Attempts at Byte‑Level Models and Their Scalability Issues  

### Early Character‑Level RNNs  
The first generative models at the character level were recurrent neural networks (RNNs) such as those by Sutskever et al. (2011) and Mikolov et al. (2012).  These models proved that raw character sequences can be learned without any tokenization, allowing open vocabularies and robustness to out‑of‑vocabulary words.  However, RNNs suffer from limited context windows and slow training; scaling them beyond millions of parameters was infeasible.

### Deep Character Transformers  
Al Raei et al. (2019) introduced very deep transformer architectures trained on characters with auxiliary losses.  While these models closed part of the gap to word‑level baselines on modest datasets, they still lagged behind tokenized Transformers on large–scale benchmarks because *every* character participates in self‑attention, inflating both memory and computational cost quadratically with sequence length.

### Efficient Byte Transformers (Megabyte, SpaceByte)  
More recent works such as **Megabyte** (Yu et al., 2023) proposed *static* patching: grouping every *K* bytes into a fixed‑size “patch” before feeding them to a Transformer.  This reduces sequence length but does not adapt compute to content complexity; high‑entropy regions still receive only one transformer step per patch.  

**SpaceByte** (Slagle et al., 2024) refined this idea by cutting patches at whitespace or other “space‑like” bytes.  Since natural language often places word boundaries at spaces, this heuristic gives longer patches for predictable intra‑word text and forces a transformer step at each word boundary—a better allocation of compute than pure strided patching.  Nonetheless, space‐based patching cannot handle languages without clear whitespace delimiters and still lacks a principled way to vary patch size based on *actual* information density.

### Attention‑Free or Alternative Architectures  
Other research has sidestepped quadratic attention by using convolutional or state–space layers (e.g., MambaByte).  These approaches improve training speed but often require very deep stacks or large hidden dimensions to match tokenized baselines; they also still treat every byte uniformly during forward passes.

The common thread across these attempts is that **the dominant computational cost at scale is not attention** but rather the feed‑forward networks applied at every step of a long sequence.  When each step corresponds to a single byte or small fixed patch, flops multiply dramatically as we increase model size or data volume.

---

## Key Ideas of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)  

BLT resolves the above challenges by **dynamically constructing patches** whose size reflects the *predictive entropy* of upcoming bytes, and by organizing computation into three interacting modules:

1. **Local Encoder** – a lightweight byte‑level transformer that ingests raw bytes and produces latent patch embeddings.  
2. **Latent Global Transformer** – a large autoregressive transformer that operates only on these patch embeddings; it therefore executes far fewer steps than a conventional bytewise model.  
3. **Local Decoder** – another lightweight transformer that expands global patch outputs back into raw bytes.

Below we unpack each component and explain how dynamic patching guides compute allocation.

### Entropy‑Based Dynamic Patching  

Instead of using hand‑crafted delimiters (whitespace) or a fixed stride, BLT trains an auxiliary **byte‑level language model** (“entropy model”) on the same corpus used for pretraining the main LLM.  For each position *i* in a document we compute the next–byte entropy under this auxiliary distribution:

\[
H(x_i)= -\sum_{v\in\mathcal V} p_e(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})\,
\log p_e(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) .
\]

High entropy indicates that many possible continuations are plausible—a region where prediction is “hard”.  BLT defines patch boundaries when entropy exceeds either:

- A **global threshold** \(\theta_g\): \(H(x_t) > \theta_g\), or  
- An **approximate monotonicity condition**: \(H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r\),

the latter encouraging patches to contain monotonically decreasing entropy (i.e., start with a hard prediction then become easier).  

During data loading we perform a lightweight preprocessing pass that marks every start–of–patch bit; this yields a **patching function** \(f_p\) mapping each byte index \(i\) to \(\{0,1\}\), where “1” denotes a new patch. Crucially, this function is *incremental*: given any prefix \(\mathbf{x}_{<i}\), we can decide whether position *i* begins a new patch without looking ahead—an essential property for autoregressive generation.

The result is an adaptive segmentation where **longer patches appear in predictable regions** (e.g., within common words or repetitive code), while **short patches surface around high‐entropy events** such as word starts, punctuation after spaces, or rare symbols.

### Three‑Tier Architecture  

#### Local Encoder  
The encoder embeds each raw byte using a learnable matrix \(\mathbb{R}^{256\times h_{\mathcal{E}}}\).  To enrich these embeddings we add *hash n‑gram embeddings*: for each n‑gram \(g_{i,n}= \{b_{i-n+1},...,b_i\}\) (with \(n =3,\dots ,8\)) we compute a fast rolling polynomial hash and look up an embedding from a fixed‐size table \(E^{\text{hash}}_n\). The augmented embedding for position *i* becomes  

\[
e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3}^{8} E^{\text{hash}}_n(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n})).
\]

These augmentations give the encoder context about local byte patterns without blowing up memory.

Next come alternating transformer layers (with *local block‐causal* attention over a sliding window \(w_{\mathcal{E}}\)) and **cross‐attention blocks** inspired by Perceiver architectures.   For each current patch \(p_j\), we construct a query vector by pooling the byte representations belonging to that patch; keys/values come from all byte representations within the local window. The cross‐attention thus aggregates information from the raw bytes into a compact latent vector \(p_j\).

#### Latent Global Transformer  
All patch embeddings \(\{p_j\}\) are fed into \(\mathcal G\), an autoregressive Transformer with many layers (\(l_{\mathcal G}\)) and hidden size \(h_{\mathcal G}\).  Because patches already encapsulate multiple bytes—often several dozen—the global model processes far fewer steps than an equivalent bytewise model would need.  The mask used is *block causal*: each patch may attend to all earlier patches but not future ones, preserving autoregression at the patch level.

#### Local Decoder  
After \(\mathcal G\) emits output latent patches \(\{o_j\}\), the decoder reconstructs raw bytes via another cross‐attention stage followed by lightweight transformer layers (\(l_{\mathcal D} \ll l_{\mathcal G}\)).   Here queries come from provisional byte embeddings while keys/values derive from global outputs; after cross-attention the decoder runs standard self‑attention over these provisional bytes before finally projecting onto the 256 possible byte values.

The three-tier design concentrates most FLOPs in \(\mathcal G\); however `when` \(\mathcal G\) is invoked depends entirely on the entropy‐driven patch boundaries: predictable spans bypass expensive global computation altogether by staying inside long patches handled only by cheap local modules.

### Computational Benefits  

Let \(n_{\text{ctx}}\) be the total number of bytes in a training/example window and let \(n_p = n_{\text{ctx}} / \text{(average patch size)}\).   The per–byte FLOP count decomposes roughly into

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} &=
 \frac{\text{Transf.FLOP}(h_{\mathcal G},l_{\mathcal G}, m=n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p)}{n_p}
 + \text{Transf.FLOP}(h_{\mathcal E},l_{\mathcal E}, m=w_{\mathcal E})\\
 &+ \text{Transf.FLOP}(h_{\mathcal D},l_{\mathcal D}, m=w_{\mathcal D})
 + \text{CrossAttn.FLOP}(\dots).
\end{aligned}
\]

Because FLOPs for self‑attention grow quadratically with input length but only linearly with hidden dimension, increasing average patch size reduces \(n_p\) and therefore dramatically cuts FLOPs spent in $\mathcal G$ while leaving local modules relatively cheap (they scale with fixed windows). Empirically BLT achieves up to **50 % fewer inference FLOPs** than an equivalently sized tokenizer‐based LLM while matching its perplexity measured as bits per byte (BPB).

---

## Summary  

Traditional sub‑word tokenizers impose static granularity that ties compute tightly to vocabulary design; this yields domain bias, noise fragility, and unequal treatment of languages with diverse scripts.  Earlier byte‐level models attempted to remove this bottleneck but suffered from uniform compute over extremely long sequences, making large‐scale training prohibitive.

The Byte Latent Transformer overturns these limitations through two synergistic innovations:

1. **Entropy‐driven dynamic patching**, which creates contextually appropriate groupings of bytes based on how uncertain their next symbol is.
2. **A hierarchical architecture** where cheap local encoders/decoders operate on raw bytes while an inexpensive global transformer processes only the much shorter sequence of latent patches.

By allocating more computation exactly where it is needed—high entropy boundaries—and less where predictions are easy—low entropy interiors—BLT matches state‐of‐the‐art tokenized LLMs at scales up to eight billion parameters and four trillion training bytes, while delivering superior inference efficiency and robustness to noisy inputs.  The remainder of this textbook will explore BLT’s detailed implementation—including cross‑attention mechanics, hash n‑gram embeddings, FLOP accounting—and examine its empirical scaling laws and downstream performance across classification, coding, multilingual translation, and character‐level robustness benchmarks.

# From Bytes to Patches: Dynamic Segmentation Strategies  

In modern large language models (LLMs) the raw text is almost always first transformed by a tokenizer, which groups the underlying bytes into a fixed‑size vocabulary of *tokens*.  Tokens are static: once a vocabulary has been constructed, every occurrence of a particular byte sequence is always mapped to the same token, regardless of its surrounding context.  While this design has enabled impressive performance at scale, it also imposes a rigid allocation of computation—each token receives the same amount of model processing, even though some tokens are trivial to predict (e.g., punctuation) while others are highly ambiguous (e.g., the first character of a new word).  

The **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)** eliminates this pre‑processing step and instead works directly on raw bytes.  Crucially, BLT does not operate on each byte individually; instead it *dynamically* groups consecutive bytes into **patches**.  A patch is a variable‑length chunk of the byte stream that is fed as a single unit to the global latent transformer.  By allowing the size of a patch to adapt to the difficulty of predicting the next byte, BLT can devote more compute where it matters most and skip unnecessary work where predictions are easy.  This chapter provides a complete, self‑contained exposition of the four patching schemes considered in BLT, how they are formalized, and why they improve efficiency relative to static tokenization.

---

## Formal definition of a patching function  

Let  

\[
\mathbf{x}= \{x_i\}_{i=1}^{n}, \qquad x_i \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{byte}} = \{0,\dots ,255\}
\]

be a sequence of $n$ raw bytes.  
A **patching function** $f_{p}$ maps this byte sequence into a shorter sequence of *patches*

\[
\mathbf{p}= \{p_j\}_{j=1}^{m}, \qquad m < n,
\]

by indicating for each position $i$ whether it marks the beginning of a new patch.  Formally we define an indicator vector  

\[
b_i = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } x_i \text{ starts a new patch},\\
0 & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\quad i=1,\dots ,n,
\]

with $b_1=1$ by convention.  The set of patch boundaries $\{t_j\}$ follows from the indices where $b_i=1$, and each patch $p_j$ consists of the contiguous bytes  

\[
p_j = \bigl(x_{t_j},\, x_{t_j+1},\,\dots ,\, x_{t_{j+1}-1}\bigr).
\]

The **average patch size**  

\[
\bar{s} = \frac{n}{m}
\]

directly determines the number of steps executed by BLT’s global transformer; larger patches mean fewer transformer steps and consequently lower FLOP consumption per byte (see Section § “FLOPs Estimation” in the paper).  Any viable patching scheme must therefore balance two competing desiderata:

1. **Predictive adequacy** – patches should be short enough that their internal bytes are not too heterogeneous in information density; otherwise a single transformer step would be forced to model highly uncertain predictions.
2. **Computational efficiency** – patches should be as long as possible in low‑entropy regions, so that unnecessary transformer steps are avoided.

Below we describe three concrete families of $f_p$ that satisfy these constraints to varying degrees.

---

## Static (strided) patching and its drawbacks  

The simplest $f_p$ partitions the byte stream into *fixed‑size* chunks; this is called **strided** or **static** patching.  Given an integer stride $k$, we set

\[
b_i = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } i \equiv 1 \pmod{k},\\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

Thus every patch contains exactly $k$ bytes (except possibly the final one).  This approach mirrors the “MegaByte” architecture [Yu et al., 2023] and has three appealing practical properties:

* **Determinism:** No preprocessing beyond slicing is required.
* **Explicit flop control:** Because $\bar{s}=k$, we can directly tune inference cost by choosing $k$.
* **Implementation simplicity:** Both training and inference pipelines can treat patches as regular tokens.

However, static patching suffers from fundamental inefficiencies:

| Issue | Explanation |
|------|--------------|
| **No dynamic compute allocation** | All patches receive an identical amount of transformer work regardless of how predictable their contents are. Predicting whitespace or repeated characters therefore wastes computation, while predicting high‑entropy tokens (e.g., the first character after a space) may not receive enough capacity. |
| **Inconsistent segmentation of identical substrings** | The same word may be split at different positions depending on its location in the stream, breaking any potential reuse of learned sub‑word patterns. |
| **Inflexible trade‑off between context length and flop budget** | Increasing $k$ reduces FLOPs but also shortens effective context for the global transformer, which can degrade performance when modeling long‑range dependencies. |

Empirically, static strided patching lags behind tokenization‑based baselines when scaling to billions of parameters (see Figure 5 left in the paper).  These shortcomings motivate more context‑aware strategies.

---

## Space (whitespace) patching  

A modest refinement over strided patching is **space patching**, introduced by [Slagle et al., 2024].  The intuition is that whitespace (or any “space‑like” byte) frequently demarcates linguistic units such as words or tokens in many scripts.  Hence we define a *space‑like* set $\mathcal{S}\subseteq \mathcal{V}_{\text{byte}}$ that includes any byte that is *not* a Latin letter, digit, or UTF‑8 continuation byte; each patch must contain at least one non‑space‑like byte.  

The indicator becomes

\[
b_i = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } x_i \in \mathcal{S},\\
0 & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad 
b_1 = 1.
\]

Effectively we start a new patch after every whitespace character.  This yields patches that correspond roughly to words (or their equivalent in languages using spaces as delimiters).  The advantages are:

* **Contextual coherence:** All occurrences of the same word are consistently grouped together across different sequences.
* **Targeted compute:** Hard predictions—typically those occurring immediately after a space because they narrow down possible continuations—receive a dedicated transformer step.

Nevertheless, space patching still has limitations:

* **Language bias:** Not all writing systems use space as a delimiter (e.g., Chinese, Japanese), so the heuristic breaks down for many multilingual corpora.
* **Fixed granularity:** Once a space occurs, we must begin a new patch; we cannot further coalesce low‑entropy subsequences within long words (e.g., common suffixes).

Consequently, while space patching improves over static striding, it does not fully exploit entropy variations inside words or across domains.

---

## Entropy‑based patching using a small byte LM  

The most data‑driven approach introduced in BLT is **entropy patching**, which leverages an auxiliary *byte‑level* language model (“entropy model”) to estimate how uncertain each next‑byte prediction is.  Let $p_e(\cdot\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{<i})$ denote the distribution over the next byte output by this small LM.  The *Shannon entropy* for position $i$ is

\[
H(x_i) = -\,\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{byte}}} p_e(x_i=v\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{<i}) \,\log p_e(x_i=v\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{<i}).
\tag{1}
\]

High entropy indicates that many possible continuations are plausible; low entropy signals that the next byte is nearly determined by its context.

### Deriving boundaries from entropies  

Two complementary criteria are used to decide whether a byte should start a new patch:

1. **Global threshold constraint**  
   A fixed scalar $\theta_g$ is chosen; if $H(x_t) > \theta_g$, we mark $x_t$ as a boundary:
   \[
   H(x_t) > \theta_g .
   \tag{2}
   \]
2. **Approximate monotonicity constraint**  
   Within any given patch we expect entropy to decline as more bytes become available (the model becomes more certain).  If this monotonic decay is violated beyond a tolerance $\theta_r$, we cut:
   \[
   H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r .
   \tag{3}
   \]

During data loading we compute entropies for every position using the pretrained entropy model and then apply either (2), (3), or both simultaneously to produce binary boundary indicators $b_i$.  The result is *incremental*: boundaries depend only on past context $\mathbf{x}_{<i}$ and thus can be decided during generation without looking ahead.

### Practical considerations  

* **Threshold selection** – Prior to training BLT, we sweep over candidate $\theta_g$ or $\theta_r$ values on a validation subset and select those yielding a desired average patch size $\bar{s}$ (e.g., $\approx4.5$ bytes).  
* **Context reset** – For very long documents entropy can drift because the auxiliary LM’s hidden state accumulates information unrelated to local uncertainty.  In large‐scale runs we periodically reset its context at line breaks and rely primarily on the monotonicity constraint; this mitigates “entropy drift” while preserving dynamic adaptivity.
* **Efficiency** – Computing entropies once during dataloading incurs negligible overhead compared with full attention over all bytes; it replaces an expensive per‑byte attention cost with cheap lookups from the pretrained entropy model.

Empirically, entropy‐based patches outperform both static striding and space‐only heuristics across all scaling regimes (Figures 5–7 in the paper).  They allocate longer patches to highly predictable regions (e.g., repeated code fragments) and short patches around unpredictable junctures such as the first character after punctuation or in mathematical expressions.

---

## Threshold selection: global vs. monotonic constraints  

Choosing an appropriate threshold directly influences both model efficiency and accuracy:

| Criterion | Effect on Patch Size | Typical Use Cases |
|-----------|----------------------|-------------------|
| **Global entropy threshold $\theta_g$** | Larger $\theta_g$ ⇒ fewer boundaries ⇒ longer patches; smaller $\theta_g$ ⇒ many short patches | Works well when overall data distribution has relatively stable entropy levels (e.g., homogeneous prose). |
| **Monotonicity gap $\theta_r$** | Controls sensitivity to local spikes in entropy; useful when overall entropy slowly decays within words but occasional spikes indicate concept changes | Preferred for heterogeneous domains where entropy fluctuates sharply within short spans (e.g., code mixed with natural language). |

In practice BLT often combines both: first enforce monotonicity to avoid pathological growth within low‑entropy stretches, then apply a global ceiling to ensure overall flop budget remains bounded.  The combined rule can be expressed as

\[
b_t = 
\begin{cases}
1 & H(x_t) > \theta_g
      \;\;\textbf{or}\;\;
      H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r ,\\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\tag{4}
\]

During hyperparameter search we monitor average $\bar{s}$ and adjust $(\theta_g,\theta_r)$ until target FLOPs per token align with our inference budget.

---

## Incremental patching vs. BPE tokenization  

A key theoretical distinction between *patches* and conventional *subword tokens* lies in **incrementality**:

A patching function $f_p$ is *incremental* if it satisfies

\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i},
\qquad
\forall i,
\tag{5}
\]

meaning that when processing only the prefix up to position $i$, one obtains exactly the same set of patches as one would obtain by first segmenting the whole sequence and then discarding later patches.  Entropy‐based and space‐based schemes both satisfy (5) because their decisions rely solely on past bytes.

Byte Pair Encoding (BPE), by contrast, does not enjoy this property: tokenizing a prefix may yield different tokens depending on future characters because BPE merges frequent byte pairs greedily based on statistics computed over whole strings. Consequently BPE fails incremental consistency:

* A word “reading” may be tokenized as `read` + `##ing` when seen alone,
* but as `readi` + `ng` when followed by “ness”.

Thus BPE models cannot determine at generation time whether they have reached a token boundary without peeking ahead—a requirement incompatible with left‑to‑right autoregressive decoding.

BLT’s incremental nature enables *online* boundary decisions during inference: after generating each byte we simply consult its precomputed entropy or whitespace flag to decide whether to invoke the costly global latent transformer for a new patch or remain within the lightweight local encoder/decoder path.  This leads directly to substantial inference‐flop savings: longer low‑entropy stretches bypass expensive computation altogether.

---

### Concluding remarks  

Dynamic segmentation via patches represents a paradigm shift from fixed vocabularies toward *compute‑aware* processing of raw text.  By formalizing patches as context‑dependent groupings determined either by simple heuristics (whitespace) or by information‑theoretic measures (entropy), BLT achieves two complementary goals:

1. **Efficiency:** Average FLOPs per processed byte drop dramatically because many predictable regions are collapsed into single transformer steps.
2. **Expressiveness:** High‑entropy regions still receive fine‐grained modeling power via short patches, preserving—or even improving—downstream performance relative to tokenized models.

The remainder of this textbook will build upon these foundations: Chapter X will detail how BLT’s encoder–decoder architecture consumes these patches, while later chapters explore scaling laws, robustness gains, and practical implementation tricks required for training billion‑parameter models on raw bytes.

**Architecture of the Byte Latent Transformer**

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) departs fundamentally from the token‑centric pipelines that dominate modern large language models.  Instead of operating on a fixed vocabulary of sub‑word tokens, BLT processes raw byte streams by first aggregating them into dynamically sized *patches*.  These patches serve as the primary computational units for a three‑stage architecture composed of a **local encoder**, a **global latent transformer**, and a **local decoder**.  The design of each component is tightly coupled to the goal of allocating compute where the data are most informative: inexpensive byte‑level processing builds compact latent representations, while a heavyweight transformer operates only on those latent patch embeddings that correspond to high‑entropy regions of the input.  The following sections describe each block in detail, their interactions, and the engineering choices that make the system both efficient and expressive.

---

### The Global Latent Transformer

At the heart of BLT lies an autoregressive transformer, denoted \(\mathcal{G}\), which we refer to as the **latent global transformer**.  Unlike conventional token‑level transformers that attend over every token in a sequence, \(\mathcal{G}\) attends only over the sequence of latent patch embeddings \(\{p_j\}_{j=1}^{m}\) produced by the encoder.  Operating on this reduced sequence dramatically shortens the effective length of the attention computation, because each patch may span several bytes.  The model is built from \(l_{\mathcal{G}}\) identical layers; each layer consists of a multi‑head self‑attention sub‑module followed by a feed‑forward network (FFN), both wrapped with pre‑layer normalisation.

A crucial design choice for \(\mathcal{G}\) is its **block‑causal attention mask**.  For patch index \(j\), attention is permitted to all patches \(k \leq j\) within the same document, but never to future patches \(k > j\).  Formally, let \(A_{jk}=1\) if \(k \leq j\) and \(A_{jk}=0\) otherwise; this binary mask is applied to the scaled dot‑product attention scores before softmax:
\[
\alpha_{jk}= \frac{\exp\bigl( (Q_j K_k^{\top})/\sqrt{d_k} \bigr)}{\sum_{k' : A_{jk'}=1}\exp\bigl( (Q_j K_{k'}^{\top})/\sqrt{d_k} \bigr)}\,,
\]
where \(Q_j,W_q p_j\) and \(K_k,W_k p_k\) are linear projections of the patch embeddings into query and key spaces of dimension \(d_k\).  The block‑causal mask guarantees strict autoregressivity: predictions for a given patch are conditioned only on previous patches, mirroring the causal nature required for language modelling.

Because \(\mathcal{G}\) consumes most of BLT’s floating‑point operations during both pre‑training and inference, its size directly determines overall model capacity.  In practice we match \(\mathcal{G}\)’s parameter budget to that of comparable token‑based transformers (e.g., Llama 3), while exploiting the reduced number of steps afforded by larger average patch sizes.  Consequently, when patches become longer—thanks to entropy‑guided segmentation—fewer transformer steps are taken per byte, freeing FLOPs that can be reallocated either to deeper global layers or to larger hidden dimensions.

---

### The Local Encoder

The **local encoder**, denoted \(\mathcal{E}\), is a lightweight transformer tasked with transforming the raw byte sequence \(\{b_i\}_{i=1}^{n}\) into a set of latent patch embeddings \(\{p_j\}_{j=1}^{m}\).  Its design balances two competing demands: (1) it must be cheap enough to run on every byte without overwhelming compute, and (2) it must produce expressive representations that capture enough context for the downstream global transformer.

#### Byte Embeddings and Hash n‑gram Augmentation

Each byte \(b_i\in \mathcal{V}=\{0,\dots,255\}\) is first mapped to an embedding vector via an lookup table
\[
x_i = E_{\text{byte}}[b_i]\in \mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathcal{E}}}.
\]
To enrich these representations with local context while keeping memory footprints modest, BLT adds *hash n‑gram embeddings*.  For every position \(i\) we form six overlapping n‑grams
\[
g_{i,n} = \bigl(b_{i-n+1},\,b_{i-n+2},\,\dots ,\,b_i\bigr), \qquad n=3,\dots ,8,
\]
discarding any n‑gram that would extend before the start of the sequence.  Each n‑gram is hashed by a fast rolling polynomial hash function,
\[
h(g_{i,n}) = \bigl(\operatorname{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n})\bigr)\bmod |E_n^{\text{hash}}|,
\]
and then looked up in a dedicated embedding table \(E^{\text{hash}}_n\).  The final augmented byte embedding is an average of all contributions:
\[
e_i = x_i + \frac{1}{7}\sum_{n=3}^{8}E^{\text{hash}}_n\bigl(h(g_{i,n})\bigr).
\]
These hash‑based n‑gram features provide cheap access to sub‑byte patterns such as common prefixes or suffixes without requiring an explicit vocabulary for every possible n‑gram.

#### Transformer Layers with Local Block Causal Attention

The sequence \(\{e_i\}_{i=1}^{n}\) feeds into a stack of \(l_{\mathcal{E}}\ll l_{\mathcal{G}}\) transformer layers.  Within each layer, self‑attention operates under a **local block causal mask**: each byte attends only to a fixed sliding window of its preceding \(w_{\mathcal{E}}\) bytes (e.g., \(w_{\mathcal{E}}=512\)).  This restriction limits quadratic cost while still allowing enough context for reliable encoding of short‐range dependencies such as character n‑grams or common syntactic constructs.

#### Cross‑Attention Pooling into Patches

After each transformer layer we apply a cross‑attention module that aggregates byte representations belonging to the same patch into a single latent vector.  Let
\(B = \bigl(e^{(l)}_i\bigr)_{i=1}^{n}\) denote byte embeddings after layer \(l\).  
For patch \(p_j\), we define its query vector as an initial pooling over its constituent bytes; typically max or mean pooling is used:
\[
q^{(0)}_j = \operatorname{Pool}\bigl(\{e^{(0)}_i : i \in P_j\}\bigr),
\]
where \(P_j\subseteq[1,n]\) indexes bytes belonging to patch \(j\).  

Cross‑attention then refines these queries:
\[
Q^{(l)} = W_q q^{(l-1)}_j,\qquad
K^{(l)}_i = W_k B_i,\qquad
V^{(l)}_i = W_v B_i,
\]
with learnable projections \(W_q,W_k,W_v\,{:}\,\mathbb R^{h_{\mathcal{E}}}\to \mathbb R^{d_k}\).
Because each query should see only its own bytes, we mask out all keys/values not in \(P_j\).  The attention update for query \(j\) becomes
\[
q^{(l)}_j = q^{(l-1)}_j +
W_o\, \sum_{i \in P_j}
   \alpha^{(l)}_{ji} V^{(l)}_i,
    \quad 
    \alpha^{(l)}_{ji}= 
    \frac{\exp((Q^{(l)}_j K^{(l)\top}_i)/\sqrt{d_k})}{%
          \sum_{i'\in P_j}
          \exp((Q^{(l)}_j K^{(l)\top}_{i'})/\sqrt{d_k}) } .
\]
The residual connection preserves information from earlier layers; after repeating this operation across all cross‑attention blocks we obtain final patch embeddings
\(p_j = q^{(L_{\mathcal E})}_j \in \mathbb R^{h_{\mathcal G}}\),
ready for consumption by the global transformer.

The cross‑attention design mirrors the Perceiver architecture but differs in that each query is confined to its own dynamic byte set rather than attending over a fixed latent array.

---

### The Local Decoder

Generation proceeds in reverse: after the global transformer predicts an output patch representation \(o_j\), these must be expanded back into concrete bytes.  The **local decoder**, denoted \(\mathcal D\), fulfills this role using another lightweight transformer stack (\(l_{\mathcal D} \ll l_{\mathcal G}\)) coupled with cross‑attention that maps from patches to bytes.

#### Cross‑Attention from Patches to Bytes

During decoding step \(j\), queries are now derived from *byte* positions while keys/values come from *patch* representations.  Let
\(H = (h_i)\) be byte‐level hidden states inherited from the final encoder layer,
and let
\(O = (o_j)\) be the sequence of output patches produced by \(\mathcal G.\)
We first compute key/value projections for every patch:
\(K_j = W_k^\text{dec}(o_j),\, V_j = W_v^\text{dec}(o_j).\)
For each byte position \(i$, we construct its query:
\(Q_i = W_q^\text{dec}(h_i).\)
Again we limit attention to those patches whose span contains byte \(i,\) enforcing a mask
\(M_{ij}=1$ if $i$ belongs to patch $j$ and $0$ otherwise.
The attention output for byte $i$ becomes
\[
b_i = h_i + 
W_o^\text{dec}
   \sum_{j : M_{ij}=1}
      \alpha_{ij} V_j,\qquad 
   \alpha_{ij}= 
   \frac{\exp((Q_i K_j^\top)/{\sqrt d_k})}{%
         \sum_{j':M_{ij'}=1}
         \exp((Q_i K_{j'}^\top)/{\sqrt d_k}) } .
\]
Thus each byte aggregates information only from its owning patch, effectively “unpacking’’ the latent representation back into byte space.

#### Byte Generation Layer

After cross‑attention, each updated byte hidden state passes through several standard transformer layers (again using local block causal attention with window $w_\mathcal D$); finally an output projection maps $b_i$ onto logits over the $256$ possible byte values:
\[
z_i = W_\text{o}^{(\text{bytes})} b_i + b_\text{o}, 
    \qquad p(b_i=v|\cdot)=
    {\rm softmax}_v(z_i).
\]
During autoregressive generation we predict one byte at a time conditioned on previously generated bytes and previously emitted patches.  Whenever a new patch boundary is reached—determined incrementally by evaluating next‐byte entropy—the decoder invokes another forward pass through $\mathcal G$, thereby intermittently expending additional compute only when needed.

---

### Mechanistic Details of Cross‑Attention

Both encoder and decoder share a common cross‑attention primitive but invert its directionality.  In encoder cross‑attention:

* **Queries** correspond to current *patch* latent vectors.
* **Keys/Values** stem from *byte* hidden states belonging to that patch.
* **Masking** restricts every query’s receptive field exclusively to its own bytes; no inter‐patch communication occurs at this stage.

In decoder cross‑attention:

* **Queries** correspond to *bytes* awaiting generation.
* **Keys/Values** are derived from already computed *patch* outputs.
* **Masking** ensures each byte attends only to its owning patch; because patches have already been generated autoregressively they contain information about all preceding bytes.

Both mechanisms employ multi–head attention with pre–LayerNorm around queries, keys and values. No positional embeddings are added inside cross‐attention; instead positional information is injected earlier via rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) applied only within self‐attention layers of $\mathcal E$, $\mathcal G$ and $\mathcal D$.  RoPE injects relative position bias directly into $Q$ and $K$, preserving absolute translational invariance while allowing long contexts without explicit learned position tables.

---

### Positional Encoding Choices

Full positional encodings are unnecessary for cross‐attention because queries already know which bytes belong to their associated patch via masking.  Consequently BLT adopts a minimalist strategy:

* **Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE)** are employed exclusively inside self‐attention modules across all three transformers.  
* The rotary angle parameter $\theta$ is set to $5\times10^5$, matching recent best practices for long contexts; this scales linearly with token distance and enables smooth extrapolation beyond training lengths.
* No learned absolute position vectors are added anywhere else—neither in embedding tables nor in feed‐forward layers—reducing memory overhead and simplifying implementation.

This design ensures that positional information influences inter–byte interactions within local windows but does not interfere with the high‐level aggregation performed by cross‐attention.

---

### Parameter Scaling Across Encoder, Decoder, and Global Model

Because most FLOPs in BLT reside in $\mathcal G$, we allocate parameters proportionally:

| Component | Typical Layer Count | Hidden Size | Relative Parameter Share |
|-----------|--------------------|------------|--------------------------|
| Global latent transformer $\mathcal G$ | $l_{\mathcal G}=24$ (for an 8 B model) | $h_{\mathcal G}=4096$ | ≈ 80 % |
| Local encoder $\mathcal E$ | $l_{\mathcal E}=1$–$3$ | $h_{\mathcal E}=768$ | ≈ 5 % |
| Local decoder $\mathcal D$ | $l_{\mathcal D}=2$–$4$ | $h_{\mathcal D}=1024$ | ≈ 15 % |

The exact numbers vary with model scale; smaller configurations may use $l_{\mathcal G}=12$, $h_{\mathcal G}=2048$, etc.  Crucially, as average patch size grows (through entropy‐driven segmentation), $m$, the number of patches per context, shrinks roughly proportionally.  Since FLOPs per forward pass through $\mathcal G$ scale as $\mathrm{O}(m\, h_{\mathcal G}^2)$ plus FFN cost $\mathrm{O}(m\, h_{\mathcal G}^2)$, longer patches directly translate into fewer global steps per byte.  This creates an additional knob: by increasing average patch length we can maintain constant inference FLOP budgets while enlarging either depth or width of $\mathcal G$, yielding better overall performance without extra computation at inference time.

The local models remain thin because they must run on every byte; their parameter counts grow slowly with overall model size so as not to dominate compute or memory consumption.  Empirically this asymmetry works well: even with just one encoder layer plus hash n‑gram embeddings, BLT attains parity with tokenizers at comparable FLOP budgets; adding more encoder layers brings marginal gains but incurs disproportionate cost because those layers would need to be executed on every byte.

---

### Synthesis

In summary, BLT’s architecture orchestrates three distinct processing stages:

1. **Local Encoder** – A fast transformer enriched with hashed n‑gram features aggregates raw bytes into compact latent patches using masked cross‑attention.
2. **Global Latent Transformer** – A heavyweight autoregressive backbone consumes only those patch embeddings whose boundaries are defined by data entropy; block–causal attention guarantees strict language modelling semantics.
3. **Local Decoder** – Another lightweight transformer unpacks each predicted patch back into bytes via inverse cross‑attention, enabling fine-grained generation while preserving rapid inference thanks to conditional invocation of the global model.

By carefully separating concerns—cheap per-byte computation versus expensive per-patch computation—BLT achieves dynamic allocation of compute proportional to information density in the data stream.  The architectural choices described here—including hash n‑gram augmentations, masked cross-attention pooling, RoPE positional handling, and deliberate parameter scaling—form a cohesive system that retains or surpasses tokenisation-based performance while offering substantial gains in efficiency and robustness.

# Computational Cost Analysis  

In this chapter we develop a detailed accounting of the floating‑point operations (FLOPs) required by the **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)**.  We begin by recalling the standard FLOP formulas for Transformer building blocks—feed‑forward networks and the QKV attention sub‑layers—because they constitute the bulk of the computational budget in any large language model.  We then explain why the byte‑embedding lookup in BLT contributes essentially zero FLOPs, before moving on to a complete breakdown of the per‑patch cost incurred by each component of the BLT architecture: the lightweight **local encoder**, the **global latent transformer**, the **local decoder**, and the two cross‑attention modules that bridge bytes and patches.  With these ingredients in hand we can derive an explicit relationship between the *average patch size* (i.e. the mean number of bytes that are grouped together) and the FLOPs incurred per processed byte, both during training and inference.  Finally we discuss how this relationship enables **inference‑flop‑controlled scaling**: keeping a fixed inference budget while increasing either model capacity or average patch size, a capability that is fundamentally unavailable to conventional token‑based models.

---

## 1. Standard Transformer FLOP Formulas  

A Transformer layer consists of two main computational stages: (i) a multi‑head self‑attention (MHSA) block, and (ii) a position‑wise feed‑forward network (FFN).  Assuming hidden dimension \(h\), sequence length \(m\), number of attention heads \(H\), and feed‑forward expansion factor \(f\) (typically \(f=4\)), the FLOP counts are:

### 1.1 QKV Projection and Attention  
Each token (or patch) produces queries, keys, and values via three linear projections from dimension \(h\) to \(h\).  The projection FLOPs are

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{proj}} = 3 \times (m \cdot h \cdot h).
\]

The scaled dot‑product attention requires forming the matrix product \(QK^{\top}\), applying softmax, and multiplying by \(V\).  The dominant matrix multiplication costs are

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{QK^{\top}} = m^{2} \cdot \frac{h}{H},
\qquad
\text{FLOPs}_{QV}   = m^{2} \cdot \frac{h}{H},
\]

where we have split the hidden dimension evenly across heads; each head handles a sub‑dimension \(h/H\).  The subsequent output projection adds another

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{out}} = m \cdot h \cdot h .
\]

Summing these terms yields the total attention FLOPs per layer:

\[
\boxed{\text{FLOPs}_{\text{attn}}(h,m)=
4mh^{2}+2m^{2}\frac{h}{H}} .
\]

In practice, for modest context windows (e.g., fixed‑width windows of size 512 used in BLT’s local models) the quadratic term is much smaller than the linear term; for the global latent transformer, which operates on *patch* sequences rather than raw bytes, the quadratic term also remains modest because patches are far fewer than bytes.

### 1.2 Feed‑Forward Network  
The FFN applies two linear layers separated by an activation (SwiGLU in BLT).  Its FLOP count per layer is

\[
\boxed{\text{FLOPs}_{\text{FFN}}(h,m)=
2\, m\, h\, f\, h = 8\,m\,h^{2}} ,
\]

since \(f=4\).  This term dominates overall Transformer cost because it is executed for *every* token/patch regardless of attention pattern.

### 1.3 Total Layer Cost  

A full Transformer layer therefore costs

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{layer}}(h,m)=
8mh^{2}+4mh^{2}+2m^{2}\frac{h}{H}
   =12mh^{2}+2m^{2}\frac{h}{H}.
\]

When counting training FLOPs we double this quantity to account for the backward pass, as customary in scaling‑law analyses.

---

## 2. Zero‑FLOP Byte Embedding Lookup  

BLT reads raw bytes \(\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{n}\) from a corpus, where each byte belongs to a fixed vocabulary \(\mathcal{V}\) of size 256.  Instead of projecting each byte through a dense matrix, BLT stores an **embedding table** \(\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{256\times h_{\mathcal{E}}}\) and retrieves rows by index:

```python
x_i = E[byte_i]   # constant‑time table lookup
```

Because a table fetch consists solely of memory reads and no arithmetic on floating point numbers, it contributes **zero FLOPs** under the Chinchilla counting convention used throughout this work.  Consequently, all subsequent FLOP expenditure originates from the three Transformer‐style modules (encoder, global latent transformer, decoder) and their cross‑attention bridges.

---

## 3. Patch‑Level FLOP Breakdown  

Let us denote:

- \(n_{\text{ctx}}\): number of *bytes* presented to BLT in a training or inference window;
- \(n_{p}\): number of *patches* produced by the patching function \(f_{p}\);
- \(\bar{s}= n_{\text{ctx}}/n_{p}\): **average patch size** (bytes per patch);
- \(h_{\mathcal{G}},l_{\mathcal{G}}\): hidden size and depth of the global latent transformer;
- \(h_{\mathcal{E}},l_{\mathcal{E}}\): hidden size and depth of the local encoder;
- \(h_{\mathcal{D}},l_{\mathcal{D}}\): hidden size and depth of the local decoder;
- \(w_{\mathcal{E}}, w_{\mathcal{D}}\): sliding window sizes used for local self‑attention (fixed to 512 bytes in all experiments);
- \(V=256\): output vocabulary size for the byte decoder head.

Below we express each component’s contribution using the generic layer formula from Section 1, substituting appropriate sequence lengths.

### 3.1 Local Encoder  

The encoder processes *bytes* with a short causal window; its sequence length is therefore \(m = w_{\mathcal{E}}\).  
Its total FLOPs per **byte** are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\mathcal{E}} &=
l_{\mathcal{E}}\,
          \bigl[12\,w_{\mathcal{E}}\, h_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}
                +2\,w_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}\frac{h_{\mathcal{E}}}{H_{\mathcal{E}}}
          \bigr] .
\end{aligned}
\]

Because every byte participates in exactly one encoder step regardless of how patches are formed, this term does not depend on average patch size.

### 3.2 Global Latent Transformer  

The global model operates on *patches*. Its effective sequence length is \(m=n_{p}=n_{\text{ctx}}/\bar{s}\). The global FLOPs per **byte** become

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\mathcal{G}}
&=
 \frac{
   l_{\mathcal{G}}\,
   \bigl[12\, n_{p}\, h_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
         +2\, n_{p}^{2} \frac{h_{\mathcal{G}}}{H_{\mathcal{G}}}
   \bigr]
 } { n_{\text{ctx}} }                                    \\[4pt]
&=
 l_{\mathcal{G}}\,
 \biggl[
    \frac{
        12\, h_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
    }{\bar{s}}
   + 
    \frac{
        2\, h_{\mathcal{G}}
    } { H_{\mathcal{G}} } 
    \frac{
        n_{p} } { n_{\text{ctx}} }
    n_{p}
 \biggr] \\[4pt]
&=
 l_{\mathcal{G}}\,
 \biggl[
    \frac{
        12\, h_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
    }{\bar{s}}
   + 
    O(\bar{s}^{-2})
 \biggr] .
\end​aligned}
\]

Since BLT chooses *moderate* patch sizes (e.g., 4–8 bytes), the quadratic term is negligible compared with the dominant linear term proportional to \(1/\bar{s}\). Hence **the global model’s per‑byte cost scales inversely with average patch size**.

### 3.3 Local Decoder  

The decoder also works on bytes, but it must predict from a softmax over all possible byte values; therefore its output projection contributes an extra factor that scales with vocabulary size \(V=256\). Its per‑byte FLOP count reads

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{
FL}_{%
   {\scriptstyle
   {\rm decoder}}
&=
l_{\mathcal D}
   \bigl[12\, w_{\mathcal D}\, h_{\mathcal D}^{2}
        +2\, w_{\mathcal D}^{2} 
           \tfrac {h_{\mathcal D}}{{H}_{%
                                               {\scriptstyle 
                                                {\rm D}}
                                           }}
   \bigr]    
\\
&\
+\;\underbrace{
      n_{\text {ctx}}\, V\, h_{{%
                {\rm D}}
             }
      }_{%
         {\rm output~projection~flops}}
\\[4pt]
&=
l_\cD   
      [12 w_\cD h_\cD^2 + O(w_\cD^2)] 
      + V h_\cD .
% note: divided by n_ctx later gives per-byte
%
\\[4pt]
&=
      \frac{
         l_\cD [12 w_\cD h_\cD^2 ] + V h_\cD
       } { n_\mathrm{{ctx}} }
      .
%
\end​aligned}
\]

Again this term does not depend on patch granularity because every byte must be emitted.

### 3.4 Cross‑Attention Between Bytes and Patches  

Both encoder–to–latent and latent–to–decoder cross‐attention modules aggregate information across variable‐size groups. Their FLOP formulas follow Eqn (1) but with asymmetric query/key/value counts. For a given module we let:

- \(r = n_{q}/n_{k}=k/n_{p}\), where \(k>1$ is typically set to 2 in BLT,
- “queries’’ have dimension \(h_{q}=h_{\star}\) (global or decoder size),
- “keys/values’’ have dimension \(h_{kv}=h_{{{\rm local}}}\).

The cross‑attention cost per **byte** can be written compactly as

| Module | Query length | Key/Value length | Per‑byte FLOP |
|--------|--------------|------------------|---------------|
| Encoder Cross‑Attention | \(n_{p}\) | \(k=n_{p}r=n_{p}(k/n_{p})=k)\) | \(\displaystyle 
      l_\cE
      \Bigl[12\, n_{p} h_\cG^2 +
            2\, n_{p} k      \tfrac {h_\cG}{H_\cE} 
      \Bigr]\! / n_\mathrm{{ctx}}\) |
| Decoder Cross‑Attention | \(k=n_{p}r=k)\) | \(n_p]\) | same form with $(h_\cD,h_\cG)$ swapped |

Because each cross-attention step processes at most one patch’s worth of bytes, its contribution scales linearly with **the number of patches**, i.e., inversely with average patch size:

\[
\null
    \text{
FL}_{%
   {\scriptstyle 
    {\rm cross}}
     = O(1/\bar{s}) .
    }
    
Thus both cross-attention modules follow exactly the same scaling pattern as the global latent transformer.

### 3.5 Full Per‑Byte FLOP Expression  

Collecting all pieces from Sections 3.1–3.4 yields BLT’s total FLOPs per processed byte:

\[
\boxed{
%
    {\rm FL}_{ {\rm BLT} } =
     {\rm FL}_{{}^{ }\!\!\!{\scriptstyle{\rm encoder}}}
   + {\rm FL}_{{}^{ }\!\!\!{\scriptstyle{\rm decoder }}}
   + {\rm FL}_{{}^{ }\!\!\!{\scriptstyle{\rm global }}}
   + {\rm FL}_{{}^{ }\!\!\!{\scriptstyle{\rm cross }}}
 } .
%
\]

Explicitly,

\[
{\rm FL}_{ {\rm BLT} }=
 l_{\cE}[12 w_{\cE} h^2_\cE ]
+ l_{\cD}[12 w_\cD h^2_\cD]+\tfrac {V h_\cD}{n_\mathrm{{ctx}}}
+ 
        O(1/\bar{s}) .
        
In words: *the only terms that depend on how we group bytes are those belonging to components that see patches rather than raw bytes*. All other computations—local encoding/decoding—are invariant to average patch size because they always run once per byte.

---

## 4. Relationship Between Average Patch Size and Inference Cost  

During inference we typically drop gradients, halving all training costs above. The dominant variable portion becomes

> **Per‑byte inference FLOPs**  
> 
> \( {\displaystyle
>     {\rm FL}^{(\text {inf})}_{\<{\rm BLT}>}
>     = C_0 
>       + C_1/\bar{s},
>     }
>   
where  
\(C_0 = l_{\cE}[6 w_{\cE} h^2_\cE ]+ l_\cD[6 w_\cD h^2_\cD]+\tfrac {V h_\cD}{n_\mathrm{{ctx}}}\) is independent of patching,
and   
\(C_1 = l_{\cG}[6 n_p h^2_\cG] + (\text {cross attn terms}) = O(l_G h_G^2)\).

Thus **doubling average patch size roughly halves** those portions of computation that involve patches—the global latent transformer and both cross-attention blocks. This inverse relationship explains why BLT can achieve up to a 50 % reduction in inference flops when moving from an average patch size of about 4 bytes (similar to Llama 3’s token length of ≈ 4 bytes) to ≈ 8 bytes: 

| Avg Patch Size | Global‐Transformer Ratio |
|----------------|--------------------------|
| ~4 bytes       | baseline                 |
| ~6 bytes       | ≈ 0.66×                  |
| ~8 bytes       | ≈ 0.50×                  |

Because **the encoder/decoder cost remains constant**, once patches become sufficiently large most of their contribution drops out relative to these fixed costs; at very large patches (> 10 bytes) additional gains diminish because $C_0$ dominates.

---

## 5. Inference‑Flop Controlled Scaling  

The inverse dependence on $\bar{s}$ affords a novel scaling knob: **maintain a strict inference flop budget while simultaneously increasing model capacity**.

### 5.1 Fixed Inference Budget  

Suppose we allocate $F$ inference FLOPs per generated byte (e.g., $F=10^9$ flops for an online service). From Eqn (5),

```
F = C_0 + C_1 / s_bar .
```

If we enlarge any hidden dimension—say increase $h_{\cG}$ or depth $l_{\cG}$—we inevitably increase $C_1$. To keep $F$ constant we must raise $\bar{s}$ accordingly:

```
s_bar_new = C_1_new / (F - C_0).
```

Thus larger models can be paired with larger patches without exceeding our budget.

### 55️⃣ Practical Example  

Consider two BLT configurations trained with identical numbers of training flops:

| Config | $l_G$ | $h_G$ | Avg Patch Size $\bar{s}$ | Inference Flops/Byte |
|--------|------|------|-----------------------------|----------------------|
| A – “small” | 12 | 1024 | 4.5 bytes | $9.8\times10^8$ |
| B – “large” | 24 | 2048 | 8 bytes   | $9.7\times10^8$ |

Even though Config B has four times more parameters in its global transformer, raising $\bar{s}$ from 4.5 to 8 reduces $C_1$ enough that total inference flops stay essentially unchanged—this is precisely what Figure 5 (“fixed inference scaling”) demonstrates empirically for BLT versus token-based baselines.

### 5.3 Why Token Models Cannot Replicate This Tradeoff  

In conventional BPE systems:
- The *vocabulary* fixes both average token length (\(\approx4 .\!7`\) bytes for Llama 3) **and** output dimension (\(|V|\)).  
- Enlarging model width/depth inevitably raises *all* components’ FLOPs because there is no separate “patch” mechanism; each token still incurs full attention+FFN cost.
- To reduce flops one must reduce token count, which can only be done by *increasing* token length via a larger vocabulary—a move that inflates embedding tables and final projection layers dramatically.

Therefore token-based models lack an orthogonal “patch size” degree of freedom: any change in model capacity unavoidably alters inference cost.

---

## 6. Summary  

We derived explicit formulas for every computational constituent of BLT—the local encoder/decoder, global latent transformer, and bidirectional cross-attention—and highlighted how only those components that process *patches* depend on average patch size $\bar{s}$. Because $\bar{s}$ appears in denominator form ($C_1/\bar{s}$), enlarging patches yields linear reductions in per‑byte inference flops without sacrificing any part of the fixed cost $C_0$. This relationship underpins **inference-flop controlled scaling:** with a fixed flop budget one may grow model depth or hidden dimension while compensating by using larger patches; such simultaneous scaling is impossible for standard tokenizer-based models whose token granularity is static and whose vocabularies impose an unavoidable tradeoff between sequence length and embedding matrix size.

The analysis confirms why BLT can match or exceed token-based performance at up to eight billion parameters while spending up to half as many flops during generation—a key empirical observation reported throughout this work—and it provides a principled framework for future research seeking even larger models under strict latency or energy constraints.
