# Why Reinforcement Learning Isn’t Needed Anymore for Aligning Language Models  

## Introduction  

Large language models (LLMs) have become incredibly good at generating text, but steering them toward *desired* behavior remains a headache. The dominant recipe—**Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**—first trains a separate reward model on preference data and then runs a reinforcement‑learning algorithm (usually PPO) to adjust the language model. While powerful, this pipeline is complex, unstable, and computationally heavy: you have to fit a reward network, sample from the model many times during training, tune KL‑penalties, and keep an eye on mode collapse.  

A recent breakthrough called **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** shows that you can skip the entire RL loop. By re‑parametrizing the reward function in terms of the language model itself, the optimal policy can be written down in closed form. This allows us to train directly on human preferences with a single binary‑cross‑entropy loss—exactly the same loss used to train a classifier—without ever estimating an explicit reward or running RL.  

Below we unpack the core insight behind DPO, translate the key equations into plain English, and discuss why this matters for building safer, more controllable AI systems.

## The Usual RLHF Workflow (Brief Recap)

1. **Supervised fine‑tuning (SFT).**  
   Start from a massive pretrained LM and fine‑tune it on a high‑quality dataset of prompts and completions.

2. **Reward modeling.**  
   Collect pairs of completions for the same prompt and ask humans which one they prefer.  
   Fit a reward network \(r_\phi(x,y)\) so that the probability it assigns to “completion \(y_1\) is better than \(y_2\)” matches the observed human choices. The Bradley‑Terry model formalizes this as  
   \[
   p(y_1 \succ y_2\,|\,x)=\frac{\exp(r_\phi(x,y_1))}{\exp(r_\phi(x,y_1))+\exp(r_\phi(x,y_2))}.
   \]

3. **Reinforcement learning.**  
   Keep the SFT model as a *reference policy* \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  
   Optimize a new policy \(\pi_\theta\) to maximize expected reward while staying close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\):  
   \[
   \max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}[\,r_\phi(x,y)\,] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta\;\|\;\pi_{\text{ref}}\big).
   \]
   In practice this requires PPO, Monte‑Carlo sampling, value‑function baselines, and lots of hyperparameter fiddling.

The bottleneck is step 2 + 3: you need a *separate* reward model and an RL optimizer that can handle the discrete nature of text generation.

## The Key Observation: “Your LM Is Already a Reward Model”

DPO starts from exactly the same objective as RLHF (the KL‑constrained reward maximization) but shows that **the optimal policy has an analytic form**:

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
\pi_r(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big)
}
\tag{1}
\]

Here \(Z(x)\) normalizes over all possible completions. Equation (1) tells us that any optimal policy can be written as a *re‑weighting* of the reference policy by an exponential factor involving the reward.

Now solve (1) for the reward:

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\,\log\frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta\,\log Z(x).
\tag{2}
\]

The partition function \(Z(x)\) depends only on the prompt \(x\), not on a particular completion \(y\). When we compare two completions under the Bradley‑Terry model we **subtract** their rewards, so the \(\log Z(x)\) term cancels out. Thus human preference probabilities can be written *solely* in terms of the two policies:

\[
p(y_w \succ y_l \mid x)=
\sigma\!\Big(
    \beta\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
  - \beta\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
\Big),
\tag{3}
\]
where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) is the logistic function and \(y_w, y_l\) denote the preferred and dispreferred completions.

**What does this mean?**  
If we treat the *policy* \(\pi_\theta\) itself as a variable in (3), we no longer need an auxiliary reward network: the difference in log‑probabilities between preferred and dispreferred completions already encodes exactly what the reward would have told us.

## Turning Preference Data into a Simple Classification Loss

Given a dataset of human comparisons
\[
\mathcal D=\{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N,
\]
the maximum‑likelihood objective for DPO becomes

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\,\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal D}\!
  \Big[
    \log\sigma
    \Big(
      \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
     -\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
    \Big)
  \Big]
}.
\tag{4}
}
\]

This is *exactly* a binary cross‑entropy loss! We are simply asking our language model to assign higher relative probability to the human‑preferred answer than to its competitor, with an importance weight controlled by \(\beta\). No extra networks, no sampling during training, no PPO clip loss—just vanilla gradient descent on a classification objective.

### Gradient Intuition

If we differentiate (4), we get an update that looks like:

- Increase log‑probability of the preferred answer \(y_w\).  
- Decrease log‑probability of the dispreferred answer \(y_l\).  
- Scale both directions by how *wrong* our current implicit reward estimate is (the term inside the logistic).  

When our model already ranks \(y_w > y_l\), the scaling factor is small; when it gets it wrong, the factor approaches 1 and pushes harder. This adaptive weighting prevents degeneration that would happen if we naïvely forced \(p(y_w) > p(y_l)\) without regard to how confident we are.

## Why Skipping RL Matters

1. **Stability.**  
   PPO relies on estimating advantage values and clipping gradients; misestimated baselines can cause high variance updates or catastrophic forgetting. DPO’s loss is convex in each example and behaves like ordinary supervised learning, which is well understood and robust.

2. **Efficiency.**  
   No need to sample multiple continuations per prompt during training—each gradient step only looks at two already–collected completions. Experiments show DPO reaches better reward/KL tradeoffs in far fewer updates than PPO.

3. **Simplicity & Reproducibility.**  
   You can implement DPO with a few lines of code on top of any transformer library: load your SFT checkpoint as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), compute log‑probabilities for both answers under both models, plug into (4), backpropagate. No custom RL loops or hyperparameter schedules.

4. **Safety Implications.**  
   Since there’s no separate reward network that could be mis‑specified or overfitted, the alignment signal stays *tightly coupled* to actual human preferences. This reduces opportunities for hidden incentives that might drive unsafe behavior when you later try to “optimize” a learned reward.

## A Concrete Example: Sentiment Steering

Imagine we want our assistant to always answer positively when asked to review a movie snippet. We collect pairs of continuations where one is more positive than the other (using an automatic sentiment classifier as a proxy for human labels).  

- The SFT model provides baseline probabilities for both strings (`π_ref`).  
- DPO adjusts `π_theta` so that log π_theta(y_pos)/π_ref(y_pos) > log π_theta(y_neg)/π_ref(y_neg).  

After training, generating text from `π_theta` yields consistently positive reviews *and* stays close enough to `π_ref` that it still sounds natural—exactly what RLHF aims for but without any policy rollouts.

## Limitations & Open Questions

- **Generalization beyond the training distribution:** DPO inherits whatever distribution bias exists in the preference data; unlike explicit rewards, it cannot be combined with self‑labeling tricks that reuse unlabeled prompts unless we devise analogous schemes.
- **Scaling:** Experiments so far use models up to 6 B parameters; extending DPO to 100 B+ models will require careful engineering but should be straightforward given its low computational demand.
- **Reward Over‑optimization:** Because DPO directly maximizes preference likelihood, one could still see diminishing returns or pathological behavior if preferences are noisy or inconsistent; more work is needed to detect and mitigate such drift.

## Bottom Line

Direct Preference Optimization reveals a surprisingly simple truth: *once you have human preference pairs, you already have everything needed to reshape your language model*. By expressing rewards as differences in log‑probabilities relative to a reference policy, we can sidestep both reward modeling and reinforcement learning entirely. The result is a stable, fast, and easy-to‑implement alignment technique that brings us one step closer to safely deploying powerful LLMs without wrestling with fragile RL pipelines.

If you’re building an AI system today and need steerable behavior—be it sentiment control, safe dialogue, or coding assistance—give DPO a try. You’ll likely save weeks of engineering effort while ending up with models that respect human preferences just as well as those trained with full-blown RLHF.



# From Preferences to Policies: How DPO Turns Human Choices Into Model Behavior  

*Why a seemingly modest change of variables lets us skip reinforcement learning altogether.*

---

## 1. The problem we’re trying to solve  

Large language models (LLMs) are trained on massive corpora of text.  
During pre‑training they pick up all sorts of knowledge **and** a mixture of behaviours—some useful, some not.  
When we want the model to *behave* in a particular way (be helpful, safe, witty, or generate only positive reviews) we need a way to **steer** it.

The dominant recipe today is **RLHF** (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback).  
In short:

1. **Supervised fine‑tuning (SFT)** – give the model a few high‑quality examples.  
2. **Reward modelling** – collect pairs of completions \((y_w, y_l)\) for the same prompt \(x\); ask humans which one they prefer. Fit a neural reward function \(r_\phi(x,y)\) that predicts those preferences.  
3. **RL optimisation** – treat \(r_\phi\) as a reward and run a policy‑gradient algorithm (usually PPO) to maximise it while staying close to the SFT model via a KL‑penalty.

While RLHF works, it is **heavy**: you need a separate reward network, you have to sample from the model during training, and PPO hyper‑parameters are notoriously finicky.

Enter **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** – a method that uses exactly the same preference data but *directly* updates the language model itself with a simple binary‑cross‑entropy loss. No extra reward head, no RL loop, no sampling‑while‑training.

---

## 2. A quick refresher: the Bradley‑Terry model  

Human preferences are often recorded as binary comparisons:

\[
y_w \succ y_l \mid x \quad\text{( “\(y_w\) is better than \(y_l\) for prompt \(x\)” )}
\]

The classic statistical model for such data is the **Bradley‑Terry (BT) model**:

\[
p(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)=
\frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}
     {\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)+\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)} .
\tag{1}
\]

Here \(r^*(x,y)\) is an *unknown* latent reward that assigns higher scores to better completions.  
If we knew \(r^*\), training would be trivial: just increase the probability of the higher‑scoring answer.

In RLHF we *approximate* \(r^*\) with a neural network \(r_\phi\), train it with the logistic loss derived from (1), and then plug it into an RL objective.

---

## 3. The RLHF objective in equations  

The KL‑constrained optimisation that PPO tries to solve is

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}
\;\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \pi_\theta}\!\bigl[ r_\phi(x,y) \bigr]
-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\pi_\theta(\,\cdot\,|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\,\cdot\,|x)\bigr).
\tag{2}
\]

- \(\pi_\theta\) is the policy we are fine‑tuning (the LM we want).  
- \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is typically the SFT model, acting as a “reference” distribution we don’t want to stray too far from.  
- \(\beta>0\) controls how conservative we stay.

Equation (2) isn’t differentiable w.r.t. token probabilities because of the expectation over discrete generations, which is why we resort to REINFORCE/PPO.

---

## 4. A hidden closed‑form solution  

If we treat \(r(x,y)\) as any reward function (not necessarily learned), (2) has an analytical optimum:

\[
\boxed{
\pi_r(y|x)=
\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr)}
     {Z(x)}
},
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y')\bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

(You can find the derivation in many control‑as‑inference papers.)  
Equation (3) says: *the optimal policy is just the reference policy re‑weighted by an exponentiated reward*.  

Now take logs on both sides and rearrange:

\[
r(x,y)=
\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\tag{4}
\]

The pesky partition function \(Z(x)\) depends only on the prompt, not on any particular completion; it will cancel out when we consider **differences** of rewards between two candidates.

---

## 5. From rewards back to preferences – the DPO trick  

Plug (4) into the BT preference probability (1):

\[
p(y_w \succ y_l | x)
=
\sigma
  \Bigl(
    \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
   -\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
  \Bigr),
\tag{5}
\]

where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\).

Crucially, **the unknown partition term vanished**. The probability that a human prefers one completion now depends *only* on ratios of policy probabilities and reference probabilities.  

Thus, if we treat our *policy* \(\pi_\theta\) as the variable we want to learn, we can directly maximise the likelihood of observed preferences:

```python
# Pseudocode for one minibatch
logit = beta * (
    torch.log(pi_theta.logprob(y_w, x)) - torch.log(pi_ref.logprob(y_w, x))
  - torch.log(pi_theta.logprob(y_l, x)) + torch.log(pi_ref.logprob(y_l, x))
)
loss = -torch.nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logit, torch.ones_like(logit))
loss.backward()
optimizer.step()
```

That’s it – a **binary cross‑entropy loss** applied to the language model’s own logits! No separate reward head, no Monte‑Carlo rollouts.

We call this loss **DPO’s objective**:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)} 
   \Bigl[
   \log\sigma
   \Bigl(
   \beta\, \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
 - \beta\, \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
   \Bigr)
   \Bigr].
}
\tag{6}
\]

When you expand gradients of (6), you see exactly what intuition suggests: increase the log‑probability of “good” completions and decrease that of “bad” ones, weighted by how badly the current model ranks them. The weighting term,
\( \sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)) \),
acts like an *automatic curriculum*: if the model already prefers \(y_w\), this term is tiny and the pair contributes little; if it gets them backwards, the gradient spikes.

---

## 6. Visual analogy – “pulling on a rope”  

Imagine each prompt \(x\) as an anchoring post with two strings attached: one leads to \(y_w\), another to \(y_l.\)  
The reference model gives each string a baseline tension (its log‑probability).  
DPO measures how much extra pull our current model applies relative to that baseline and nudges it until the preferred string is tauter than the dispreferred one by an amount proportional to \(\beta.\)

Because every pair contributes only its **relative** tension, we never need to know all possible strings—just those two that humans have compared.

---

## 7. Why DPO works better than PPO (empirically)

| Aspect | PPO | DPO |
|--------|-----|-----|
| Reward modelling | Separate network → extra variance | Implicit inside policy |
| Sampling during training | Required for on‑policy gradients | Not needed |
| Hyper‑parameters | KL target, clipping range, learning rate schedule | Single scalar β (and optimizer settings) |
| Stability | Sensitive to value‑function bias & KL estimate | Closed‑form optimum → no variance from Monte Carlo |
| Compute | Often needs many GPU hours per epoch | Essentially just supervised fine‑tuning cost |

In benchmark experiments (sentiment control, Reddit TL;DR summarisation, single‑turn dialogue), DPO matched or surpassed PPO while using far fewer resources and virtually no hyper‑parameter sweep.

---

## 8. Code sketch – training loop in practice  

Below is a minimal PyTorch–style snippet that shows how you could plug DPO into an existing 🤗 Transformers trainer.

```python
import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("gpt2-large")
ref   = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("gpt2-large")   # copy before fine tuning
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2-large")

beta = 0.1                # tradeoff between reward strength & KL penalty
opt  = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-5)

def logprob(model, inputs):
    """Returns sum_i log p(token_i | prefix_i)."""
    logits = model(**inputs).logits
    # shift so that logits[i] predicts token[i]
    shift_logits = logits[:, :-1].contiguous()
    shift_labels = inputs["input_ids"][:, 1:].contiguous()
    log_probs   = torch.nn.functional.log_softmax(shift_logits, dim=-1)
    token_logp  = log_probs.gather(2, shift_labels.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)
    return token_logp.sum(dim=1)       # shape: [batch]

for batch in dataloader:               # each entry: x, y_w, y_l as raw texts
    # Tokenize everything once
    enc_x   = tokenizer(batch["prompt"],      return_tensors="pt", padding=True)
    enc_ww  = tokenizer(batch["chosen"],      return_tensors="pt", padding=True)
    enc_ll  = tokenizer(batch["rejected"],    return_tensors="pt", padding=True)

    # Concatenate prompt + completion for logprob calculation
    inp_w = {k: torch.cat([enc_x[k], enc_ww[k][:,:-1]], dim=1) for k in enc_x}
    inp_l = {k: torch.cat([enc_x[k], enc_ll[k][:,:-1]], dim=1) for k in enc_x}

    # Log probabilities under current policy and reference
    logp_w_cur = logprob(model, inp_w)
    logp_l_cur = logprob(model, inp_l)
    with torch.no_grad():
        logp_w_ref = logprob(ref , inp_w)
        logp_l_ref = logprob(ref , inp_l)

    # DPO logits → binary cross entropy
    dpo_logit = beta * ((logp_w_cur - logp_w_ref) - (logp_l_cur - logp_l_ref))
    loss = torch.nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
        dpo_logit,
        torch.ones_like(dpo_logit)          # label=1 i.e. w should beat l
    )

    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
```

A few practical notes:

- `ref` can be either the original SFT checkpoint or a *pseudo* reference obtained by maximising likelihood on all chosen completions when an SFT checkpoint isn’t available.
- The loss automatically respects the KL constraint because any deviation from `ref` appears in the numerator/denominator terms.
- You can still use mixed precision (`torch.cuda.amp`) or gradient accumulation; DPO behaves like ordinary supervised fine‐tuning.

---

## 9. When does DPO shine?  

- **Low‑resource setups:** If you have only a few hundred thousand preference pairs and limited GPU budget, DPO reaches good performance in a single epoch.
- **Rapid prototyping:** Because there’s no RL loop you can experiment with new prompts or datasets without worrying about PPO hyper‑parameters.
- **Stability critical applications:** The closed‑form objective removes variance coming from Monte Carlo rollouts or value function approximations—helpful for safety‑critical fine‑tuning where divergence would be unacceptable.

---

## 10. Open questions & future directions  

1️⃣ **Out-of-distribution generalisation** – early results show DPO behaves similarly to PPO on unseen domains (e.g., CNN/DailyMail after training on Reddit TL;DR), but systematic studies remain needed.  

2️⃣ **Self‑labelled data** – can we generate new preference pairs by prompting our own DPO policy (“self‐ranking”) and keep training? This would mirror how PPO leverages large amounts of unlabeled prompts through its value estimator.  

3️⃣ **Scaling** – experiments have proven DPO up to ~6 B parameters; extending to >100 B will test whether numerical stability or memory constraints appear in the log‐ratio terms.

4️⃣ **Beyond text** – any generative system where preferences can be expressed as pairwise comparisons (images, music, code snippets) could adopt DPO by simply swapping out `logprob` for an appropriate likelihood estimator.

---

## 11. Takeaway

The heart of RLHF was always about aligning two distributions: “what humans like” vs “what the model emits”.  
What DPO shows us is that ***the alignment can be expressed directly in terms of those same distributions***—no extra reward head required and no reinforcement learning loop needed.

By reparameterising rewards as log‐ratios between policy and reference probabilities,
the Bradley–Terry likelihood becomes a simple binary cross entropy over language-model logits.
The result is an algorithm that is:

- **Simple** – one line of loss code,
- **Stable** – no variance from sampling,
- **Efficient** – comparable compute to ordinary supervised fine‐tuning,
- **Effective** – matches or exceeds PPO on sentiment control, summarisation and dialogue tasks.

If you’ve been hesitant to dive into RLHF because of its engineering overhead,
give DPO a try: collect some preference pairs (or reuse existing datasets), load your favourite LM as both `model` and `reference`, set a β value you’re comfortable with, and start training with that cross–entropy loss block.
Your language model will start behaving exactly as your human annotators intended—without ever stepping foot in an RL gym.



# The Theory Behind Direct Preference Optimization: Rewards Hidden Inside the Model  

## Introduction  

Large language models (LLMs) are incredibly capable, but steering them toward *desired* behavior still hinges on collecting human preference data and then “training it to listen.”  Most pipelines do this in two steps: first they learn a **reward model** that predicts which of two completions a human would pick, and then they run a reinforcement‑learning (RL) algorithm (often PPO) to push the language model toward higher‑reward outputs while staying close to its original distribution.

The recent **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** algorithm shows that the second step can be dropped entirely. By re‑parameterizing the reward model in terms of the policy itself, DPO turns the whole RLHF objective into a simple binary‑cross‑entropy loss over the language model’s probabilities. The key theoretical insight is that every reward function compatible with the Bradley‑Terry (or more generally Plackett‑Luce) preference model can be expressed as a log‑ratio of the *optimal* policy and a fixed *reference* policy. This means we lose no expressivity when we replace an explicit reward network with the policy’s own logits.

Below we unpack the main theoretical guarantees behind DPO, step by step. Readers who love proofs will find concrete statements, while the surrounding narrative keeps things accessible.

---

## 1. Preference Modeling Recap  

Given a prompt \(x\) and two candidate completions \(y_{1}, y_{2}\), a human judgment is recorded as  

\[
y_{w} \succ y_{l}\mid x,
\]

where \(y_{w}\) is the preferred answer.  The classic **Bradley‑Terry** model assumes that there exists an unknown latent reward function \(r^{*}(x,y)\) such that  

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=\frac{\exp\!\big(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\big)}{\exp\!\big(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\big)+\exp\!\big(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\big)}.
\tag{1}
\]

Because only differences of rewards matter, adding any function of the prompt alone does not change the preference probabilities:

\[
r'(x,y)=r^{*}(x,y)+f(x)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p^{*}\text{ unchanged}.
\]

This observation underlies all of DPO’s theory.

---

## 2. From Rewards to an Optimal Policy  

Standard RLHF solves

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{x,\;y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\,r_{\phi}(x,y)\,]
   \;-\; \beta \, \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big),
\tag{2}
\]

where \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is the pretrained “reference” model (usually an SFT checkpoint).  
A well‑known result from entropy‑regularized RL shows that the **optimal policy** for any fixed reward function \(r\) takes a closed form:

\[
\boxed{
\pi_{r}(y|x)=
   \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
   \exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big)
}
\tag{3}
\]

with normalizer  

\[
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big).
\]

*(Derivation appears in Appendix A of the paper.)*  
Equation (3) tells us exactly how a reward reshapes the reference distribution.

---

## 3. Inverting the Relationship – Rewards as Log‑Ratios  

Take logs on both sides of (3):

\[
\log \pi_{r}(y|x)=
   \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)+
   \frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)-\log Z(x).
\]

Rearranging yields a **reparameterization** of the reward:

\[
\boxed{
r(x,y)=
   \beta\, \log \frac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
   + \beta\, \log Z(x)
}
\tag{4}
\]

Notice that the partition term \(\log Z(x)\) depends only on \(x\); it disappears when we consider *differences* of rewards. Plugging (4) into the Bradley‑Terry preference probability (1) eliminates \(Z(x)\) completely:

\[
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)
=
\sigma
   \Big(
     \beta\, \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{w}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)}
     - 
     \beta\, \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}
   \Big),
\tag{5}
\]

where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\). Equation (5) expresses *human preferences directly in terms of policies*, without ever mentioning an explicit reward network.

---

## 4. Defining the DPO Objective  

If we parametrize a candidate policy as \(\pi_{\theta}\), we can fit it by maximizing the likelihood of observed preferences under (5). The negative log‑likelihood becomes

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)
=
-\, \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}
   \Big[
      \log\sigma
      \Big(
        \beta\, \log
        \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
        -
        \beta\, \log
        \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
      \Big)
   \Big].
}
\tag{6}
\]

Training with ordinary stochastic gradient descent on (6) yields **the exact optimal policy** for *some* reward function that belongs to the same equivalence class as the true latent reward \(r^{*}\). No separate reward model or RL loop is needed.

---

## 5. Equivalence Classes of Rewards  

### Definition (Reward Equivalence)  
Two rewards \(r, r'\) are **equivalent** if there exists a function \(f(x)\) such that  

\[
r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x),~\forall x,y.
\tag{7}
\]

Because preference models depend only on differences \(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\), equivalent rewards induce identical preference distributions:

- **Lemma 1 (Preference Invariance).** Under Bradley‑Terry or Plackett‑Luce, equivalent rewards generate identical human‑preference probabilities.

Similarly, plugging an equivalent reward into (3) yields exactly the same optimal policy:

- **Lemma 2 (Policy Invariance).** If \(r\simeq r'\), then their optimal policies satisfy \(\pi_r = \pi_{r'}.\)

The proofs use simple algebraic substitution and are detailed in Appendix B of the paper.

### Consequence  
All that matters for alignment is *the equivalence class*, not a particular numeric scale or offset. DPO’s reparameterization (4) selects a **canonical representative** from each class: the one whose additive term equals zero, i.e.,  

\( r_{\text{canon}}(x,y)=
   \beta\, \log\dfrac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}.\)

Thus DPO retains **full expressivity**—any reward function that could be learned by an explicit reward network can be represented by some policy via this log‑ratio mapping.

---

## 6. Formal Guarantee: Any Compatible Reward Can Be Expressed  

**Theorem (Representability).**  
Given any reward function \(r(x,y)\) that defines a valid Bradley‑Terry preference model, there exists a policy \(\pi(y|x)\) such that  

\( r(x,y)=
   \beta\, \log\dfrac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + c(x), 
  \)

where \(c(x)= -\,\beta\,\log Z(x)\) depends only on \(x.\)

*Proof Sketch.*  
Start from Eq. (3) to write the optimal policy for \(r.\) Define an operator  

\( f(r;x,y)= r(x,y)-c(x), 
   ~~c(x)= -\,\beta\,\\log Z(x).\)

Since \(c(x)\) does not depend on \(y,\) \(f(r)\) is equivalent to \(r.\) Substituting Eq. (3)’s expression for \(Z(x)\) shows that  

\( f(r;x,y)=
   \beta\,\\log\dfrac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}, 
   ~~~{\rm with}~ 
   \pi_r(y|x)=
     {\rm opt.~policy~for~} r.
   
Hence every equivalence class contains at least one reward of the log‑ratio form, establishing representability.

∎

The theorem guarantees that optimizing Eq. (6) is **not** a restriction—it simply picks that canonical member automatically.

---

## 7. Why DPO Is More Stable Than PPO  

When PPO optimizes Eq. (2), it must estimate expectations over sampled continuations and often introduces separate baselines or value networks to reduce variance. The term  

\( -\,\beta\,\\log Z(x)\)

in Eq. (4) acts as an *exact soft value function*. PPO approximates it with learned critics; any error propagates as high variance gradients and can cause instability or divergence.

In contrast, DPO’s loss (6) embeds this normalizer analytically via the log‑ratio; no extra baseline is required. The gradient of (6),

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{DPO}
=
-\beta\, 
\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)} 
    [
       \sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))
       (\nabla_\theta \!\log\nu_\theta(y_w)- 
        \nabla_\theta!\log\nu_\theta(y_l))
    ],
\\
~~
where~ 
    $\hat r_\theta = 
        \beta\,\\log\dfrac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\rm ref}}
    $,
weights each pair by how badly its current implicit reward order disagrees with humans. This weighting naturally prevents “policy collapse” observed in naïve unlikelihood training.

---

## 8. Summary of Theoretical Takeaways  

| Concept | Classical RLHF | Direct Preference Optimization |
|---------|----------------|--------------------------------|
| **Reward modeling** | Separate network trained on binary comparisons | Implicitly encoded as policy log‑ratio |
| **Policy optimality** | Obtained via RL (e.g., PPO) on learned reward | Closed form via Eq. (3); solved by maximizing likelihood |
| **Expressivity loss?** | None (if reward well‑trained) | None – Theorem guarantees every compatible reward has an equivalent policy |
| **Stability source** | Approximate value baselines → high variance | Exact analytic normalizer → no extra critic |
| **Training pipeline** | 3 stages: SFT → Reward → RL | 2 stages: SFT → Direct cross‑entropy on preferences |

In essence, *the language model itself hides a reward function*. By pulling this hidden reward out and training it directly, DPO sidesteps both an explicit reward network and expensive RL loops while keeping all theoretical guarantees intact.

---

## 9. Looking Ahead  

The theory behind DPO opens several research directions:

1. **Out‑of‑distribution generalization:** Since DPO never learns an explicit scalar reward, does it inherit better robustness when faced with prompts far from training data?  
2. **Self‑labeling:** One could sample new pairs from a trained DPO policy, label them with its own implicit rewards, and continue fine‑tuning—a form of semi‑supervised learning without extra human data.  
3. **Scaling:** The derivation holds for any model size; understanding how gradient noise behaves at billions of parameters will be crucial for industrial deployment.

For anyone who enjoys clean mathematics meeting practical AI, DPO illustrates a beautiful principle: *sometimes you don’t need to build something new—you just have to look at what you already have in a different light.*



# DPO vs. PPO: A Head‑to‑Head Comparison on Real‑World Tasks  

In the past year, “reinforcement learning from human feedback” (RLHF) has become the go‑to recipe for turning a massive, unsupervised language model into a helpful assistant. Most open‑source pipelines still rely on **proximal policy optimization** (PPO) to maximize a learned reward while keeping the model close to its original “reference” checkpoint.  

A new paper *Direct Preference Optimization* (DPO) argues that we can skip the entire RL loop, train directly from preference data with a single binary‑cross‑entropy loss, and still respect the same KL‑regularized objective that PPO optimizes. Below I walk through the paper’s three real‑world experiments—sentiment control, summarization, and single‑turn dialogue—show how DPO stacks up against PPO (and a few baselines), and explain why the numbers matter in plain language.

---

## The Core Idea in One Sentence  

Both PPO and DPO try to **maximise human‑aligned reward** while **penalising drift** from the original language model.  
- PPO does this by learning a separate reward model, then running a policy‑gradient RL algorithm.  
- DPO rewrites the KL‑constrained objective so that the **policy itself encodes the reward**:  
  \[
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\Big[\log\sigma\big(\beta\!\log\!\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta\!\log\!\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\big)\Big].
  \]  
No sampling, no value network, no extra hyper‑tuning—just a standard cross‑entropy loss over pairs of preferred/dispreferred completions.

Because the loss directly reflects the Bradley–Terry preference model, optimizing it yields **the exact optimal policy** for the underlying (unknown) reward function. In practice this translates into faster training and fewer stability headaches.

---

## 1️⃣ Sentiment Control: The Cleanest Test Bed  

### What we did  
- Prompt: a movie‑review prefix from IMDb.  
- Goal: generate a *positive* continuation.  
- Preference labels were generated automatically by a pre‑trained sentiment classifier (so we had ground‑truth rewards).  
- Trained three families of models:  
  * **PPO** (reward from classifier, KL target values 3–12)  
  * **PPO‑GT** (oracle that sees the true sentiment score)  
  * **DPO** (β ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 1, 5})  

### How we measured success  
Two axes are crucial:  

| Metric | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| **Reward** (average sentiment positivity) | Higher reward = more positive text. |
| **KL divergence** from reference model | Controls how far we wander from the original distribution; too high KL often means loss of fluency or “gaming” of the reward. |

We plotted every training run on a **reward–KL frontier** (think of it as a racing chart where you want to be as far up and as far left as possible).

### The headline numbers  

- **DPO dominates the frontier**: for any given KL budget, DPO attains *more* positive sentiment than PPO or even PPO‑GT.
- At KL ≈ 6, DPO’s reward is ≈ 5 % higher than PPO’s best point.
- When we allow a larger KL budget (≈ 12), PPO’s reward plateaus while DPO continues to climb.

### Everyday analogy  

Imagine you have a car that can speed up (increase reward) but you must stay within a city speed limit (KL). With PPO you need to constantly switch gears and risk overshooting; with DPO you simply press the accelerator and the car smoothly obeys the limit without stalling.

---

## 2️⃣ Summarization: TL;DR on Reddit Threads  

### Setup  

- Data: Reddit posts with human written TL;DR summaries (the “gold” reference).  
- Preference dataset: ~170 k pairwise judgments collected by prior work.  
- Base model: GPT‑J fine‑tuned on preferred summaries (the SFT checkpoint).  

We evaluated with two complementary metrics:

1. **Win rate vs. reference summary** using GPT‑4 as an evaluator (the model decides which of two summaries is better).  
2. **Temperature sweep** (0.0–1.0) to test robustness to sampling noise.

### Results at a glance  

| Method | Best win rate (%) | Temperature where it peaks |
|--------|-------------------|----------------------------|
| SFT (no preference finetuning) | 53 | 0.0 |
| Preferred‑FT (supervised on chosen completions) | 55 | 0.0 |
| PPO (learned reward) | 57 | 0.0 |
| **DPO** | **61** | **0.25** |
| Best‑of‑128 (oracle that samples many candidates then picks highest‑reward) | 63 | N/A |

Key observations:

- **Higher win rate:** DPO beats PPO by about 4 percentage points—a notable jump given both start from the same SFT checkpoint.
- **Temperature robustness:** DPO’s performance stays above 58 % across temperatures 0–0.5, while PPO drops sharply above 0.1.
- **Less tuning needed:** We used default β = 1 for DPO; PPO required careful reward normalisation and multiple KL targets.

### Why it matters  

Summarization is notoriously hard for automatic metrics; human preference is the gold standard. DPO improves that gold standard *without* needing an extra reward network or expensive sampling at inference time.

---

## 3️⃣ Single‑Turn Dialogue: Helpful & Harmless Chat  

### Experiment details  

- Dataset: Anthropic Helpful & Harmless dialogues (~170 k human–assistant exchanges).  
- No pre‑existing SFT model, so we first fine‑tuned Pythia‑2.8B on the *preferred* responses only (“Preferred‑FT”) to create a reasonable reference policy.
- Trained DPO on top of this reference.
- Baselines: zero‑shot GPT‑J prompting, few‑shot Pythia prompting, Preferred‑FT alone, and a “Best of 128” heuristic that picks the highest‐scoring response out of many samples.

### Evaluation  

GPT‑4 judged each candidate against the human‐preferred response in the test set; we also ran a small human study confirming GPT‑4’s judgments line up with real people.

### Numbers

| Method | Win rate vs. reference response |
|--------|--------------------------------|
| Pythia zero/few shot | ~45 |
| Preferred‑FT | ~49 |
| PPO (public implementation) | ~52 |
| **DPO** | **58** |
| Best of 128 Preferred‑FT | ~59 |

Notice how DPO closes almost the entire gap to the expensive “Best of 128” oracle while requiring *only one forward pass* at inference time.

### Takeaway  

For dialogue—where nuance and safety are critical—DPO delivers higher helpfulness scores without any RL instability or lengthy hyperparameter sweeps.

---

## The Bigger Picture: Reward–KL Tradeoff, Stability & Simplicity  

### Reward–KL Tradeoff  

Both PPO and DPO optimise

\[
\max_\pi \; \mathbb{E}[r(x,y)] - \beta \,\text{KL}(\pi\,||\,\pi_{\text{ref}}),
\]

but DPO solves it **in closed form**, turning the KL term into an importance weight inside a binary cross entropy loss. The result is an *exact* front–line optimisation: every improvement in reward comes with a provably minimal increase in KL.

### Training Stability  

PPO suffers from three well known pain points:

1. High variance policy gradients → noisy updates.  
2. Need for a separate value network or baseline to reduce variance.  
3. Hyperparameter sensitivity (clip ε, KL target, learning rate).

DPO removes all three:

- No variance because we never sample actions during training—only static pairs from the preference dataset.
- No value network required; the KL term is deterministic given π and π_ref.
- Only one new scalar β to tune; experiments show default values work well across tasks.

In practice this translates into smoother loss curves and fewer “explosions” where the model starts spitting gibberish.

### Computational Footprint  

A single epoch of DPO on a 6 B parameter model took roughly **1/3 of the GPU hours** compared to PPO with identical data size—even though both see the same number of preference pairs. The savings come from:

- No generation loop inside each training step.
- Simpler optimiser (standard AdamW works fine).
- No need for experience replay buffers or advantage estimation.

For labs with limited compute, this is a game changer.

---

## Visual Summary (the charts you’d see in the paper)

1️⃣ *Reward–KL frontier*: DPO’s curve sits above all PPO curves—a classic “dominates” plot.

2️⃣ *Win rate vs temperature*: Lines for each method; DPO’s line is flatter and higher overall.

3️⃣ *Dialogue win rates*: Bar chart where DPO nearly matches Best of 128 while other methods lag behind.

These plots reinforce what we’ve already described numerically: **Higher quality + lower drift + less hassle = DPO wins**.

---

## TL;DR – When Should You Choose DPO?  

| Scenario | Choose DPO if… |
|----------|----------------|
| You already have an SFT checkpoint & pairwise preference data | You want to upgrade it quickly without building a separate reward model |
| Compute budget is tight | You need a single epoch training loop instead of costly RL rollouts |
| Stability matters (e.g., production pipelines) | You prefer deterministic losses over noisy policy gradients |
| You care about clean KL budgeting | You like having one knob β that directly controls divergence |

If you’re comfortable running RL pipelines and already have tuned PPO code, you can still use PPO—but be prepared for extra engineering overhead and potentially lower performance on modest datasets.

---

## Looking Ahead  

The authors acknowledge open questions:

* How does DPO behave on models >100 B parameters? Early signs suggest it scales because it never needs additional networks.
* Can we combine DPO with self‑labeling tricks used in RLAIF? That would let us recycle unlabeled prompts without ever training an explicit reward function.
* What does over‑optimization look like when you push β very low? Preliminary curves hint at diminishing returns beyond a certain point—something worth exploring before deploying at extreme temperatures.

Nevertheless, for most practitioners who want to bring human preferences into their models without wrestling with RL intricacies, DPO offers a compelling alternative—simple enough for rapid prototyping yet powerful enough to beat state-of-the-art PPO baselines on sentiment control, summarisation, and dialogue tasks.

# How to Deploy Direct Preference Optimization in Your Own Project  

*Turn human preferences into a fine‑tuned language model without any reinforcement learning. In this tutorial we walk through building a preference dataset, picking a reference model, coding the DPO loss with PyTorch / 🤗 Transformers, and fine‑tuning a ~2 B‑parameter LM. We finish with practical tips on hyper‑parameters and common pitfalls.*

---

## 1. Why Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)?

Traditional RLHF pipelines train three separate components:

1. **Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT)** – a base LM that already knows how to generate text.  
2. **Reward Model** – learned from human pairwise comparisons.  
3. **RL step (usually PPO)** – maximizes the reward while staying close to the SFT model via a KL penalty.

This pipeline is powerful but heavyweight: you need to generate samples during training, tune PPO clipping parameters, and keep the reward model stable.  

DPO replaces the whole reward‑model + RL stage with a single binary‑cross‑entropy loss that directly reshapes the policy’s probability distribution:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=-
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\log\sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}-
\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
\Bigr)
\]

Here  

* \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is the reference (usually the SFT) model,  
* \(\pi_\theta\) is the model we are fine‑tuning,  
* \(y_w\) / \(y_l\) are the preferred and dispreferred completions, and  
* \(\beta\) controls how strongly we push the policy toward the preferred answer (larger \(\beta\) → tighter KL constraint).

Because the loss is just a standard cross‑entropy term, training reduces to ordinary gradient descent—no sampling loop, no value function, no PPO hyper‑parameters.

---

## 2. Preparing a Preference Dataset  

### 2.1 Collecting Pairwise Comparisons  

A DPO dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) consists of triples \((x, y_w, y_l)\). The easiest way to collect them:

| Step | What you do | Tooling ideas |
|------|--------------|---------------|
| **Prompt selection** | Choose a set of inputs \(x\) relevant to your task (e.g., “Write a positive review of …”). | Pull examples from your downstream corpus or generate synthetic prompts with GPT‑4. |
| **Sample two completions** | Run your *reference* model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) twice (or use two different models) to obtain \(y_1, y_2\). | `model.generate` with `do_sample=True`, `temperature=0.7`. |
| **Human labeling** | Show annotators the prompt and both completions; ask which is *better* (or “more helpful”). | Use lightweight UIs like **Prolific**, **Scale AI**, or an internal Google Form. |
| **Store triples** | Record `prompt`, `preferred`, `rejected`. | CSV / JSONL works fine (`{"prompt":..., "chosen":..., "rejected":...}`).

If you lack budget for human annotators, you can bootstrap with a *proxy* classifier—e.g., a sentiment model for sentiment control or an automatic summarization metric for TL;DR tasks—as long as you treat it as “pseudo‑preferences”. The original DPO paper demonstrated that such synthetic preferences still yield useful policies.

### 2.2 Formatting for PyTorch  

```python
import json
from pathlib import Path

def load_preferences(path: str):
    data = []
    for line in Path(path).read_text().splitlines():
        obj = json.loads(line)
        data.append((obj["prompt"], obj["chosen"], obj["rejected"]))
    return data
```

You’ll later feed these strings into the tokenizer of your LM.

---

## 3. Choosing & Loading the Reference Model  

The reference model should be *the same architecture* you intend to fine‑tune because DPO needs both \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and \(\pi_\theta\) probabilities for each token. Typical workflow:

```python
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

model_name = "EleutherAI/pythia-2b"   # ~2 B parameters
tokenizer   = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)
tokenizer.pad_token = tokenizer.eos_token   # ensure padding works

# Load reference (SFT) checkpoint
ref_model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
    "path/to/your/sft_checkpoint",  # can be same as base if you skip SFT
    device_map="auto"
)
ref_model.eval()   # keep it frozen during DPO training
```

If you have an existing SFT checkpoint (e.g., after instruction‑tuning), use it as the reference; otherwise just load the raw pretrained checkpoint—this matches how DPO papers initialize \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

---

## 4. Implementing the DPO Loss  

Below is a compact PyTorch implementation that works with 🤗 Transformers.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

beta = 0.1                # typical starting value; see §7 for tuning

def log_prob(model, input_ids, attention_mask):
    """
    Returns log probabilities of each token given its prefix.
    Shape: (batch, seq_len)
    """
    with torch.no_grad():                     # ref model is frozen
        outputs = model(input_ids=input_ids,
                        attention_mask=attention_mask,
                        output_hidden_states=False,
                        return_dict=True)
    # logits shape: (batch, seq_len, vocab)
    logits = outputs.logits
    # shift so that token i predicts token i+1
    shift_logits = logits[:, :-1, :].contiguous()
    shift_labels = input_ids[:, 1:].contiguous()
    log_probs = F.log_softmax(shift_logits, dim=-1)

    # Gather log prob of the true next token
    token_logp = log_probs.gather(2,
                 shift_labels.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)   # (batch, seq_len-1)

    # Mask padding tokens
    if attention_mask is not None:
        token_logp = token_logp * attention_mask[:, 1:]

    # Sum over sequence dimension -> total log prob of whole completion
    seq_logp = token_logp.sum(dim=-1)      # (batch,)
    return seq_logp

def dpo_loss(theta_model,
             ref_model,
             batch):
    """
    batch: dict with keys `prompt_ids`, `chosen_ids`, `rejected_ids`,
           each already padded + attention mask tensors.
    """
    # ----- logs under current policy -----
    pi_chosen   = log_prob(theta_model,
                           batch["chosen_ids"],
                           batch["chosen_mask"])
    pi_reject   = log_prob(theta_model,
                           batch["rejected_ids"],
                           batch["reject_mask"])

    # ----- logs under reference -----
    pi_ref_chosen = log_prob(ref_model,
                             batch["chosen_ids"],
                             batch["chosen_mask"])
    pi_ref_reject = log_prob(ref_model,
                             batch["rejected_ids"],
                             batch["reject_mask"])

    # Compute the DPO score inside σ()
    diff = beta * ( (pi_chosen - pi_ref_chosen)
                  - (pi_reject - pi_ref_reject) )   # shape (batch,)
    
    loss = -F.logsigmoid(diff).mean()
    return loss
```

### DataLoader preparation

```python
from torch.utils.data import Dataset, DataLoader

class DPODataset(Dataset):
    def __init__(self, triples, tokenizer, max_len=256):
        self.triples = triples
        self.tok = tokenizer
        self.max_len = max_len
    
    def __len__(self): return len(self.triples)

    def __getitem__(self, idx):
        prompt, chosen, rejected = self.triples[idx]

        # Encode each piece separately then concatenate prompt+completion.
        enc_prompt   = self.tok(prompt,
                                truncation=True,
                                max_length=self.max_len,
                                add_special_tokens=False)
        enc_chosen   = self.tok(chosen,
                                truncation=True,
                                max_length=self.max_len,
                                add_special_tokens=False)
        enc_reject   = self.tok(rejected,
                                truncation=True,
                                max_length=self.max_len,
                                add_special_tokens=False)

        def combine(p,c):
            ids   = p['input_ids'] + c['input_ids'] + [self.tok.eos_token_id]
            mask  = p['attention_mask'] + c['attention_mask'] + [1]
            ids   = ids[:self.max_len]
            mask  = mask[:self.max_len]
            return torch.tensor(ids), torch.tensor(mask)

        chosen_ids, chosen_mask       = combine(enc_prompt, enc_chosen)
        reject_ids, reject_mask       = combine(enc_prompt, enc_reject)

        return {
            "prompt_ids": torch.tensor(enc_prompt['input_ids']),
            "chosen_ids": chosen_ids,
            "chosen_mask": chosen_mask,
            "rejected_ids": reject_ids,
            "reject_mask": reject_mask,
        }

dset   = DPODataset(preferences, tokenizer)
loader  = DataLoader(dset,
                     batch_size=8,
                     shuffle=True,
                     collate_fn=lambda x: {k: torch.stack([d[k] for d in x])
                                          for k in x[0]})
```

### Training loop

```python
from transformers import AdamW, get_scheduler

model      = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(model_name).to("cuda")
optimizer  = AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=5e-6)

num_epochs   = 3
num_steps    = len(loader) * num_epochs
scheduler    = get_scheduler("linear",
                            optimizer=optimizer,
                            num_warmup_steps=int(0.1*num_steps),
                            num_training_steps=num_steps)

model.train()
for epoch in range(num_epochs):
    for step,batch in enumerate(loader):
        loss = dpo_loss(model, ref_model, batch)
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        scheduler.step()
        optimizer.zero_grad()

        if step % 100 == 0:
            print(f"epoch {epoch} step {step} loss {loss.item():.4f}")
```

That’s it—once training finishes you have a model that directly embodies the preferences encoded in your dataset.

## 5. Hyper‑parameter Guidelines  

| Hyper‑parameter | What it controls | Reasonable range / tip |
|-----------------|------------------|------------------------|
| `beta`          | Strength of KL regularization (larger → less drift from reference). | Start with `0.1`. For aggressive steering try `0.5`. If KL blows up (>10 bits per token) reduce it. |
| Learning rate (`lr`) | Step size for policy update. | Because DPO gradients are similar magnitude to standard LM fine‑tuning, `5e-6`–`2e-5` works well for 2 B models. |
| Batch size      | Number of preference triples per update; larger batches reduce variance of the importance weight term (`σ` argument). | With GPU memory ~24 GB you can push to 32; otherwise 8–16 is fine. |
| Max sequence length (`max_len`) | Upper bound on total prompt+completion tokens used for log‑prob computation. | Keep it low enough (<256 tokens) to avoid excessive padding while still covering your completions fully. |
| Number of epochs / steps | Training horizon before over‑fitting preferences becomes visible. | Monitor validation win‑rate; early stopping after ~3–5 epochs usually suffices for ~10k preference pairs. |

**Tip:** Track two metrics on a hold‑out preference set:  

1. **Preference accuracy** – fraction of pairs where your current policy ranks the preferred completion higher (just compute Eq.(5)).  
2. **Average KL** – compute sequence‑level KL between \(\pi_\theta\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  

A good DPO run will climb accuracy while keeping KL modest (<5 bits per token). If KL skyrockets early on, lower `beta`.

---

## 6. Troubleshooting Common Issues  

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---------|--------------|-----|
| **Policy collapses to always output the preferred completion** (very low diversity). | `beta` too high → KL term dominates; model overfits to narrow set of tokens.| Reduce `beta` or add small temperature during generation at inference time. |
| **Training diverges (loss becomes NaN or spikes)** | Log probabilities underflow because of extremely long sequences or missing padding masks.| Verify that masks are correctly aligned; clip sequence length; add `torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_`. |
| **Preference accuracy stalls at ~60% despite many epochs** | Preference data may be noisy or insufficiently varied.| Increase dataset size; optionally augment with synthetic preferences from a high‑quality classifier; double‑check label quality. |
| **Large KL but high reward** | Model drifts far from reference; may be okay if you intentionally want aggressive steering.| If you care about preserving base knowledge (e.g., factuality), lower `beta` or keep a small fraction of SFT data mixed in (simple “mixed fine‑tune”). |

---

## 7. Evaluation Checklist  

After training:

1️⃣ **Win rate against reference** – Use GPT‑4 or human judges to compare generated outputs from \(\pi_\theta\) vs.\ \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  
2️⃣ **KL measurement** – Compute average per‑token KL on an unseen prompt set:  

```python
def avg_kl(policy_model, ref_model, prompts):
    ks = []
    for p in prompts:
        ids   = tokenizer(p, return_tensors="pt").input_ids.to("cuda")
        # sample two completions per prompt for stable estimate
        out_pi  = policy_model.generate(ids,
                                        max_length=ids.shape[-1]+50,
                                        do_sample=True)
        out_ref= ref_model.generate(ids,
                                    max_length=ids.shape[-1]+50,
                                    do_sample=True)

        pi_logp   = log_prob(policy_model,out_pi,out_pi.ne(tokenizer.pad_token_id))
        ref_logp  = log_prob(ref_model ,out_pi,out_pi.ne(tokenizer.pad_token_id))
        ks.append((pi_logp - ref_logp).exp().log().mean().item())
    return sum(ks)/len(ks)
```

3️⃣ **Task-specific metrics** – ROUGE for summarization, sentiment classifier score for sentiment control etc., but always corroborate with human judgment because automated metrics can be misaligned.

## 9️⃣ Final Thoughts  

Direct Preference Optimization removes the most cumbersome part of RLHF: reinforcement learning itself. By reparameterizing rewards as log‑probability ratios relative to a frozen reference model we obtain a clean cross‑entropy objective that can be trained with any modern deep learning stack.

*Key takeaways*  

* You only need **one** model at inference time—no separate reward network or PPO actor/critic pair.
* The method scales exactly like standard supervised fine‑tuning; training cost is dominated by forward passes through both policy and reference models.
* Hyper‑parameters are minimal: essentially `beta`, learning rate and batch size.
* Empirically DPO matches or exceeds PPO on sentiment control, summarization and dialogue tasks while being far easier to reproduce.

Give it a try on your next alignment experiment—whether you’re nudging an assistant toward helpfulness or biasing generation toward a specific style—without spending weeks wrestling with PPO clipping schedules! Happy fine‑tuning!
