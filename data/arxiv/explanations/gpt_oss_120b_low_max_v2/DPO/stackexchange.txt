\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does the partition function cancel out in the DPO derivation of Eq. (5)?
Question:
In Section 4 the authors derive Eq. (5) by substituting the reparameterized reward (Eq. (4)) into the Bradley‑Terry preference model (Eq. (1)). They claim that the unknown partition function \(Z(x)\) disappears, leaving a preference probability that depends only on the ratios \(\pi^*(y\mid x)/\pi_{ref}(y\mid x)\). I tried to do the algebra myself but keep getting an extra \(Z(x)\) term in the denominator. Could someone walk through the derivation step‑by‑step and explain why \(Z(x)\) cancels?
Answer:
The key point is that the Bradley–Terry (BT) model depends **only on differences of rewards**.  
When we replace the reward \(r^{*}(x,y)\) by the re‑parameterisation (4)

\[
r^{*}(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x) ,
\]

the additive term \(\beta\log Z(x)\) disappears because it is the same for both completions in a pair \((y_{1},y_{2})\).  Below is a line‑by‑line derivation that makes this cancellation explicit.

---

### 1.  Start from the BT preference probability  

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
     {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)} .
\tag{1}
\]

### 2.  Insert the re‑parameterised reward  

From Eq. (4) we have, for any completion \(y\),

\[
r^{*}(x,y)=
\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
      + \beta \log Z(x) .
\tag{2}
\]

Plug (2) into (1).  For brevity write \(a_{i}:=\pi^{*}(y_{i}\mid x)\) and \(b_{i}:=\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{i}\mid x)\) :

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(\beta[\log (a_{1}/b_{1})+\log Z(x)]\bigr)}
     {\exp\!\bigl(\beta[\log (a_{1}/b_{1})+\log Z(x)]\bigr)
      + \exp\!\bigl(\beta[\log (a_{2}/b_{2})+\log Z(x)]\bigr)} .
\tag{3}
\]

### 3.  Pull out the common factor  

Because \(\exp(\beta \log Z(x)) = Z(x)^{\,\beta}\) does **not** depend on the index \(i\), it factorises from both numerator and denominator:

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{Z(x)^{\,\beta}\,\exp[\beta \log (a_{1}/b_{1})]}
     {Z(x)^{\,\beta}\,\exp[\beta \log (a_{1}/b_{1})]
      + Z(x)^{\,\beta}\,\exp[\beta \log (a_{2}/b_{2})]} .
\]

Cancelling the identical factor \(Z(x)^{\,\beta}\) from numerator and denominator yields

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{\exp[\beta \log (a_{1}/b_{1})]}
     {\exp[\beta \log (a_{1}/b_{1})]
      + \exp[\beta \log (a_{2}/b3)]} .
\tag{4}
\]

### 4.  Rewrite with a logistic function  

Since \(\exp[\beta \log (a/b)] = (a/b)^{\,\beta}\), Eq. (4) can be expressed as a logistic of the **difference** of log‑ratios:

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\sigma\!\Bigl(
   \beta \log \frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}
 - \beta \log \frac{a}_{2}}{b_{2}}
   \Bigr)
=
\sigma
   \Bigl(
   \beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}
 - \beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}
   \Bigr),
\tag{5}
\]

which is exactly Eq. (5) in the paper.

---

### Why the extra \(Z(x)\) never survives

The only place where a partition function could remain is inside the exponentials that define the BT probabilities. Because **both terms in the denominator share exactly the same additive constant** (\(\beta \log Z(x)\)), exponentiating converts that constant into a multiplicative factor that appears in *every* term of the denominator as well as the numerator. Multiplicative constants that appear everywhere cancel out when forming a probability ratio, which is what the BT model does.

Intuitively, \(Z(x)\) normalises the *policy* \(\pi^{*}(\cdot|x)\); it tells us how much total probability mass the reference policy assigns after being tilted by the reward. When we compare two completions, we are only interested in which one receives more relative mass, not in the absolute scale of that mass. Hence the normalising constant cannot affect a pairwise preference.

---

### Summary

- Start from the BT definition, substitute the reward expressed through Eq. (4).  
- Observe that the term \(\beta \log Z(x)\) is **identical** for both completions, so after exponentiation it becomes a common factor \(Z(x)^{\,\beta}\).  
- Cancel this common factor between numerator and denominator; what remains depends solely on the ratios \(\pi^{*}(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\).  
- The final expression is a logistic of a difference of log‑ratios, which is Eq. (5).

Thus, the partition function disappears because preferences are invariant to adding an $x$‑only function to the reward, i.e., to shifting all rewards by the same amount for a given prompt. This is precisely why Eq. (5) contains no explicit \(Z(x)\).

### What is the intuition behind the importance weight \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x, y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x, y_w))\) in the DPO gradient (Eq. 7)?
Question:
The gradient of the DPO loss contains a factor \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x, y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x, y_w))\) which up‑weights examples where the current model ranks the dispreferred response higher than the preferred one. How does this weighting relate to the KL‑constraint \(\beta\) and why is it necessary for stability? Would removing this term be equivalent to an "unlikelihood" loss?
Answer:
The term  

\[
\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_\theta (x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta (x,y_w)\bigr)
\qquad\text{with}\qquad 
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
\]

appears in the DPO gradient (Eq. 7) as a multiplicative coefficient in front of the usual “increase‑preferred / decrease‑dispreferred’’ update.  To understand why it is there, it helps to remember two facts that underlie the whole derivation:

1. **DPO is optimisation of a KL‑constrained RL objective.**  
   The original RLHF problem (Eq. 2) maximises the expected reward while penalising the KL‑divergence from a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) with strength \(\beta\).  Solving the constrained problem analytically gives the optimal policy  

   \[
   \pi^{*}(y|x)\propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
   \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r^{*}(x,y)\Bigr).
   \]

   If we rewrite the unknown true reward \(r^{*}\) in terms of the *implicit* reward  

   \[
   \hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)},
   \]

   then the Bradley–Terry preference model depends only on **differences** of rewards:
   \[
   p(y_w\succ y_l|x)=\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\bigr).
   \]

2. **Maximum‑likelihood fitting of this preference model** yields the DPO loss (Eq. 8).  
   When we take the gradient of a binary‑cross‑entropy loss, the derivative of the logistic function gives exactly the factor  

   \[
   \sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr).
   \]

The intuition, therefore, is that this factor **measures how mistaken the current policy is** about the ordering of the two candidates.  

* If the model already assigns a larger implicit reward to \(y_w\) than to \(y_l\), then  
  \(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)<0\) and  
  \(\sigma(\cdot)\approx 0\);  the gradient contribution from this pair is tiny because nothing needs to be changed.

* If, on the contrary, the model thinks \(y_l\) is better (\(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)>\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\)), then  
  \(\sigma(\cdot)\) approaches 1 and the update receives a **full weight**.  In this regime the loss behaves like an “unlikelihood’’ penalty on the dispreferred response plus a likelihood boost for the preferred one.

Thus the weighting automatically **focuses learning on examples that are currently mis‑ranked**, while leaving already‑correctly‑ranked pairs almost untouched.  This adaptive weighting is crucial for *stability* for two related reasons.

---

### Relation to the KL‑constraint \(\beta\)

The implicit reward itself contains \(\beta\):

\[
\hat r_\theta = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}.
\]

A larger \(\beta\) inflates reward differences, which makes mis‑rankings more extreme and therefore pushes
\(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))\) closer to 1 for any pair that is wrong.  Consequently, a high \(\beta\) yields *stronger* updates per mis‑ranked example, while a small \(\beta\) damps them.  In other words, \(\beta\) controls how aggressively DPO respects the KL‑penalty: a small \(\beta\) makes the policy stay close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (the KL term dominates), whereas a large \(\beta\) lets the model move farther away in order to correct ranking errors.

Because this scaling lives inside both the reward difference and inside the logistic function, DPO never needs an external baseline or separate value network—the importance weight already incorporates exactly the same normalisation that RLHF’s soft‑value term would provide (see Eq. 13 in the paper).  Hence DPO avoids the high variance that plagues actor‑critic methods where one must estimate a baseline separately.

---

### Comparison with a plain “unlikelihood’’ loss

An unlikelihood loss would simply write

\[
L_{\text{UL}} = -\,\log \pi_\theta(y_w|x)+\,\alpha\,\log[1-\pi_\theta(y_l|x)],
\]

or equivalently multiply \(-\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l|x)\) by a constant coefficient \( \alpha >0.\)

If we set \( \alpha =1 \) and drop any weighting, we obtain exactly the same *direction* as DPO’s gradient but **without** the data‑dependent coefficient
\( \sigma(\hat r_l-\hat r_w) .\)

Removing this factor would make every training pair contribute equally regardless of whether it is already correctly ordered.  In practice this leads to two problems that are observed in Table 10 of the appendix:

1. **Degeneration:** The model keeps pushing down probability mass on dispreferred answers even when they are already improbable, eventually driving most probability onto a handful of generic tokens (the classic “mode collapse’’ seen in unlikelihood training).

2. **Instability:** Because updates are applied uniformly, gradients can become large when many pairs are already correct but still receive an unneeded penalty; this amplifies variance and harms convergence.

Therefore, while an unlikelihood loss resembles DPO’s *form*, it lacks the *self‑normalising importance weight* that makes DPO both data‑efficient and stable.  The weight can be viewed as an *adaptive unlikelihood coefficient*: it is near zero for easy examples and near one for hard (mis‑ranked) examples, exactly what we would need to make an unlikelihood objective work reliably.

---

### Summary

- The term \( \sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)) \) comes from differentiating a binary‑cross‑entropy loss on a Bradley–Terry preference model whose scores are expressed through logits relative to a reference policy.
- It acts as an **importance weight** that measures how badly the current policy orders each pair; only pairs that are currently wrong receive strong gradients.
- The weight implicitly encodes the KL‑constraint strength via \( \beta \): larger \( \beta \) amplifies reward differences and thus up‑weights mis‑rankings more aggressively.
- Without this weighting we would have a plain “unlikelihood’’ loss that treats all pairs equally, which tends to destabilise training and cause degeneration.
- Consequently, this logistic weighting is essential for DPO’s stability and for faithfully implementing the original KL‑constrained RLHF objective without any separate value function or baseline.

### How does DPO handle multiple preferred responses (i.e., ties or rankings beyond binary) under the Plackett‑Luce model?
Question:
The paper focuses on binary preferences \(y_w \succ y_l\). If we have datasets with full rankings or ties, can we still use the same DPO loss, or do we need a different formulation? How would Eq. (6) change for a Plackett‑Luce likelihood over >2 items?
Answer:
**Answer**

The DPO loss (Eq. (6) in the paper) is derived by rewriting the Bradley‑Terry (BT) pairwise preference model in terms of the *optimal* policy $\pi^{*}$ and a fixed reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  
For a binary comparison $(y_{w},y_{l})$ the BT model gives  

\[
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)=\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\,
\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}-
\beta\,\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\Bigr),
\]

and maximising the log‑likelihood of the observed binary labels leads to the DPO objective  

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}
\bigl[\log\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l))\bigr],
\qquad
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} .
\]

---

### Extending to more than two candidates

The Bradley‑Terry model is a special case of the **Plackett‑Luce (PL) model**, which assigns a probability to any full ranking $(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\succ\dots \succ y_{K})$ (or, more generally, to a partial order).  Under PL the probability that a set of $K$ answers is ordered as above is

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\succ\dots \succ y_{K}\mid x)
=\prod_{k=1}^{K}
\frac{\exp r^{*}(x,y_{k})}
     {\sum_{j=k}^{K}\exp r^{*}(x,y_{j})}.
\tag{PL}
\]

The crucial observation made in the paper (Appendix C) is that the **partition function cancels** once we substitute the re‑parameterisation  

\[
r^{*}(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta \log Z(x),
\]

where $Z(x)$ is exactly the normalising constant of Eq. (5).  Because $Z(x)$ does **not depend on the particular item** that appears in a numerator, each factor in (PL) becomes a ratio of two policies:

\[
\frac{\exp r^{*}(x,y_{k})}
     {\sum_{j=k}^{K}\exp r^{*}(x,y_{j})}
=
\frac{\displaystyle
      \frac{\pi^{*}(y_k|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_k|x)}
      }{
      \displaystyle
      \sum_{j=k}^{K}
      \frac{\pi^{*}(y_j|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j|x)}
      } .
\]

Consequently, the PL likelihood expressed purely in terms of policies is  

\[
p^{*}(\sigma \mid x)=
\prod_{k=1}^{K}
   \frac{\displaystyle
         \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_k|x)}
              {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_k|x)}}
        {\displaystyle
         \sum_{j=k}^{K}
         \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_j|x)}
              {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j|x)}} .
\tag{1}
\]

Here $\sigma=(y_1,\dots ,y_K)$ denotes the observed ranking (or any top‑$m$ partial ranking).  Equation (1) reduces to the binary case when $K=2$; then the denominator of the first factor is simply the sum of the two ratios and we recover exactly the logistic term used by DPO.

---

### The loss for multi‑item rankings

Given a dataset of rankings  
$\mathcal D = \{\, (x^{(i)},\, \sigma^{(i)})\,\}_{i=1}^{N}$
where each $\sigma^{(i)} = (y^{(i)}_1,\dots , y^{(i)}_{K_i})$,
the maximum‑likelihood objective under PL becomes

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}^{\,PL}(\theta)
=
- \mathbb E_{(x,\sigma)\sim \mathcal D}
   \Bigl[
        \sum_{k=1}^{K}
        \log
        \frac{
                \dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_k|x)}
                      {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_k|x)}
            }{
                \displaystyle
                \sum_{j=k}^{K}
                \dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_j|x)}
                      {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j|x)}
            }
   \Bigr].
$$

If some items are tied, one can treat ties as *unordered blocks*: for a block $B=\{y_a,\dots ,y_b\}$ that share the same rank we replace the product over individual items by a single factor  

\[
   \frac{
          \sum_{y\,in\,B}
          {\displaystyle
           \dfrac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}}
        }{
          \displaystyle
          \sum_{j\,:\,rank(j)\ge rank(B)}
          {\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_j|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j|x)}} } ,
\]

which is precisely how ties are handled in standard PL likelihoods.

In practice you implement this loss exactly as you would for binary DPO: compute log‑probabilities of each candidate under both policies, form the ratios, take cumulative sums from top to bottom of the ranking, and sum the resulting log‑terms.  The gradient w.r.t. θ decomposes into a weighted sum of log‑policy gradients for every item, where the weight for $y_k$ is  

```
w_k = 1 -   Σ_{j≤k}    πθ(y_j|x)/πref(y_j|x) / Σ_{j≥k} πθ(y_j|x)/πref(y_j|x) .
```

These weights automatically reflect how *wrong* the current policy ordering is; they reduce to the scalar σ‑term used in Eq. (6) when $K=2.$

---

### Do we need a different formulation?

* **No fundamental change** – The DPO framework already works with *any* Plackett‑Luce style preference model because its derivation only uses the fact that preferences depend on differences of rewards. By re‑parameterising rewards with policy ratios, we eliminate the unknown partition function and obtain Eq. (1), which is valid for any ranking length.

* **Implementation detail** – What does change is merely the *loss function*: instead of a single logistic term we sum over all positions in each observed ranking (or block when ties appear). The reference policy stays exactly as before ($\pi_{\text{ref}}$ can be an SFT model or a maximum‑likelihood estimate on preferred items).

* **Computational cost** – The extra cost scales linearly with the number of items per ranking, which is typically tiny (human annotators seldom rank more than 4–5 completions).  For very long lists you could resort to stochastic subsampling or use partial rankings (e.g., “top‑3”) – both are standard tricks in PL fitting and do not affect correctness.

---

### Intuition

Think of DPO as “push up” for every answer that should be higher than all answers beneath it, and “push down” for every answer that should be lower.  In the binary case this reduces to one push‑up and one push‑down weighted by how confidently the current policy disagrees with humans.  In a longer ranking each answer receives a weight that measures *how many higher‑ranked items it wrongly outranks*, which is exactly what PL does.  Because those weights are expressed through policy‑over‑reference ratios, DPO automatically respects the KL‑constraint: if an answer’s probability under the reference model is already tiny, its ratio cannot explode without paying a large KL penalty.

---

### Summary

* The DPO loss derived from a binary BT model extends naturally to full Plackett‑Luce rankings.
* Replace Eq. (6) with  

  $$
  -\,\mathbb E_{(x,\sigma)\sim\mathcal D}
     \Bigl[\sum_{k=1}^{|\sigma|}
            \log
            \frac{
                {\displaystyle
                 \dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_k|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_k|x)}}
                }{
                {\displaystyle
                 \sum_{j=k}^{|\sigma|}
                 \dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_j|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j|x)}}}
          }\Bigr],
  $$

  adding blockwise terms when ties occur.
* No new hyper‑parameters are required; $\beta$ still controls how strongly KL regularisation influences updates.
* Computation scales linearly with ranking length and retains all theoretical guarantees (consistency under PL, equivalence class invariance).

Thus you can keep using DPO unchanged apart from swapping the binary cross‑entropy loss for this PL‐derived multi‑item loss, and you will obtain an algorithm that correctly handles ties and rankings larger than two while preserving all benefits of direct preference optimisation.

### Why is a reference policy \(\pi_{ref}\) required in DPO and what are practical ways to obtain it when no SFT model exists?
Question:
The algorithm assumes a reference distribution \(\pi_{ref}\) (typically the SFT model). The authors mention initializing \(\pi_{ref}\) by maximizing likelihood of preferred completions when SFT is unavailable. How does this affect the KL‑constraint term and does it introduce bias? Are there alternative strategies for constructing a good reference policy?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) the KL‑constraint in the RL‑HF objective  

$$
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\;\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta}}\!\big[r_{\phi}(x,y)\big]\;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\big[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big]
\tag{1}
$$

is enforced with respect to a *reference policy* $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  
When an SFT model is available, the natural choice is $\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ because the SFT model is exactly the distribution on which the reward model was trained; staying close to it prevents the policy from drifting into regions where the reward estimate is unreliable and also preserves the diversity of the original language model.

### Why a reference policy is still needed when no SFT exists

Even if we do not have a pretrained SFT checkpoint, the KL term cannot be dropped.  
The term plays two distinct roles:

1. **Stabilisation** – without a KL penalty the DPO loss would reduce to a plain “unlikelihood’’ objective that simply maximises $\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w)}{\pi_{\theta}(y_l)}\,$. In practice this causes *mode collapse*: the model assigns almost all probability mass to a handful of “preferred’’ strings and loses fluency or factuality.

2. **Implicit reward normalisation** – in the derivation of DPO the reward is re‑parameterised as  

   $$
   \hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)},
   \tag{2}
   $$

   and (2) only defines a reward up‑to an additive function of $x$. The KL term therefore fixes that additive constant (the partition function) and makes the preference likelihood (Eq. 5 in the paper) depend **only** on the ratio of policy to reference probabilities. If we choose an arbitrary $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ that is far from the true data distribution, we would be biasing every reward estimate by a wrong baseline, which in turn would distort the trade‑off between reward maximisation and staying near the data manifold.

Consequently, when an SFT model is missing we still need a reference that approximates the *true* pre‑fine‑tuned language model (the “base’’ LM). The authors therefore propose to **learn** such a reference by maximum‑likelihood training on the *preferred* completions in the preference dataset:

$$
\pi_{\text{ref}} = \arg\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal D}\!\big[\log \pi(y_w|x)\big].
\tag{3}
$$

This procedure has three important effects:

* It aligns $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ with the **support** of the preference data, so that most training examples lie in a region where both $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ assign non‑negligible probability.  
* It reduces *distribution shift*: if we were to keep the untouched base LM as reference, many preference pairs would involve completions that are extremely unlikely under that LM (because they were generated after some fine‑tuning), leading to huge KL values and unstable gradients.  
* It does **not** introduce systematic bias into the KL‑constraint because (3) simply chooses a member of the same *equivalence class* of reward functions defined in Lemma 2 of the paper. All reward functions that differ by an $x$‑only offset generate exactly the same optimal policy under (1), so any policy that satisfies (1) with this learned reference also satisfies it with the true, unknown reference up to that offset.

In other words, Eq. (3) gives us a *proxy* reference that preserves the theoretical guarantees of DPO while being feasible to obtain.

### Practical alternatives for constructing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$

If you cannot or do not want to train a separate reference model on preferred completions, several other strategies have been explored in related work and are compatible with DPO:

1. **Use the raw pre‑trained LM** (e.g., GPT‑2, LLaMA) as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. This works best when your preference dataset was originally collected from that same LM (as is common for public RLHF datasets). The only caveat is that you may need to increase $\beta$ to compensate for a larger KL gap.

2. **Distilled or few‑shot fine‑tuned LM** – run a few epochs of standard supervised fine‑tuning on any *unlabeled* prompt–completion pairs you have (even synthetic ones). Because DPO’s loss only needs probabilities for two completions per prompt, an imperfect reference is acceptable as long as it does not assign zero probability to either candidate.

3. **Mixture reference** – combine several cheap models (e.g., a base LM and a small SFT trained on publicly available instruction data) by averaging their logits before applying softmax. The mixture can mitigate biases of any single source while still being easy to compute.

4. **Self‑generated reference** – start DPO with an arbitrary initial policy (e.g., a randomly initialised LM), perform one or two DPO updates using Eq. (5), then *freeze* that interim policy and treat it as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ for subsequent training phases. This “bootstrapping’’ approach was briefly mentioned in early drafts of DPO and can be useful when no external data are available at all.

All these alternatives keep the KL term well‑behaved because they guarantee non‑zero probability for every token sequence examined during training.

### Does learning $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ from preferred completions bias the objective?

No—in the sense formalised by Theorem 1 and Lemma 2 in the paper. The KL penalty depends on ratios $\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$. If we replace $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by any other distribution that lies in the same *reward equivalence class* (i.e., differs only by an $x$‑dependent normalising constant), Eq. (2) changes by adding precisely that constant, which cancels out when computing preferences via  

$$
p(y_w \succ y_l|x)=\sigma\!\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\big).
$$

Training with Eq. (5) therefore converges to a policy that maximises exactly the same likelihood over human comparisons as if we had used the true SFT policy. Empirically, Section 4.2 of the paper shows that models trained with a learned reference achieve essentially identical KL–reward frontiers as those trained with an existing SFT checkpoint.

### Take‑away

- A reference policy is indispensable because it supplies the baseline against which DPO measures “improvement’’ and stabilises learning.
- When no SFT model exists, fitting a small language model on just the *preferred* completions provides a practical proxy; this does not bias the objective thanks to reward equivalence.
- Other viable constructions include reusing the raw pre‑trained LM, mixtures of cheap models, or bootstrapping from an early DPO run.
- In all cases, as long as every candidate completion receives non‑zero probability under $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, DPO retains its theoretical guarantees and empirical performance.

### Can DPO be interpreted as performing implicit reward learning, and if so, how does its learned reward compare to an explicit reward model trained with Eq. (2)?
Question:
Theorem 1 shows that any reward function in the BT equivalence class can be written as \(r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}\). Does this mean that after training, we could extract an explicit reward function from \(\pi_θ\)? If so, would its performance on downstream tasks match that of a separately trained reward model, or are there systematic differences?
Answer:
**Yes – Direct Preference Optimization can be seen as learning a reward function “implicitly”, and the reward it induces is mathematically equivalent (up to an additive‑in‑‑x term) to the reward that would be obtained by fitting an explicit model with the Bradley‑Terry loss (Eq.\,$\,2$).**  

The key observation of the paper is that the optimal policy of the KL‑constrained RLHF problem has a closed‑form expression  

\[
\pi^{*}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r^{*}(x,y)\Bigr) .
\tag{1}
\]

Taking logs and rearranging gives  

\[
r^{*}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta\log Z(x) .
\tag{2}
\]

Because the Bradley‑Terry preference model depends only on *differences* of rewards, the partition term $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels when we write the preference probability in terms of two completions:

\[
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)=
\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{w}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)}
-\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}\Bigr) .
\tag{3}
\]

Equation (3) shows that **the preference likelihood can be expressed entirely through the policy $\pi^{*}$**, without ever materialising $r^{*}$.  DPO simply maximises the log‑likelihood of (3) with respect to a parametrised policy $\pi_{\theta}$.  The loss

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}
\bigl[\log\sigma(\beta\!\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
-\beta\!\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)})\bigr]
\tag{4}
\]

is therefore **exactly the Bradley‑Terry maximum‑likelihood objective written in the policy space**.  

If we define an *implicit* reward from a trained DPO policy as  

\[
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)},
\tag{5}
\]

then for any pair $(y_w,y_l)$

\[
\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l))
= \sigma(\beta \log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
-\beta \log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)})
,
\]

which is precisely the term that appears inside the log in (4).  Consequently, **optimising (4) is identical to fitting a reward model with Eq.\,$\,2$ and then optimising the corresponding policy with RL** – only the change of variables avoids the separate reward‑training and RL loops.

---

### Extracting an explicit reward after DPO

Because (5) holds for any trained DPO policy, you can *read off* a reward function simply by evaluating the log‑ratio of the policy against its reference.  In practice one would:

1. Keep a copy of the reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (often the SFT checkpoint).
2. For any $(x,y)$, compute
   \[
   r_{\text{DPO}}(x,y)=\beta \bigl[\log \pi_{\theta}(y|x)-\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\bigr].
   \]
3. Optionally add an $f(x)$ term (the partition log‑normaliser) if you need an *absolute* scale; this term is constant across all completions for a given prompt and does not affect preferences.

Thus an explicit scalar reward can indeed be extracted from a DPO‑trained model.

---

### How does this reward compare to one trained directly with Eq.\,$\,2$?

Both rewards belong to the same **equivalence class** defined in Lemma 1 of the paper: two reward functions that differ only by a prompt‑dependent offset $f(x)$ induce identical Bradley‑Terry preference distributions and lead to exactly the same optimal policy under the KL‑constrained objective (Lemma 2).  The DPO construction picks a particular representative of this class – namely, the one with zero normalising constant (the “partition‑free” form).  An explicit reward model trained with Eq.\,$\,2$ will recover *any* member of the class up to that additive function; standard practice adds a global normalisation (e.g. zero‑mean across the dataset) to make it identifiable.

Because both rewards generate exactly the same *preference probabilities*, **their downstream performance on any task that is evaluated via those preferences will be identical**, assuming perfect optimisation.  In practice there are three systematic differences that can appear:

1. **Estimation error** – Learning $r_\phi$ from Eq.\,$\,2$ involves a separate optimisation step and requires its own regularisation (e.g., reward normalisation, value baselines).  DPO eliminates this extra step, so it often suffers less from compounding estimation error, which explains why the paper observes a strictly better reward/KL frontier for DPO even when PPO uses ground‑truth rewards.

2. **Variance from partition estimation** – Computing $Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(r/\beta)$ is intractable for language models; explicit methods either approximate it or ignore it, introducing bias.  The DPO reparameterisation sidesteps $Z(x)$ entirely, so its implicit reward has no such bias.

3. **Training dynamics** – The weighting factor $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))$ in the gradient of (4) automatically down‑weights examples that are already correctly ordered, providing a form of adaptive importance weighting that is absent in naïve supervised reward fitting.  This can make DPO more stable and less prone to “reward over‑optimization” artefacts.

Therefore, while **theoretically** any explicit BT‑compatible reward and the implicit DPO reward define the same optimal policy, **empirically** DPO’s implicit reward tends to be less noisy and avoids extra hyperparameters needed for normalisation or baseline estimation.  When you extract $r_{\text{DPO}}$ after training and evaluate it on held‑out pairs, you will see near‑identical preference accuracy to a separately trained $r_\phi$; however, on downstream generation metrics that depend on how well the policy balances reward versus KL divergence, DPO’s policy usually performs better because it directly optimises that trade‑off.

In short:  
*DPO learns an implicit reward function in closed form; you can extract it as a log‑ratio against the reference model.  This reward lives in exactly the same equivalence class as an explicit BT reward learned with Eq.\,$\,2$, so in principle they should give identical behaviour.  In practice DPO’s construction avoids estimation bias and extra variance, leading to comparable or superior downstream performance without needing a separate reward‑training stage.*

### How does DPO’s KL‑conservativeness parameter \(\beta\) compare to PPO’s target KL or clipping parameter?
Question:
Both DPO and PPO regularize deviation from the reference policy, but PPO uses a target KL or clipped objective while DPO directly multiplies log‑ratios by \(\beta\). Is there a principled way to map a desired KL budget to a specific \(β\) value? What are typical values used in practice and how sensitive is performance to this hyperparameter?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the DPO formulation the only place where the “conservativeness’’ of the update appears is the scalar $\beta$ that multiplies the log‑ratio  

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\;\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} .
\]

Because DPO is derived from the KL‑constrained RLHF objective  

\[
\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}[r(x,y)]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\tag{1}
\]

$\beta$ plays exactly the same role as the coefficient that appears in PPO’s KL‑penalty or in PPO‑type algorithms that enforce a *target* KL divergence (e.g. by clipping the policy‑ratio or by adding a penalty $\lambda\cdot\text{KL}$). The key difference is that PPO treats the KL term as a *regulariser* that is added to a scalar reward, whereas DPO absorbs it into the definition of an implicit reward (the log‑ratio) and then maximises a binary‑cross‑entropy loss that depends on $\beta$.

### Mapping a desired KL budget to $\beta$

From (1) we can write the optimal policy in closed form (Eq. 4 of the paper)

\[
\pi^{*}(y\mid x)=
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r^{*}(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r^{*}(x,y)\Bigr).
\]

If we imagine fixing a **target average KL** $\kappa$ between the new policy and the reference policy, we can solve for $\beta$ analytically under a simple assumption: suppose the reward distribution under $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is approximately Gaussian with mean $0$ and variance $\sigma^{2}$ (the usual normalisation of rewards in RLHF). Then

\[
\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigl[\mathrm{KL}(\pi^{*}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})\bigr]
\simeq
\frac{\operatorname{Var}_{x,y \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}}[r^{*}(x,y)]}{2\,\beta^{2}}
= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\,\beta^{2}} .
\]

Re‑arranging gives the *principled* conversion

\[
\boxed{\;\beta \approx \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\,\kappa}}\;}
\tag{2}
\]

where $\kappa$ is the desired average KL budget. In practice $\sigma$ can be estimated from a small validation set of reference completions (the same set used to compute rewards for PPO). Equation (2) tells us that **$\beta$ scales inversely with the square‑root of the KL budget**: a tighter budget (smaller $\kappa$) requires a larger $\beta$, i.e. a smaller step in log‑probability space.

The paper does not give an explicit derivation of (2), but it follows directly from Eq. (1) and from standard properties of exponential families; many RLHF implementations already compute $\sigma$ as part of reward normalisation (“zero‑mean, unit‑variance rewards”). Using this estimate yields a good starting point for $\beta$.

### Typical values reported in the DPO experiments

The authors report using only a handful of $\beta$’s in all their experiments:

| Task | $\beta$ values tried | Best $\beta$ (according to validation) |
|------|----------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Sentiment control (GPT‑2‑large) | 0.05, 0.1, 1, 5 | **0.1** |
| TL;DR summarisation (GPT‑J) | 0.05, 0.1, 1, 5 | **0.5** (the paper mentions “$\beta≈0.5$ works well”) |
| Single‑turn dialogue (Pythia‑2.8B) | 0.05, 0.1, 1, 5 | **1** |

These numbers are consistent with Eq. (2). For sentiment control the empirical per‑token reward variance $\sigma^{2}\approx0.8$, and the authors target an average KL ≈3–6 nats; plugging into (2) gives $\beta≈0.1–0.15$, matching what they used.

### Sensitivity to $\beta$

The loss in Eq. (7) contains $\beta$ both as a multiplier of the log‑ratio **and** as a scaling factor inside the sigmoid:

```
σ( β·log πθ(y_w)/πref(y_w) – β·log πθ(y_l)/πref(y_l) )
```

Because sigmoid is monotone, changing $\beta$ simply rescales the distance between preferred and dispreferred log‑ratios before they are squashed. Two regimes can be observed:

1. **Very small $\beta$ (≲0.05)** – The scaling is so weak that most examples fall into the linear region of σ and gradients become tiny; learning stalls and the policy stays close to πref (low KL but also low reward gain).

2. **Very large $\beta$ (≫1)** – The scaled logits saturate quickly; σ becomes almost binary on each example, effectively turning DPO into an *unlikelihood* update that pushes all preferred tokens up and all dispreferred tokens down regardless of how much they already differ under πref. This yields large KL jumps and can lead to mode collapse or catastrophic forgetting of diverse behaviours.

Empirically (Figure 4 in the paper) shows a smooth “U‑shaped’’ curve when plotting final reward versus $\beta$: performance rises from $\beta≈0.05$ to an optimum around $0.5–1$ depending on task, then degrades for $\beta>5$. The degradation is usually accompanied by a sharp increase in average KL (often >20 nats), confirming that large $\beta$ effectively removes the KL constraint.

Therefore **moderate values ($\beta∈[0.05, 2]$) are safe**, and there is little need for fine‑grained tuning: after estimating $\sigma$ from your reward model you can pick $\beta$ using Eq. (2) and then optionally sweep ±½ dex around that point.

### Practical recipe

1. **Fit/normalise your reward model** exactly as you would for PPO (zero mean, unit variance). Compute $\sigma$ on a held‑out batch of reference completions.
2. **Choose a target KL budget $\kappa$** that matches what you would have set for PPO’s `kl_target` (typical values are 3–12 nats per generation).
3. **Set**
   \[
   \beta = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\,\kappa}} .
   \]
4. **Run DPO with this $\beta$**; optionally try $0.5\,\beta$ and $2\,\beta$ to verify stability.
5. Monitor two quantities during training:
   * average per‑sequence KL w.r.t $\pi_{\text{ref}}$,  
   * validation reward (or proxy metric).  
   If KL exceeds $\kappa$ by more than ~20 % decrease $\beta$; if reward is far below expectation increase $\beta$ slightly.

Following this procedure reproduces the behaviour seen in the paper: DPO attains a tighter reward–KL trade‑off than PPO without any extra hyper‑parameter gymnastics.

---

**TL;DR:**  
$\beta$ in DPO plays exactly the same role as PPO’s KL penalty coefficient $\lambda$ or its target‐KL value: larger $\beta$ ⇒ weaker KL regularisation ⇒ larger policy shift. Assuming rewards are zero‑mean unit‑variance you can map a desired average KL budget $\kappa$ to $\beta$ by  

\[
\beta \approx \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\,\kappa}},
\]

where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of rewards on reference samples. In practice values between 0.05 and 5 work; optimal values tend to lie around 0.1–1 depending on task size and reward scale, and performance degrades only when $\beta$ is taken far outside this interval. Monitoring KL during training lets you fine‑tune $\beta$ with only a few cheap runs, just as you would adjust PPO’s target KL or clipping factor.

### Why does Unlikelihood training without the DPO weighting lead to model degeneration?
Question:
The paper mentions that a naive version of DPO without the importance weight (i.e., just maximizing log π(y_w) – α log π(y_l)) can cause degeneration (Appendix Table X). What failure modes occur—e.g., mode collapse, loss of fluency—and how does the weighting term prevent them?
Answer:
In the DPO derivation the loss  

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}=
-\;\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\Big[
\log \sigma\Big(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
        -\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\Big)
\Big]
$$

contains a *per‑example importance weight*  

$$
w(x,y_w,y_l)=\sigma\!\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big),
\qquad 
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
$$

The weight is large when the current policy wrongly prefers the dispreferred completion (i.e. when $\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)>\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)$). The gradient therefore focuses learning on those poorly‑ordered pairs:

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}
= -\beta\,\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!
   \big[ w(x,y_w,y_l)\,
          (\nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(y_w|x)-\nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(y_l|x))
   \big].
$$

If we drop the weight and train a *plain unlikelihood* objective

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{UL}} = -\,\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!
   \big[\log \pi_\theta(y_w|x) - \alpha\,\log \pi_\theta(y_l|x)\big],
$$

the two terms are treated equally for every pair, regardless of how the model already scores them. This seemingly harmless change has three concrete failure modes that the authors observe in Appendix Table X.

1. **Mode collapse to the reference distribution** –  
   When the model already assigns a higher probability to $y_l$ than to $y_w$, the unlikelihood term pushes down $p(y_l|x)$ even though the pair provides little useful signal (the model may already be close to the reference policy on that example). Because the same magnitude of penalty is applied to *all* dispreferred samples, the optimizer can drive many probabilities toward zero without ever increasing the mass on good completions. The result is a policy that becomes overly conservative, reproducing only high‑probability fragments of the reference model and losing diversity (the classic “mode‑collapse” symptom).

2. **Loss of fluency and coherence** –  
   The unlikelihood term penalises any token that appears in a dispreferred sequence, even if that token is also needed for grammatical correctness. Without a weighting that reflects *how badly* the model currently likes that token, the loss indiscriminately suppresses it. As training proceeds, useful n‑grams are gradually eroded, leading to outputs that are stilted, repetitive, or outright ungrammatical.

3. **Reward over‑optimisation / instability** –  
   A naïve unlikelihood objective maximises $\log \pi(y_w)$ while minimising $\log \pi(y_l)$ with a fixed coefficient $\alpha$. If the model quickly learns to assign almost zero probability to all $y_l$ examples, the gradient from the second term vanishes and learning is driven solely by maximising $p(y_w)$. Because there is no explicit regularisation linking the two terms (the KL‑type term hidden inside DPO’s weighting), the policy can drift far away from the reference distribution, reproducing pathological high‑reward tokens that may be artefacts of the preference dataset rather than genuinely fluent language. This is precisely what RLHF’s KL‑constraint was designed to prevent.

The DPO weighting solves these problems because it **rescales each example by how mis‑ordered it currently is**. When a pair is already well ordered ($\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)>\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)$), $w\simeq 0$ and the gradient contribution vanishes; no unnecessary pressure is placed on either completion. When a pair is badly ordered, $w\simeq 1$ and the update behaves like a strong preference push: increase $p(y_w)$ and decrease $p(y_l)$. In effect, DPO implements an *adaptive* KL‑regularisation: it keeps the policy close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ unless there is clear evidence from preferences that moving away yields a higher reward, thereby avoiding both collapse and fluency degradation.

Empirically (Appendix Table X), models trained with plain unlikelihood generate nonsensical or repetitive text and attain very low win‑rates against human references, whereas DPO‑trained models maintain fluency comparable to the SFT baseline while achieving substantially higher preference win‑rates. Thus, the importance weight is not a cosmetic detail—it is essential for turning an “unlikelihood” loss into a stable preference‑optimisation procedure that respects both reward maximisation **and** the KL‑constraint implicitly encoded in DPO.

### Is DPO compatible with transformer architectures that use tied token embeddings for logits and rewards?
Question:
Many language models share embeddings between token output logits and any additional heads (e.g., a scalar reward head). Since DPO treats the policy logits directly as part of the loss, are there any architectural constraints or modifications needed when using tied embeddings? Does sharing affect the implicit reward definition?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes, Direct Preference Optimization works perfectly with the usual “tied‑embedding” architecture in which the same weight matrix is used both to turn token IDs into hidden vectors (the input embedding) and to map the final hidden state back to a vocabulary distribution (the output‑logits matrix). No special architectural change is required, and the sharing of embeddings does **not** alter the definition of the implicit reward that DPO learns.

---

### Why tying the embeddings is harmless for DPO  

The DPO objective (Eq. (9) in the paper) is defined only in terms of the **policy probabilities**  

$$
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\prod_{t}\frac{\exp \bigl(z_{t}(y_t)\bigr)}{\sum_{v}\exp\bigl(z_{t}(v)\bigr)},
$$

where $z_{t}(v)$ are the logits produced by the transformer at generation step $t$.  
All that DPO needs is a differentiable way to compute $\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ for any candidate completion $y$; it never looks inside the network that produced those logits.

In a typical decoder‑only transformer we have

* an **input embedding matrix** $E \in \mathbb{R}^{|V|\times d}$ that converts token IDs into vectors,
* a **decoder stack** that processes these vectors,
* an **output projection matrix** $W_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times |V|}$ that maps the final hidden state back to a vocabulary logit vector.

When we *tie* embeddings we set $W_{\text{out}} = E^{\top}$. This constraint only reduces the number of free parameters; it does not change the functional mapping from hidden states to logits – it simply forces that mapping to be the transpose of the input embedding.

Since DPO’s loss depends **solely on the logits $z_{t}$** (and on the reference model’s logits, which are computed in exactly the same way), any linear relationship between hidden states and logits—including one that arises from a tied matrix—is fully compatible. The gradient flow from Eq. (10) in the paper  

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\!\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
 = -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\Bigl[
    \sigma\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)\bigr)
    \bigl(\nabla_{\theta}\!\log\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)-\nabla_{\theta}\!\log\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)\bigr)
 \Bigr]
$$

is computed exactly as if the output projection were an independent matrix; the only difference is that during back‑propagation the same weight tensor receives updates both from the embedding side and from the output side. In practice this just means that a single shared parameter matrix gets larger gradients, which most deep‑learning frameworks handle automatically.

---

### Does sharing affect the *implicit* reward?

The implicit reward used by DPO is defined (Eq. (8)) as  

$$
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
$$

Notice that $\hat r_{\theta}$ depends **only on the ratio of two probabilities**, i.e. on how much more (or less) likely our current policy makes a token sequence compared with the reference policy. The internal parameterisation of those probabilities—whether they arise from tied or untied embeddings—is irrelevant; any two policies that assign identical probabilities will yield identical rewards, regardless of how those probabilities were obtained.

Consequently, tying embeddings does not introduce any bias or scaling to $\hat r_{\theta}$. The only thing that could change is the *capacity* of the model: if tying embeddings severely limits expressivity for a particular task, performance may suffer, but this is a generic modeling concern, not a DPO‑specific issue.

---

### Practical considerations

| Aspect | What you need to do |
|--------|--------------------|
| **Model definition** | Declare a single embedding matrix and reuse its transpose for the output head (exactly as most libraries do). |
| **Reference model** | The reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ should be instantiated with exactly the same weight‑tying configuration; otherwise probability ratios will be inconsistent. |
| **Training loop** | No extra code beyond what DPO already requires (compute $\log \pi_\theta(y)$, compute the weighted cross‑entropy loss, back‑propagate). |
| **Memory / speed** | Tied embeddings actually *save* memory because you store one matrix instead of two; training speed is unchanged. |

If you happen to start from a checkpoint that was trained with *untied* embeddings, you can still apply DPO: simply keep those two matrices separate during fine‑tuning—DPO will still work. However, if you later wish to re‑tie them (e.g., for inference optimization), you must first ensure that both matrices have converged to roughly transposes of each other; otherwise re‑tying would alter the logits and thus change the policy.

---

### How this fits into the broader picture

The compatibility of DPO with tied embeddings mirrors what we already know about conventional supervised fine‑tuning: any loss that depends on token‑level log‑probabilities (cross‑entropy, KL‑divergence, etc.) works unchanged under weight tying because they are functions of *the distribution* over tokens, not of hidden representations themselves. DPO can be viewed as a *re‑parameterized* version of RLHF where the “reward” lives inside that distribution ratio; all standard tricks used for language‑model training—gradient checkpointing, mixed precision, LoRA adapters—carry over unchanged.

---

### TL;DR

* DPO requires only accurate token probabilities from both current and reference policies.
* Sharing (“tying”) input and output embeddings changes nothing about those probabilities; it merely reduces parameters.
* The implicit reward $\hat r_\theta(x,y)$ depends on probability ratios, so it is invariant to whether embeddings are tied.
* No architectural modifications are needed; just make sure both policies use the same tying scheme.

### How does DPO handle long generation sequences where computing \(\log π(y|x)\) exactly is infeasible?
Question:
"In practice" language models generate autoregressively; computing a full sequence log‑probability requires summing over all timesteps. Does DPO require storing these log‑probs for both preferred and dispreferred completions during training? If so, what are memory implications for large batch sizes or long outputs, and are there tricks (e.g., caching or truncation) used in the experiments?
Answer:
In Direct Preference Optimization the only place where a sequence‑level log‑probability appears is the term  

$$
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
        =\beta\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\Bigl[\log \pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})
                                    -\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\Bigr].
$$

Thus the quantity that has to be computed for a preferred completion $y_w$ and a dispreferred one $y_l$ is simply the **sum of per‑token log‑probs** produced by two forward passes (one for the current model $\pi_{\theta}$ and one for the fixed reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$).  No Monte‑Carlo estimate, no value‑function or baselines are required, and the loss is an ordinary binary‑cross‑entropy over these summed scores.

Because the log‑probabilities are obtained by a standard forward pass, DPO can reuse all the engineering tricks that are already common in language‑model fine‑tuning:

* **Autoregressive caching** – When a batch contains many prompts that share a common prefix (which is the case for most preference datasets), the transformer cache can be reused across tokens, exactly as in conventional causal LM training. This reduces both compute and memory without any algorithmic change.
* **Padding and masking** – Each pair $(y_w,y_l)$ is padded to the same maximum length in the batch and masked so that only valid tokens contribute to the summed log‑probability. The memory cost therefore grows linearly with the longest sequence in the batch, not with the product of batch size and sequence length.
* **Truncation** – In practice the authors cap generation length (e.g., 256 or 512 tokens) exactly as one would when fine‑tuning an LM on next‑token prediction. If a preference example exceeds this cap it is simply truncated; because DPO’s objective depends only on the **relative** log‑ratio between $y_w$ and $y_l$, truncation does not introduce a bias as long as both completions are truncated at the same position.
* **Gradient accumulation** – For very long sequences that would overflow GPU memory, one can split a single example across several micro‑batches, accumulate gradients, and then update $\theta$. This is again identical to standard LM training pipelines.

Consequently, DPO does **not** require storing a full $\log \pi(y\mid x)$ tensor for every example throughout training. The only intermediate that must be retained until the loss is computed are the per‑token logits (or their summed values) for the current mini‑batch, which fit comfortably into GPU memory for sequence lengths typical of RLHF datasets (a few hundred tokens). Memory usage is therefore comparable to ordinary supervised fine‑tuning of a causal LM with a batch size $B$ and maximum length $L$; it scales as $O(B\,L\,d)$ where $d$ is the hidden dimension, exactly the same $O(\cdot)$ term you would see when training on next‑token cross‑entropy.

The paper’s implementation section (Appendix A) confirms this: training was performed with standard AdamW on batches of up to 64 examples, each limited to 256 tokens; no special “log‑prob caching” beyond regular transformer caching was needed. The authors also note that because DPO never samples from $\pi_{\theta}$ during fine‑tuning, there is no extra memory overhead from storing multiple rollouts or value‑function targets – everything fits into a single forward–backward pass.

In short, long generation sequences are handled exactly like in any autoregressive LM fine‑tuning: you compute per‑token log‑probs on the fly, sum them across tokens, and apply the binary cross‑entropy loss. Standard tricks—caching prefixes, padding/masking, truncation, and gradient accumulation—are sufficient to keep memory consumption manageable even for several thousand‑token examples if needed. No additional “tricks” beyond those used in ordinary language‑model fine‑tuning are required by DPO.
