\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

# Motivation and Challenges of Controlling Large Language Models  

## Capabilities of large unsupervised LMs  

In the past few years, language models trained on massive corpora of text—often referred to simply as “large unsupervised language models” (LMs)—have demonstrated capabilities that go far beyond next‑token prediction.  When a model such as PaLM, GPT‑3, LLaMA, or other similarly scaled transformers is exposed to a prompt, it can answer trivia questions, write code, generate poetry, translate between languages, and even perform rudimentary reasoning about novel situations.  These abilities emerge from the sheer volume of data (hundreds of billions of tokens) and the depth of the neural architecture (often dozens of layers with billions of parameters).  Empirically, researchers have observed that such models can solve tasks in a zero‑shot fashion—producing reasonable answers without any task‑specific fine‑tuning—and can also be prompted in a few‑shot manner to adapt quickly to new domains.  

The technical source of these abilities is the self‑supervised objective used during pre‑training: the model maximizes the likelihood of each token given its preceding context.  Formally, for a dataset \(\mathcal{D}=\{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N\) consisting of token sequences, the training objective is  

\[
\max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{|x|}\log p_{\theta}(x_t \mid x_{<t})\Bigr],
\]

where \(p_{\theta}\) is the conditional distribution defined by the transformer parameters \(\theta\).  
Because the objective makes no distinction between “useful” and “harmful” text, the resulting model inherits **all** statistical regularities present in its training corpus: factual knowledge, idiomatic expressions, but also biases, misconceptions, and unsafe content.

## Misalignment risks and the need for controllability  

The breadth of knowledge encoded in a large LM is a double‑edged sword.  On the one hand, it provides a rich substrate from which an assistant can draw accurate information and exhibit sophisticated language skills.  On the other hand, it also encodes many behaviors that are undesirable for downstream deployment.  Consider two illustrative scenarios:

1. **Selective expertise** – A coding assistant should be able to *recognize* common programming mistakes so that it can offer corrective suggestions.  Yet when actually *generating* code it ought to favor high‑quality solutions rather than reproducing the average quality present in the training data.

2. **Misinformation handling** – A conversational model may have learned that roughly half of internet users believe a certain false claim.  While awareness of this belief can be useful for detection, the model must not present the claim as factual in its responses.

If we were to deploy an LM directly after unsupervised pre‑training, we would observe frequent occurrences of toxic language, hallucinations, or overly verbose answers—symptoms of what researchers term **misalignment**.  Aligning a model therefore means “steering” it toward **desired** responses while retaining its broad knowledge base.  The steering must be precise enough that a user can reliably obtain safe and helpful outputs across diverse prompts.

## Overview of preference‑based alignment (RLHF)  

A powerful paradigm for achieving this steering is **preference‑based alignment**, most famously instantiated as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).  The pipeline typically comprises three stages:

1. **Supervised fine‑tuning (SFT)** – Starting from the pre‑trained LM \(\pi_{\text{base}}\), we collect a dataset of high‑quality (human‑written) demonstrations for the target task(s) and fine‑tune the model to maximize their likelihood.  The result \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\) already exhibits improved task performance.

2. **Preference collection & reward modeling** – We prompt \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\) on many inputs \(x\) to generate pairs of completions \((y_1,y_2)\).  Human annotators label which completion they prefer; we denote a labeled triple by \(y_w \succ y_l \mid x\).  Assuming an unknown latent reward function \(r^*(x,y)\) governs these judgments, we fit a parametric reward model \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) by maximizing the Bradley‑Terry likelihood:  

   \[
   \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\bigl[\log\sigma(r_{\phi}(x,y_w)-r_{\phi}(x,y_l))\bigr],
   \]
   where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\).

3. **Policy optimization via RL** – Using the learned reward \(r_{\phi}\), we define a KL‑constrained objective that balances reward maximization against deviation from a reference policy (usually \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\)):  

   \[
   \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\,\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[\,r_{\phi}(x,y)\,] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi^{\text{SFT}}(\cdot|x)\bigr).
   \]
   Because language generation is discrete and non‑differentiable, practitioners typically employ policy‑gradient methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).  

In practice this pipeline has produced conversational agents that are noticeably more helpful and less toxic than their SFT counterparts.

## Limitations of current RLHF (complexity, instability, computational cost)  

Despite its empirical success, RLHF suffers from several practical drawbacks that impede widespread adoption:

* **Multi‑stage engineering** – The pipeline requires training three separate models: an SFT policy, a reward model, and finally a policy updated by RL.  Each stage demands its own data handling logic and hyperparameter tuning.

* **Sampling overhead** – The RL stage repeatedly samples full sequences from the current policy to estimate gradients.  For large transformers this sampling step dominates wall‑clock time and memory consumption.

* **Stability concerns** – Policy‐gradient methods are notorious for high variance gradients.  PPO mitigates this with clipping and value baselines, yet finding stable learning rates and KL targets often involves extensive trial–and–error.  Small misconfigurations can cause catastrophic forgetting of language fluency or mode collapse to repetitive outputs.

* **Reward model drift** – The reward function is learned on a static preference dataset but then applied to distributions shifted by successive policy updates.  If the policy drifts far from the data generating distribution of the reward model, reward estimates become unreliable—a phenomenon known as “reward hacking.”

* **Hyperparameter sensitivity** – The KL coefficient \(\beta\), PPO clipping thresholds, learning rates for both actor and critic—all must be jointly tuned for each new task or model size.  This tuning effort scales poorly with growing model capacities.

Because each component must be trained sequentially and precisely calibrated, RLHF pipelines remain computationally heavy and fragile compared with standard supervised fine‑tuning.

## Research question: Can we align LMs without explicit reward modeling or RL?  

Given these obstacles, a natural question arises: *Is it possible to satisfy human preference data directly at the level of the language model policy, bypassing both an explicit reward network and any reinforcement learning loop?*  

If we could reparameterize the preference likelihood so that it depends only on the policy’s own probability assignments—perhaps through an analytical mapping between rewards and optimal policies—then learning could be reduced to minimizing a single cross‑entropy loss over preference triples.  Such an approach would inherit all advantages of supervised learning (stable gradients via backpropagation, no need for on‑policy sampling) while still optimizing exactly the same KL‑constrained objective used in traditional RLHF.

The paper “Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model” proposes precisely this idea.  By expressing the optimal policy under a KL constraint as  

\[
\pi_r(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
\]

and then solving for \(r(x,y)\) in terms of \(\pi_r\), the authors derive a loss that directly trains \(\pi_{\theta}\) from preference data:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\Bigl[\log\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
-\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\Bigr)\Bigr].
\]

This formulation eliminates an explicit reward network and any RL optimization loop; learning proceeds by standard stochastic gradient descent on a binary cross‑entropy objective that naturally incorporates the KL regularization through the reference probabilities.

Thus, the central research problem addressed by the paper is whether **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** can achieve alignment quality comparable to or exceeding conventional RLHF while being markedly simpler, more stable, and computationally cheaper.

Foundations: Preference Modeling and the Standard RLHF Pipeline  

# Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT) of a Reference Policy  

Before any preference information can be exploited, a large language model (LM) must first acquire a base capability for the downstream task of interest.  In the standard RLHF pipeline this step is called **supervised fine‑tuning** (SFT).  An SFT model, denoted \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\), is obtained by taking a pretrained, unsupervised language model \(\pi^{\text{pre}}\) and further training it with maximum‑likelihood estimation on a collection of high‑quality input–output pairs \(\{(x, y)\}\).  Concretely, the objective is  

\[
\max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}}\bigl[\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\]

where \(\pi_{\theta}\) denotes the parametric policy (the LM) and \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}\) is the supervised dataset.  The result is a **reference policy** \(\pi_{\text{ref}} := \pi^{\text{SFT}}\) that already produces reasonable completions for the tasks at hand (dialogue, summarization, coding assistance, etc.).  This reference policy serves two purposes in later stages: it provides a stable starting point for further optimization and it defines the “status‑quo” distribution that the RL stage is constrained not to deviate from too drastically.  

Because SFT relies only on explicit demonstrations (complete answers written by humans), it is straightforward to implement: one simply runs standard cross‑entropy training on a GPU cluster.  However, collecting enough high‑quality demonstrations for all possible tasks can be expensive, motivating the use of *relative* human judgments—pairwise preferences—as a cheaper source of supervision.

# Collecting Pairwise Human Preferences  

Instead of asking annotators to craft an ideal response for each prompt, we can ask them to compare two candidate completions and indicate which one they prefer.  Formally, for a given prompt \(x\) we sample two continuations \(y_1, y_2\) from the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\).  A human labeler then supplies a binary judgment  

\[
y_w \succ y_l \mid x,
\]

where \(y_w\) is the *winner* (preferred) and \(y_l\) is the *loser* (dispreferred).  Collecting many such triples \((x, y_w, y_l)\) yields an offline dataset  

\[
\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

which captures human notions of quality—safety, helpfulness, factuality, sentiment alignment, etc.—without requiring fully written demonstrations.  Pairwise judgments are typically easier and faster for annotators because they merely need to decide “which sounds better?” rather than construct a perfect answer from scratch.

# Bradley‑Terry (and Plackett‑Luce) Preference Formulation  

To turn the raw preference data into a statistical model we adopt the **Bradley‑Terry (BT)** formulation, a special case of the more general **Plackett‑Luce** ranking model.  The BT model assumes that each answer \(y\) paired with its context \(x\) possesses an unobserved scalar *reward* \(r^{*}(x,y)\).  The probability that a human prefers \(y_1\) over \(y_2\) is then

\[
p^{*}(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)
   = \frac{\exp \bigl(r^{*}(x,y_1)\bigr)}
          {\exp \bigl(r^{*}(x,y_1)\bigr)+\exp \bigl(r^{*}(x,y_2)\bigr)}.
\tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) captures the intuition that larger rewards make a completion more likely to be chosen; the logistic denominator ensures probabilities sum to one across any pair.

The Plackett‑Luce model extends this idea to rankings of three or more items by multiplying together BT‐type factors for each successive pair in the ranking.  In practice most RLHF datasets contain only binary comparisons, so BT suffices.

# Maximum‑Likelihood Reward Model Training  

Given a parametric reward function \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\)—often implemented as an additional linear head on top of the frozen SFT transformer—we fit its parameters by maximizing the likelihood of the observed preferences under Eq. (1).  The **negative log‑likelihood** loss is

\[
\mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D})
   = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}
      \Bigl[\,\log\sigma\!\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_w)-r_{\phi}(x,y_l)\bigr)\Bigr],
\tag{2}
\]

where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) denotes the logistic sigmoid.  Intuitively, Eq. (2) encourages the reward difference \(r_{\phi}(x,y_w)-r_{\phi}(x,y_l)\) to be positive whenever humans prefer \(y_w\).  In many implementations the reward head is initialized from the SFT model’s final hidden state so that it inherits linguistic knowledge before learning to discriminate preferences.

A practical detail often applied in RLHF pipelines is **reward centering**: after each optimization step we subtract the mean reward over all examples so that  

\[
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}[r_{\phi}(x,y)] = 0.
\]

Centering reduces variance of subsequent RL updates without affecting Eq. (2), because adding a constant to every reward leaves preference probabilities unchanged.

# KL‑Constrained Reward Maximization Objective  

Once we have an estimate \(r_{\phi}\) of human preferences, we wish to adjust the language model so that it generates responses with high expected reward.  At the same time we want to avoid moving too far from the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  This trade‑off is formalized as a **KL‑constrained objective**:

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}
    \;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p(x),\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}
        \bigl[\, r_{\phi}(x,y) \,\bigr]
    - \beta\, 
      D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

The first term encourages high reward; the second penalizes divergence from the reference distribution with strength controlled by hyperparameter \(\beta>0\).  The KL penalty serves two purposes:

1. **Safety of reward extrapolation** – The learned reward model is only reliable near the distribution on which it was trained (the SFT data).  
2. **Preservation of diversity** – Without any penalty the policy could collapse to always emit a single “high‑reward” token sequence.

Because language generation proceeds token by token, Eq. (3) actually involves a sum of per‑token KL divergences; nevertheless it can be written compactly as above.

# Reinforcement Learning Methods Used in RLHF (REINFORCE, PPO)  

The objective in Eq. (3) is not differentiable with respect to the LM parameters when sampling discrete tokens.  Consequently traditional RL techniques are employed.

* **REINFORCE** – The simplest Monte Carlo policy gradient method estimates  

  \[
  \nabla_{\theta} J(\theta)
   = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_{\theta}}
       \bigl[\, (r_{\phi}(x,y)-b(x))\,
              \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y|x)
       \bigr],
  \]

  where \(b(x)\) is a baseline (often an estimate of expected reward) used to reduce variance.  REINFORCE directly optimizes Eq. (3) but suffers from high variance and unstable learning when applied to large language models.

* **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** – PPO improves stability by clipping probability ratios and adding an explicit KL penalty term during optimization.  In practice RLHF implementations rewrite Eq. (3) into an *advantage* form

  \[
  A(x,y)= r_{\phi}(x,y)-
          \beta\,(\log \pi_{\theta}(y|x)-\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)),
  \]

  and then maximize an objective such as  

  \[
  L^{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=
     \mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[\,
        \min
        \Bigl(
            A(x,y)\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y|x)},
            {\rm clip}\bigl(A(x,y),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\bigr)
        \Bigr)
     \Bigr],
  \]

  where \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) denotes the policy before the current update and
  \(\epsilon\simeq0.2.\)
  
Both REINFORCE and PPO require **sampling** from the current policy at every training step in order to obtain trajectories \(y\).   This sampling loop dramatically increases computational cost compared with ordinary supervised learning because each update needs fresh forward passes through a large decoder network for many candidate completions.

# Why the Partition Function Makes Classic RLHF Expensive  

In Eq. (3), under exact solution theory one can solve for the optimal policy analytically:

\[
\pi_{r}^{*}(y|x)=
   \frac{
      \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
      e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta}
   }{
      Z_{r}(x)
   }, 
   \qquad 
   Z_{r}(x)=
   \sum_{y'} 
       \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)
       e^{\,r(x,y')/\beta}.
   \tag{4}
\]

The factor \(Z_{r}(x)\)—the **partition function**—normalizes probabilities so that they sum to one.  
Computing \(Z_{r}(x)\) exactly requires summing over all possible continuations \(y'\), which is infeasible because language generation spaces are astronomically large (exponential in sequence length). 

Standard RLHF circumvents this obstacle by never explicitly evaluating \(Z_{r}(x)\); instead it treats Eq. (3) as an expectation over samples and uses stochastic gradient estimators like REINFORCE or PPO.  
While this bypasses direct computation of \(Z_{r}\), it introduces several practical burdens:

* **Sampling overhead** – Each training iteration needs many forward passes to generate candidate completions for each prompt.
* **High variance** – Monte‑Carlo estimates of gradients depend heavily on noisy sampled rewards.
* **Hyperparameter sensitivity** – PPO introduces clipping thresholds, learning rates for both policy and value networks, and KL penalty coefficients that must be carefully tuned.
* **Memory pressure** – Storing multiple sampled trajectories per prompt consumes GPU memory proportional to batch size times rollout length.

These costs motivate research into alternative formulations that either approximate or eliminate the partition function entirely while still respecting human preferences—a key motivation behind Direct Preference Optimization introduced later in this text.

--- 

In summary, this chapter has laid out all mathematical components underlying conventional RLHF: obtaining a supervised reference policy, gathering binary human comparisons, modeling those comparisons via Bradley‑Terry/Plackett‑Luce, learning an implicit reward by maximum likelihood, formulating a KL‑constrained maximization problem, and finally solving it with reinforcement‐learning algorithms such as REINFORCE or PPO despite the prohibitive expense introduced by the partition function. Understanding each piece prepares us for later sections where these ingredients are rearranged into a simpler yet theoretically equivalent algorithm—Direct Preference Optimization.

**Deriving Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**  

In this chapter we unravel the central theoretical insight that makes Direct Preference Optimization possible: by re‑parameterizing the reward function in terms of the language‑model policy, the optimal solution to the KL‑constrained reward‑maximization problem can be written in closed form.  This transformation eliminates the need for an explicit reward model and for any reinforcement‑learning loop, reducing the entire RLHF pipeline to a single binary‑cross‑entropy loss over log‑probability ratios.  We proceed step by step, beginning with the optimal policy under a KL constraint, then performing an algebraic change of variables that replaces rewards with policies, showing how the partition function cancels when we work with Bradley–Terry preference differences, and finally arriving at the DPO objective.  We conclude by interpreting the resulting gradient and explaining why an importance‑weighting term is essential for stable learning.

---

### Optimal policy for KL‑constrained reward maximization  

The standard RLHF objective (e.g. in PPO‑based RLHF) asks for a policy \(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\) that maximizes expected reward while staying close to a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) – typically the supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model.  Formally,

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}
\Bigl[\,r(x,y)\Bigr]
\;-\;
\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr),
\tag{1}
\]

where \(r(x,y)\) is a scalar reward (the output of a learned reward model) and \(\beta>0\) controls how strongly we penalize divergence from the reference.  

Because the KL term is a convex regularizer and the reward term is linear in the policy, Eq. (1) is a classic constrained‑optimization problem whose solution can be derived analytically using Lagrange multipliers.  The optimal policy \(\pi_{r}\) for a given reward function \(r\) satisfies

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)
=
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y')\Bigr).
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (2) shows that the optimal policy is obtained by **tilting** the reference distribution toward actions with higher reward, then renormalizing with the partition function \(Z(x)\).  This expression appears frequently in “control‑as‑inference’’ formulations of RL; its importance here is that it makes explicit how rewards translate into a probability distribution over completions.

---

### Algebraic change of variables: from reward to policy  

While Eq. (2) provides an explicit mapping from rewards to policies, directly using it for training is impractical because computing \(Z(x)\) requires summing over all possible completions – an astronomically large set for language generation.  The key observation behind DPO is that we can **invert** Eq. (2), expressing the reward in terms of any candidate policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) and the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  

Taking logarithms of both sides of Eq. (2),

\[
\log \pi_{r}(y\mid x)
=
\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)
+
\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)
-
\log Z(x).
\]

Rearranging gives

\[
r(x,y)
=
\beta\, \Bigl[
\, \log \frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
+ \log Z(x)
\, \Bigr].
\tag{3}
\]

Thus any reward that would induce \(\pi_{r}\) must be of the form (3).  The *only* unknown term remaining is \(\log Z(x)\), which depends on \(x\) but not on a specific completion \(y\).  Crucially, **the difference of two rewards eliminates this term**, because it cancels out:

\[
r(x,y_{1}) - r(x,y_{2})
=
\beta\, \bigl[ 
   \log \tfrac{\pi_{r}(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}
 - \log \tfrac{\pi_{r}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}
\bigr].
\tag{4}
\]

Equation (4) tells us that, for any pair of completions, we can compute their *reward difference* solely from log‑probability ratios; no partition function appears.

---

### Eliminating the partition function via Bradley–Terry differences  

Human preference data are typically modeled with the **Bradley–Terry (BT)** pairwise comparison likelihood:

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
=
\sigma \bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1}) - r^{*}(x,y_{2}) \bigr),
\qquad
\sigma(t)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}}.
\tag{5}
\]

Because only differences of rewards matter, we can substitute Eq. (4) for \(r^{*}(x,\cdot)\).  Let \(\pi^{*}\) denote the *optimal* policy that would arise if we had access to the true reward \(r^{*}\).  Plugging (4) into (5),

```math
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
=
\sigma \!\Bigl(
   \beta \,\log
   \frac{\displaystyle
        \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}
       }{
        \displaystyle
        \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}
       }
   \Bigr).
```

Simplifying,

```math
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
=
\sigma \!\Bigl(
   \beta\, 
   \log
   \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}\mid x)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}
        {\pi^{*}(y_{2}\mid x)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}
   \Bigr).
```

If we now replace the *unknown* optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\) with a *learnable* parametric policy \(\pi_{\theta}\), we obtain a **likelihood model directly over policies**:

```math
p_{\theta}(y_{w}\succ y_{l} \mid x)
=
\sigma
   \Bigl(
      \beta\, 
      \log 
      \frac{\displaystyle
            \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
           }{
            \displaystyle
            \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
           }
   \Bigr).
```

Here \(y_w\) (winner) and \(y_l\) (loser) denote the human‑preferred and dispreferred completions respectively.  Because this expression contains **no partition function**, it can be evaluated for any observed pair without any approximation.

---

### Final DPO loss as a binary cross‑entropy over log‑probability ratios  

Given an offline dataset of preference triples  

\[
\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{
   (x^{(i)},\, y_w^{(i)},\, y_l^{(i)})
\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

the maximum‑likelihood objective for the policy becomes a binary cross‑entropy loss:

```math
%
L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta;\,\pi_{\text{ref}})
=
-
\, {\mathbb E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal D}
   [
      \log
      \sigma
      (
         \beta\, 
         \log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
         -
         \beta\, 
         \log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
      )
   ] .
%
```

We denote this loss as \(L_{\mathrm{DPO}}\).  It has precisely the same functional form as the classic reward‑model loss (Eq. 5), but **the parameters are now those of the language model itself** rather than those of a separate scalar predictor.  Training proceeds by standard stochastic gradient descent on \(L_{\mathrm{DPO}}\); no reinforcement learning algorithm, no value network, and no Monte‑Carlo estimation of a baseline are required.

---

### Interpretation of the DPO gradient and importance weighting  

To see what drives learning, compute the gradient of \(L_{\mathrm{DPO}}\) with respect to model parameters:

```math
%
\nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{DPO}}
=
-\beta
\, {\mathbb E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim {\cal D}}
   [
      w_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)
      \
      (\nabla_\theta\rule[0pt]{0pt}{10pt}
          \log\rule[-0pt]{0pt}{10pt}
              {\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}
       -
       \nabla_\theta\rule[0pt]{0pt}{10pt}
          log\rule[-0pt]{0pt}{10pt}
              {\pi_\theta(y_l|x)})
   ],
%
```

where 

```math
w_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)
=
\sigma
   (\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\,\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)),
%
```

and we define an **implicit reward** induced by the current policy,

```math
%
    \hat r_\theta(x,y)
    = 
    \beta\, 
    log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
%
```

The factor \(w_\theta =\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w)) = 1-\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_w)-\\hat r_\theta(y_l))\\in (0,1)\) plays a crucial role:

* If the current implicit reward already ranks \(y_w> y_l\), then  
  \(w_\theta\simeq 0.\)  The gradient contribution is tiny because there is little learning signal – the model already respects this preference.

* If the model mistakenly places higher probability on the loser (i.e., predicts \(r(y_l)> r(y_w)\)), then  
  \(w_\theta\simeq 1.\)  The gradient magnitude is maximal, pushing probability mass from \(y_l$ toward $\(y_w).\\)

Thus **the DPO gradient automatically weights each example by how badly it violates human preference**, scaled by β which governs how strongly we enforce closeness to π_ref.  This importance weighting prevents two pathological behaviours observed with naïve “increase winner / decrease loser’’ updates:

1. **Degeneration** – without weighting, even correctly ranked pairs would continuously drive down probabilities of losers, eventually collapsing diversity.
2. **Instability** – some pairs may have extremely high loss if β is too large; scaling by σ bounds gradients and yields smoother optimisation.

In practice this weighting emerges naturally from maximizing likelihood under the BT model; no ad‑hoc coefficient needs to be tuned.

---

### Summary  

By recognising that KL‑constrained RL induces a closed‑form relationship between rewards and policies, we can replace an explicit reward model and a reinforcement­learning loop with a simple change of variables.  The partition function disappears when we work with reward *differences*, which are exactly what Bradley–Terry preferences depend upon.  Substituting these differences into the BT likelihood yields a tractable binary cross‑entropy loss defined directly on log‑probability ratios between a learnable policy and a fixed reference policy.  Optimizing this loss gives us **Direct Preference Optimization**, whose gradient automatically emphasizes incorrectly ordered pairs through an importance weight derived from an implicit reward function.

The result is an algorithm that retains every desideratum of classic RLHF—maximizing human‐aligned reward while staying close to an SFT baseline—yet it does so with only one straightforward supervised‐learning pass, dramatically simplifying implementation and improving stability.

**Practical Implementation of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**  

In this chapter we describe how the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) algorithm, introduced in the preceding sections, can be integrated into a real‑world language‑model training pipeline.  We follow the logical order of an actual engineering workflow: beginning with the creation of a static preference dataset, moving on to the selection and initialization of a reference policy, then detailing the core DPO loop (sampling, loss computation, and parameter updates), and finally addressing practical concerns such as the absence of a supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model, the choice of the KL‑constraint coefficient β, and low‑level implementation tricks that improve stability and speed.  Throughout we keep the exposition faithful to the formulation presented in the original paper; no external concepts are introduced.

---

### Constructing the Offline Preference Dataset \( \mathcal{D} \)

The DPO method works entirely from an offline collection of human (or high‑quality model) preference triples  
\[
\mathcal{D}= \bigl\{\, (x^{(i)},\, y^{(i)}_{\!w},\, y^{(i)}_{\!l}) \bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
where each \(x^{(i)}\) is a prompt (for example a user query or a document prefix), \(y^{(i)}_{\!w}\) is the completion judged to be better, and \(y^{(i)}_{\!l}\) is its less‑preferred counterpart.  The dataset must be constructed before any model parameters are updated; consequently it is customary to generate candidate completions with a fixed policy and then ask annotators to choose the preferred one.

The generation step proceeds as follows.  A *reference* language model—typically the SFT model that will later serve as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)—is queried with each prompt \(x\).  Two independent completions, denoted \(y_{1}\) and \(y_{2}\), are sampled from the reference distribution \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\).  The sampling can be performed with any temperature or nucleus‑sampling configuration; what matters is that both candidates come from the same underlying policy so that the resulting preference data reflect realistic trade‑offs between quality and likelihood.

Once the pair \((y_{1},y_{2})\) is available, annotators are presented with the prompt and both responses in random order.  Their binary decision—“\(y_{w} \succ y_{l}\)”—is recorded.  In practice large‑scale preference collection pipelines use crowd‑sourcing platforms or expert raters; quality control is achieved by including gold standard checks or by aggregating multiple judgments per pair.  After filtering out ambiguous or low‑agreement items, the remaining high‑confidence triples constitute \(\mathcal{D}\).  Because DPO treats preferences as *observations* from a Bradley–Terry model, no absolute scores are required; a binary label is sufficient.

It is important to note that \(\mathcal{D}\) is *static*: during DPO training we never resample new completions.  This contrasts sharply with conventional RLHF pipelines where each policy update requires fresh roll‑outs from an ever‑changing policy.  The static nature of \(\mathcal{D}\) eliminates expensive on‑policy sampling loops and simplifies data management considerably.

---

### Choosing and Initializing the Reference Policy \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \)

The reference policy appears explicitly in every DPO loss term through a log‑ratio of probabilities:
\[
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
Consequently its choice determines both the *starting point* of optimization and the magnitude of the KL regularization enforced by β.

In an ideal scenario a supervised fine‑tuned model \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\) already exists; it has been trained on high‑quality demonstrations for the downstream task (e.g., instruction following or summarization).  When such a model is available we simply set
\[
\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi^{\text{SFT}}.
\]
Because DPO updates begin from exactly this reference distribution, only minimal divergence is needed to satisfy preferences, ensuring that policies remain close to a strong baseline.

When an SFT checkpoint is not present—a situation that arises when working with novel domains or proprietary corpora—the paper proposes an alternative initialization that relies only on preferred completions.  Specifically we solve
\[
\pi_{\text{ref}} = \argmax_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w})\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\,\log \pi(y_{w}\mid x)\,\bigr],
\]
which is ordinary maximum likelihood training on the set of *winning* responses.  Practically this means creating a small supervised fine‑tuning dataset that contains only pairs \((x,y_{w})\); we train a language model on this data until convergence, treating it as an approximate reference.  This “preferred‑only” initialization aligns the reference distribution with the empirical support of the preference data, reducing distribution shift between \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and the true underlying optimal policy.

Regardless of how \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is obtained, it must be *frozen* during DPO training: its parameters are never updated.  The frozen nature guarantees that the log‑ratio terms remain well defined throughout optimization and that β truly controls how far the learned policy may drift from this fixed anchor.

---

### Algorithmic Steps of DPO  

With \(\mathcal{D}\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) prepared, DPO proceeds through a straightforward loop that resembles standard supervised learning.  For completeness we outline each component in narrative form.

1. **Batch Sampling**  
   We iterate over minibatches drawn uniformly from \(\mathcal{D}\).  Each minibatch supplies tuples \((x, y_{w}, y_{l})\).  Because all components have already been generated offline, no additional forward passes through any model are required for data acquisition.

2. **Probability Evaluation**  
   For every example in the batch we forward‑pass both completions through **the current policy** \(\pi_{\theta}\).  Since language models produce logits for each token conditioned on previously generated tokens, we obtain token‑wise log probabilities
   \[
   \log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{w}|}\log p_{\theta}(y_{w,t}\mid x,y_{w,<t}),
   \]
   and similarly for \(y_{l}\).  In parallel we compute log probabilities under the frozen reference:
   \[
   \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x),\qquad
   \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x).
   \]

3. **Log‑Ratio Construction**  
   The core quantity entering DPO’s loss is
   \[
   s(x,y)=\beta\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
          =\beta\,\bigl[\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr].
   \]
   Notice that this expression can be computed robustly by first forming differences of log probabilities before scaling by β; doing so avoids numerical overflow when probabilities themselves are extremely small.

4. **Loss Computation**  
   The DPO objective for one example follows directly from Equation (9) in the paper:
   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = 
      -\,\log\sigma
        \Bigl(
          s(x,y_{w}) - s(x,y_{l})
        \Bigr),
   \]
   where σ denotes the logistic sigmoid.  Intuitively this encourages an increase in \(s(x,y_{w}) - s(x,y_{l})\); equivalently it raises probability mass on preferred outputs while lowering it on dispreferred ones.

5. **Gradient Backpropagation**  
   The loss is differentiable with respect to all parameters of θ because both terms \(s(\cdot)\) depend linearly on log πθ via backpropagation through the transformer network.  After averaging (or summing) over all examples in the minibatch we obtain an unbiased stochastic estimate of 
   ∇₍θ₎ \(L_{\text{DPO}}\), which is passed to an optimizer such as AdamW.

6. **Parameter Update**  
   A standard optimizer step updates θ.  No auxiliary value networks, reward baselines, or KL penalty terms are introduced—the KL constraint is already baked into β through its appearance inside \(s(x,y)\).  

7. **Training Schedule**  
   The loop repeats for many epochs over 𝔇 or until convergence criteria (e.g., validation win rate plateaus). Because each iteration touches only stored data and does not require sampling fresh generations from πθ, wall‑clock time per epoch resembles ordinary supervised fine–tuning.

Overall this pipeline reduces “preference learning” to a single binary cross–entropy loss applied directly to a language model’s own logits.

---

### Handling Missing SFT Models: Initializing π₍ref₎ via Preferred Completions  

When an SFT checkpoint does not exist we must synthesize a reference distribution solely from information present in 𝔇.  The method described earlier—maximizing likelihood on preferred completions—has two practical ramifications.

First, it yields a *biased* but useful approximation: since every preferred response has already been judged superior to some alternative under identical prompts, training on these responses alone drives π₍ref₎ toward regions of token space that human annotators consider high quality. Second, because only positive examples appear during this pretraining stage there is no explicit regularization pushing π₍ref₎ toward diversity; therefore it can be advantageous to apply standard language‑model regularizers (weight decay, dropout) to avoid overfitting to a narrow set of sequences.

In implementation one typically allocates a modest number of epochs (e.g., three to five passes over all preferred pairs) using a learning rate schedule similar to that employed for larger SFT runs. Once π₍ref₎ has stabilized its perplexity on held‑out preferred completions reaches a reasonable level (often comparable to public SFT checkpoints for similar domains), after which DPO training commences using this frozen reference.

---

### The Role of β and Its Influence on KL‑Constraint Strength  

The scalar β appears in two places: inside each log‑ratio and as an overall scaling factor for those ratios before they enter σ. From Equation (8),
\[
p^{*}(y_{1} {\succ} y_{2} | x)
    = 
    \frac{
        1}
    {1+\exp
        \Bigl(
            β\, 
            [
                \log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_2|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2|x)}
                -
                \log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_1|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1|x)}
            ]
        )},
}
\]
we see that β modulates how strongly differences in log‐ratio affect predicted human preference probabilities. Small values of β shrink these differences, making preferences easier for any policy to satisfy but also permitting larger deviations from π₍ref₎ because their contribution to loss becomes muted. Conversely large β amplify discrepancies; improving alignment with preferences now requires more substantial changes in log probabilities relative to π₍ref₎, which effectively enforces a tighter KL constraint.

A convenient interpretation derives from rearranging Equation (5):
\[
r(x,y)=β\,\log\tfrac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y|x)}+β\,\log Z(x).
\]
Since KL divergence between πθ and π₍ref₎ equals 
\(𝔼_{π_\theta}[\log\tfrac{\π_\theta}{π_\text{ref}}]\),
the multiplicative factor β acts as exactly the coefficient used in classic KL‐constrained RLHF objectives (see Equation (3)). Therefore by selecting β one directly specifies how conservative or aggressive DPO should be relative to its reference anchor.

Practically one tunes β by sweeping over logarithmically spaced values (e.g., {0.05,0.1,1,5}) while monitoring two metrics on validation data: (i) average reward under an external evaluator (or sentiment classifier if available) and (ii) average sequence‐level KL divergence against π₍ref₎. The Pareto frontier obtained guides choosing β that best balances performance against drift.

### Implementation Tricks for Stable and Efficient Training  

Although DPO’s mathematical formulation is compact, careful engineering decisions make large‐scale runs practical.

- **Batching at Token Level**  
  Since each example contains two completions possibly of different lengths, we pad them to a common maximum within each batch and mask out padded positions when computing log probabilities. This enables parallel forward passes through modern transformer libraries without wasted computation.

- **Stable Log‑Ratio Evaluation**  
  Directly computing ratios of tiny probabilities can lead to underflow. By retaining everything in log space we avoid this issue entirely: compute 
  \(a = \logπ_\theta(y_w|x)-\logπ_\text{ref}(y_w|x)\)   
  and   
  \(b = \logπ_\theta(y_l|x)-\logπ_\text{ref}(y_l|x)\),   
  then form \(β(a-b)\).  This subtraction cancels large common components when πθ≈π₍ref₎, yielding numerically well behaved scalars even for long sequences.

- **Gradient Clipping**  
  Because DPO gradients may be amplified when many examples have incorrectly ordered implicit rewards (the σ term approaches one), clipping at norm 1–2 prevents occasional spikes from destabilizing training.

- **AdamW Hyperparameters**  
  Empirically modest weight decay (≈0.01) together with a learning rate around 5e−5 works well across models up to six billion parameters. A linear warmup for several thousand steps followed by cosine decay mirrors schedules used for SFT fine–tuning.

- mixed precision training lowers memory pressure while preserving enough numerical precision for log‐probability differences; using bfloat16 on hardware that supports it typically yields speedups without harming stability.

- **Checkpointing Reference Logits**  
  Since π₍ref₎ never changes we can precompute its token logits for every completion present in 𝔇 once at dataset construction time and store them alongside each tuple. During training we then only need to compute logits for πθ; reading stored reference logits reduces GPU workload dramatically when datasets contain millions of pairs.

- **Evaluation Pipeline**  
  To monitor progress without contaminating training data we maintain a held–out subset of preference triples; periodically compute win rates using Equation (9)’s sigmoid output averaged over this set. Because no sampling occurs during evaluation either, statistics are low variance and comparable across different β settings.

These heuristics collectively ensure that DPO scales from small research prototypes up to multi‑billion parameter models while preserving its hallmark simplicity—training reduces to ordinary stochastic gradient descent on a binary cross entropy loss with no auxiliary critics or reward classifiers required.

---

### Summary  

Implementing Direct Preference Optimization consists of three conceptual stages:

1. **Collecting static human preference triples** using any fixed generative policy,
2. **Establishing a frozen reference distribution**, preferably an existing SFT checkpoint but otherwise derived via maximum likelihood on preferred responses,
3. **Executing a straightforward supervised learning loop** where each minibatch contributes a cross‑entropy term built from log ratio differences scaled by β.

The algorithm’s elegance stems from recasting RLHF’s KL‑constrained reward maximization into exact closed form via Equation (5), thereby eliminating reinforcement learning altogether. By paying attention to practical details—stable log computations, batching strategies, proper choice of β, and optionally precomputing reference logits—engineers can harness DPO in production pipelines with computational cost comparable to ordinary fine tuning while achieving alignment performance at least as good as traditional PPO–based RLHF methods described earlier in this text.
