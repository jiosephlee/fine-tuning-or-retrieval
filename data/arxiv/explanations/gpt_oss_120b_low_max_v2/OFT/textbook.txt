# Introduction to Text‑to‑Image Diffusion and the Need for Controlled Finetuning  

## Overview of recent text‑to‑image diffusion models  

Over the past few years, diffusion‑based generative models have become the dominant paradigm for text‑to‑image synthesis.  Prominent examples such as **Stable Diffusion**, **DALL‑E 2**, **Imagen**, and the earlier **GLIDE** model all share a common structure: a latent diffusion process that iteratively denoises a random variable while being guided by a joint vision–language embedding.  In Stable Diffusion, for instance, an autoencoder first maps an image into a compact latent space learned by a VQ‑GAN; a UNet conditioned on CLIP text embeddings then performs the denoising steps.  DALL‑E 2 and Imagen follow a similar latent‑space pipeline, whereas GLIDE operates directly in pixel space and learns its own text encoder jointly with the diffusion prior.  These models are trained on massive paired image–text datasets, which endows them with the ability to generate high‑fidelity, photorealistic pictures from natural language prompts.

The sheer scale of the pretraining data and the depth of the diffusion architecture give rise to two important consequences.  First, the models capture a very rich semantic manifold: different regions of weight space correspond to distinct visual concepts (e.g., “a tabby cat on a sofa”, “a sunrise over mountains”).  Second, because the diffusion process is stochastic and conditioned only on a textual description, the model’s output is inherently underdetermined; many plausible images satisfy the same prompt.  This flexibility is a strength when diversity is desired, but it also means that **plain textual prompting often fails to provide fine‑grained control** over specific aspects of the generated image such as the exact appearance of a particular object or the precise geometry dictated by an external signal (edges, segmentation maps, landmarks).

## Limitations of plain text prompting for fine‑grained control  

A natural way to influence generation is to enrich the textual prompt with adjectives (“a red vintage car”) or stylistic cues (“in the style of Van Gogh”).  While such augmentations can steer global attributes like color palette or artistic style, they are insufficient for two families of downstream tasks that require **precise alignment** between an external source of information and the generated picture:

1. **Subject specificity**: When a user wishes to insert a particular person, pet, or artwork into new scenes—perhaps only a handful of reference photos are available—the model must retain the identity of that subject while adapting its pose, background, or lighting.  Simple prompting cannot guarantee that the subject’s facial features or texture will be reproduced faithfully.

2. **Conditional signals**: Many applications provide an auxiliary modality (e.g., canny edges, semantic segmentation masks, facial landmarks) that encodes geometric or structural constraints.  The model must obey these constraints pixel‑wise while still producing realistic textures guided by text.  Text alone does not contain enough information to satisfy such pixel‑level requirements.

Consequently, researchers have framed these problems as **controlled finetuning**: given a pretrained diffusion model \( \mathbf{M}^{0} \) (the superscript “0’’ denotes the original weights), we adapt it to a new task while preserving as much as possible of its original generative capability.

## Two key downstream tasks  

### Subject‑driven generation  

The goal here is to create images of a *specific* subject in novel contexts.  Formally, let \( \{ \mathbf{x}_{i} \}_{i=1}^{k} \) be a small set (often \(k\le 5\)) of photographs of a target object or person.  The finetuning procedure should produce a model \( \mathbf{M} \) such that for any textual prompt \( \tau \) describing an arbitrary scene (e.g., “a portrait of \<subject\> standing in front of a waterfall”), sampling from \( \mathbf{M} \) yields images where the visual appearance of \<subject\> matches that observed in \( \{ \mathbf{x}_{i} \} \).  The seminal DreamBooth method tackles this problem by adding a learnable token to the text encoder and jointly optimizing reconstruction and class‑preservation losses.

### Controllable generation  

In this scenario the model receives *both* a textual description \( \tau \) and an additional control map \( c \) (edge map, segmentation map, landmark set).  The desired conditional distribution can be expressed as  
\[
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\tau,c)=\text{DiffusionModel}_{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{y}\mid\tau,c),
\]  
where \( \mathbf{y} \) denotes the final image latent.  The diffusion network must therefore align its denoising trajectory with features extracted from \( c\) while still respecting semantics from \( \tau\).  Existing approaches such as ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter augment the pretrained UNet with additional adapter layers trained on paired (control map, image) data.

Both tasks share a common difficulty: any update that overly perturbs \( \mathbf{M}^{0} \) may degrade its ability to generate high‑quality images for unrelated prompts; conversely, updates that are too conservative may fail to inject the needed subject or control information.

## Desired properties of finetuning  

From an engineering standpoint two desiderata emerge:

1. **Training efficiency** – The finetuning should involve only a modest number of trainable parameters relative to the full model (which may contain hundreds of millions of weights).  Moreover, convergence should require few epochs or iterations so that adaptation can be performed on modest hardware or with limited data.

2. **Preservation of pretrained generative ability** – After adaptation, the model must still be capable of producing diverse, photorealistic images for *any* prompt not related to the downstream task.  In other words, finetuning should not collapse the large semantic manifold learned during pretraining.

Any practical finetuning method therefore needs to strike a balance between *flexibility* (the capacity to encode new concepts or obey new control signals) and *regularity* (the constraint that prevents catastrophic forgetting).

## Existing finetuning strategies and their shortcomings  

### Small‑learning‑rate updates  

The most straightforward approach leaves all parameters trainable but reduces the optimizer’s learning rate dramatically (as done in early DreamBooth implementations).  This encourages each weight matrix \( \mathbf{W} \) to stay close—in Euclidean sense—to its pretrained counterpart \( \mathbf{W}^{0}\):
\[
\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{F}^{2}\;\text{is small}.
\]  
While this heuristic sometimes works, it provides no guarantee that *semantic* relationships among neurons are retained.  Even tiny Euclidean drifts can alter pairwise angular relationships crucial for representing high‑level concepts.

### Low‑rank adaptation (LoRA)  

LoRA proposes an additive low‑rank decomposition
\[
\Delta\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top},\qquad
\operatorname{rank}(\Delta\mathbf{W}) = r',
\]  
so that during inference we compute
\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{W}^{0}+ \Delta\mathbf{W}.
\]  
The low rank \( r'\) drastically reduces trainable parameters and implicitly limits how far \( \mathbf{W}\) can wander from \( \mathbf{W}^{0}\).  Nevertheless LoRA still updates weights additively; it does not preserve any structural invariant such as pairwise neuron similarity.  Empirically LoRA sometimes fails to keep subject identity when only few reference images are available.

### Adapter layers  

Methods like ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter prepend additional small neural blocks whose outputs are summed with those of existing layers.  The base weights remain frozen while adapters learn task‑specific transformations from control signals.  Although this isolates task knowledge from pretrained parameters, it introduces extra inference cost (the adapters must be evaluated at test time) and does not directly address how much *semantic* information from the base model is altered during training—especially when adapters are jointly trained with large learning rates.

### Lack of a principled preservation measure  

All aforementioned strategies share an implicit assumption: keeping Euclidean distance between old and new weights small suffices for preserving generation quality.  Yet research on hyperspherical representations has shown that **angular similarity**, rather than raw magnitude, encodes semantic relationships among neurons more faithfully (\cite{liu2017deep}, \cite{chen2020angular}).  Consequently there has been no formal metric in prior work that explicitly quantifies how well finetuning maintains these angular relationships.

---

In summary, modern text‑to‑image diffusion models provide powerful unconditional generation capabilities but are ill‑suited for fine‑grained downstream tasks without additional guidance.  Existing finetuning techniques either sacrifice training efficiency or neglect important structural properties of neural representations.  This motivates a new paradigm—**Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**—which explicitly preserves pairwise neuron angles via orthogonal transformations while remaining parameter efficient and stable during training.  The remainder of this textbook will develop OFT in detail, prove its invariance properties, and demonstrate its practical benefits on both subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks.

# Geometric Perspective on Neural Representations: Hyperspherical Energy  

In modern deep learning, especially when dealing with large generative models such as text‑to‑image diffusion networks, it is not enough to consider a weight matrix merely as a collection of numbers.  The *geometry* of the weight vectors—how they are oriented with respect to each other—plays a decisive role in determining what kinds of semantic information the network can represent and how that information is altered during finetuning.  This chapter introduces **hyperspherical energy (HE)**, a quantitative measure of the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, and explains why HE is a natural and powerful metric for preserving the generative capabilities of a pretrained model while adapting it to new tasks.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Definition of Hyperspherical Energy and Pairwise Cosine Similarity  

Consider a fully‑connected layer (or any linear mapping) with weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf{W}= \{\mathbf{w}_1,\mathbf{w}_2,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]

where each column \(\mathbf{w}_i\) is the parameter vector of the *i‑th neuron* (or feature map).  We first normalise every neuron onto the unit hypersphere:

\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|_2}, \qquad i=1,\dots,n .
\]

The cosine similarity between two normalised neurons \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\) is simply their inner product

\[
\operatorname{cos}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j)=\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{\!\top}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j,
\]

which equals the cosine of the angle \(\theta_{ij}\) between them.  Since the neurons lie on the unit sphere, the Euclidean distance between two normalised vectors satisfies  

\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|_2^2 = 2(1-\operatorname{cos}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j)).
\]

**Hyperspherical energy** aggregates these pairwise distances over all distinct neuron pairs:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) 
:= \sum_{i\neq j} \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\bigr\|_2^{-1}
   = \sum_{i\neq j} \frac{1}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|_2 }.
}
\tag{1}
\]

Because the denominator is small when two neurons point in nearly the same direction (small angular separation), a *low* value of HE corresponds to a set of neurons that are **well‑spread** over the hypersphere, whereas a *high* value indicates that many neurons cluster together.  In practice, HE serves as a proxy for *pairwise angular similarity* across an entire layer.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Intuition: Angles Encode Semantic Information  

### SphereNet and Normalised Representations  

Empirical work on **SphereNet** demonstrated that training neural networks with all hidden units *normalised* to lie on a unit hypersphere does not harm expressive capacity; on the contrary, it often improves generalisation.  The key observation is that once magnitudes are removed, the *direction* of a weight vector becomes the sole carrier of information.  In other words, for many visual tasks the network’s decision boundaries depend more strongly on *relative angles* among features than on their absolute scales.

### Decoupling Magnitude and Direction  

Subsequent studies (e.g. “Decoupled Networks” and “Angular Margin” works) showed that separating magnitude from direction enables more discriminative representations.  When two images share high cosine similarity in their feature embeddings, they are usually semantically related, even if their raw Euclidean distances differ substantially due to varying illumination or contrast.  This phenomenon suggests that **angular information captures semantics**, while magnitudes encode nuisance factors.

### Toy Auto‑Encoder Experiment  

To make this intuition concrete, imagine training a simple convolutional auto‑encoder on a set of flower pictures.  The encoder consists of convolution kernels \(\mathbf{w}\); during training we use ordinary inner products \(\mathbf{x}^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{w}\) to obtain feature maps.  After training, we freeze the kernels and evaluate three ways of constructing features at test time:

1. **Standard inner product** – identical to training.
2. **Magnitude‑only** – replace each inner product by \(\|\mathbf{x}\|\cdot \|\mathbf{w}\|\), discarding direction.
3. **Angular‑only** – replace each inner product by \(\|\mathbf{x}\|\cos(\theta)\), i.e., use only the cosine similarity.

When reconstructing images from these three feature sets, experiments show that **the angular‑only representation nearly perfectly recovers the original flowers**, whereas magnitude‑only yields almost no recognizable structure.  Crucially, this result holds even though during training we never explicitly used cosine activation; it emerges because the learned kernels have aligned themselves such that semantic content resides primarily in their directions.

The toy experiment therefore validates two claims:

- The *direction* (angle) of each neuron contains most of the semantic signal.
- Altering directions – rather than magnitudes – is an effective way to modify semantics without destroying them.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why Euclidean Distance Between Weights Is Insufficient  

A naïve approach to measure how much a finetuned model deviates from its pretrained counterpart is to compute an Euclidean norm  

\[
\bigl\|\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{M}^{0}\bigr\|_{F},
\]

where \(\mathbf{M}^{0}\) denotes the pretrained weight matrix and \(\mathbf{M}\) its finetuned version.  This measure treats every entry as independent and penalises any change in magnitude or sign equally.

However, consider two orthogonal matrices \(\mathbf{R}_1,\mathbf{R}_2 \in O(d)\).  Both satisfy  

\[
\bigl\|\mathbf{R}_1 - \mathbf I\bigr\|_{F}= 
\bigl\|\mathbf{R}_2 - \mathbf I\bigr\|_{F},
\]

yet they rotate weight vectors by entirely different angles.  If one rotation aligns neurons with a new downstream task while preserving *pairwise angles*, it may keep HE unchanged; another rotation could scramble those angles and dramatically alter generated semantics despite having exactly the same Euclidean distance from identity.  

Thus **Euclidean distance conflates changes that matter (altering relative angles) with changes that are largely irrelevant (uniform scaling)**.  A metric that isolates structural relationships—namely hyperspherical energy—offers a finer grain view: two models whose HE values differ only slightly have retained almost exactly the same angular geometry, hinting that their semantic capacities remain similar.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Properties of Hyperspherical Energy: Invariance Under Orthogonal Transformations  

A central property that makes HE attractive for finetuning is its **invariance under orthogonal transformations**.  

Let \(\mathbf{R}\in O(d)\) be any orthogonal matrix (\(\mathbf R^{\!\top}\!\mathbf R = \mathbf I\)).  Define a transformed weight matrix  

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf W}= \mathbf R\, \mathbf W^{0}.
\]

Each neuron becomes  

\[
\tilde{\mathbf w}_i = \mathbf R\, \mathbf w_i^{0}.
\]

Because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products,

\[
(\tilde{\mathbf w}_i)^{\!\top} (\tilde{\mathbf w}_j)
   = (\mathbf w_i^{0})^{\!\top} (\underbrace{\mathbf R^{\!\top}\!\!\;\! \mathbf R}_{=\;\mathbb I})\, \mathbf w_j^{0}
   = (\mathbf w_i^{0})^{\!\top} \! \mathbf w_j^{0},
\]

the cosine similarity between any pair \(i,j\) remains unchanged after applying \(\mathbf R.\) Consequently every term in (1) is invariant, yielding

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol W}) = 
    \operatorname{HE}(\boldsymbol W^{0}).
    \tag{2}
\]

Intuitively, an orthogonal transformation simply rotates or reflects all neuron vectors *together*; it does not alter their relative angles.  Hence HE serves as an exact **conserved quantity** under such transformations.

This invariance inspires **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**: instead of allowing arbitrary additive updates to weights (which can arbitrarily distort pairwise angles), we restrict finetuning to learn a layer‑wise orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf R.\) By construction, OFT guarantees that HE remains identical to its pretrained value—preserving precisely those angular relationships empirically linked to semantic competence.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary  

- **Hyperspherical energy** aggregates inverse Euclidean distances between normalised neurons; it directly reflects pairwise angular similarity across a layer.
- Empirical evidence (SphereNet, decoupled networks, toy auto‑encoder) shows that **angles encode semantics**, while magnitudes capture more superficial variations.
- Pure Euclidean distance between weight tensors fails to distinguish harmless uniform scaling from harmful angular distortion; HE isolates structural changes that truly affect generative behavior.
- Because HE is **invariant under orthogonal transforms**, learning an orthogonal matrix per layer provides a principled way to adapt a model while guaranteeing preservation of its intrinsic relational geometry.

In later chapters we will build upon this geometric foundation to derive concrete finetuning algorithms (OFT and its constrained variant COFT), demonstrate their theoretical guarantees, and evaluate their practical benefits on subject‑driven and controllable text‑to‑image generation tasks.

**Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Theory and Formulation**

In this chapter we develop the theoretical foundation of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the finetuning paradigm that underlies the experimental successes reported in the paper *Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning*. We begin by stating finetuning as a constrained optimisation problem, then show how preserving hyperspherical energy (HE) leads naturally to an orthogonal transformation of the pretrained weights.  After establishing the condition mathematically, we present the concrete formulation \( \mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0} \) together with the orthogonality constraint \( \mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I} \).  We interpret this transformation as rotating the canonical coordinate system of neurons within a layer, and finally contrast OFT with two commonly used alternatives: standard gradient‑descent finetuning and low‑rank additive updates such as LoRA.

---

### 1. Problem Statement: Finetuning as Constrained Optimisation  

Let a pretrained text‑to‑image diffusion model be described by a collection of weight matrices \(\{\mathbf{W}^{0}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}\), where each \(\mathbf{W}^{0}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}\times n_{\ell}}\) represents the parameters of layer \(\ell\).  For a downstream task (e.g., subject‑driven generation or controllable generation) we seek a finetuned model \(\{\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\}\) that minimises a task‑specific loss \(\mathcal{L}(\{\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\})\), typically a combination of reconstruction, perceptual, and regularisation terms.  

If finetuning were unconstrained, one would simply apply stochastic gradient descent (SGD) or Adam to minimise \(\mathcal{L}\).  However, in large diffusion models the parameter space is high‑dimensional and naïve updates often destroy the delicate semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining.  Consequently we formulate finetuning as a **constrained optimisation** problem

\[
\min_{\{\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\}} \; \mathcal{L}(\{\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\}) 
\quad
\text{s.t.}
\quad
\mathcal{C}(\{\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\},\{\mathbf{W}^{0}_{\ell}\}) = 0,
\]

where \(\mathcal{C}\) encodes a structural preservation requirement.  The key contribution of OFT is to choose \(\mathcal{C}\) as **preservation of hyperspherical energy**, because empirical evidence shows that HE captures pairwise angular relationships among neurons that are tightly linked to semantic generation capability.

---

### 2. Hyperspherical Energy and Its Invariance  

For a fully connected layer with weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}= [\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), define the *normalised* neuron vectors

\[
\hat{\mathbf w}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf w_{i}}{\|\mathbf w_{i}\|}, \qquad i=1,\dots,n .
\]

The **hyperspherical energy** (HE) of this layer is

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)
:= 
\sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\ i\neq j}}^{n}
    \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf w}_{j}\bigr\|^{-1}.
\tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) aggregates the reciprocal Euclidean distances between every pair of normalised neurons; equivalently it measures how uniformly the neurons are distributed on the unit hypersphere.  Smaller values correspond to more uniform spread, whereas any change in pairwise angles directly alters HE.

A crucial algebraic property follows from elementary geometry: **pairwise angular similarities are invariant under any common orthogonal transformation**.  Let \(\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\) satisfy \(\mathbf R^{\!\top}\mathbf R = \mathbf I_{d}\).  Consider transformed neurons

\[
\tilde{\mathbf w}_{i}= \mathbf R\, \mathbf w_{i},
\qquad
\widehat{\tilde{\mathbf w}}_{i}= 
    \frac{\tilde{\mathbf w}_{i}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf w}_{i}\|}
    = 
    \frac{\mathbf R\, \mathbf w_{i}}{\|\mathbf w_{i}\|}
    = 
    \mathbf R\, \hat{\mathbf w}_{i}.
\]

Because an orthogonal matrix preserves Euclidean norm, we have

\[
\bigl\|\widehat{\tilde{\mathbf w}}_{i}- 
      \widehat{\tilde{\mathbf w}}_{j}
\bigr\|
=
\bigl\|
   \mathbf R\, (\hat{\mathbf w}_{i}- 
                \hat{\mathbf w}_{j})
\bigr\|
=
\bigl\|
   \hat{\mathbf w}_{i}- 
   \hat{\mathbf w}_{j}
\bigr\|,
\]

where we used the fact that orthogonal transformations preserve distances.  Substituting into (1),

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\tilde {\mathbf W})
=
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W).
\tag{2}
\]

Thus **any orthogonal transformation applied uniformly to all neurons in a layer leaves hyperspherical energy unchanged**.  This invariance provides a tractable route to enforce HE preservation: rather than adding a regulariser that attempts to keep HE equal to its pretrained value (which would be difficult to optimise), we restrict ourselves to the *exact* manifold on which HE is constant—the set of orthogonal rotations/reflections applied to the whole weight matrix.

---

### 3. Orthogonal Finetuning Formulation  

Guided by (2), OFT chooses its structural constraint to be exactly an orthogonal mapping from pretrained weights to finetuned weights:

> **Orthogonal Finetuning Constraint**  
> For every layer \( \ell \),
> there exists an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf R_{\ell} \in O(d_{\ell}) \) such that  
> \( \displaystyle
>     \boxed{
>         \;\;\mathbf W_{\ell}= 
>         \mathbf R_{\ell}\,\mathbf W^{0}_{\ell},
>     }
>   }   .
>   \tag{3}
>   `

The full optimisation problem becomes

\[
%
\begin{aligned}
    & \min_{\{\mathbf R_{\ell}\}} 
      && 
      \mathcal L
      (
        \,\bigl[\,
          (\mathbf R_{\ell}\,\mathbf W^{0}_{\ell})^\top
        ]_{\ell=1}^{L}
      )
      \\[4pt]
    & 
      && 
      {\text{s.t.}}\;
      \forall\, \ell:\;
      \mathbf R_{\ell}^{\,\top} 
      \,\mathbf R_{\ell}
      = 
      \mathbf I_{d_{\ell}} .
    %
    % optional epsilon constraint omitted here; appears later for COFT
%
    .
%
    &
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%

%
    
    
%

%

\
end{aligned}
    %   
    %
    %  
```

In words: we keep every pretrained weight matrix unchanged up to a *layer‑shared* rotation/reflection defined by \( \mathbf R_{\ell}.\) Because each \( {\bf R}_l^T {\bf R}_l = I,\) gradient based optimisation can be performed on an unconstrained parameterisation of orthogonal matrices (e.g., via Cayley transform or exponential map; see Section 4).  Importantly, after training we may fuse the learned rotations into a single conventional weight tensor:

```
   $\displaystyle
   {\bf W}_{{l}}
   = {\bf R}_{{l}}
     {\bf W}^{0}_{{l}}
   $,
```

so that inference proceeds exactly as with any standard diffusion model—no extra computational graph or auxiliary modules are required.

---

### 4. Interpreting Orthogonal Finetuning as Rotating the Canonical Basis  

A linear layer computes \( {\bf z}= {\bf W}^{T}{\bf x}.  
Write \( {\bf W}= [\, {\bf w}_1,\dots, {\bf w}_n\,]\).  
Each column \( {\bf w}_i}{ represents a basis vector in input space that extracts a particular feature direction from the incoming activation \( {\bf x}.  

When we replace \( {\bf W}^{0}  by \( {\bf W}= {\bf R}{\\bf W}^{0},  
the mapping becomes

```
   ${\\bf z}= ({\\bf R}{\\bf W}^{0})^{\\!\\top}{\\bf x}
            = ({\\bf W}^{0})^{\\!\\top}{\\bf R}^{\\!\\top}{\\bf x}.
```

Thus finetuning does *not* alter the set of basis vectors themselves; rather it rotates the whole input space before those bases are applied.  Geometrically, we may think of OFT as rotating the **canonical coordinate system** attached to each layer’s neurons while preserving their mutual angles.  Because semantic semantics in diffusion models have been empirically shown to be carried predominantly by angular relationships among neuron directions (see Figure 1 in the paper’s toy experiment), such a rotation can modify high‑level generation behaviour without destroying the distributional structure learned during pretraining.

---

### 5. Relationship to Additive Low‑Rank Updates (LoRA) and Vanilla Gradient Descent  

#### 5.1 Standard Gradient Descent  

In ordinary finetuning each weight matrix is updated additively:
\( {\bf W}= {\bf W}^{0}+ \\Delta {\bf W},  
with \\Delta {\\bf W}$ obtained by back‑propagation through \\( \\mathcal L\\).   
No explicit structural restriction is imposed; consequently \\( \\Delta {\\bf W}\\) can arbitrarily distort both norms and angles of individual neurons. While this flexibility allows large representational changes, it also frequently leads to *semantic drift*: because pairwise angles change arbitrarily, hyperspherical energy typically deviates substantially from its pretrained value, resulting in loss of fidelity and instability observed empirically in DreamBooth or LoRA when training for many epochs.

#### 5.2 Low‑Rank Additive Updates (LoRA)  

LoRA proposes an intermediate parametrisation:

```
   ${\\bf W}= {\\bf W}^{0}+ {\\bf A}{\\bf B},
```

where \\( {\\bf A}\\in \\mathbb R^{d\times r'},\
 {\\bf B}\\in \\mathbb R^{r'\\times n}\\) with \( r'\ll \\min(d,n)\).  
The additive term is constrained to lie in a low‑rank subspace; intuitively this limits how far \\( {\\bf W}\\) can drift from \\( {\\bf W}^{0}\\). Nevertheless LoRA still perturbs *both* norms and directions at each column independently; nothing guarantees that pairwise angular relationships remain intact.

#### 5.3 Orthogonal Finetuning as Multiplicative Full‑Rank Constraint  

OFT can be rewritten as

```
   ${\\bf W}= ({\\bf I}+({\\bf R}-{\\bf I})) {\\bf W}^{0},
```

so that

```
   ${\\Delta {\\bf W}} = ({\\bf R}-{\\bf I}) {\\bf W}^{0}.
```

When \\( {\\bf R}-{\\bf I}\\) has low effective rank (e.g., through block‑diagonal parametrisation), OFT also yields a low‑rank update – but crucially **the update is multiplied on the left**, i.e., it acts uniformly across all columns of \\( {\\bf W}^{0}\\). This left multiplication enforces that every neuron experiences exactly the same rotation, preserving all pairwise inner products:

```
   $⟨ \\hat{{\\bf w}}_i , \\hat{{\\bf w}}_j ⟩
   = ⟨ \\widehat{{\\bf R}{\\bf w}_i},
       \\widehat{{\\bf R}{\\bf w}_j} ⟩ .
```

Consequently OFT guarantees exact preservation of hyperspherical energy, something neither vanilla gradient descent nor LoRA can assure without an explicit regulariser.

---

### 6. Summary  

By casting finetuning for text‑to‑image diffusion as an optimisation under a hyperspherical‑energy preservation constraint, we obtain a clean closed form for admissible updates: **layer‑wise orthogonal matrices** multiplying the pretrained weights. This yields three intertwined benefits:

1. **Semantic stability** – because pairwise angles among neurons are invariant under any orthogonal transformation, semantic information encoded during pretraining remains intact.
2. **Parameter efficiency** – only the parameters defining each orthogonal matrix need to be learned; with block‑diagonal or shared‐block structures this scales sub‑quadratically with neuron dimension.
3. **Zero inference overhead** – after training one simply multiplies \( {\bf R}_l {\bf W}_l^{0} , producing an ordinary weight tensor ready for deployment.

The remainder of this textbook will build upon this foundation: Section 7 will detail efficient parameterisations of orthogonal matrices (Cayley transform and block structures); Section 8 will introduce Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which augments OFT with an explicit radius bound; and later chapters will analyse empirical outcomes on subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks.

# Efficient Parameterization of Orthogonal Matrices  

In modern deep‑learning systems, orthogonal transformations have emerged as a powerful tool for finetuning large pretrained models while preserving important geometric properties of the weight space.  An orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies  
\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}= \mathbf{I},
\]  
so that it represents a rotation (determinant $+1$) or a reflection (determinant $-1$) of the underlying vector space.  Applying an orthogonal matrix to the weight matrix of a layer leaves the pair‑wise angular relationships among its neurons unchanged, and consequently the **hyperspherical energy**—the sum of inverse pairwise distances on the unit hypersphere—remains invariant.  This invariance is the key ingredient behind Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) for text‑to‑image diffusion models.

While the theory is clean, naively storing an arbitrary orthogonal matrix incurs a quadratic parameter cost $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$, which quickly becomes prohibitive for the high‑dimensional linear layers that dominate diffusion backbones.  In this chapter we develop practical parameterizations that dramatically reduce that cost while retaining full orthogonality.  We introduce three ideas in increasing order of efficiency: the **Cayley transform**, a **block‑diagonal decomposition**, and finally **parameter sharing** across blocks.  We also discuss how these schemes compare to low‑rank updates such as LoRA, and present practical tips for stable implementation.

---

## 1. Cayley Transform: From Skew‑Symmetric Matrices to Orthogonal Matrices  

A classic way to generate an orthogonal matrix without ever performing an explicit QR or SVD decomposition is the **Cayley transform**.  Let $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be a *skew‑symmetric* matrix, i.e. $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q}$.  The Cayley transform maps $\mathbf{Q}$ to an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ via  
\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\;}
\tag{1}
\]  
provided that $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$ is invertible.  Equation (1) guarantees $\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}$ because  

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}
&= \big[(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-\top}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{\top}\big] (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\\
&= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}^{\top})(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\\
&= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-\top}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}= \mathbf I.
\end{aligned}
\]  

Because $\mathbf Q$ is skew‑symmetric, its degrees of freedom are exactly $d(d-1)/2$, matching the dimension of the orthogonal group $\mathrm O(d)$.  Hence by learning the independent entries of $\mathbf Q$, we can explore the entire set of orthogonal matrices *except* those with determinant $-1$ (the Cayley transform maps only to the special orthogonal group $\mathrm {SO}(d)$).  In practice this limitation does not harm performance: any reflection can be expressed as a rotation followed by a sign flip on a single coordinate, which can be absorbed into subsequent linear layers.

### 1.1 Parameter Count and Learning Dynamics  

If we store all unique entries of $\mathbf Q$, we need $d(d-1)/2$ trainable parameters.  For a moderately sized layer with $d=1024$, this amounts to roughly $5\times10^{5}$ parameters, already far fewer than a full $d\times d$ matrix ($>10^{6}$).  Moreover, gradients with respect to $\mathbf Q$ are obtained by differentiating through (1); modern automatic‑differentiation frameworks handle the matrix inverse efficiently using back‑propagation rules for linear solves.

### 1.2 Numerical Stability  

The inversion in (1) can become unstable when eigenvalues of $\mathbf Q$ approach $+1$, because $(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)$ becomes near singular.  A common safeguard is to **clip** the spectral norm of $\mathbf Q$ during training:

```python
# PyTorch-style pseudocode
max_norm = 0.9
norm = torch.linalg.norm(Q, ord=2)
if norm > max_norm:
    Q = Q * (max_norm / norm)
```

Alternatively one can add a tiny damping term $\delta \,\mathbf I$ inside the inverse:
$\;(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q + \delta \,\mathbf I)^{-1}$, with $\delta \sim 10^{-5}$.

---

## 2. Block‑Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices: Reducing $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ to $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r)$  

Even $d(d-1)/2$ parameters can be excessive when $d$ reaches several thousands, as is typical for transformer feed‑forward or attention projection matrices in diffusion models.  A natural way to cut down the parameter budget is to restrict $\mathbf R$ to a **block‑diagonal** form:

\[
\boxed{\;
\mathbf R = \operatorname {diag}\bigl(\,\underbrace{\!\!\!\!\!\! \;\;\; \!\!\! \underbrace{\;\;}_{
r~{\text {blocks}}}\big)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
   \!\!\!\! \displaystyle \underbrace{\;\;\; \color {gray}{ \!\! R_{1}}}_{d/r\times d/r}
   &       &        \\
        & \ddots &        \\
        &       &   \displaystyle \underbrace{\;\;\; \color {gray}{ R_{r}}}_{d/r\times d/r}
   \\[4pt]
   &       &
)
}
$
where each block $\displaystyle R_{i}\in {\rm O}\bigl(\tfrac d r\bigr)$ is itself an orthogonal matrix.  

### 2.1 Parameter Savings  

A full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix needs $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters.  When we split it into $r$ equal blocks, each block has size $d/r$, so its parameter count is $(d/r)(d/r-1)/2$. Summing over all blocks yields

\[
N_{\rm blk}= r\, \frac {d}{r}\Bigl(\frac d r -1 \Bigr)\! /2
= \frac {d}{2}\Bigl(\frac d r -1 \Bigr)
= \mathcal O \!\left(\frac {d^{2}} {r}\right).
\]

Thus increasing $r$ linearly reduces the quadratic term: if $r=8$, we retain only $12.5\,\%$ of the original parameters.

### 2.2 Expressive Trade‑off  

Restricting the transformation to block‑diagonal form means that neurons belonging to different blocks never interact via the orthogonal rotation.  Consequently, each block can only re‑orient its own subset of dimensions independently.  Empirically this restriction still provides enough flexibility for finetuning tasks such as subject‑driven generation or control signal injection, while substantially alleviating memory pressure.

### 2.3 Construction via Independent Cayley Transforms  

Each block $R_i$ can be generated independently using its own skew‑symmetric matrix $Q_i$, i.e.

\[
R_i = (\boldsymbol I_{d/r}+ Q_i)(\,\boldsymbol I_{d/r} - Q_i)^{-1}.
\]

All blocks share the same code path; during back‑propagation gradients only flow through their respective $Q_i$, keeping implementation clean and parallelizable.

---

## 3. Parameter Sharing Across Blocks: From $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r)$ to $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r^{2})$

When storage is at an absolute premium—e.g., when deploying on edge devices or when finetuning millions of layers simultaneously—we can push efficiency further by *sharing* parameters across blocks.  

### 3.1 Shared Block Matrix  

Instead of learning distinct $Q_i$ for each block, we enforce  
\[
Q_1 = Q_2 = \dots = Q_r =: Q_{\text shared},
\]
which yields identical orthogonal submatrices

\[
R_1 = R_2 = \dots = R_r =: R_{\text shared}.
\]

Now only one skew‑symmetric matrix needs to be stored, costing $(d/r)(d/r-1)/2$ parameters overall:

\[
N_{\rm shared}= \frac {d}{r}\Bigl(\frac d r -1 \Bigr)\! /2
= \mathcal O \!\left(\frac {d^{2}} {r^{2}}\right).
\]

For example, with $d=1024$ and $r=16$, we go from roughly $32\,000$ parameters (independent blocks) down to about $500$ parameters—an order‐of‐magnitude reduction.

### 3   .  3   Practical Implications  

Sharing makes every block rotate in exactly the same way; effectively we are applying a *structured* orthogonal transformation that respects a pre‑defined partitioning of dimensions (e.g., channel groups in convolutional layers).  This structure can be interpreted as a depthwise rotation applied independently across groups—a useful perspective when adapting convolutional kernels where each group corresponds to one input channel.

Although sharing reduces expressivity compared with independent blocks, experiments reported in the OFT literature show that even heavily shared configurations retain enough capacity to achieve state‑of‑the‑art controllability on diffusion models while dramatically cutting down memory usage.

---

## 4. Comparison with LoRA and Full‑Matrix Updates  

| Approach | Trainable Parameters | Structural Constraint | Typical Use Case |
|----------|---------------------|-----------------------|------------------|
| **Full weight update** ($W=W^0+\Delta W$) | $dn$ (for layer $W^0\in{\mathbb R}^{d\times n}$) | None (maximal flexibility) | Fine‐grained editing but high risk of collapse |
| **LoRA** ($W=W^0 + A B^\top,\; A,B$ low rank) | $r'(d+n)$ where rank $r'\ll \min(d,n)$ | Low‑rank additive change | Memory efficient yet still Euclidean‑based |
| **OFT (full Cayley)** | $d(d-1)/2$ | Orthogonal multiplicative change → preserves hyperspherical energy | Semantic preserving finetuning |
| **OFT – Block diagonal** | $\displaystyle d\,(\tfrac d r -1)/2 = {\cal O}(d^2/r)$ | Orthogonal per block → intra‐block angle preservation | Tradeoff between capacity and parameter budget |
| **OFT – Shared blocks** | ${\cal O}(d^2/r^2)$ | One common orthogonal transformation applied across groups | Extreme compression; still suitable for control tasks |

The key distinction lies in **what geometric property is preserved**: LoRA constrains updates to lie in a low-dimensional subspace but does not guarantee any invariance on angular relationships among neurons; OFT explicitly enforces orthogonality, which mathematically guarantees that pairwise cosine similarities—and thus hyperspherical energy—are unchanged.

---

## 5. Implementation Details and Numerical Considerations  

### 5.1 Initialization at the Identity  

Finetuning should start from the pretrained weights untouched. To achieve this we initialize every skew‑symmetric matrix as zero:

```python
# shape (block_dim, block_dim)
Q = torch.zeros(block_dim, block_dim)
# enforce skew symmetry explicitly
Q = Q - Q.T   # still zero but maintains constraint during updates
```

Because $(\boldsymbol I+0)(\boldsymbol I-0)^{-1}= \boldsymbol I$, each block produces an identity orthogonal matrix at step 0, ensuring that the forward pass initially replicates exactly the pretrained model.

### 5.2 Enforcing Skew Symmetry During Optimization  

Gradient updates will inevitably break strict skew symmetry if we treat `Q` as an unrestricted tensor. Two common remedies:

* **Projection after each optimizer step:**  
```python
with torch.no_grad():
    Q.copy_(0.5 * (Q - Q.T))
```
* **Parameterization via an unconstrained matrix `U`:**  
`Q = U - U.T`. The optimizer sees `U`, which has no constraints; `Q` is constructed on-the-fly before forming `R`. This approach yields cleaner autograd graphs.

### 5.3 Stable Inverse Computation  

The inverse $(\boldsymbol I-\boldsymbol Q)^{-1}$ can be obtained efficiently by solving a linear system rather than forming an explicit inverse:

```python
def cayley(Q):
    I = torch.eye(Q.shape[0], device=Q.device)
    num = I + Q
    den = I - Q          # den will be used as coefficient matrix
    # solve den @ X = num for X
    R = torch.linalg.solve(den, num)
    return R
```

Solvers such as `torch.linalg.solve` employ LU factorization with partial pivoting and are numerically robust even for moderately ill-conditioned matrices—as long as we keep ‖Q‖ below 1 (see Section 1.2).

### 5.4 Gradient Scaling  

Because $(I-\!Q)^{-1}$ amplifies gradients when `$I-\!Q`' is close to singular, it is advisable to scale gradients or use adaptive optimizers (AdamW). Additionally, clipping the Frobenius norm of `Q` after each update prevents it from drifting into regions where inversion becomes dangerous.

### 5.5 Integration Into Existing Diffusion Frameworks  

In diffusion pipelines such as Stable Diffusion, most trainable linear layers are either

* attention projection matrices (`W_q`, `W_k`, `W_v`, `W_o`) of shape `(hidden_dim, hidden_dim)`
* feed­forward MLP weights (`W_↑`, `W_↓`) also typically square.

For each target layer we replace `W` with `R·W⁰`. The code pattern is:

```python
class OFTLinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, W0, block=4, share=False):
        super().__init__()
        self.W0 = nn.Parameter(W0.clone().detach(), requires_grad=False) # frozen
        d = W0.shape[0]
        self.block_dim = d // block

        if share:
            self.U = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(self.block_dim,
                                              self.block_dim))
        else:
            self.U = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(block,
                                              self.block_dim,
                                              self.block_dim))

    def forward(self, x):
        # build orthogonal R from U
        if self.U.dim() == 3:                              # independent blocks
            Rs = [cayley(self.U[i] - self.U[i].T) 
                  for i in range(self.U.shape[0])]
        else:                                               # shared block
            Q_shared = self.U - self.U.T
            R_shared = cayley(Q_shared)
            Rs = [R_shared] * (self.W0.shape[0] // self.block_dim)

        R = torch.block_diag(*Rs)                          # assemble full R
        W = torch.matmul(R.t(), self.W0.t()).t()           # equivalent weight
        return F.linear(x, W)
```

All hyperparameters (`block`, `share`, learning rate for `U`) are tunable; typical settings used in OFT experiments are `block = 8` and `share=False`.

---

## Summary  

We have presented three complementary strategies for representing large orthogonal matrices efficiently:

1. **Cayley Transform** – a differentiable bijection from skew–symmetric matrices to special orthogonal matrices.
2. **Block‑Diagonal Decomposition** – partitions the full rotation into independent sub‑rotations, cutting parameter complexity from $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ to $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r)$.
3. **Parameter Sharing Across Blocks** – forces all sub‑rotations to be identical, further shrinking complexity to $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r^{2})$.

These techniques enable Orthogonal Finetuning to be applied at scale on high‑dimensional layers found in state‑of‑the‑art text‑to‑image diffusion models while preserving hyperspherical energy and thus maintaining generative semantics. Compared with low‑rank additive schemes such as LoRA, OFT’s multiplicative orthogonal updates trade off some flexibility for strong geometric invariance and empirical stability—qualities essential for downstream tasks like subject driven generation and controllable image synthesis.

Practical tips—including identity initialization, skew–symmetry enforcement, spectral clipping and solving linear systems rather than forming explicit inverses—ensure numerically stable training even on GPUs with limited precision. Armed with these tools, researchers and engineers can incorporate principled orthogonal transformations into any large neural architecture without incurring prohibitive memory costs or inference overheads.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) – Adding a Radius Constraint  

In the previous chapter we introduced **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, a principled way to adapt large text‑to‑image diffusion models by learning a layer‑wise orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \). OFT guarantees that the hyperspherical energy – the sum of pairwise angular similarities among neurons – remains exactly unchanged, because an orthogonal transformation preserves all inner products on the unit sphere. While this invariance gives COFT a solid theoretical footing, it still leaves a degree of freedom: any orthogonal matrix can be applied, ranging from the identity (no change) to a full rotation or reflection.  

In practice, when we finetune a model for a downstream task such as subject‑driven generation or controllable generation, we would like to **limit how far the finetuned model can drift** from its pretrained parent. If the transformation is too large, subtle but crucial aspects of the pretrained distribution may be overwritten, leading to instability or collapse. Conversely, if the transformation is too restrictive, the model may not acquire enough capacity to satisfy the new task. **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)** augments OFT with an explicit radius constraint that directly controls this drift.

The central idea is to keep \( \mathbf{R} \) orthogonal **and** force it to stay within an \( \varepsilon \)-ball around the identity matrix:
\[
\| \mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I} \| \;\le\; \varepsilon .
\]
Here \( \| \cdot \| \) denotes a matrix norm (typically the Frobenius norm) and \( \varepsilon > 0\) is a hyperparameter set by the practitioner. The resulting formulation writes the forward pass of a finetuned linear layer as
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\! \mathbf{x},
\qquad
\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I},\;
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \varepsilon .
\tag{1}
\]
In what follows we explain how this constraint is embedded within a differentiable training pipeline, why it matters for stability, and how to pick an appropriate \( \varepsilon\) for different downstream scenarios.

---  

## The “\( \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\)-ball” Constraint: Why It Matters  

### Intuition from Geometry  
Consider each layer’s weight matrix as a set of vectors attached at the origin of a \(d\)-dimensional space. An orthogonal matrix rotates or reflects all vectors simultaneously; geometrically it moves each vector along a great‑circle on the unit hypersphere while preserving pairwise angles. If we allow any rotation, these vectors can end up arbitrarily far from their original locations on that sphere, albeit still maintaining relative distances.  

The **\( \varepsilon\)-ball** constraint says: *the whole set of vectors must not travel farther than a prescribed angular radius*. Since for small perturbations,
\[
\| \mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I} \|_{\!F} \approx 2\,\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_2,
\]
where \( \boldsymbol{\theta}\) encodes the rotation angles (via exponential map), bounding \( \| \mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\|\) directly bounds the maximal rotation angle applied to any neuron. Thus COFT limits **how much directional information** can be altered while still guaranteeing that pairwise angles stay intact.

### Stability vs. Flexibility Trade‑off  
If \( \varepsilon\) is set **very small**, COFT behaves almost like conventional fine‑tuning with a tiny learning rate: weights move only minimally, preserving most of the pretrained generation quality but possibly failing to incorporate new control signals. When \( \varepsilon\) is **large**, COFT approaches unrestricted OFT: the model enjoys maximal flexibility to adapt its representation but may experience instability akin to naive finetuning (e.g., mode collapse or loss of subject identity). The proper choice therefore sits somewhere in between, offering enough freedom for task‑specific adaptation while keeping the model anchored to its pretrained manifold.

---  

## Embedding the Constraint via Cayley Parameterization and Projected Gradient Descent  

### Cayley Transform Recap  
Recall that any orthogonal matrix with determinant \(+1\) can be expressed via the **Cayley transform**:
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad
\mathbf{Q}^{\!\top}= -\,\mathbf{Q}.
\tag{2}
\]
Here \( \mathbf{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric and serves as an unconstrained latent variable. When we update \( \mathbf{Q}\) with gradient descent, Eq. (2) automatically yields an orthogonal \( \mathbf{R}\).

### Translating the Radius Constraint  
The constraint in Eq. (1) involves \( \mathbf{R}\), not directly \( \mathbf{Q}\). To enforce it efficiently we first approximate the Cayley transform with its **Neumann series**:
\[
(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}
 =  \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\!   \mathbf{Q}^{\,k}
   =  \mathbf{I}+  \mathbf{Q}+  O(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{2}),
\]
so that for sufficiently small \( \|\mathbf{Q}\|\):
\[
\mathbf{R}
   = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}+  \mathbf{Q}+O(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{2}))
   =  \mathbf{I}+2\,\mathbf{Q}+ O(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{2}). 
   \tag{3}
\]
Dropping higher‑order terms (reasonable because COFT explicitly limits how far we move from identity), we obtain an approximate linear relationship between deviation in \( R\) and magnitude of \( Q:\)
\[
   \|\!\;  \underbrace{\bigl(\,\mathbf R-\!\,\mathbf I\,\bigr)}_{\approx\,2\,\mathbf Q}\;\|_F
   ~~\approx~~ 2\,  \|\!\,\mathbf Q\,\|_F .
   \tag{4}
\]
Thus enforcing
\[
    \|\!\,\bigl(\,\mathbf R-\!\,\mathbf I\,\bigr)\!\|_F    ~~\le~~    ε
    ~~  ⇔  ~~   2\,\|\!\,\mathbf Q\,\|_F   ~~≤~~ ε,
    ~~⇔~~   ‖ 𝑄 ‖_F ≤ ε' := ε/2 .
    \tag{5}
\]

Consequently we can **embed the radius constraint directly on \( 𝑄 )**: after each gradient step on \(𝑄,\) we project it back onto the Frobenius ball of radius \(ε'\):
```python
# pseudo‑code for one update step
Q ← Q - η * ∇_Q L            # ordinary gradient descent on loss L
if ‖Q‖_F > ε' :
    Q ← ε' * Q / ‖Q‖_F       # projection onto Frobenius ε'-ball
```
Because projection onto a convex ball is differentiable almost everywhere (the scaling operation), this procedure integrates seamlessly into standard automatic‑differentiation frameworks (PyTorch, JAX, etc.). Moreover, initializing \(𝑄=0_{d\times d}\) guarantees that at training start we have exactly \( R = I.\)

### Maintaining Exact Orthogonality  
While Eq. (3) provides an approximation useful for setting \(ε',`)`the actual forward pass continues to use Eq. (2). That is, after projection we recompute 
\( R = (\boldsymbol I + Q)(\boldsymbol I - Q)^{-1}\)
for every forward pass. Because this expression yields an exact orthogonal matrix regardless of how large \( Q )\), COFT respects both constraints simultaneously:

1. **Orthogonality** – guaranteed analytically by Cayley construction.
2. **Radius bound** – enforced approximately via projected updates on \( Q.\)

Empirically (see Figure 9 in the original paper), this two‑step scheme yields stable training even when ε is moderately large (e.g., ε≈0.3). The residual error introduced by neglecting higher orders in Eq. (3) becomes negligible after projection because any overshoot would push ‖𝑄‖ beyond ε′ and be immediately rescaled.

---  

## Effect of \( ε\) on Flexibility vs. Stability  

To illustrate how varying \(ε)\ influences model behavior, consider three representative values:

| ε | Expected Rotation Angle (≈ ‖θ‖₂ ) | Observed Behaviour |
|---|----------------------------------|--------------------|
| **0.05** | < 5° | Model hardly departs from pretrained distribution; subject identity preserved but control signal often ignored; training loss may stagnate early. |
| **0.15** | ≈ 15° | Balanced regime: model learns to inject new control cues while maintaining high fidelity to pretrained semantics; convergence is fast and stable. |
| **0.35** | ≈ 35° | Near‑unconstrained OFT; higher adaptability but occasional spikes in loss; risk of mode collapse if learning rate or batch size are not tuned carefully. |

A conceptual plot (analogous to Figure 10 in the paper) would show three curves of validation CLIP‑text similarity versus epoch:

- The **low‑ε curve** rises quickly but saturates at a suboptimal value.
- The **mid‑ε curve** rises similarly fast but reaches a higher plateau.
- The **high‑ε curve** may initially lag due to larger updates but eventually surpasses others if training proceeds long enough—at cost of larger variance.

These observations underscore that **ε works as a knob for regularization strength**: smaller values provide stronger regularization (more similar to classic low‑learning‑rate fine‑tuning), while larger values grant greater representational freedom akin to OFT.

---  

## Practical Guidelines for Choosing \( ε)\  

### 1️⃣ Identify Task Demands  

- **Subject‑Driven Generation (DreamBooth style)** – preserving fine subject details is paramount; modest angular changes are sufficient because text prompts already supply most semantic directionality. Recommended range:  
  $$ ε ∈ [0.10,\;0.20] $$  
  In experiments this range yielded stable convergence within 400–800 iterations and avoided identity loss observed in unconstrained OFT.

- **Controllable Generation (ControlNet/T2I‑Adapter style)** – additional modalities (edges, segmentation maps, landmarks) require more drastic reorientation of feature space so that new conditioning can be absorbed effectively. A slightly larger value works better:  
  $$ ε ∈ [0.15,\;0.30] $$  
  For extremely fine-grained control such as facial landmark manipulation, practitioners have reported optimal results near ε≈0.25.

### 2️⃣ Consider Data Regime  

- **Few‑shot settings (< 10 images)** – over‑parameterization can cause overfitting if transformations are too large; keep ε toward the lower end of its recommended interval.
- **Large datasets (> few thousand samples)** – model can safely explore bigger rotations; raising ε improves final generation quality without risking collapse.

### 3️⃣ Relate ε to Learning Rate  

Because both learning rate η and radius bound govern step size on the manifold, they should be balanced:

- With a *high* η (e.g., η=1e-3), choose *smaller* ε to prevent overshooting.
- With *low* η (e.g., η=5e-5), you may enlarge ε up to ~0.35 without destabilizing training.

A practical rule-of-thumb:
```text
if η ≥ 5×10⁻⁴ → set ε ≤ 0.15
else                → allow ε up to 0.30
```

### 4️⃣ Monitor Diagnostic Signals  

During training watch two quantities every epoch:

1. **Hyperspherical Energy Difference**:  
   $$ ΔHE = |\text{HE}(W)-\text{HE}(W^0)| $$
   Should stay around zero by construction; sudden spikes indicate projection failure or numerical issues inside Cayley inversion.

2. **Radius Violation Ratio**:  
   $$ r_{\text{viol}} = 
      {\frac{\max_{l}\bigl(\lVert R_l - I\rVert_F\bigr)}{\varepsilon}} $$
   where index *l* runs over all finetuned layers.
   If `r_viol` consistently hits 1 across many layers, you might have chosen an overly restrictive ε; consider increasing it modestly.

When both metrics remain stable and `r_viol` stays comfortably below 1 (e.g., < 0.8), training is proceeding within intended bounds.

---  

## Summary  

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning augments OFT with an explicit radius constraint
\(‖R-I‖ ≤ ε.\)
By recasting this bound in terms of the skew‑symmetric Cayley parameter \(Q,\)
the constraint becomes a simple Frobenius‐norm ball projection that integrates cleanly into stochastic gradient descent pipelines.
The hyperparameter \(ε)\ governs a smooth trade‑off between preserving pretrained semantic structure and granting enough flexibility for new downstream control signals:

- Small \(ε)\ → strong regularization → high fidelity but limited adaptation.
- Moderate \(ε)\ → balanced performance → fast convergence and stable control.
- Large \(ε)\ → near‑unconstrained OFT → maximal adaptability at risk of instability.

Practical recommendations suggest using ε between 0.10–0.20 for subject preservation tasks and between 0.15–0.30 for controllable generation tasks, adjusting further according to data size and learning rate.

With these tools in place, researchers and engineers can confidently finetune massive text‑to‑image diffusion models while keeping them anchored to their powerful pretrained knowledge—a crucial step toward reliable, controllable generative AI systems.
