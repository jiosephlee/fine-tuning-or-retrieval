\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# From Tokens to Whole Sentences: Why Sequence‑Level Importance Ratios Matter in RL for LLMs  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to tool for polishing large language models (LLMs) so they can solve math problems, write code, or follow complex instructions. The classic RL recipe for language models—PPO or its value‑free cousin GRPO—applies an *importance‑sampling* correction at the **token level**. In practice this looks sensible: each token is a tiny decision, so we weight its gradient by how much the new policy’s probability differs from the old one.

The newest algorithm, **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**, flips this idea on its head. Instead of correcting every token individually, GSPO computes a single importance ratio for the **entire generated sequence** and clips that ratio. The result? far more stable training, better utilization of compute, and a big simplification for Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models.

Below we unpack why moving from token‑level to sequence‑level ratios makes such a difference, using intuitive analogies and a few equations (kept light on the math).

---  

## 1. The original intuition: token‑level importance sampling  

Imagine you are voting on a long essay one word at a time. After the essay is written, you receive a single score (the *reward*) that says how good the whole essay is. In GRPO the algorithm pretends that each word earned its own separate score by **copying the overall reward to every token** and then weighting each word’s contribution by  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

If the new policy makes a word slightly more likely than the old one, \(w_{i,t}>1\); if it makes it less likely, \(w_{i,t}<1\). We then clip these weights to keep them from exploding.

At first glance this mirrors classic importance sampling: “re‑weight the old samples so they look like they came from the new distribution.”  
But there’s a hidden mismatch.

### 1.1 Why it goes wrong

* **Reward granularity vs. importance weight granularity** – The reward lives on the *whole* essay, yet we apply the correction to each *word*. The two units don’t line up.
* **Single‑sample noise** – Importance sampling works because we average many samples from the behavior distribution to smooth out variance. GRPO uses only **one** sample per token to compute \(w_{i,t}\). That single observation is very noisy.
* **Error accumulation over length** – For a 200‑token answer, 200 noisy ratios stack up. Small errors compound into huge gradients, especially when clipping forces some ratios to stay near their bounds.
* **MoE volatility** – In sparsely activated MoE models different tokens may be processed by different experts after an update. The token‑level probability can shift dramatically even if the overall sentence likelihood stays stable, inflating \(w_{i,t}\) further.

The empirical consequence: training sometimes “collapses” irreversibly—the model quickly forgets how to generate coherent text.

---  

## 2. Aligning units: sequence‑level importance ratios  

The core principle of importance sampling states:

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{new}}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{old}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{new}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
\]

Here \(z\) is any random variable—in our case a **complete response** \(y\). The ratio \(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)}\) therefore measures exactly how much the new policy’s *sequence distribution* diverges from that of the old policy.

GSPO defines its importance ratio as

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
      = \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                    {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        \Bigr).
\]

A few points make this definition attractive:

* **One ratio per sequence** – All tokens share the same scalar \(s_i\). The noisy per‑token fluctuations are averaged out through the log‑sum.
* **Length normalization** – Raising to \(1/|y_i|\) keeps short and long responses on comparable scales, preventing extremely long answers from dominating clipping decisions.
* **Direct alignment with reward** – The advantage \(\widehat A_i\) is computed from rewards of whole responses; clipping now happens on the same unit (the whole response).

The final GSPO objective looks just like PPO’s clipped objective, but with \(s_i(\theta)\) instead of token ratios:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\Bigl[
    \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
    \min\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\,
    \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\bigr)
\Bigr].
\]

## 3. Intuitive analogies  

### 3.1 Grading an exam vs. grading each answer sheet line  

Think of RL training as grading essays for a class. The teacher gives one score per essay (the reward). GRPO tries to adjust each word’s “grade” based on how likely that word was under the new policy. If one word’s probability changes wildly, that single grade can outweigh everything else, even though the overall essay might still be good.

GSPO instead grades the *entire essay* as a single unit—if the new policy writes essays that are generally more probable under its own distribution, then all words receive a consistent bump or penalty. The process mirrors how teachers actually work: they look at the whole piece before deciding what to change.

### 3.2 Navigation with GPS vs. step‑by‑step corrections  

Imagine driving with a GPS that only corrects you after every meter (token level). A tiny sensor error at any step leads you off course quickly because corrections keep stacking up. A GPS that re‑calculates only after reaching a waypoint (whole route) smooths out small fluctuations and gives you a stable direction.

Sequence‑level ratios act like waypoint corrections: they consider the whole path before deciding whether to trust or reject it.

---  

## 4. Empirical payoff  

| Metric | GRPO (token) | GSPO (sequence) |
|--------|--------------|-----------------|
| Training stability | Frequent collapses after long runs; especially on MoE models | No collapses observed across thousands of GPU hours |
| Fraction of clipped tokens | ~0.5 % (very few clipped) | ~10 % of *responses* clipped (still higher efficiency) |
| Sample efficiency | Requires “Routing Replay” for MoE (extra memory & communication) | Works out‑of‑the‑box on MoE; no routing tricks needed |
| Benchmark gains (Qwen3 finetunes) | Baseline | +3–5 % Pass@1 on AIME/CodeBench, higher Elo on Codeforces |

Even though GSPO discards more tokens during clipping (because an entire response may be dropped), it still learns faster because the remaining gradients are far less noisy. The counter‑intuitive result—more clipping → higher efficiency—highlights how crucial it is for the importance weight to match the reward’s granularity.

---  

## 5. A lightweight variant: GSPO-token  

Sometimes we need fine‑grained control—e.g., multi‑turn dialogues where early turns deserve different credit than later ones. GSPO offers a token‑level variant called **GSPO-token** that keeps the sequence ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) but lets us attach distinct advantages \(\widehat A_{i,t}\) per token. Mathematically it reduces to exactly the same gradient as GSPO when all token advantages are equal, yet it gives us flexibility without reintroducing GRPO’s instability.

---  

## 6. Ripple effects beyond stability  

### 6.1 Simpler infrastructure  
Because GSPO only needs whole‑sequence log probabilities, we can reuse likelihoods directly from inference engines (SGLang, vLLM). No extra pass through a training engine is required to recompute token‑wise probabilities—a win for distributed training pipelines where inference and training run on different hardware.

### 6.2 Unlocking MoE potential  
Mixture‑of‑Experts models shine when every expert can be used freely. GRPO forced us to freeze routing decisions (“Routing Replay”) to prevent volatile token ratios—a costly hack that limited capacity. GSPO’s sequence focus removes this dependency entirely; experts can switch freely between updates without destabilizing training.

---  

## 7. Takeaway  

The key insight behind GSPO is simple but powerful: **the unit of importance sampling must match the unit of reward**. Token-level ratios look granular but actually inject high variance because each ratio is estimated from a single sample and misaligned with sequence rewards. By aggregating to a sequence-level importance ratio—and clipping at that level—we obtain:

* Cleaner gradient estimates  
* Robustness to long responses and MoE routing changes  
* Faster learning despite discarding more data  
* A cleaner engineering stack

If you’re building RL pipelines for ever larger LLMs—or even just trying to avoid those dreaded “model collapse” moments—switching from per-token to per‑sequence importance ratios is worth trying today. The mathematics is straightforward; the stability gains are dramatic; and as demonstrated in Qwen 3’s latest results, this shift can translate directly into higher scores on real world benchmarks.

Happy RL training!
