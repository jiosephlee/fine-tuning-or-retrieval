\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 4: # Empirical Discovery of Positional Non‑Uniformities  

In this chapter we detail the systematic investigations that uncovered two previously hidden sources of non‑uniformity in rotary positional embeddings (RoPE). By probing how different RoPE dimensions and different token positions react to interpolation, we arrived at a more faithful way to stretch an LLM’s context window. The experiments described here form the empirical backbone of the LongRoPE method: they show that naïve, uniform scaling of rotation angles is sub‑optimal, that preserving the original encoding for the earliest tokens is beneficial, and that the discovered non‑uniform scaling improves both fine‑tuned and non‑fine‑tuned models. Throughout, we focus on intuition and the logical steps that led from raw perplexity measurements to concrete design choices for extending context windows to millions of tokens.

## Experimental Setup: Evolutionary Search Guided by Perplexity  

The central hypothesis driving our analysis was that the optimal way to interpolate RoPE should be **data‑driven**, not fixed by hand‑crafted formulas. To test this hypothesis we framed the search for suitable interpolation factors as an **evolutionary optimization** problem, where each candidate solution specifies a set of per‑dimension rescaling factors \(\lambda_i\) (one for each RoPE dimension) and a token‑position threshold \(\hat n\).  

### Datasets and Evaluation Metric  

Two large, heterogenous corpora were used as test beds:

1. **PG19** – a collection of long literary works (~19 GB). Because these texts are naturally organized into books with coherent narrative structure, they stress a model’s ability to retain distant information over many thousands of tokens.  
2. **Proof‑Pile** – a subset of the Pile focused on scientific proofs and technical documentation. This dataset evaluates how well a model maintains precise factual consistency across long spans.

For each candidate interpolation, we measured **perplexity** on five randomly selected validation samples from PG19 and on a comparable number of samples from Proof‑Pile. Perplexity, denoted \(\mathcal{L}\), quantifies the model’s next‑token prediction loss; lower values indicate that the positional embedding better preserves the information learned during pre‑training.

### Evolutionary Search Procedure  

Each individual in the population encoded a vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda} = (\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{d/2-1})\) together with a scalar \(\hat n\). The search space was deliberately large:

- \(\lambda_i\) could take any value in \([1.0,\; s\times 1.25]\) with step size 0.01, where \(s = L'/L\) is the desired extension ratio (e.g., \(s=8\) for an 8× stretch).  
- \(\hat n\) was drawn from a small discrete set \(\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}\), representing how many initial token positions would be left untouched.

Because evaluating perplexity for every possible combination is infeasible (the combinatorial space quickly exceeds \(10^{160}\) configurations for modest extensions), we employed an **evolutionary algorithm** with two efficiency tricks:

1. **Seeded Initialization** – The initial population contained three well‑known baselines (linear Position Interpolation (PI), NTK‑based scaling, and YaRN grouping). The remaining individuals were random perturbations of these seeds.
2. **Monotonicity Constraint** – We required \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) for all dimensions \(i\). This leverages insights from Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory: higher‑frequency dimensions (low \(i\)) should be scaled less aggressively than lower‑frequency ones. Imposing monotonicity pruned away many implausible candidates and reduced the number of perplexity evaluations dramatically.

After each generation we kept the top‑\(k\) individuals (lowest perplexities), applied mutation (random adjustments respecting monotonicity) and crossover (swapping sub‑vectors between parents), and repeated for a fixed number of iterations (typically 40). The final output of this process was a **searched RoPE scaling schedule**—a concrete set of \(\lambda_i\) values together with an optimal \(\hat n\).

## Finding 1 – Dimension‑wise Optimal Scaling Factors \(\lambda_i\)

The first surprise emerged when examining the best‐performing individuals: **the optimal rescale factors varied considerably across RoPE dimensions**. Recall that each dimension encodes a sinusoid with frequency \(\beta^{-i}\); lower indices correspond to high frequencies (rapid oscillations), while higher indices correspond to low frequencies (slow oscillations). If we denote the original rotary angle at position \(n\) as \(\frac{n}{\beta^i}\), then after interpolation it becomes

\[
\cos\Bigl( \tfrac{1}{\lambda_i} \tfrac{n}{\beta^i} \Bigr), 
\qquad 
\sin\Bigl( \tfrac{1}{\lambda_i} \tfrac{n}{\beta^i} \Bigr).
\]

A uniform choice such as \(\lambda_i = s\) (the linear PI rule) forces *all* dimensions to stretch or compress by the same factor. Our evolutionary search consistently discovered schedules where:

- **High‑frequency dimensions (\(i\) small)** received **small rescaling** (\(\lambda_i \approx 1.0 - 1.3\)). This preserves fine‑grained relative positions among nearby tokens.
- **Mid‑frequency dimensions** adopted intermediate values (\(\lambda_i \approx 2 - 4\)).
- **Low‑frequency dimensions (\(i\) large)** were allowed **larger rescaling** (\(\lambda_i\) approaching the global extension ratio \(s\times 1.25\)).

Intuitively, high frequencies encode “local” ordering; compressing them too much would cause distinct adjacent tokens to map onto nearly identical sinusoidal phases—a phenomenon we refer to as *positional crowding*. Low frequencies capture “global” ordering over long spans; they can tolerate more compression because their phase changes slowly anyway.

When evaluated on PG19 and Proof‑Pile **without any fine‑tuning**, this non‑uniform schedule reduced perplexity by up to **30 % relative** compared with PI and by about **15 % relative** compared with NTK or YaRN. The improvement was most pronounced at context lengths beyond 8 k tokens, exactly where uniform scaling begins to break down. Hence we concluded that *dimensionwise non‑uniformity is a fundamental property of RoPE that must be respected when interpolating*.

## Finding 2 – Reduced Interpolation for the First \(\hat n\) Tokens  

While unraveling dimensionwise behavior we also examined whether all token positions should be treated identically. Prior work on streaming LLMs and infinite‑length language models suggests that **early tokens receive disproportionately large attention weights**, because they appear in every subsequent attention matrix row/column pair. If these early positions are severely distorted by interpolation, downstream attentions may suffer dramatically.

To test this intuition we conducted a controlled experiment: using linear PI or NTK scaling we varied the number of *unmodified* initial tokens \(\hat n\). For each choice (\(\hat n = 0, 2, 8, 16,\dots ,256\)) we kept the first \(\hat n\) token embeddings exactly as originally trained (no rescaling), while applying the chosen uniform scaling to all later positions.

The results showed a clear pattern:

| Extension Length | Best \(\hat n\) (tokens) | Perplexity Improvement vs. \(\hat n=0\) |
|------------------|--------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| 8 k              | 16                       | ~8 % lower |
| 16 k             | 32–64                    | ~12 % lower |
| 32 k             | 128                      | ~15 % lower |

Two insights follow:

1. **Preserving early RoPE clearly helps** irrespective of whether we use linear or NTK scaling.
2. **The optimal amount of preserved tokens grows with the target extension length**; longer stretches benefit from protecting more initial positions because their influence propagates through more attention steps.

Consequently our final formulation introduces an explicit binary mask:

\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)=
   \begin{cases}
     1 & n < \hat n \\[4pt]
     {\displaystyle \frac{1}{\lambda_i}} & n \ge \hat n,
   {\end{cases}
}
\]

which leaves early angles untouched while applying per-dimension scaling thereafter.

## Finding 3 – Non‑Uniform Scaling Benefits Both Fine‑Tuned and Non‑Fine‑Tuned Regimes  

Having identified superior scaling rules under pure inference conditions, we next asked whether these rules would also serve as better *initializations* for subsequent fine‑tuning on longer contexts—a crucial practical question because extending beyond roughly \(4\times L\) typically requires some amount of gradient update.

We performed two complementary experiments:

1. **Pure Inference Extension** – Using only searched non‑uniform factors (\(\lambda_i,\hat n\)) we evaluated perplexity at context lengths up to 64 k without any additional training.
2. **Fine–Tuning after Initialization** – Starting from the same searched configuration we fine‑tuned LLaMA2‑7B for a modest 400 steps on documents segmented at either 64 k or 128 k length (the latter being more aggressive). After fine‑tuning we re-evaluated perplexity across a range of lengths.

The outcomes were striking:

- In pure inference mode, non‑uniform scaling already outperformed PI/YaRN at every evaluated length beyond 8 k tokens.
- After fine‐tuning, models initialized with our searched schedule achieved *lower final perplexities* than those initialized with PI or YaRN even when both received identical training budgets. For instance, at a target length of 64 k token windows:

| Initialization | Post–FT Perplexity (PG19) |
|----------------|---------------------------|
| Linear PI      | 12.8 |
| YaRN           | 12.5 |
| **Non‑Uniform Search** | **11.3** |

The gap widened for longer targets (128 k and beyond), confirming that respecting both dimensionwise and positional non‑uniformities yields an embedding space that is already close to what gradient descent would otherwise have to discover from scratch.

In summary, *non-uniform positional interpolation is universally advantageous*: it reduces information loss when acting alone and provides a superior starting point when fine-tuning is necessary.

## Visualizing Learned \(\lambda_i\) Curves (Illustrative Insight)

Although not required for downstream usage, visualizing the discovered scaling curves helps build intuition about how RoPE reshapes itself across dimensions. A typical curve for an \(s=8\times\) extension appears as follows:

- The curve starts near unity for dimensions \(i=0,\dots,15\), gently rising.
- Around mid–range dimensions (\(i≈30–40\)) it accelerates sharply.
- Near the highest dimensions (\(i≈63–95\)) it plateaus close to \(s\times1.2 ≈9.6\).

Superimposing curves from different target extensions shows that larger extensions simply push the high‐frequency tail upward while preserving the low‐frequency baseline—exactly mirroring our earlier theoretical reasoning about local versus global positional information.

## Concluding Remarks on Empirical Discoveries  

The systematic evolution‐driven analysis led us to two concrete “hidden” non‑uniformities in rotary embeddings:

1. **Dimensionwise frequency variation:** higher frequencies require milder interpolation; lower frequencies can absorb stronger scaling.
2. **Token position variation:** early tokens should retain their original encoding because they dominate attention dynamics.

By explicitly modeling both aspects—through per–dimension rescale factors \(\lambda_i\) and an early‐token threshold \(\hat n\)—we obtain an interpolation scheme that dramatically lowers perplexity across diverse long-text corpora both before and after fine‐tuning. These findings directly motivate the design of LongRoPE: an evolutionary search algorithm discovers optimal non‑uniform schedules; progressive fine‐tuning leverages them; and a final re‐adjustment restores performance on short contexts.

The remainder of this textbook will build upon these empirical foundations: Chapters on algorithmic implementation will describe how LongRoPE searches efficiently despite an astronomically large solution space, while later sections will demonstrate how this machinery enables context windows up to two million tokens without sacrificing accuracy on standard benchmarks or downstream tasks such as passkey retrieval.
