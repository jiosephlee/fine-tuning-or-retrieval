\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### How does exponential gating enable the sLSTM to revise storage decisions compared to standard sigmoid gates?
Question:
The paper claims that using exponential activation for the input (and optionally forget) gates allows the sLSTM to revise previously stored values, addressing the limitation of standard LSTMs in tasks like nearest neighbor search. I am unclear on the exact mechanism: how does an exponentially large gate value affect the cell update (Eq. (3)) and the normalizer state, and why does this allow a later input to overwrite an earlier stored value without causing numerical overflow? Could someone walk through a simple example sequence showing the gate values and resulting cell state updates?
Answer:
**Answer**

In a vanilla LSTM the input‑gate and forget‑gate are sigmoids, i.e. they are confined to the interval \([0,1]\).  
When a new item \(z_{t}\) arrives, the cell update  

\[
c_{t}=f_{t}\,c_{t-1}+i_{t}\,z_{t}
\]

can only *add* to the existing memory (if \(i_{t}>0\)) or *attenuate* it (if \(f_{t}<1\)).  
Because \(i_{t}\le 1\), the contribution of a single time step can never be larger than the raw input itself; consequently a value that was stored early in the sequence can never be completely overwritten later – it is always “diluted’’ by the product of all subsequent forget‑gates.  
This is precisely the problem shown in the nearest‑neighbour search experiment: once a candidate vector has been written into the scalar cell, a later more similar vector can only move the cell value a little bit, never replace it.

---

### What exponential gating changes

In the sLSTM (eq. (4) of the paper) the input gate is **exponential**:

\[
i_{t}= \exp (\tilde i_{t}) \qquad (\tilde i_{t}= \mathbf w^{\top}_{i}\mathbf x_{t}+r_{i}h_{t-1}+b_{i}),
\]

and the forget gate may also be exponential (or sigmoid).  
Because \(\exp(\cdot)\) is unbounded above, a single time step can produce an *arbitrarily large* multiplicative factor for its contribution.  

To see why this does not blow up numerically, note that every gate that participates in the cell update is **renormalised** by a second state variable

\[
n_{t}=f_{t}\,n_{t-1}+i_{t},
\qquad
\tilde h_{t}=c_{t}/n_{t}.
\]

The normaliser \(n_{t}\) accumulates exactly the same weights that have been used to scale the raw contributions in \(c_{t}\).  
Thus the hidden state \(\tilde h_{t}\) is always a *weighted average* of past inputs:

\[
\tilde h_{t}= \frac{\sum_{\tau\le t} i_{\tau}\,\bigl(\prod_{\kappa=\tau+1}^{t} f_{\kappa}\bigr)\,z_{\tau}}
               {\sum_{\tau\le t} i_{\tau}\,\bigl(\prod_{\kappa=\tau+1}^{t} f_{\kappa}\bigr)} .
\]

If at some step \(i_{\tau}\) becomes very large, both numerator and denominator are multiplied by the same factor, leaving their ratio unchanged and keeping the hidden state bounded.  

The paper further introduces a **stabiliser state** \(m_t\) (eq. (5)) that subtracts a running log‑maximum from each gate before exponentiating:

\[
i'_t = \exp(\tilde i_t - m_t), \qquad 
f'_t = \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t).
\]

Because we always exponentiate numbers that have been shifted by \( -m_t\), their magnitude stays close to 1 and no overflow occurs on standard 32‑bit floating point hardware.

---

### Simple walk‑through

Consider a scalar sLSTM that stores “the most similar vector’’ in its cell.  
We feed three timesteps; let us ignore hidden‑dependence and set all recurrent weights to zero for clarity.

|   t |   raw input \(z_t\) |   pre‑activation \(\tilde i_t\) |   \(i_t=\exp(\tilde i_t)\) |   pre‑activation \(\tilde f_t\) |   \(f_t=\sigma(\tilde f_t)\) |   \(c_t = f_t c_{t-1}+i_t z_t\) |   \(n_t = f_t n_{t-1}+i_t\) |   hidden \(\tilde h_t = c_t/n_t\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | – | – | – | – | – | \(c_0=0\) | \(n_0=0\) | – |
| 1 | 0.2 |  −2 | 0.135 |  0 | 0.5 | \(c_1 = 0.5·0 + 0.135·0.2 ≈0.027\) | \(n_1 = 0.5·0 + 0.135 ≈0.135\) | \(≈0.20\) |
| 2 | 0.9 (more similar) | **2**| **7.39**|  0 | 0.5 | numerator: \(0.5·c_1 + 7.39·0.9 ≈6.66\); denominator: \(0.5·n_1 + 7.39 ≈7.46\); → \(\tilde h_2≈0.90\). |
| 3 | 0.4 (less similar) | −3 | 0.050 |  0 | 0.5 | numerator: \(0.5·6.66 + 0.050·0.4 ≈3.33+0.02≈3.35\); denominator: \(0.5·7.46+0.050≈3.73+0.05≈3.78\); → \(\tilde h_3≈0.89\). |

Explanation of what happens:

* At step 1 the gate is small (\(i_1≈0.13\)), so the cell stores a faint imprint of 0.2.
* At step 2 we deliberately make \(\tilde i_2=2\), which yields an exponential gate of about **7** – an order of magnitude larger than any previous gate.
* Both numerator and denominator are multiplied by roughly 7; consequently the hidden state becomes almost exactly the new input (≈ 0.9). The earlier value has been *overwritten* because its weight in the weighted average is now negligible compared with the huge weight contributed by step 2.
* Step 3 adds a tiny contribution; because its gate is tiny (\(i_3≈0.05\)) it does not change the average much, showing that later small gates cannot disturb an already‑dominant entry.

Notice that even though we produced an exponential gate of size 7 (and we could make it much larger), the hidden state remains in \([−1,1]\) because division by the normaliser cancels out the magnitudes.

---

### Why this solves “cannot revise storage”

In a conventional LSTM, once an item has been written with an input‑gate ≤ 1, every later update can at most *add* something on top of it; to truly erase it one would need an infinite product of forget‑gates equal to zero, which never happens in practice with sigmoids bounded away from zero.

With exponential gating, a single time step can assign an arbitrarily large weight to its candidate value; after normalisation this weight dominates all earlier contributions and thus **replaces** them in the hidden representation while keeping numerical values stable thanks to division by \(n_t\). The stabiliser state guarantees that during backpropagation we still see well‑scaled gradients, because every gate is effectively expressed as  

\[
i'_t = e^{\,\tilde i_t - m_t}, \qquad 
f'_t = e^{\,\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t},
\]

so that their product over time never exceeds about one order of magnitude in floating‑point space.

Consequently, sLSTM can “revise’’ a storage decision: when a more relevant vector appears later in the sequence it can be given a huge exponential gate, making its contribution dominate and effectively overwrite what was stored earlier—exactly what is needed for tasks such as nearest‑neighbour search where only the *best* match should survive.

The paper’s experiments (Figure 2 left panel) show this effect empirically: standard LSTMs’ error plateaus because they cannot fully discard earlier candidates, whereas sLSTM with exponential gating continues to improve as it learns to give large gates to better matches and thereby overwrites inferior ones.

--- 

**In short:** exponential gating means “the cell can weight a new input arbitrarily strongly”. The accompanying normaliser accumulates exactly those weights so that division restores bounded hidden states, and the stabiliser shift prevents overflow during training. This combination lets sLSTM replace previously stored values rather than merely diluting them—a capability missing from ordinary sigmoid‑gated LSTMs.

### What is the purpose of the normalizer state \(n_t\) in sLSTM and mLSTM, and how is it used during hidden state computation?
Question:
Both sLSTM (Eq. (3)) and mLSTM (Eq. (7)) introduce a normalizer state \(n_t\) that aggregates weighted inputs over time. The hidden state is then computed as \(\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t\) or \(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}\). I am confused about why this normalization is needed, what intuition it captures, and whether it corresponds to any known technique (e.g., attention normalization). Also, how does the normalizer interact with exponential gates to prevent overflow?
Answer:
The normalizer $n_{t}$ (or $\mathbf n_{t}$ in the matrix case) is introduced for the very same reason that the attention‑normalisation term $\frac{1}{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}}$ appears in a soft‑max: it records how much total “mass’’ the gates have deposited into the memory up to time $t$.  
In an ordinary LSTM the hidden state is obtained by applying a non‑linearity $\psi$ to the cell value, e.g. $\tilde h_{t}= \psi(c_{t})$.  When the gates are changed from sigmoid to an **exponential** activation ($i_{t}=e^{\tilde i_{t}}$, $f_{t}=e^{\tilde f_{t}}$ or $\sigma(\tilde f_{t})$), the quantities that are added to the memory can grow arbitrarily large.  Without a counter‑balance, the cell would quickly overflow, and the hidden state would be dominated by the most recent large gate values rather than by an *average* of everything that has been stored.

The normalizer solves this problem by keeping a running weighted sum of the **gate strengths**:

*scalar sLSTM*  
\[
n_{t}=f_{t}\,n_{t-1}+i_{t},
\]
so that $n_{t}$ equals $\sum_{\tau=1}^{t} i_{\tau}\prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t}f_{k}$, i.e. the total contribution of all past inputs after they have been attenuated by later forget gates.

*matrix mLSTM*  
\[
\mathbf n_{t}=f_{t}\,\mathbf n_{t-1}+i_{t}\,\mathbf k_{t},
\]
which is the same idea, but now each key vector $\mathbf k_{\tau}$ is accumulated with its own gate weight.

Because each update to the memory is multiplied by the same factor that updates $n_{t}$, dividing the memory output by $n_{t}$ (or by $\max\{|\mathbf n^{\top}_{t}\mathbf q_{t}|,1\}$ in the matrix case) yields a **gate‑normalised average** of all past contributions:

*scalar sLSTM*  
\[
\tilde h_{t}= \frac{c_{t}}{n_{t}}
          = \frac{\displaystyle
               \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} i_{\tau}z_{\tau}
               \prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t} f_{k}}
               {\displaystyle
               \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} i_{\tau}
               \prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t} f_{k}}.
\]

*matrix mLSTM*  
\[
\tilde{\mathbf h}_{t}= 
   \frac{\mathbf C_{t}\,\mathbf q_{t}}
        {\max\bigl\{|\mathbf n^{\top}_{t}\mathbf q_{t}|,\,1\bigr\}}
   = 
   \frac{\displaystyle
        \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} i_{\tau}\,\mathbf v_{\tau}
        (\mathbf k_{\tau}^{\top}\mathbf q_t)
        \prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t} f_k}
        {\displaystyle
        \max\Bigl\{\Bigl|
           \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} i_{\tau}\,
           (\mathbf k_{\tau}^{\top}\mathbf q_t)
           \prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t} f_k
         \Bigr|,\,1\Bigr\}} .
\]

Thus $n_t$ plays exactly the same role as the denominator of a soft‑max attention weight: it guarantees that hidden‐state values remain **scale‑invariant** with respect to the absolute magnitude of the exponential gates.  If all gates were small, both numerator and denominator shrink together, leaving their ratio unchanged; if some gate becomes large, its effect on the hidden state is tempered because it also inflates $n_t$.

From an intuitive standpoint this can be seen as a **running average** of what has been written into memory.  The exponential gates provide a flexible way to *focus* on salient inputs (large $i_t$) and *forget* older content (small $f_t$), while $n_t$ ensures that we never end up with an unbounded hidden representation.  

The paper explicitly mentions that exponential gating “can lead to large values that cause overflows”, and introduces a stabiliser state $m_t$ (Eq. (9)) that subtracts a common logarithmic offset from both $i_t$ and $f_t$.  This offset does not change the ratio $c_t/n_t$; it only rescales both numerator and denominator to stay within floating‑point range.  Consequently, overflow is avoided without affecting gradients, as proved in Appendix C.

In summary:

* The normalizer state accumulates **gate‑weighted contributions** so that division by $n_t$ yields a properly normalised hidden state.
* It is mathematically equivalent to the denominator of an attention weighting scheme, turning exponentially gated updates into a bounded average.
* Together with the stabiliser $m_t$, it prevents numerical overflow while preserving exact gradients, allowing exponential gates to be used safely.

Hence $n_t$ (or $\mathbf n_t$) is essential for making exponential gating tractable and for giving sLSTM/mLSTM a well‑behaved hidden‑state computation analogous to attention normalisation.

### How does the stabilizer state \(m_t\) avoid overflow when using exponential gates?
Question:
Equations (4)–(6) define a stabilizer state \(m_t = \max(\log(f_t)+m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))\) and then rescale the gates to \(i'_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)\) and \(f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t)+m_{t-1}-m_t)\). I understand this shifts the log‑values, but I don't see why this preserves the original update dynamics or gradients. Could anyone provide a step‑by‑step derivation showing that replacing \((i_t,f_t)\) with \((i'_t,f'_t)\) leaves the forward pass unchanged, and explain how gradients are unaffected?
Answer:
In the sLSTM the input‑ and forget‑gate activations are allowed to be **exponential**:

\[
i_t = \exp (\tilde{i}_t), \qquad    
f_t = \exp (\tilde{f}_t)\; (\text{or }\sigma(\tilde{f}_t)).
\]

Because an exponential can grow arbitrarily large, the product that appears in the cell update  

\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1}+i_t\,z_t
\]

may overflow the floating‑point representation after a few time steps.  
The paper follows Milakov & Nikolaev (2018) and introduces a *stabilizer* scalar \(m_t\) that keeps track of the **log‑scale** of the cumulative product of gates.  

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 1.  Definition of the stabilizer state  

\[
m_t \;=\;\max\!\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\; \log i_t\bigr).
\tag{1}
\]

If we write \(g_t = \log f_t + m_{t-1}\) and \(h_t=\log i_t\), then \(m_t=\max(g_t,h_t)\).  
Intuitively, \(m_t\) stores the logarithm of the *largest* contribution among

* the term that continues the previous cell, \(f_t c_{t-1}\);
* the term that writes a fresh value, \(i_t z_t\).

Thus \(e^{m_t}\) is an upper bound on the magnitude of both contributions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 2.  Rescaled (stabilised) gates  

Using (1) we define

\[
\begin{aligned}
i'_t &= \exp\bigl(\log i_t - m_t\bigr)
      = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t),\\[4pt]
f'_t &= \exp\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

Both \(i'_t\) and \(f'_t\) lie in \([0,1]\) because we subtract the *maximum* log‑value
\(m_t\); therefore the exponent is never positive.  No overflow can occur.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 3.  Forward‑pass equivalence  

Insert (2) into the original cell update:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t
&= f_t\,c_{t-1}+i_t\,z_t\\[4pt]
&= e^{\,m_t}\Bigl[ e^{\,\log f_t+m_{-1}-m_t}\,c_{t-1}
               + e^{\,\log i_t-m_t}\,z_t \Bigr]\\[4pt]
&= e^{\,m_t}\Bigl[ f'_t\,c_{t-1}+ i'_t\,z_t \Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]

Equation (3) shows that **the unscaled cell value** is simply a constant factor
\(e^{m_t}\) times a *scaled* cell value that uses the stabilised gates.
The same constant factor multiplies every term that later appears in the
normaliser state

\[
n_t = f'_t n_{t-1}+ i'_t,
\]

so when we compute the hidden state

\[
h_t = o_t \,\frac{c_t}{n_t},
\]

the factor \(e^{m_t}\) cancels:

\[
h_t = o_t \,\frac{e^{m_t}\bigl(f'_t c_{t-1}+ i'_t z_t\bigr)}
                     {e^{m_t}\bigl(f'_t n_{t-1}+ i'_t\bigr)}
     = o_t \,\frac{f'_t c_{t-1}+ i'_t z_t}{f'_t n_{t-1}+ i'_t}.
\]

Thus **the observable hidden state is exactly the same** as if we had used
the raw exponential gates.  The only extra quantity is the scalar
\(m_t\), which does not appear in any downstream computation.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 4.  Gradient preservation  

During back‑propagation we need derivatives of the loss \(\mathcal L\) with respect
to the pre‑activation logits \(\tilde{i}_t,\tilde{f}_t\).  
From (2)

\[
i'_t = e^{\,\tilde{i}_t-m_t}, \qquad 
f'_t = e^{\,(\log f_t)+m_{t-1}-m_t}=e^{\,\tilde{f}_t+m_{-1}-m_{t}} .
\]

Because \(m_{t}\) itself depends on \(\tilde{i}_t,\tilde{f}_t,\dots,m_{t-1}\),
the chain rule yields terms of the form  

\[
\frac{\partial i'_k}{\partial \tilde{i}_j}=i'_k(\delta_{kj}-p_k),
\qquad 
p_k = 
  \begin{cases}
    1 & \text{if } m_k=\log i_k,\\[2pt]
    0 & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
\]

and analogous expressions for \(f'_\ell\).  
These are precisely the derivatives you would obtain by differentiating
the original (unstabilised) update, only multiplied by the same cancelling
factor \(e^{-m_k}\).  Since every forward term in (3) carries a common factor
\(e^{m_k}\), **the Jacobian of the whole recurrent block is multiplied by an
identity‑scaled factor that also appears in its inverse** when forming the
gradient of later losses. Consequently the gradients with respect to all
parameters (weights feeding into \(\tilde{i}, \tilde{f}, \tilde{o}, \tilde{z}\))
are unchanged.

A more formal statement is given in Appendix C of the paper: letting
\(g(\cdot)\) denote any composition of cell updates, one can write

\[
g(i,f)=e^{m_T} g(i',f')
\]

with a scalar \(e^{m_T}\). Because loss functions depend on ratios or on final
hidden states where this factor cancels, we have  

\[
\nabla_{\theta} g(i,f)= \nabla_{\theta} g(i',f').
\]

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 5.  Why this matters  

The stabiliser does **not** alter dynamics; it merely factors out a time‑varying
scale that would otherwise cause overflow or underflow.  
All quantities that matter for inference—\(c_t, n_t, h_t,\) and ultimately the loss—remain numerically identical, while all intermediate multiplications stay safely inside
the representable range of floating point numbers.  Hence training proceeds
without numerical instabilities and with exactly the same gradients as if we
had used raw exponential gates.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
**Summary** – The scalar state \(m_t=\max(\log f _ t+m_{ t-1},\,\log i _ t)\)
captures the log‑scale of the dominant term in the gate product.
Subtracting it from both gate logs yields rescaled gates \(i'_t,f'_t∈[0,1]\)
that avoid overflow.
Because every term in the cell and normaliser updates acquires a common factor
\(e^{m_t}\), this factor cancels when computing hidden states,
and it also cancels in back‑propagation, leaving both forward activations and
gradients unchanged.  The stabiliser therefore provides numerical safety without
affecting any learning dynamics.

### Why does mLSTM use a matrix memory \(\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) instead of a vector, and how does the covariance update rule relate to classic Fast Weight Programmers?
Question:
The paper states that storing key‑value pairs as outer products \(\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top\) implements a covariance update rule optimal for maximal separability. I am familiar with Fast Weight Programmers but not with their connection to associative memory or BAMs. How exactly does the update \(\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top\) implement associative recall, and why is this more expressive than a scalar cell state? A concrete illustration with low dimensionality (e.g., \(d=2\)) would help.
Answer:
In the original LSTM the cell state is a **scalar** (or a vector of independent scalars).  
Each time step the network can only add a single number  

\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1}+i_t\,z_t ,
\]

so the memory can store at most one real‑valued quantity per hidden unit.  
When we want to retrieve a value that depends on a *key* – e.g. “store the vector
\(\mathbf v\) under the key \(\mathbf k\) and later retrieve \(\mathbf v\) with a query
\(\mathbf q\)” – a scalar cannot express the association between two
\(d\)-‑dimensional vectors.  The association needs a bilinear map
\(\mathbf q^\top\mathbf C\) that multiplies a **matrix** \(\mathbf C\) by a query.
That is why the authors replace the scalar cell with a matrix  
\(\mathbf C_t\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\).

---

### Covariance (outer‑product) update as associative memory  

Consider a classic **Bidirectional Associative Memory (BAM)**: we are given a set of
key–value pairs \(\{(\mathbf k^{(m)},\mathbf v^{(m)})\}_{m=1}^M\).  The BAM stores them
in a weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf C = \sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf v^{(m)}{\mathbf k^{(m)}}^{\!\top},
\tag{1}
\]

which is exactly an outer‑product (a rank‑one covariance) for each pair.
If we later present a query \(\mathbf q\), the retrieved vector is  

\[
\widehat{\mathbf v}= \mathbf C\,\mathbf q
      = \sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf v^{(m)}({\mathbf k^{(m)}}^{\!\top}\!\mathbf q).
\tag{2}
\]

When the query is equal to one of the stored keys, say
\(\mathbf q=\mathbf k^{(r)}\), the dot product in (2) becomes
\( {\mathbf k^{(r)}}^{\!\top}\!\mathbf k^{(r)} = \|\mathbf k^{(r)}\|^2 \),
while for all other keys it is (ideally) close to zero.  Thus the signal
corresponding to the desired value is amplified relative to the noise coming from
the other pairs – this is what Dayan & Sejnowski (1991) call **maximal separability**
or optimal signal‑to‑noise ratio.

Equation (1) is precisely the *covariance update rule* used in the paper,
except that it is made *online* by adding one outer product at a time:

\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf C_t = f_t\,\mathbf C_{t-1}+ i_t\,\mathbf v_t{\mathbf k_t}^{\!\top}\;}
    \tag{3}
\]

The forget gate \(f_t\in[0,1]\) plays the role of an exponential decay
(the “learning‑rate’’ of classic fast‑weight programmers), while the input gate
\(i_t>0\) scales how much of the new outer product is written.  When \(f_t=1,\,
i_t=1\) we recover exactly (1); when \(f_t<1\) older associations fade, which is
useful for non‑stationary streams.

Thus (3) implements **associative recall**: any past key can be retrieved by
multiplying the current matrix \(\mathbf C_t\) with a query vector.

---

### Why a matrix is more expressive than a scalar  

A scalar cell can only perform an *additive* accumulation:
\(c_t = \sum_m w_m z_m.\)  
All stored information collapses onto one dimension, so two different key–value
pairs inevitably interfere; there is no way to ask “give me the value that belongs
to key A”.  In contrast, the matrix stores a *full bilinear map*; each row of
$\mathbf C_t$ corresponds to a linear combination of values weighted by how
their keys align with the query.  Consequently, up to $d^2$ independent pieces of
information can be retained simultaneously (subject to stability constraints).

---

### Concrete illustration with $d=2$

Let us store two pairs in a 2‑dimensional system.

*Key–value pairs*  

\[
(\mathbf k_1,\mathbf v_1)=
   \Bigl(\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},
        \begin{bmatrix}a\\b\end{bmatrix}\Bigr), \qquad
(\mathbf k_2,\mathbf v_2)=
   \Bigl(\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix},
        \begin{bmatrix}c\\d\end{bmatrix}\Bigr).
\]

Assume perfect gates $f_t=1,\;i_t=1.$

1. **First update ($t=1$)**  

   \[
   \mathbf C_1 = \mathbf v_1\,{\mathbf k_1}^{\!\top}
      = \begin{bmatrix}a\\ b\end{bmatrix}
        \begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}
      = \begin{bmatrix}
          a & 0\\[2pt]
          b & 0
        \end{bmatrix}.
   \]

2. **Second update ($t=2$)**  

   \[
   \mathbf C_2 = \mathbf C_1 + 
                 \mathbf v_2\,{\mathbf k_2}^{\!\top}
      = 
        \begin{bmatrix}
          a & 0\\ b & 0
        \end{bmatrix}
        +
        \begin{bmatrix}c\\ d\end{bmatrix}
        \begin{bmatrix}0&1\end{bmatrix}
      = 
        \begin{bmatrix}
          a & c\\[2pt]
          b & d
        \end{bmatrix}.
   \]

Now we query with each key:

*Query = $k_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}$*  

\( \widehat{\mathbf v}= \mathbf C_2\,k_1 =
    \begin{bmatrix}a\\ b\end{bmatrix}= \mathbf v_1.\)

*Query = $k_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}$*  

\( \widehat{\mathbf v}= \mathbf C_2\,k_2 =
    \begin{bmatrix}c\\ d\end{bmatrix}= \mathbf v_2.\)

Thus with only two outer‑product updates we have stored two completely separate
associations and can retrieve each correctly.  A scalar LSTM could never keep both
pairs distinct because it would have had to compress four numbers ($a,b,c,d)$
into one scalar.

If we now apply decay, e.g. $f_t=0.9,\;i_t=0.9,$ after many steps older pairs are multiplied repeatedly by $0.9^n,$ automatically removing stale information—exactly what fast‑weight programmers do when they multiply the previous weight matrix by a constant before adding the new outer product.

---

### Relation to classic Fast‑Weight Programmers  

The earliest fast‑weight models (Schmidhuber 1992; Ba et al. 2016) use

```
W_{t} = λ W_{t-1} + η v_{t}k_{t}^{⊤},
```

with fixed decay $\lambda$ and learning rate $\eta$.  
The mLSTM Eq. (3) is **the same recurrence**, but $\lambda$ and $\eta$ are no longer fixed:
they are produced by learned gates $f_t=\sigma(\tilde f_t)$ or 
$f_t=\exp(\tilde f_t)$ and $i_t=\exp(\tilde i_t)$.  Consequently the network can
adapt how quickly old memories fade and how strongly new ones are written,
while still preserving the associative retrieval property proved for the classic case.

---

### Summary  

* The matrix memory enables an associative mapping between keys and values,
something impossible with a scalar cell.
* Updating it with an outer product implements exactly the BAM / fast‑weight rule,
which gives optimal separability of retrieved vectors.
* The forget and input gates turn the static fast‑weight update into a learnable,
time‑varying decay and learning rate, allowing flexible revision of stored facts.
* A tiny example with $d=2$ shows how two independent key–value pairs are stored
and later recovered without interference, illustrating why this structure is far more expressive than a scalar cell state.

### What is ‘memory mixing’ in sLSTM and how does it differ from hidden‑hidden connections in standard LSTMs?
Question:
The paper mentions that sLSTM retains hidden‑hidden recurrent connections (memory mixing) across multiple cells, whereas mLSTM abandons them for parallelizability. In standard LSTMs, recurrent weight matrices mix hidden states across cells; is memory mixing simply this same mechanism applied after exponential gating, or is there an additional operation? How does memory mixing enable solving state tracking problems such as parity or formal language tasks that Transformers cannot solve?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the original LSTM the recurrent connection from the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ to the four pre‑activations $\tilde z_t,\tilde i_t,\tilde f_t,\tilde o_t$ is carried by a set of *scalar* weights $r_{z},r_{i},r_{f},r_{o}$.  When we stack many scalar cells into a vector $\mathbf c_t\in\mathbb R^{d}$ we can also give each gate a *matrix* recurrent weight $\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$.  Those matrices mix the hidden components of the *different* cells at the next time step – this is what the LSTM literature usually calls **hidden‑hidden (or cell‑cell) recurrent connections**.

The sLSTM introduced in *xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory* keeps exactly that mechanism, but because the gates are now **exponential** (the input gate is $\exp(\tilde i_t)$ and the forget gate can be either $\sigma(\tilde f_t)$ or $\exp(\tilde f_t)$), the effect of the mixing is amplified in a controlled way.  The paper calls this amplified recurrent coupling **memory mixing**.  Concretely, for a multi‑cell sLSTM we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf z_t &= \varphi\!\bigl(\mathbf W_z \mathbf x_t + \mathbf R_z \mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf b_z\bigr),\\
\mathbf i_t &= \exp\!\bigl(\mathbf W_i \mathbf x_t + \mathbf R_i \mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf b_i\bigr),\\
\mathbf f_t &= \sigma\!\bigl(\mathbf W_f \mathbf x_t + \mathbf R_f \mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf b_f\bigr)
            \;\text{or}\;\exp\!\bigl(\dots\bigr),\\
\mathbf o_t &= \sigma\!\bigl(\mathbf W_o \mathbf x_t + \mathbf R_o \mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf b_o\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$

The recurrence for each cell’s scalar state $c^{(k)}_t$ (or, equivalently, for the whole vector of states) still follows

$$
c^{(k)}_t = f^{(k)}_t\,c^{(k)}_{t-1}+ i^{(k)}_t\,z^{(k)}_t,
$$

but now the *gate values* $i^{(k)}_t, f^{(k)}_t, o^{(k)}_t$ themselves already contain contributions from all hidden components at the previous step via the matrices $\mathbf R_\*$.  Because these contributions are exponentiated, a small change in a single hidden unit can be multiplied many times across steps – this is precisely what the authors refer to when they say that memory mixing “enables revising storage decisions”.

So **memory mixing** is not an extra operation beyond ordinary hidden‑hidden connections; it is exactly those connections, but used together with exponential gating and, when multiple heads are employed, confined to be *within* each head (no mixing across heads).  The paper stresses two consequences:

1. **State‑tracking power.**  For formal‑language tasks such as parity, balanced parentheses or any regular language that requires remembering a Boolean flag over an unbounded distance, a model must be able to **update** its internal flag repeatedly while preserving it when irrelevant symbols appear.  The exponential input gate can increase a stored flag arbitrarily fast, and the exponential forget gate can decay it arbitrarily slowly.  Because the gate values themselves are mixtures of all hidden units (the “mixing”), a single flag can be propagated through many cells without loss of precision – something that pure linear‑RNNs or Transformers (which lack any recurrent mixing) cannot do.  This explains why in Fig. 5 of the paper sLSTM solves parity whereas all Transformer‑based baselines stay at chance level.

2. **Revisability.**  In tasks like “nearest‑neighbor search” where a better candidate may appear later in the sequence, we need to overwrite an earlier stored value.  Exponential gating alone would make overwriting difficult because large values tend to dominate forever; however memory mixing allows a later cell’s hidden output to influence *all* gates of earlier cells through the recurrent matrices, effectively providing a pathway for a new decision to retroactively suppress an older one.  This is why sLSTM (with memory mixing) outperforms vanilla LSTM on that task.

In contrast, mLSTM removes those recurrent matrices altogether (see Eq. 15–20).  Its update rule

$$
\mathbf C_t = f_t\,\mathbf C_{t-1}+ i_t\,\mathbf v_t\,\mathbf k_t^\top
$$

depends only on the current input‑derived key/value vectors; there is no term involving $h_{t-1}$.  Consequently each time step can be computed in parallel once all keys, values and queries are known – exactly what the authors mean by “fully parallelizable”.  The price is that mLSTM cannot perform state tracking that needs hidden‑hidden feedback; it excels only at large associative memories.

Summarising:

* **Memory mixing = hidden‑hidden recurrent matrices applied to exponential gates**, possibly restricted inside heads when multiple heads are used.
* It provides *feedback* from $h_{t-1}$ to every gate at time $t$, enabling precise revision of stored information and long‑range Boolean state tracking.
* Transformers lack any such recurrent feedback; therefore they cannot solve parity or similar regular‑language tasks that require faithful state tracking over arbitrarily long distances.
* mLSTM abandons memory mixing for parallelism, trading away that state‑tracking capability while gaining matrix‑memory capacity.

Thus “memory mixing” in sLSTM is just the classic LSTM hidden‑hidden connections, but with exponential gating and head‑wise confinement which together give the model expressive power that Transformers do not possess.

### How do pre‑up‑projection and post‑up‑projection residual blocks differ, and why are they paired with mLSTM vs. sLSTM respectively?
Question:
Section 3.4 describes two residual block designs: (i) post up‑projection (used with sLSTM) where non‑linear summarization happens before linear expansion, and (ii) pre up‑projection (used with mLSTM) where expansion occurs first. I’m unsure why each design is preferred for its respective LSTM variant. Does pre up‑projection help exploit the larger matrix memory of mLSTM, while post up‑projection better preserves sequential information for sLSTM? Any intuition or empirical evidence would be appreciated.
Answer:
Both residual‑block designs that appear in the paper are meant to **project the hidden representation to a higher‑dimensional “embedding” space, apply a non‑linear transformation there, and then project back**.  
The only difference is *where* the linear up‑projection is placed relative to the non‑linear summarisation performed by the LSTM cell that sits inside the block.

### What the two blocks do

* **Post up‑projection (used with sLSTM).**  
  The sequence is first processed by the sLSTM in its native dimensionality (the “original space”). The sLSTM therefore aggregates the past, mixes memory cells via its recurrent weight matrices, and produces a hidden vector \(h_t\). This vector is then fed through an up‑projection matrix \(W_{\uparrow}\) that expands it to a larger dimension, a non‑linear activation (typically GELU), and finally a down‑projection \(W_{\downarrow}\) back to the original size. In symbols,
  \[
  h_t^{\text{high}} = \sigma\!\bigl(W_{\uparrow} h_t\bigr),\qquad
  \tilde h_t = W_{\downarrow} h_t^{\text{high}} .
  \]

* **Pre up‑projection (used with mLSTM).**  
  Here the input is first mapped up to the high‑dimensional space, **the LSTM cell itself works in that space**, and only after the cell’s non‑linear summarisation do we project back. Concretely,
  \[
  x'_t = W_{\uparrow} x_t,\qquad
  h'_t = \text{mLSTM}(x'_t),\qquad
  \tilde h_t = W_{\downarrow} h'_t .
  \]

Both follow Cover’s theorem: a non‑linear mapping in a high‑dimensional space makes different histories easier to separate linearly. The two variants simply differ in **whether the expansion happens before or after the recurrent summariser**.

### Why the paper pairs each design with a specific LSTM variant

1. **Memory capacity of the cell**  
   The mLSTM introduces a *matrix* memory \(\mathbf C_t\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\). Its capacity grows quadratically with the hidden dimension \(d\); consequently, when you give it a larger dimensionality it can store many more key‑value pairs (the covariance update rule). Putting the up‑projection *before* the cell lets the mLSTM operate on this enlarged hidden size, directly exploiting its larger matrix memory. If you were to keep the cell small and only expand afterwards (post‑projection), you would waste most of that capacity because the matrix memory would still be limited to the low‑dimensional cell.

2. **Sequential mixing vs. parallelism**  
   The sLSTM retains *memory mixing*: recurrent weight matrices \(\mathbf R\) connect hidden states across time steps. This mixing is inherently sequential – each step depends on the previous hidden state – and therefore it is natural to keep it in the low‑dimensional space where computational cost stays modest. After the sLSTM has performed its mixing and produced \(h_t\), we can safely expand to a high dimension just for a richer non‑linear embedding; doing so **does not affect** the sequential dynamics because all mixing has already happened.

3. **Parallelizability of mLSTM**  
   By design, mLSTM removes hidden‑hidden connections; its recurrence reduces to a *linear* update of \(\mathbf C_t\) that can be expressed as a scan and executed in parallel (see Appendix B). Because there is no hidden–hidden dependency, expanding before the cell does not introduce any new sequential bottleneck—the matrix operations are still fully parallelizable on GPU cores. Hence a pre‑up‑projection does not hurt efficiency while giving the mLSTM more room to store information.

4. **Empirical confirmation**  
   The ablation study in Section 4.2 (Table 2) shows that swapping block types degrades performance: using a post up‑projection with mLSTM or a pre up‑projection with sLSTM leads to higher validation perplexity. The authors therefore adopt **pre up for mLSTM** and **post up for sLSTM** consistently throughout all experiments (see Section 4.1 where they define “xLSTM[a:b]”).

### Intuition in one sentence

*The pre up‑projection gives mLSTM a larger hidden dimension so its matrix memory can store more key–value associations, while the post up‑projection lets sLSTM perform its necessary sequential mixing cheaply before expanding for a richer non‑linear embedding.*

### In the scaling experiments, how is the ratio xLSTM[a:b] interpreted when constructing architectures?
Question:
The notation xLSTM[7:1] is said to mean 7 mLSTM blocks and 1 sLSTM block out of 8 total blocks. However, later they mention a total of 48 blocks translating to 6 sLSTM and 42 mLSTM blocks. I’m confused about whether the ratio always refers to blocks per group or to proportion of total blocks, and how this impacts model depth and parameter count. Could someone clarify the construction rule for arbitrary total block numbers?
Answer:
The notation **xLSTM[a : b]** that the authors use in the scaling experiments is a *ratio of block types* – it tells you how many **mLSTM‑based** blocks and how many **sLSTM‑based** blocks are present **relative to each other**, not a fixed proportion of the total number of blocks.

In the paper (see §4.1, “For all experiments, we use the notation xLSTM[a:b] for the ratio a/b of mLSTM‑based versus sLSTM‑based xLSTM blocks.”) the authors give two concrete examples:

* **xLSTM[7:1]** – “out of eight blocks, seven are mLSTM‑based blocks and one is an sLSTM‑based block.”
* For a “common total block number of 48” this same ratio “translates to 6 sLSTM‑based blocks and 42 mLSTM‑based blocks.”

Both statements are consistent once you realise that the ratio **a : b** defines the *relative counts* and that the absolute numbers are obtained by scaling the pair *(a,b)* so that their sum equals the desired total number of blocks, *N*.

### How to convert a ratio to an actual architecture

1. **Write the ratio as a pair of integers** \((a,b)\).  
   For xLSTM[7:1] we have \((a,b) = (7,1)\).

2. **Compute the sum of the two numbers**  
   \(s = a + b\).  
   Here \(s = 7 + 1 = 8\).

3. **Decide on the total number of blocks** you want in the model, call it \(N\).  
   In the paper they often use \(N = 48\), but any integer is admissible (e.g. 12, 24, 96).

4. **Scale the pair \((a,b)\) by the factor \(\frac{N}{s}\).**  
   The scaling factor is an integer only when \(N\) is a multiple of \(s\); otherwise you must round to the nearest integer or choose a different \(N\) that respects divisibility.  
   For \(N = 48\):  
   \[
   \text{scale} = \frac{48}{8} = 6,
   \quad
   (a', b') = (7\times6,\;1\times6) = (42,\;6).
   \]
   Hence you obtain 42 mLSTM blocks and 6 sLSTM blocks.

5. **Place the blocks in any order** you like – typically the authors keep them grouped (all mLSTM blocks first, then sLSTM) but ordering does not affect the definition of the architecture.

### Why this matters for depth and parameters

Each **xLSTM block** contains a single recurrent cell (either an mLSTM or an sLSTM) together with a residual backbone (pre‑ or post‑up‑projection as described in §3.2). The *depth* of the model is simply the total number of stacked blocks, i.e. \(N\). The *parameter count* grows linearly with \(N\), but each block type contributes a different amount:

* An **mLSTM block** carries extra parameters for the key/value projections (\(\mathbf{W}_k,\mathbf{W}_v\)) and for handling the \(d\times d\) matrix memory; its parameter budget per block is therefore larger than that of an sLSTM block.
* An **sLSTM block** has only scalar memory and thus fewer projection matrices, resulting in fewer parameters per block.

Consequently, for a fixed total number of parameters you can obtain different effective depths by varying $a:b$. A higher proportion of sLSTM blocks yields more *blocks* for the same parameter budget (because each sLSTM is cheaper), while a higher proportion of mLSTM blocks yields fewer but more *expressive* layers.

### General construction rule

Given any desired total number of blocks $N$ and any ratio $(a,b)$:

1. Verify that $N$ is divisible by $a+b$. If not, either choose a nearby divisible value or round after scaling.
2. Compute
   \[
   n_{\text{m}} = a \times \frac{N}{a+b}, \qquad
   n_{\text{s}} = b \times \frac{N}{a+b},
   \]
   where $n_{\text{m}}$ and $n_{\text{s}}$ are respectively the numbers of mLSTM‑ and sLSTM‑based blocks.
3. Build an architecture with exactly $n_{\text{m}} + n_{\text{s}} = N$ residual xLSTM blocks, using pre‑up‑projection for every mLSTM block and post‑up‑projection for every sLSTM block (as mandated by §3.2).

If you need a non‑integer result—for example, trying to construct a 30‑block model with ratio 7:1—choose the nearest integer that keeps the overall proportion roughly constant (e.g., 27 blocks → 24 m + 3 s, or 35 blocks → 31 m + 4 s). The paper always picks multiples where division is exact, so no rounding ambiguity appears in their experiments.

In summary, **xLSTM[a:b] is a relative count**, not a percentage; you obtain absolute numbers by scaling so that their sum matches your chosen depth. This directly controls both model depth (the sum) and parameter budget (through how many expensive mLSTM vs cheap sLSTM blocks you include).

### Why do Transformers and SSMs fail on regular grammar tasks like parity, while xLSTM succeeds?
Question:
Figure 2 reports that models lacking memory mixing cannot solve parity tasks, which are regular languages requiring state tracking. How exactly does memory mixing provide the necessary mechanism to maintain deterministic state across long sequences? Is there a formal argument relating memory mixing to finite‑state automata capacity that explains this empirical gap?
Answer:
The parity task (and any regular language that requires counting modulo 2, keeping track of “even/odd’’ or similar state) can be solved by a deterministic finite‑state automaton (DFA) because the automaton only has to remember a single bit of information while it scans the sequence.  
A recurrent model can emulate a DFA if it is able to **propagate a discrete internal state from one time step to the next without being overwritten**. In the original LSTM this propagation is possible, but only when the hidden‑to‑hidden connections (the recurrent weight matrices that mix the hidden vector of step $t\!-\!1$ into the gates of step $t$) are present. The paper calls this *memory mixing*.  

### What “memory mixing’’ does

In an sLSTM (the scalar‑memory variant) each cell’s gates are computed as  

\[
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf w_i^{\top}\mathbf x_t + r_i h_{t-1}+b_i,\qquad
\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf w_f^{\top}\mathbf x_t + r_f h_{t-1}+b_f,
\]

and similarly for the input and output gates. The term $r_{\*}h_{t-1}$ is exactly the hidden‑to‑hidden connection that mixes the **previous hidden state** into the current gate activations. Because the forget gate multiplies the previous cell value $c_{t-1}$ and the input gate adds a new contribution, the pair $(c_t ,n_t)$ evolves according to

\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1}+ i_t z_t,\qquad 
n_t = f_t n_{t-1}+ i_t .
\]

If we initialise the forget gate to be close to 1 (so that $f_t\approx 1$) and let the exponential input gate produce either a very large or a very small value, the update reduces essentially to  

\[
c_t \approx c_{t-1}\quad\text{or}\quad c_t \approx c_{t-1}+z_t .
\]

Thus every time step either *keeps* the previous memory unchanged or *adds* a deterministic increment that depends on the current symbol. Because the hidden‑to‑hidden term feeds back the current hidden representation, the network can **store a logical flag** (the parity bit) in its scalar cell and keep it unchanged until a symbol that toggles parity arrives. In other words, the recurrent connection implements exactly what a DFA does: read a symbol, look up a transition table (the learned linear mapping from $h_{t-1}$ to the gates), and move to a new state.

### Why Transformers and SSMs fail without mixing

Both Transformer layers and linear‑state‑space models compute each token’s representation **independently of later tokens**; their recurrence is limited to an *output‑only* linear dynamics (the state matrix in an SSM) or to attention that mixes *query* vectors with *key* vectors but does not feed back a hidden vector into its own gate computation. Consequently there is no term analogous to $r_i h_{t-1}$ or $r_f h_{t-1}$. The state update becomes

\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,\qquad
f_t,i_t = \sigma(\mathbf W_f \mathbf x_t + b_f),\;\sigma(\mathbf W_i \mathbf x_t+b_i),
\]

which depends **only on the current input**. The forget gate can therefore not be conditioned on whether the previous hidden state encoded “odd’’ or “even’’; it will either decay all past information at once or keep it regardless of parity. This forces the network to treat every position as independent, which is insufficient for regular languages that need deterministic state tracking.

Formally, recent work cited in the paper (\citep{Merrill:24}, \citep{Deletang:23}) shows that any architecture whose recurrence can be written as  

\[
\mathbf h_t = \phi(\mathbf W \mathbf x_t + \mathbf U \mathbf h_{t-1})
\]

with **fixed (input‑independent) $\mathbf U$** is at most as expressive as a finite‑state automaton with a number of states bounded by $\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf U)$. When $\mathbf U=0$ (no hidden‑to‑hidden term), the effective number of states collapses to one, i.e. the model cannot maintain any deterministic memory beyond what is stored in fixed‐size parameters. Adding memory mixing restores a non‑zero $\mathbf U$, and because sLSTM uses exponential gates its effective transition matrix can represent arbitrary Boolean functions of the previous hidden bit, giving it full DFA power.

### Connecting to finite‑state capacity

A DFA with $S$ states can be simulated by an RNN whose hidden vector encodes a one‑hot representation of those states. Memory mixing supplies precisely this one‑hot transition: each gate computes a weighted sum of $h_{t-1}$, so for a scalar memory there are essentially two reachable values (e.g., “0’’ and “1’’ after normalisation). The exponential gating makes these two values numerically stable—large positive exponentials push one gate near 1 while suppressing the other—so learning an exact transition table becomes easy. Hence sLSTM with memory mixing is *computationally equivalent* to a DFA of size 2, enough to solve parity.

In contrast, Transformers and SSMs without such mixing are equivalent to an automaton with only one effective state; they can recognise only trivial regular languages (e.g., “all strings’’ or “empty language’’). This explains why Figure 2 in the paper shows zero accuracy for those models on parity while sLSTM (and consequently xLSTM when at least one sLSTM block is present) attains perfect performance.

### Summary

Memory mixing introduces hidden‑to‑hidden connections that let each time step condition its forget/input gates on the previously stored hidden value. Combined with exponential gating this yields a deterministic update rule identical to a DFA transition table, enabling exact tracking of binary state such as parity. Models lacking this mixing cannot condition their updates on past hidden information, so they cannot maintain any non‑trivial deterministic state and consequently fail on regular‑grammar tasks that require state tracking. This provides both an intuitive explanation and a formal link between memory mixing and finite‑state automaton capacity observed in the experiments.

### What are the computational trade‑offs of mLSTM’s matrix memory on GPU hardware?
Question:
The authors acknowledge that mLSTM’s \(d\times d\) updates are computationally expensive yet parallelizable, mentioning a ≈4× slowdown compared to FlashAttention. I would like to understand which operations dominate runtime (e.g., matrix‑matrix multiply vs. outer‑product accumulation), how memory bandwidth affects scaling with dimension \(d\), and whether there are any known tricks (e.g., low‑rank approximations) that could reduce cost without sacrificing the associative recall properties.
Answer:
The mLSTM cell replaces the scalar cell of a classic LSTM by a full **$d\times d$** “fast‑weight’’ matrix $\mathbf C_t$ and updates it with an outer‑product term
$$
\mathbf C_t = f_t\,\mathbf C_{t-1}+ i_t\,\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^{\!\top}.
$$
Because the recurrence has been rewritten in a **fully parallel** form (see Appendix B), every time step can be executed as a collection of dense linear‑algebra kernels that run independently on the GPU.  
From the implementation details given in § 4 (“Memory and Speed Considerations”) and the limitations section we can infer which operations dominate the wall‑clock time and how they scale with the hidden dimension $d$.

### What actually costs time?

1. **Matrix–matrix multiply for the readout**  
   The hidden state is obtained by a matrix‑vector product
   $$
   \tilde{\mathbf h}_t = \frac{\mathbf C_t\mathbf q_t}{\max\{|\mathbf n_t^{\!\top}\mathbf q_t|,1\}},
   $$
   i.e. a dense multiply of an $d\times d$ matrix with a length‑$d$ vector.  
   This kernel is essentially identical to a GEMV (or GEMM when batched) and therefore has a computational cost of $\mathcal O(d^2)$. On modern GPUs it is bandwidth‑limited only for very small $d$; for the typical model sizes used in the paper ($d=1024$–$2048$) it is compute‑bound.

2. **Outer‑product accumulation for the write**  
   The term $i_t\,\mathbf v_t \mathbf k_t^{\!\top}$ is an outer product, also $\mathcal O(d^2)$. In practice this is implemented as a rank‑1 update (a fused “ger” kernel). Although mathematically simple, it still touches $d^2$ memory locations each step because both $\mathbf v_t$ and $\mathbf k_t$ are dense. The authors note that this update “is computationally expensive through its $d\times d$ matrix memory and $d\times d$ update”, confirming that the outer‑product dominates memory traffic.

3. **Normaliser update**  
   The normaliser state $\mathbf n_t = f_t\,\mathbf n_{t-1}+i_t\,\mathbf k_t$ is only vector‑level ($\mathcal O(d)$) and therefore negligible.

Consequently, the **dominant runtime components are two dense $d\times d$ operations per time step**: one GEMV/GEMM for reading and one rank‑1 update for writing. Because both kernels can be launched in parallel across timesteps (the whole sequence is materialised as a batch), their cost adds linearly with sequence length but quadratically with hidden size.

### How does memory bandwidth affect scaling?

The GPU must move roughly $2d^2$ floats per token (the matrix *and* its rank‑1 increment). If we denote the memory bandwidth of the device by $B_{\text{GB/s}}$, the achievable throughput $T(d)$ obeys roughly

$$
T(d) \approx \frac{B_{\text{GB/s}}}{8\,d^2},
$$

where 8 bytes is the size of a single‑precision float. As $d$ grows, the required bandwidth quickly exceeds what even high‑end GPUs provide, so performance becomes **bandwidth bound** rather than compute bound beyond $d\simeq 1500$. Empirically the paper reports a ≈4 × slowdown compared with FlashAttention (which also performs two dense matrix multiplications per token but benefits from carefully tiled kernels and half‑precision tricks). The slowdown therefore reflects both extra memory traffic from the outer product and a less aggressive kernel implementation.

### Can we reduce the cost without losing associative recall?

The associative recall property of mLSTM hinges on storing full key–value covariances in $\mathbf C_t$. Several avenues have been explored in related work, but only low‑rank approximations preserve the key theoretical guarantee:

* **Rank‑$r$ factorisation** – replace $\mathbf C_t = \mathbf U_t \mathbf V_t^{\!\top}$ with $\mathbf U,\mathbf V \in \mathbb R^{d\times r}$. The update becomes 
  $$
  \mathbf U_t = f_t\,\mathbf U_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf v_t\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t^{\!\top}, 
  \qquad 
  \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t = \text{solve}(\mathbf V_{t-1}^{\!\top}\mathbf k_t)
  $$
  which reduces both readout ($\mathbf U_t^\top \mathbf q_t$) and write to $\mathcal O(dr)$. When $r \ll d$ this yields an order‑$d/r$ speedup. However, because compression discards part of the covariance information, retrieval quality degrades unless additional regularisation (e.g., orthogonalisation of columns) is applied.

* **Sparse or blockwise updates** – partition the matrix into independent blocks or enforce a sparsity pattern on $\mathbf C_t$. The outer product then touches only a subset of entries, cutting memory traffic roughly by the sparsity factor. This approach works well when keys are known to occupy distinct subspaces (as in block‑structured attention), but it again compromises maximal separability guarantees that underpin classic BAM theory.

* **Mixed precision / kernel fusion** – adopting half‑precision (FP16/BF16) for both storage and arithmetic cuts bandwidth by 2 × while keeping numerical stability thanks to the exponential gate stabilizer (Eq. 12). Fusion of the read/write kernels into a single CUDA kernel—as done for FlashAttention—also removes intermediate writes to global memory, shaving another factor of ~1.5–2.

The authors themselves remark in § 5 (limitations) that *“the CUDA kernels for mLSTM are not optimized, and therefore the current implementation is about 4 times slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba.”* This signals that most of the slowdown is engineering rather than algorithmic; an implementation similar to FlashAttention’s tiled shared‑memory approach would likely bring mLSTM within ≈1.5–2 × of FlashAttention even without low‑rank tricks.

### Bottom line

*The runtime per token is dominated by two dense $d\times d$ operations: a matrix–vector multiplication for retrieval and an outer‑product rank‑1 update for storage.* Memory bandwidth becomes the bottleneck as $d$ grows because each step must read/write roughly $2d^2$ floats. Current kernels give about a 4× slowdown versus FlashAttention, largely because they lack heavyweight tiling, fusion and mixed‑precision optimisations.

If one wishes to keep the associative recall guarantees while reducing cost, low‑rank factorisation of the fast weight matrix is the most principled route; it trades exact covariance storage for an asymptotic speedup proportional to $r/d$, at the price of some degradation in recall fidelity unless compensated by additional regularisation. In practice, major gains are expected from better kernel engineering (tiling, fusion, FP16/BF16), which should narrow the gap to FlashAttention without changing the underlying algorithmic properties.
