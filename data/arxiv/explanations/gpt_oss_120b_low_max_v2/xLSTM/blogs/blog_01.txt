# From LSTM to xLSTM: Why Exponential Gating Is a Game‑Changer  

## Introduction  

Long Short‑Term Memory networks (LSTMs) have been the workhorse of sequential deep learning for three decades. Their hallmark—additive “constant error carousel” updates guarded by sigmoid gates—solved the vanishing‑gradient problem and enabled everything from handwriting synthesis to AlphaStar. Yet, after the rise of Transformers, classic LSTMs began to look cramped: they can’t easily revise a decision once a value is stored, their scalar cell limits how much information they can keep, and the hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence forces strictly sequential computation.

The recent **xLSTM** paper proposes a surprisingly simple but powerful fix: replace the traditional sigmoid gates with **exponential gating**, together with a small set of stabilisation tricks. In this post we unpack what exponential gating means, why it lets recurrent models **revise past decisions**, how it stays numerically stable, and how it helps xLSTM beat Transformers and State‑Space Models on large‑scale language modeling.

---

## 1. The bottleneck of classic LSTM gates  

In a vanilla LSTM the cell state evolves as  

\[
c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t,
\]

where  

* \(i_t = \sigma(\tilde{i}_t)\) is the **input gate**,  
* \(f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\) is the **forget gate**, and  
* \(z_t\) is a transformed version of the current input.

Both gates are bounded between 0 and 1 because they are passed through the sigmoid \(\sigma(\cdot)\). This boundedness is double‑edged:

* **Pros** – it prevents runaway activations and keeps gradients well behaved.  
* **Cons** – once a large value has been added to \(c_{t-1}\) (via a big \(i_t\)), the forget gate can only *gradually* decay it because each step can at most multiply by a number ≤ 1. If later in the sequence we discover that the stored value was wrong (e.g., a nearer neighbor appears in a Nearest‑Neighbor Search task), the LSTM can’t quickly overwrite it; it can only decay it slowly, leading to high error.

In other words, classic sigmoid gates give the network **limited revision power**.

---

## 2. What does “exponential gating’’ mean?  

Exponential gating simply swaps the sigmoid non‑linearity for an **exponential** activation:

\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
f_t = 
\begin{cases}
\sigma(\tilde{f}_t) & \text{(sigmoid variant)}\\[4pt]
\exp(\tilde{f}_t)    & \text{(exponential variant)}.
\end{cases}
\]

Because the exponential function is unbounded above, its output can be *greater than* 1. This changes the update dynamics dramatically:

| Gate | Sigmoid (\([0,1]\)) | Exponential (\([0,\infty)\)) |
|------|---------------------|-------------------------------|
| Input | Can add only a *fraction* of \(z_t\). | Can add *any multiple* of \(z_t\). |
| Forget | Can only *shrink* old memory. | Can *amplify* or *shrink* old memory. |

Now a single time step can **boost** an important memory entry or **dampen** an outdated one much more aggressively than in a standard LSTM. In practice this translates to being able to “undo’’ or “reinforce’’ past decisions almost instantly.

### Visual analogy  

Imagine you’re writing on a chalkboard (the cell state). With sigmoid gating you have only a soft eraser: you can erase a little bit each line you write, but if you made a huge mistake early on you need many passes to clean it away. Exponential gating gives you a *full‑size eraser*: you can wipe out an entire region in one swipe (or make the ink bolder with an extra thick brush). The trade‑off is that you need to control how strong your eraser/brush is—otherwise you might accidentally erase everything or over‑darken the board.

## 3. Stabilising exponential growth  

Unbounded gates sound risky: if we let exponentials explode, numerical overflow or exploding gradients could cripple training. The xLSTM paper solves this with two complementary tricks.

### 3.1 Normaliser state  

Both sLSTM (scalar memory) and mLSTM (matrix memory) keep a **normalizer state** that tracks the cumulative “strength’’ of gates:

* For sLSTM:  
  \[
  n_t = f_t\, n_{t-1} + i_t.
  \]
* For mLSTM:  
  \[
  \mathbf{n}_t = f_t\, \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{k}_t ,
  \]
  where \(\mathbf{k}_t\) is the key vector used for associative retrieval.

When producing the hidden output we divide by this normalizer (or its dot‑product with the query). This effectively rescales the cell state so that its magnitude stays in a sensible range, regardless of how large individual gates become.

### 3.2 Log‑space stabiliser  

A second safeguard works in log space, inspired by Milakov & LeCun (2018). Define

\[
m_t = \max\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\, \log i_t\bigr).
\]

Then re‑express gates as

\[
i'_t = \exp(\log i_t - m_t), \qquad
f'_t = \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t).
\]

Because we always subtract the same maximum \(m_t\), both \(i'_t\) and \(f'_t\) are guaranteed ≤ 1, yet their ratio (which actually drives dynamics) stays unchanged. The paper proves that this transformation leaves forward outputs *and* gradients untouched while preventing overflow in floating‑point arithmetic.

Together these two mechanisms let exponential gating enjoy its expressive power without destabilising training.

---

## 4. How exponential gating fixes classic LSTM limitations  

### 4.1 Revising storage decisions  

The Nearest‑Neighbor Search experiment from the paper demonstrates this vividly. When scanning a sequence for the most similar vector, an ordinary LSTM keeps overwriting its cell with each new candidate but can only *fade* earlier candidates using its forget gate (which stays ≤ 1). As soon as a better neighbor appears later in the sequence, the LSTM cannot fully replace the earlier entry; error accumulates.

With exponential gating, once a superior neighbor arrives, its input gate can produce a large value (e.g., > 10), instantly dominating the cell content. Simultaneously, a large forget factor can aggressively shrink whatever was stored before. The result: near‑perfect recall even when revisions occur many steps after an initial storage.

### 4.2 Enlarged storage capacity  

In rare‑token prediction tasks (e.g., low‑frequency words on WikiText‑103), classic LSTMs suffer because each scalar cell must compress all contextual information into one number per dimension. The xLSTM’s **mLSTM** extends that scalar to an entire matrix memory \(\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\). Exponential input and forget gates control how much of each key–value pair (\(\mathbf{k}_t,\mathbf{v}_t\)) contributes to \(\mathbf{C}\). Because updates are multiplicative rather than additive bounded by 1, rare signals can be amplified enough to survive long runs of common tokens.

### 4.3 Parallelisable updates (for mLSTM)  

While sLSTM still carries hidden‑to‑hidden connections (“memory mixing’’), mLSTM drops those connections entirely: every time step’s update depends only on the current input and on \(\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\). Thanks to exponential gating controlling decay (\(f_t\)) and addition (\(i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top\)), we can rewrite all recurrences as batched matrix operations—the same trick that makes FlashAttention efficient for Transformers. Thus training and inference scale linearly with sequence length while preserving full recurrent expressivity.

---

## 5. Putting it all together – From sLSTM/mLSTM to xLSTM blocks  

The paper folds sLSTM or mLSTM cells into modern residual blocks:

* **Post up‑projection block** (used with sLSTM) first collapses past hidden states into their original dimensionality, then projects up before nonlinearity—mirroring what Transformers do.
* **Pre up‑projection block** (used with mLSTM) projects inputs *before* summarising them; this matches State‑Space Models’ strategy of operating in a high‑dimensional latent space where matrix memories shine.

Stacking these blocks with pre‑LayerNorm residual connections yields full **xLSTM architectures** that can be scaled to billions of parameters—exactly what modern language models demand.

Empirically, xLSTMs trained on just 15 B tokens already beat GPT‑3–scale Transformers (350 M parameters) on validation perplexity; scaling to 300 B tokens further narrows gaps on downstream tasks and maintains low perplexity even when extrapolating context length up to 16 k tokens—something vanilla Transformers struggle with due to quadratic attention cost.

---

## 6. Takeaway: why exponential gating matters  

* **Revision power:** Because gates are no longer capped at 1, an LSTM can *replace* old memory instantly instead of slowly decaying it.
* **Numerical safety:** Normalizer states and log‑space stabilisers keep values bounded without sacrificing expressive range.
* **Compatibility:** Exponential gating works hand‑in‑hand with other modern tricks—layer normalisation, residual stacking, matrix memories—so we don’t need an entirely new architecture.
* **Performance impact:** Experiments show consistent gains across synthetic formal-language tasks, associative recall benchmarks, long-range arena tests, and full language modelling at billions of parameters.

In short, swapping sigmoids for exponentials transforms an already solid recurrent building block into something that rivals—and in many regimes surpasses—the Transformer family.
