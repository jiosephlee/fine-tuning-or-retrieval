\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 2: Fundamentals of Low‑Bit Quantization
====================================

In recent years, the unprecedented growth of Large Language Models (LLMs) has brought the cost of training and inference to the forefront of research.  A dominant source of this cost is the extensive use of matrix multiplications performed with 16‑bit floating‑point values (FP16 or BF16).  The **BitNet** family of models demonstrates that by severely reducing the precision of the weight parameters—down to a ternary set \(\{-1,0,1\}\), which we refer to as **1.58‑bit**—one can retain the predictive power of full‑precision Transformers while dramatically cutting memory, latency, and energy consumption.  This chapter develops the theoretical foundations underlying such low‑bit quantization schemes, focusing on the concepts that directly enable the BitNet b1.58 design.

---

## Quantization basics: bits, dynamic range, and precision

A **quantizer** maps a real‑valued tensor \(X\) to a discrete set of representable values.  The size of this set is determined by the number of bits \(b\) allocated for each element; formally a \(b\)‑bit unsigned quantizer can represent at most \(2^{b}\) distinct levels.  The **dynamic range** denotes the interval \([x_{\min},x_{\max}]\) over which the quantizer provides meaningful representations.  Within this interval, **precision**—the smallest distinguishable change in value—is roughly \((x_{\max}-x_{\min})/2^{b}\).

When applied to neural network weights, reducing \(b\) shrinks both storage and bandwidth requirements:

* **Storage**: a tensor with \(N\) elements occupies \(N\cdot b\) bits instead of \(N\cdot 16\) bits for FP16.
* **Bandwidth**: transferring weights from DRAM to on‑chip memory (e.g., SRAM) scales with the same factor, directly affecting inference latency.

Nevertheless, extreme compression raises two intertwined concerns: (i) does the reduced set retain enough expressive capacity to model complex functions? and (ii) can we still compute matrix products efficiently given the new representation?  The BitNet line of work answers these questions positively for a particular ternary alphabet.

---

## Uniform quantization and clipping

A **uniform quantizer** partitions the dynamic range into equally spaced intervals and maps each real value to the centre (or boundary) of its interval.  Mathematically, given a scaling factor \(\gamma>0\), a uniform quantizer with clipping limits \([-1,1]\) can be expressed as

\[
\widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\tag{1}
\]

where  

\[
\operatorname{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max \bigl(a,\;\min(b,\;\operatorname{round}(x))\bigr)
\tag{2}
\]

and  

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|
\tag{3}
\]

is the **average absolute magnitude** (“absmean”) of a weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\).  Adding a tiny constant \(\epsilon\) prevents division by zero.  Equation (1) first normalizes each entry by \(\gamma\), thereby bringing most values into a modest range around zero; then it rounds them to one of three integers: \(-1,0,\) or \(+1\).  The subsequent clipping step guarantees that any outlier beyond \([-1,1]\) is forced back onto the nearest endpoint.

In BitNet b1.58 this uniform scheme is used for **weights**, while activations are quantized similarly but are scaled symmetrically to \([ -Q_b , Q_b ]\) per token (instead of forcing them into a non‑negative range).  The symmetric scaling eliminates the need for an explicit zero point and simplifies hardware implementations, yet empirical results in the paper show only negligible performance loss compared with more elaborate schemes.

Uniform quantization benefits from its simplicity: both forward and backward passes involve only integer operations after an initial per‑layer scaling factor is computed.  Moreover, because every weight shares the same scale \(\gamma^{-1}\), hardware can treat whole matrices homogeneously, enabling fast **bit‑wise** or **integer‑addition** kernels.

## Ternary (\{-1,0,1\}) and binary (\(\pm1\)) weight representations

The three‑valued alphabet used by BitNet b1.58 can be viewed as an extension of classical binary quantization:

| Representation | Set                | Bit budget (theoretical) |
|----------------|--------------------|--------------------------|
| Binary         | \(\{-1,+1\}\)      | \(1\,\text{bit}\)        |
| Ternary        | \(\{-1,0,+1\}\)    | \(\log_{2}3 \approx 1.58\,\text{bits}\) |

The inclusion of a zero value introduces **feature filtering**: connections whose weight becomes zero are effectively pruned during computation without any explicit sparsity mechanism.  This provides two advantages:

1. **Expressivity** – Zero allows the network to “turn off” certain pathways while still benefitting from the sign information carried by non‑zero entries.
2. **Computation reduction** – Multiplying any activation by zero yields zero instantly; thus multiplication operations are avoided entirely for those entries.

In practice, binary quantization already eliminates multiplications because multiplying by \(\pm 1\) is equivalent to copying or negating an activation—a purely sign‑flip operation achievable with cheap wiring logic.  Adding zero adds a third possible outcome: *skip* the addition entirely.  Consequently, matrix multiplication between a ternary weight matrix \(\widetilde{W}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n\times m}\) and an activation vector \(a\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\) reduces to an accumulation of signed integer additions:

```
out_i = Σ_j   (sign = + → +a_j,
               sign = - → -a_j,
               sign = 0 → skip)
```

No floating‑point multiplications appear after quantization; only integer additions remain.

## Impact of quantization on arithmetic operations (add vs. multiply)

The energy consumption model described in the paper distinguishes between three elementary operations common in LLM inference:

* **FP16 addition**
* **FP16 multiplication**
* **INT8 addition** (used for BitNet b1.58 after binarizing weights)

Empirical silicon studies cited in the work suggest that on modern process nodes (e.g., 7 nm), an INT8 addition consumes roughly two orders of magnitude less energy than an FP16 multiply–add pair.  Because BitNet b1.58 replaces *every* weight multiplication with a conditional sign flip or omission, its dominant arithmetic becomes INT8 addition.

Figure 4 in the original manuscript (referred to as Fig. 5 in implementations) shows that for a 7 nm chip:

* BitNet b1.58 saves **71.4×** arithmetic‑operations energy compared with an FP16 baseline when only matrix multiplication is considered.
* This advantage grows with model size because linear layers occupy an increasing fraction of total compute; embedding layers remain full precision but become proportionally smaller.

Thus, low‑bit quantization directly translates into lower energy per token generated—a critical metric for both data center deployment and edge devices.

---

## Energy models for different arithmetic precisions

To quantify these savings rigorously, we consider a simple additive energy model:

```
E_total = Σ_op   N_op × E(op)
```

where \(N_{op}\) is the number of occurrences of operation `op`, and \(E(op)\) denotes its per‑operation energy cost derived from silicon measurements such as those in~[energycost] and~[pokebnn].

For a conventional FP16 LLM:
* Each elementwise product inside a linear layer requires one FP16 multiply plus one FP16 add.
* Let \(N_{\text{mul}}\approx N_{\text{add}} = n\times m\times B\), where \(B\) is batch size (or token count).

Hence,
\(E_{\text{FP16}} = N_{\text{mul}}\,E(\text{FP16 mul}) + N_{\text{add}}\,E(\text{FP16 add})\).

For BitNet b1.58:
* After ternary quantization all multiplications disappear.
* Only INT8 additions remain; however each non‑zero weight still induces an add or subtract.
* If we denote by \(p_0 = P(\widetilde{W}_{ij}=0)\) the proportion of zeros (empirically observed around ~30–40% in trained models), then effective adds become
\(N_{\text{add}}^{(b)} = (1-p_0)\times n\times m\times B.\)

Thus,
\(E_{\text{b1.58}} = N_{\text{add}}^{(b)}\,E(\text{INT8 add}).\)

Because
\(E(\text{INT8 add}) \ll E(\text{FP16 mul}), E(\text{FP16 add})\),
the ratio
\(E_{\text{FP16}}/E_{\text{b1.58}}\)
matches closely with the reported factor **71.4×** for 7 nm technology.

This quantitative perspective explains why Table 5 in the paper shows that latency and memory improvements are amplified as model size grows: larger models have proportionally more linear algebra (hence more opportunities for substitution), while fixed overheads such as embeddings remain unchanged.

### Synthesis

Low‑bit quantization—particularly ternary (“1.58‑bit”) representation—offers a *dual advantage*: it dramatically reduces memory footprint while simultaneously converting expensive floating‑point multiplications into cheap integer additions or even skips altogether when weights become zero.  The BitNet b1.58 architecture builds upon these principles through a simple yet effective *absmean* uniform quantizer that enforces ternary weights during training from scratch, paired with an eight‑bit activation pipeline that avoids zero‑point complications.

Understanding these mechanisms equips researchers and engineers with intuition needed to read and extend works such as “The Era of 1-bit LLMs”.  The remainder of this textbook will leverage this foundation to explore training recipes, scaling laws, and hardware co‑design opportunities opened by ultra‑low‑precision language models.
