# Speed, Memory, Energy: The New Scaling Law for 1‑bit Large Language Models  

*How a 13 B “BitNet b1.58” can out‑perform a 3 B FP16 model, and a 70 B BitNet beats a 13 B FP16 – all while using less memory, lower latency, and far less energy.*

---

## Why “1‑bit” Matters  

Large language models (LLMs) have exploded in size, but the cost of running them grows roughly linearly with the number of floating‑point operations (FLOPs) and the amount of data that must be moved from DRAM to the accelerator. Traditional LLMs store every weight as 16‑bit floating point (FP16/BF16). The **BitNet** family takes a radical step: it reduces each weight to a *ternary* value {‑1, 0, +1}.  

Because the three values can be encoded in **1.58 bits** (the extra “0” adds a tiny amount of information), matrix multiplication no longer needs multiplications—just integer additions. The result is a dramatic drop in:

| Metric | FP16 LLM | BitNet b1.58 |
|--------|----------|--------------|
| Weight size per parameter | 2 bytes | ≈0.2 bytes |
| Dominant operation | FP16 multiply‑add | INT8 add |
| Energy per MAC (7 nm) | ≈3 pJ | ≈0.04 pJ |

That’s a **>70× reduction in arithmetic energy** for the core multiply‑add step.

---

## The New Scaling Law: Performance vs. Inference Cost  

The authors of *The Era of 1‑bit LLMs* discovered that because BitNet’s inference cost scales far slower than FP16, we can compare **different model sizes** on an equal footing of latency, memory, and energy. Their empirical curves (Figures 2‑4 in the paper) suggest three simple equivalence rules:

| BitNet size | Equivalent FP16 size (better latency & memory) |
|-------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| 13 B        | > 3 B                                         |
| 30 B        | > 7 B                                         |
| 70 B        | > 13 B                                        |

In other words, a **13 B BitNet b1.58 runs faster and uses less GPU memory than a 3 B FP16 LLaMA**, while delivering comparable perplexity and zero‑shot accuracy. The pattern holds as we go larger: a 70 B BitNet can replace a 13 B FP16 model with **4.1× lower latency** and **~4× lower memory**.

## How Does BitNet Achieve This?  

### Ternary Weights (+0 Support)  

The original pure 1‑bit models could only store {‑1,+1}. BitNet b1.58 adds a zero, which serves two purposes:

1. **Feature Filtering:** Zero weights effectively prune connections during inference, giving the model more expressive capacity without extra bits.
2. **Sparsity Benefits:** The extra state enables better alignment between the quantized weights and the underlying distribution of learned parameters.

The quantization pipeline works as follows:

\[
\widetilde{W}= \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},\,-1,\,1\right), \qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}\lvert W_{ij}\rvert,
\]

where `RoundClip` forces every entry into {‑1,0,+1}. Because we scale by the *average absolute value* (`absmean`), the ternarization preserves the overall magnitude of each matrix.

### 8‑bit Activations  

While weights are ternary, activations stay in INT8 instead of full precision. This keeps the dynamic range sufficient for transformer non‑linearities while still cutting activation bandwidth by **half** compared to FP16.

### Compatibility with LLaMA Stack  

BitNet b1.58 swaps every `nn.Linear` for a custom `BitLinear`, but otherwise inherits LLaMA’s RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and bias‑free design. This means you can drop it into popular pipelines (HuggingFace 🤗 Transformers, vLLM, or even `llama.cpp`) with minimal code changes.

## Benchmarks: Perplexity & Zero‑Shot Accuracy  

The paper trained models on **100 B tokens** from RedPajama and evaluated them on standard benchmarks (ARC‑Easy/Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ). Highlights:

- **Perplexity:** Starting at 3 B parameters, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA perplexity on WikiText2 and C4.
- **Zero‑Shot Accuracy:** Gaps shrink as models grow; at 13 B BitNet the average score is within *0.2%* of its FP16 counterpart.
- **Throughput:** On two A100 80GB cards with pipeline parallelism, a 70 B BitNet processes **~9×** more tokens per second than a 70 B FP16 LLaMA.

These results confirm that *the reduction in bits does not sacrifice quality*—it merely removes wasted floating‑point arithmetic.

## Real‑World Impact: What Does This Mean for Practitioners?  

### Faster Prototyping & cheaper inference  
If you’re running an API that serves millions of requests daily, moving from an FP16 LLM to BitNet b1.58 can cut your GPU bill by roughly **⅓** for the same latency target.

### Deployments on Edge & Mobile  
With weights stored at ~0.2 bytes each and activations at 8 bits, even a 7 B BitNet fits comfortably inside modern high‑end smartphones (≈15 GB total footprint). That opens doors for on‑device assistants that never need to talk to the cloud.

### Future Hardware Opportunities  
Because matrix multiplication reduces to integer additions only, designers can build *LPUs* (logic processing units) that omit floating‑point units entirely—think “digital ASICs for language” rather than GPUs designed for graphics.

---

## The Takeaway  

The era of “bigger is better” for LLMs is giving way to “smaller bits are smarter.” **BitNet b1.58 demonstrates a new scaling law:** as you increase model size *and* adopt ternary weights + INT8 activations, you simultaneously improve performance *and* slash inference cost dramatically.

- A **13 B** ternary model beats a **3 B** FP16 model in latency, memory usage, and energy.
- A **70 B** ternary model outperforms even a **13 B** FP16 baseline across all three metrics.
- The gains stem from eliminating multiplications and dramatically reducing data movement—the two biggest energy hogs in modern AI chips.

If you’re building next‑generation AI services—whether in cloud data centers or on edge devices—consider flipping the switch to **1.58‑bit LLMs** today. Your servers will run faster, your GPUs will stay cooler, and your carbon footprint will shrink—all without compromising on language understanding.
