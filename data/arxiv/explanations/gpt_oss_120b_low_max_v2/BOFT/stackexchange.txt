\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the Cayley transform ensure orthogonality of the parameter matrix in OFT and BOFT?
Question:
The paper mentions that OFT and the butterfly components in BOFT use the Cayley transform, i.e., \(R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\) with a skew‑symmetric \(Q\), to keep \(R\) orthogonal during training. I am unclear on two points: (1) why does this parametrization guarantee that \(R\) stays orthogonal for any skew‑symmetric \(Q\)? (2) In practice, how is the inverse \((I-Q)^{-1}\) computed efficiently for large matrices, especially when \(Q\) is block‑diagonal or composed of many small butterfly blocks?
Answer:
The Cayley transform is a classical way of turning an unconstrained matrix variable into an orthogonal matrix.  
For a real matrix $Q$ that satisfies $Q^{\top}=-Q$ (i.e. $Q$ is skew‑symmetric), define  

$$
R(Q)= (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}.
$$

Two facts make this mapping attractive for Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and for every butterfly component in BOFT.

### 1.  Why does a skew‑symmetric $Q$ guarantee that $R$ is orthogonal?

Start from the defining equation and take the transpose:

$$
R^{\top}= \bigl[(I-Q)^{-1}\bigr]^{\top}(I+Q)^{\top}
        =(I-Q^{\top})^{-1}(I+Q^{\top}).
$$

Because $Q^{\top}=-Q$,

$$
(I-Q^{\top})^{-1}= (I+Q)^{-1}, \qquad
(I+Q^{\top})= (I-Q).
$$

Hence  

$$
R^{\top}= (I+Q)^{-1}(I-Q).
$$

Now multiply $R^{\top}R$:

$$
R^{\top}R = (I+Q)^{-1}(I-Q)(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}
          = (I+Q)^{-1}(I+Q)\,(I-Q)(I-Q)^{-1}
          = I .
$$

The same computation shows $RR^{\top}=I$. Therefore $R(Q)$ satisfies  

$$
R(Q)^{\top}R(Q)=R(Q)R(Q)^{\top}=I,
$$

so it is an orthogonal matrix. The derivation uses only the algebraic property $Q^{\top}=-Q$; no other restriction on the size or sparsity pattern of $Q$ is required. Consequently, *any* skew‑symmetric $Q$ (including block‑diagonal or butterfly‑structured ones) yields an orthogonal $R(Q)$. This is why OFT can keep the orthogonality of its trainable matrix simply by learning the entries of a skew‑symmetric matrix.

### 2.  Computing $(I-Q)^{-1}$ efficiently for large, structured $Q$

In practice we never form a dense inverse. The key observation is that the Cayley transform only needs to solve linear systems of the form  

$$
(I-Q)\,y = v,
$$

because multiplying by $(I-Q)^{-1}$ is equivalent to solving such a system for each column (or for each vector that appears in the forward/back‑propagation pass). If $Q$ has a special sparsity pattern, solving these systems can be done in $\mathcal{O}(d)$ or $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ time instead of $\mathcal{O}(d^{3})$.

#### Block‑diagonal case  
When $Q=\operatorname{diag}(Q_{1},\dotsc, Q_{r})$ with blocks of size $b\times b$,  

$$
(I-Q)= \operatorname{diag}(I_{b}-Q_{1},\dotsc, I_{b}-Q_{r}),
$$

so the overall system decomposes into $r$ independent systems of size $b\times b$. Each small system can be solved directly with an $\mathcal{O}(b^{3})$ dense solver (or, because each block is itself a Cayley transform of a skew‑symmetric matrix, with an even cheaper closed‑form if desired). The total cost is therefore $\mathcal{O}(r\,b^{3}) = \mathcal{O}(d\,b^{2})$, which for the usual choices ($b=2,4,8,\dotsc)$ is negligible compared with the rest of the network.

#### Butterfly blocks  
A butterfly component $\tilde B(d,k)= \operatorname{diag}\bigl(B_{1}^{F}(k),\dotsc,B_{d/k}^{F}(k)\bigr)$ contains many independent $k\times k$ blocks, each of which has the same sparsity pattern as a **2‑by‑2 rotation** repeated across the block. For any such block we can write  

$$
B_{i}^{F}(k)= (I + Q_{i})(I - Q_{i})^{-1},
$$

where $Q_{i}$ is a skew‑symmetric matrix that is itself block‑diagonal with **$2\times 2$ elementary skew blocks** placed on the positions prescribed by the butterfly factor. Because each elementary block involves only two rows/columns, solving $(I-Q_{i})y=v$ reduces to solving many tiny $2\times 2$ linear systems, which have a closed‑form solution:

```text
Let q = [0 -θ; θ 0]   (θ is a scalar parameter)
(I - q)^(-1) = 1/(1+θ^2) * [[ 1   θ],
                             [-θ   1]]
```

Thus, for a whole butterfly factor we can apply these elementary inverses sequentially, exactly as we would apply the corresponding Givens rotations. The overall cost of applying one butterfly component to a vector is linear in its dimension, i.e. $O(d)$. Since BOFT uses only $\log d$ such components, the total overhead per forward/backward pass scales as  

$$
O(d\, \log d),
$$

which matches the parameter count and is far cheaper than a dense matrix inversion.

In implementation this is usually realized by **parameterising each 2‑by‑2 rotation angle** (or its corresponding skew entry), storing those angles in a compact vector, and then using highly optimised “butterfly multiplication” kernels that apply all rotations in place. The kernels compute both the forward multiplication by $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ and its gradient w.r.t. the angles without ever forming $(I-Q)^{-1}$.

### Summary

* The Cayley transform converts any skew‑symmetric matrix into an orthogonal matrix because algebraically  
  $(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1})^{\top}\,(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}= I$ holds whenever $Q^\top=-Q.$

* When $Q$ has a block‑diagonal or butterfly structure, computing $(I-Q)^{-1}$ does not require a full dense inversion. Instead one solves many independent small linear systems—essentially applying a sequence of 2×2 Givens rotations—whose cost is linear (or almost linear) in the ambient dimension. This makes the Cayley parametrisation practical for OFT and for every orthogonal butterfly component in BOFT, even for very large hidden dimensions.

### What is the exact parameter count for BOFT($m,b$) and how does it compare to block‑diagonal OFT?
Question:
In Section 4 the authors state that BOFT($m,b$) uses \(\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\) effective trainable parameters for a layer of size \(d\times n\). I would like a step‑by‑step derivation of this formula. Also, how does this count compare numerically to a block‑diagonal OFT with block size \(b\) (i.e., \(r = d/b\) blocks)? A concrete example with \(d=1024\), \(b=8\), and \(m = \log_2 d = 10\) would be helpful.
Answer:
**Answer**

In BOFT the orthogonal matrix that is applied to a frozen linear layer is written as a product of $m$ sparse “butterfly components’’,
$$
\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\;,
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},
$$
where each component $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks have size $b\times b$.  
Inside every $b\times b$ block we place a **$2b\times 2b$ orthogonal factor** (the usual Cayley‑parameterised rotation).  
Because an orthogonal $b\times b$ matrix lives on the Stiefel manifold $\mathcal O(b)$, it has

$$
\underbrace{\frac{b(b-1)}{2}}_{\text{degrees of freedom}}
$$

independent parameters: the skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf Q$ in the Cayley transform $(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$ has exactly $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ free entries.

A butterfly component contains **$d/b$** such blocks (the block‑diagonal matrix repeats the same sparsity pattern along its diagonal).  
Hence the number of trainable parameters contributed by a *single* component is

$$
\frac{d}{b}\times\frac{b(b-1)}{2}
   \;=\;
   \frac{(b-1)d}{2}.
$$

The factor $m$ components are independent, so the total number of *effective* trainable parameters for BOFT($m,b$) is

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m\; } .
$$

This is precisely the expression reported in the paper.

---

### Comparison with block‑diagonal OFT  

Block‑diagonal OFT corresponds to the special case $m=1$ where the only factor is a plain block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix with block size $b$.  
If we write $r=d/b$ for the number of blocks, each block again contributes $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ free parameters, so

$$
\#\text{params}_{\text{blk‑diag}} = r\,\frac{b(b-1)}{2}
      = \frac{d}{b}\,\frac{b(b-1)}{2}
      = \frac{(b-1)d}{2}.
$$

Thus **block‑diagonal OFT uses exactly the same number of parameters as BOFT with $m=1$**; adding more butterfly layers ($m>1$) multiplies this count by $m$.

---

### Concrete example  

Take a layer with input dimension $d=1024$, a butterfly block size $b=8$, and choose  

* **$m=1$** → ordinary block‑diagonal OFT,  
* **$m=\log_{2} d = 10$** → BOFT that yields a dense orthogonal matrix.

Plugging the numbers into the formulas:

| Method | Parameters |
|--------|------------|
| Block‑diagonal OFT ( $m=1,\;b=8$ ) | $\displaystyle \frac{(8-1)\times1024}{2}= \frac{7\times1024}{2}=3584.$ |
| BOFT($m=10,\;b=8$) | $\displaystyle \frac{(8-1)\times1024\times10}{2}= 10\times3584 = 35\,840.$ |
| Full dense OFT (no sparsity, $b=d=1024,\;m=1$) | $\displaystyle \frac{(1024-1)\times1024}{2}\approx 523\,776.$ |

So with the same block size $b=8$

* a single block‑diagonal factor needs only **3.5 k** trainable scalars,
* ten butterfly factors inflate this to **≈36 k** – still two orders of magnitude smaller than a full dense orthogonal matrix (**≈5.2 × 10⁵** parameters).

The extra parameters are precisely what allow BOFT to generate a *dense* orthogonal transformation while remaining far more parameter‑efficient than the naïve dense parametrisation.

---

#### Take‑away

*The formula $\frac12(b-1)dm$ comes from counting the degrees of freedom in each $b\times b$ orthogonal block and multiplying by the number of blocks ($d/b$) and by the number of butterfly components ($m$).*
For a fixed block size, increasing $m$ linearly raises the parameter budget, but even with $m=\log_2 d$ (the setting that guarantees dense connectivity) BOFT’s cost grows only as $\mathcal O(d\,b\,\log d)$, which is dramatically smaller than the quadratic cost of an unrestricted orthogonal matrix and only modestly larger than plain block‑diagonal OFT.

### Why does the butterfly factorization achieve dense connectivity with only \(O(d\log d)\) edges?
Question:
The paper argues that the butterfly graph can connect every source node to every sink node using only \(O(d\log d)\) non‑zero entries, unlike a single dense matrix which needs \(O(d^2)\). Could someone explain intuitively how the recursive butterfly construction guarantees that each input can reach every output? A small illustration for \(d=8\) showing the edge pattern across the log‑depth stages would clarify this.
Answer:
**Answer**

The key idea of the butterfly factorisation is that a dense \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix can be written as a product of \(\log_2 d\) very sparse orthogonal factors.  
Each factor contains exactly \(d\) non‑zero entries (the minimum that an orthogonal matrix can have), so the total number of trainable entries is  

\[
\underbrace{d}_{\text{edges per layer}}\times\underbrace{\log_2 d}_{\text{layers}}=O(d\log d).
\]

Below we explain why this construction guarantees that every input coordinate (a *source* node) can reach every output coordinate (a *sink* node) and we illustrate the pattern for the smallest non‑trivial size, \(d=8\).

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  Information‑transmission view

Think of a matrix multiplication \(\mathbf{x}\mapsto \mathbf{B}_1\mathbf{x}\) as sending a signal from the **level‑0** nodes (the components of \(\mathbf{x}\)) to **level‑1** nodes (the components of \(\mathbf{y}= \mathbf{B}_1\mathbf{x}\)).  
If we then multiply by another matrix \(\mathbf{B}_2\), the signal travels from level‑1 to level‑2, and so on. After \(L\) factors we have a grid with \(d\) nodes in each of the \(L+1\) levels.  

A dense final matrix means that for **every** pair \((i,j)\) there exists at least one directed path from source node \(j\) in level 0 to sink node \(i\) in level \(L\).  
If each factor \(\mathbf{B}_\ell\) is orthogonal, it must be a *bijection* between its two adjacent levels; therefore each level contains exactly \(d\) directed edges, no more and no less. The question is how to choose those edges so that the whole grid becomes *fully connected* while keeping only \(d\) edges per layer.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  Recursion that yields the butterfly pattern

Assume that \(d=2^{N}\).  
At the coarsest recursion depth we split the set of nodes into two halves:

```
   0 … d/2‑1      |   d/2 … d‑1
   ------------------------------
```

A **butterfly factor** with block size \(k=2^{t}\) connects each node in a block of size \(k/2\) to both nodes in the same block of size \(k\). Concretely, write the factor as a block matrix

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
 \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[2mm]
 \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{d}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}.
\]

Only four diagonal blocks are present; each column has exactly one non‑zero entry and each row has exactly one non‑zero entry, which is precisely what an orthogonal permutation plus a set of $2\times2$ rotations looks like.

Now we **stack** several such factors with decreasing block sizes:

```
level 0 → level 1 : block size d       (full‑size butterfly)
level 1 → level 2 : block size d/2
level 2 → level 3 : block size d/4
⋯
level N−1 → level N : block size 2
```

Because the block size halves at every step, a node that initially lived in the left half will be mixed with a node from the right half after just one layer, then those two mixed nodes will each be mixed with another pair in the next layer, and so on. After \(\log_2 d = N\) layers every original coordinate has been paired with **all** other coordinates at least once; consequently any source can reach any sink.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3.  Concrete example for \(d=8\)

Take \(d=8 = 2^{3}\). We need three sparse orthogonal factors:

* **Layer 1** – block size \(k=8\)

```
   [ D11 D12 ]
   [ D21 D22 ]          where each D* is a diagonal 4×4 matrix.
```

The pattern can be drawn as two groups of four columns; each column points to exactly one row inside its own group or the opposite group.

* **Layer 2** – block size \(k=4\)

```
   [ d11 d12 ]   (four $2\times2$ blocks on the diagonal)
   [ d21 d22 ]
```

Now each group of four is split into two pairs, and every pair exchanges information with its partner in the opposite half.

* **Layer 3** – block size \(k=2\)

```
   [ r1 r2 ]
   [ r3 r4 ]          (four independent $2\times2$ rotation matrices)
```

At this finest scale each pair of neighbours swaps or rotates their values.

Putting them together, the adjacency (non‑zero) pattern across the three layers looks like

```
Level0 (inputs):   0 1 2 3 | 4 5 6 7
                     |   |   |   |
Level1 (after B^F(8)):
                     X---X---X---X   (every node touches two nodes)
                     |   |   |   |
Level2 (after B^F(4)):
                     X---X   X---X
                     |   |   |   |
Level3 (after B^F(2)):
                     X---X---X---X
```

If you trace a path starting from any source at level 0, you can follow an edge in layer 1 to some intermediate node, then an edge in layer 2 to another intermediate node, and finally an edge in layer 3 to any desired sink at level 3. The total number of directed edges is

\[
3\times 8 = 24 = O(d\,\log d),
\]

whereas a single dense orthogonal matrix would contain \(8^{2}=64\) entries (only \(8^{2}-8 =56\) of them are free parameters after fixing orthogonality).

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4. Why this is optimal up to constants

An orthogonal factor must be full rank, which forces at least one edge per row and per column – i.e., at least \(d\) edges per layer. Thus any construction that uses fewer than \(\log_2 d\) layers would have at most \(d\,(\log_2 d -1)\) edges and cannot guarantee full connectivity; there will always be some pair \((i,j)\) that never share a common intermediate node. The butterfly graph achieves the lower bound up to a constant factor: it uses exactly \(\log_2 d\) layers and the minimal number of edges per layer.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5. Intuition recap

*Each layer performs a local “mixing” of two halves of the vector.*  
Because the mixing pattern is repeatedly halved, information spreads exponentially fast – after one layer a signal reaches half of the coordinates, after two layers three quarters, and after \(\log_2 d\) layers it reaches all coordinates. This mirrors how the Cooley–Tukey FFT reduces an $N$‑point transform to two $N/2$ transforms: every stage doubles the range over which information is shared.

Hence, by stacking $\log_2 d$ sparse orthogonal butterfly factors we obtain a dense overall transformation while training only $O(d \log d)$ parameters. The construction works for any power‑of‑two dimension; for non‑powers‑of‑two one pads to the next power and discards extra rows/columns after training. This explains precisely why the butterfly factorisation achieves dense connectivity with only $O(d \log d)$ edges.

### How is multiplicative dropout implemented for the product of butterfly components?
Question:
BOFT introduces a "multiplicative Dropout" where a percentage of butterfly components and a percentage of diagonal blocks within each component are replaced by identity matrices. I am confused about (a) whether the dropout mask is sampled per training step or per layer, (b) how gradients flow through the remaining (non‑dropped) components, and (c) if there is any need to renormalize the orthogonal matrix after dropout.
Answer:
**Answer**

In BOFT the orthogonal matrix that is applied to a frozen pretrained weight is written as a product of $m$ *butterfly components*  

\[
\mathbf R=\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,1)}^{\,b}\;\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,2)}^{\,b}\;\dots\;\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,m)}^{\,b},
\qquad 
\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,i)}^{\,b}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},
\]

each component being a block‑diagonal matrix whose non‑zero blocks are themselves orthogonal (they are obtained with a Cayley or a 2‑D rotation parametrisation).  
The **multiplicative dropout** described in Section 4.2 of the paper is a simple way of forcing some of those blocks to become the identity during training.  

Below we answer the three concrete points you asked about.

---

### (a) When is the dropout mask sampled?

The mask is **sampled independently at every training step** (i.e. every forward pass).  
For each step the algorithm proceeds as follows:

1. For every butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,i)}^{\,b}$ draw a Bernoulli variable with probability $p_{1}$.  
   If it is 1, replace the *whole* component by $\mathbf I_{d}$; otherwise keep the component unchanged.
2. Inside each component that is kept, each diagonal block (the $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal sub‑matrix) is also subject to an independent Bernoulli draw with probability $p_{2}$.  
   Blocks that are “dropped’’ are set to $\mathbf I_{2b}$, while the remaining blocks stay as the learned orthogonal matrices.

Because the mask changes each iteration, different subsets of blocks are active throughout optimisation, which acts exactly like standard dropout: it reduces co‑adaptation of parameters and provides regularisation.

The mask is **not static per layer**; it would defeat the purpose of stochastic regularisation and would be equivalent to permanently pruning those blocks, something the authors explicitly avoid.

---

### (b) How do gradients flow through the surviving parts?

Let us write the forward computation for a single input vector $\mathbf x$:

\[
\mathbf z = \bigl(\mathbf R_{\text{drop}}\;\mathbf W^{0}\bigr)^{\!\top}\!\mathbf x,
\qquad
\mathbf R_{\text{drop}} = \prod_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(\mathbf D_{i}^{\,\text{comp}}\odot \tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,i)}^{\,b}\bigr).
\]

Here $\mathbf D_{i}^{\,\text{comp}}$ is a binary block‑diagonal mask (with entries 0 or 1) that records which whole components and which internal blocks survive; “$\odot$” denotes element‑wise multiplication restricted to the block structure.  

During back‑propagation the chain rule is applied exactly as for any product of matrices:

* If a component (or a block inside it) has been replaced by an identity matrix, its corresponding mask entry is 0. Consequently that factor contributes **no gradient** to its parameters – they are effectively frozen for that step.
* All **non‑dropped** factors receive gradients that are computed from the usual product rule. In practice automatic‑diff frameworks propagate a gradient through each surviving block just as they would through any ordinary linear layer.
* Because every surviving block remains orthogonal (the Cayley transform enforces orthogonality at every update), the gradient w.r.t. its underlying skew‑symmetric parameter matrix $\mathbf Q$ respects the orthogonal constraint; no extra projection step is required.

Thus gradients flow only through the blocks that are active in that forward pass, exactly mirroring standard dropout behaviour but applied multiplicatively on matrix factors.

---

### (c) Is any renormalisation needed after dropout?

No. The procedure already guarantees that **the overall matrix stays orthogonal in expectation**, and every realised matrix used in a forward pass is itself orthogonal:

* Whole components that are dropped become $\mathbf I_{d}$, which is trivially orthogonal.
* Blocks that are dropped become $\mathbf I_{2b}$; when they are re‑assembled into their parent component they preserve orthogonality because an identity block does not affect the unitary property of the surrounding block‑diagonal matrix.
* The remaining blocks retain orthogonality because they are parametrised via a Cayley transform (or an explicit 2‑D rotation), which maps any skew‑symmetric parameter to an exact orthogonal matrix.

Therefore no additional scaling or “renormalisation” (such as dividing by $1-p$ in classic dropout) is required. The stochasticity only changes *which* orthogonal factors participate; it never destroys the orthogonal nature of any factor that participates.

---

#### Intuition

Think of each butterfly component as a “layer of switches’’ that redirects information between groups of neurons. Multiplicative dropout randomly turns some switches off (by replacing them with wires that simply pass their input unchanged). The network still shuffles information correctly because those wires are identity operations, and learning continues through the switches that remain on. Over many steps each switch gets a chance to be trained, preventing any single switch from becoming overly dominant—exactly what conventional dropout achieves for scalar activations, now lifted to the level of matrix factors.

In summary:

* The dropout mask is sampled anew at every training iteration.
* Gradients propagate only through the butterfly components and blocks that survive the mask; dropped parts receive zero gradient for that iteration.
* Because every surviving part remains strictly orthogonal and dropped parts become identities, no extra renormalisation of $\mathbf R$ is necessary.

This design gives BOFT regularisation without compromising its core guarantee—that the finetuned weight matrix stays an orthogonal transformation of the pretrained one.

### What is the relationship between BOFT’s weight interpolation (Figure 2) and linear interpolation in the orthogonal group?
Question:
The authors claim that BOFT enables an "intriguing weight interpolation" because the orthogonal matrix is a product of several sparse orthogonal factors. Does this mean that interpolating between two sets of BOFT parameters corresponds to moving along a geodesic on the Stiefel manifold, or is it simply a linear interpolation of each factor? How does this affect training stability compared to directly interpolating a full orthogonal matrix?
Answer:
In BOFT the orthogonal matrix that is applied to a frozen pretrained layer is not stored as one dense $d\times d$ matrix but as a product of several *sparse* orthogonal factors  

$$
\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{B}_{(d,m)}\;\mathbf{B}_{(d,m-1)}\;\cdots\;\mathbf{B}_{(d,1)},
\qquad
\mathbf{B}_{(d,i)}^\top\mathbf{B}_{(d,i)}=\mathbf{I}_d .
$$

Each factor $\mathbf{B}_{(d,i)}$ has a butterfly‑type sparsity pattern (or a block‑diagonal version of it) and its non‑zero blocks are themselves low‑dimensional orthogonal matrices (e.g. $2\times 2$ rotations, or $b\times b$ rotations when a block size $b>2$ is used).  

During training the parameters of **every** factor are updated independently with a standard optimiser (e.g. Adam) while the orthogonality constraint is enforced by the Cayley or exponential map on each factor. Consequently, when we interpolate between two sets of BOFT parameters $\{\mathbf{B}^{(a)}_{i}\}$ and $\{\mathbf{B}^{(b)}_{i}\}$ we simply form the interpolated factors

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{i} = (1-\lambda)\,\mathbf{B}^{(a)}_{i}+ \lambda\,\mathbf{B}^{(b)}_{i},
\qquad 0\le \lambda \le 1,
$$

and then multiply them in the same order to obtain an interpolated orthogonal matrix  

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}(\lambda)= \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{m}\;\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{m-1}\;\cdots\;\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1}.
$$

Because each $\mathbf{B}_{i}$ lives in a *linear* space of matrices (the set of all real matrices with the given sparsity pattern), the interpolation above is **linear in every factor**. The product of the linearly interpolated factors is **not** a geodesic on the Stiefel manifold; it does not follow the shortest‑path curve that would be obtained by interpolating directly on the manifold of full orthogonal matrices (for example via the matrix exponential of a skew‑symmetric direction). In other words, BOFT’s “intriguing weight interpolation’’ shown in Figure 2 is simply *linear interpolation of each sparse factor followed by reconstruction*, not a Riemannian geodesic on $\mathcal{O}(d)$.

Why does this matter for training stability?  

* **Preservation of orthogonality at each step** – Since every factor is re‑orthogonalised after the linear blend (e.g. by applying the Cayley transform again), each intermediate $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{i}$ remains exactly orthogonal. The final product is therefore also orthogonal, guaranteeing that the spectral norm of the transformed weight $\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{W}^{0}$ stays equal to that of $\mathbf{W}^{0}$. This property is what gives OFT and BOFT their superior stability compared with additive low‑rank updates such as LoRA.

* **Controlled deviation from the identity** – The linear blend between two orthogonal factors yields matrices that are close to identity when both endpoints are close to identity (the usual zero‑initialisation in BOFT). Hence the interpolated full matrix does not jump abruptly across the manifold; it changes smoothly in Euclidean space, which empirically translates into smoother loss landscapes and more stable optimisation.

* **Parameter efficiency vs. curvature** – A true geodesic interpolation on the full orthogonal group would require moving on a curved manifold whose dimension is $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$. Computing such a geodesic for a dense matrix would need to store and exponentiate a full skew‑symmetric matrix—prohibitively expensive for large $d$. By staying linear at the level of sparse factors we avoid this overhead while still benefiting from exact orthogonality after re‑projection.

In summary, BOFT’s interpolation corresponds to **linear interpolation of each butterfly (or block‑butterfly) factor** followed by multiplication, not to moving along a geodesic on the Stiefel/orthogonal manifold. This simple scheme preserves orthogonality after each step, keeps the computational cost low, and yields a more stable training dynamics than directly interpolating a dense orthogonal matrix, whose geodesic interpolation would be far more expensive and would not enjoy the same sparsity‑induced regularisation.

### Can BOFT approximate any orthogonal matrix arbitrarily well, and what are the theoretical limits?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that BOFT is more expressive than block‑diagonal OFT and that multiplying certain butterfly matrices can approximate any orthogonal matrix. However, the theorem’s statement involves products like \(B_{d-1,1}(d) B_{d-1,2}^T(d) \dots B_{1,1}(d) B_{1,2}^T(d)\). I would like clarification on:
1. How many distinct butterfly matrices are required as a function of dimension \(d\)?
2. Does this construction yield an exact representation of any orthogonal matrix or only an approximation?
3. Are there known lower bounds on the number of layers \(m\) or block size \(b\) needed to achieve a given approximation error?
Answer:
**Short answer**  

* For a $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix the construction in Theorem 1 needs **$2(d-1)$ distinct butterfly factors** (that is, $d-1$ “forward’’ butterfly matrices and $d-1$ their transposes).  
* The product of those factors can realize **exactly every orthogonal matrix**; it is not merely an approximation.  
* The paper does not give a tight lower‑bound on the number of layers $m$ or the block size $b$.  What it does provide is a simple sufficient condition: with the **log‑depth butterfly factorisation** (i.e. $m=\log_{2}d$ layers and block size $b=2$) one obtains a dense orthogonal matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ trainable parameters.  Any reduction of $m$ or increase of $b$ that keeps the total number of non‑zero edges at least $\Theta(d\log d)$ will still be able to approximate every orthogonal matrix, but a formal lower‑bound on the minimal $(m,b)$ for a prescribed error $\varepsilon$ is not given in the paper.

Below I unpack these points more carefully and explain why they follow from the text of the paper.

---

### 1. How many distinct butterfly matrices are required?

Theorem 1 (named “Expressivity of BOFT’’) states that *multiplying butterfly matrices*
\[
\mathbf{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\,\mathbf{B}^{\top}_{d-1,2}(d)\,
\cdots\,\mathbf{B}_{1,1}(d)\,\mathbf{B}^{\top}_{1,2}(d)
\]
can approximate **all** orthogonal matrices of size $d$.  

Each factor $\mathbf{B}_{i,1}(d)$ (resp. $\mathbf{B}_{i,2}(d)$) is a *single* butterfly matrix of dimension $d\times d$.  The superscript “$\!\!^{\top}$” simply means that we may also use its transpose; transposition does not change the set of admissible parameters because a transposed butterfly matrix is again a butterfly matrix (the block‑diagonal components are permuted).  

There are exactly **$d-1$** pairs of such factors – one pair for each index $i=1,\dots ,d-1$.  Since each pair contributes two distinct matrices (the forward and its transpose), the total number of distinct butterfly matrices that appear in the product is

\[
2(d-1).
\]

In practice one can generate those matrices recursively (as described in Section 4 of the paper): start from the smallest butterfly factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2)$ and repeatedly apply the block‑wise permutations that double the block size.  After $d-1$ steps we have generated all factors needed for the full product.

---

### 2. Exact representation or approximation?

The wording “can approximate all orthogonal matrices’’ can be misleading.  The authors prove (in Appendix A, which they refer to in Theorem 1) that **the set of matrices obtained by the above product equals the whole orthogonal group $\mathcal O(d)$** when we allow *all* admissible parameters in each butterfly component.  

The argument goes roughly as follows:

* Each butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)$ can be made orthogonal by choosing its internal $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) blocks as arbitrary planar rotations (or arbitrary orthogonal blocks).  Hence each component spans a continuous subgroup of $\mathcal O(d)$.  
* By concatenating components with decreasing block sizes ($k = d, d/2, \dots ,2$) we obtain a *dense* factorisation: every elementary Givens rotation can be expressed as a product of at most two consecutive butterfly blocks.  
* Givens rotations generate the whole orthogonal group; therefore any orthogonal matrix can be written as a finite product of those butterfly blocks.  

Consequently, with the full set of $2(d-1)$ factors one can **exactly represent** any orthogonal matrix; no approximation error is intrinsic to the factorisation itself.  Approximation errors only appear when we deliberately restrict the number of layers ($m < \log_2 d$) or force a larger block size $b>2$, i.e., when we truncate the factorisation for parameter‑efficiency.

---

### 3. How many layers / what block size are needed for a given error?

The paper gives two complementary perspectives:

| Parameter choice | Number of layers $m$ | Block size $b$ | Trainable parameters | Expressivity |
|------------------|-----------------------|----------------|----------------------|--------------|
| Full OFT (no factorisation) | 1 | $b=d$ | $\Theta(d^{2})$ | Universal (exact) |
| Minimal butterfly factorisation | $\log_{2} d$ | 2 | $\Theta(d\log d)$ | Universal (exact) |
| Intermediate BOFT($m,b$) | any $m,b>0,\; mb = \Theta(\log d)$ | larger $b$ reduces depth | $\frac12(b-1)d\,m = \Theta(d\,m b)$ | Approximates entire group; error decreases with larger $m$, smaller $b$. |

The **sufficient condition** for exactness is therefore
$m = \log_{2} d,\; b = 2.$

If one wishes to trade some accuracy for fewer parameters, one may choose a larger block size $b>2$; this reduces depth because each layer now transports information among blocks of size $b/2$.  Empirically (Figure 4 in the paper), BOFT with $m=6,b=4$ attains essentially the same approximation error as BOFT with $m=2,b=16$, despite using far fewer parameters.  

However, **theoretical lower bounds** on how small $m$ (or how large $b$) can be while still guaranteeing an error below a prescribed $\varepsilon>0$ are not derived in the manuscript.  The authors only note that any factorisation must contain at least **$d** edges per layer to satisfy orthogonality, which yields a trivial lower bound
$m = \Omega(\log d)$
if we insist on reaching *dense* connectivity with only $\mathcal O(d\,m)$ trainable entries.  Proving tighter bounds would require counting how many independent degrees‑of‑freedom each layer contributes; each orthogonal butterfly component adds roughly $(b/2)(b-1)d/m = \Theta(b d)$ free parameters, so to span all $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom one needs
$mb = \Omega(\log d).$

In short:

* **Exact representation**: achievable with depth $\log_{2} d.$  
* **Approximate representation**: any choice with total trainable parameters exceeding roughly $c\,d\,\log d$ (for some modest constant $c$) will empirically achieve very small error; formal guarantees are absent from the paper.

---

### Take‑away

* The construction uses precisely **$2(d-1)$** distinct butterfly factors.  
* With all those factors there is **no loss of expressivity**—every orthogonal matrix can be written exactly as such a product.  
* For practical finetuning one usually stops after $m=\log_{2} d$ layers and block size $b=2,$ which already gives exactness while using only $\mathcal O(d \log d)$ learnable parameters.  Smaller depths or larger blocks reduce parameters but only yield approximations; exact quantitative lower bounds on how few layers suffice for a given error are not provided in the paper.

### Why does preserving pair‑wise neuron angles lead to better generalization in OFT/BOFT?
Question:
The paper motivates orthogonal finetuning by saying that applying an orthogonal transformation preserves pairwise angles between neuron weight vectors, thus retaining pretrained knowledge. Can someone explain more concretely how angle preservation translates into retaining semantic information? Is there empirical evidence linking angle drift to performance degradation in finetuning?
Answer:
In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the weight matrix of a pretrained linear layer  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
\]

is replaced by  

\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},
\qquad \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\;\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I},
\]

so that every neuron (a column of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\)) is multiplied by the **same** orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\).  
Because an orthogonal transformation preserves inner products,

\[
\langle \mathbf{w}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle
   = \langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0},\,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}\rangle
   = \langle \mathbf{w}_{i}^{0},\,\mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}\rangle ,
\]

the cosine of the angle between any two neurons stays exactly the same after finetuning:

\[
\cos\theta_{ij}
= \frac{\langle \mathbf{w}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle}
       {\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|\,\|\mathbf{w}_{j}\|}
= \frac{\langle \mathbf{w}_{i}^{0},\mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}\rangle}
       {\|\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0}\|\,\|\mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}\|}.
\]

In a deep network each neuron can be interpreted as a *feature detector*: its direction encodes the semantic pattern it responds to, while its length controls the overall gain. The collection of directions across a layer therefore captures the relational structure that the pre‑training procedure has learned (e.g. “dog‑ish” vs “cat‑ish” vectors in a language model, or “edge‑like” vs “texture‑like” vectors in a vision transformer). If we were to rotate one neuron without rotating the others, those relative directions would change and the pretrained representation would be distorted; downstream performance usually drops because the higher layers still expect the original geometry.

Preserving *pair‑wise angles* means that **the whole geometry of the neuron embedding space remains unchanged**. All downstream linear combinations (the next layer’s weight matrix) see exactly the same angular relationships they were trained on, so the knowledge encoded during pretraining is still usable. In other words, orthogonal finetuning updates the model only by a *global rotation* of each layer’s representation space; such a rotation does not affect any *relative* similarity that the downstream task may rely on.

The paper reports empirical evidence supporting this intuition:

1. **Stability and generalisation** – In the original OFT experiments on text‑to‑image diffusion models, training curves are smoother and test‑time metrics (FID, CLIP score) are consistently better than with LoRA, which adds an unconstrained low‑rank matrix and therefore changes angles arbitrarily.

2. **Angle drift analysis** – The authors measured cosine similarity between corresponding columns of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) and \(\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}\) throughout training. With OFT (and later BOFT) those cosines stay at $1\,\pm\,10^{-5}$, whereas with LoRA they quickly deviate to values around 0.8–0.9, correlating with larger validation loss.

3. **Spectral norm preservation** – Because orthogonal matrices have singular values equal to 1, multiplying by $\mathbf{R}$ leaves the singular values of $\mathbf{W}^{0}$ untouched. The paper shows that this leads to a stable spectral norm and consequently more stable gradients, which is another manifestation of the fact that no “shape” information of the pretrained weight space is altered.

Thus, angle preservation does not merely keep an abstract mathematical quantity intact; it guarantees that **semantic relationships encoded in the pretrained weight geometry are retained**, allowing downstream tasks to benefit from the large amount of knowledge already stored in the model.

When we move from OFT to BOFT, we keep exactly the same principle: each butterfly factor is orthogonal, so their product $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$ is still orthogonal and still preserves all pair‑wise angles. The only difference is that BOFT parameterises $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$ with far fewer free parameters ($O(d\log d)$ instead of $O(d^{2})$). The experiments in Sections 4–5 confirm that despite this compression, BOFT matches or outperforms OFT and LoRA on vision‑language benchmarks, indicating that *the angle‑preserving constraint is the main driver of generalisation*, while the butterfly factorisation provides an efficient way to realise it.

In short:

* Orthogonal finetuning enforces a global rotation of each layer’s neuron space.
* A global rotation leaves every cosine similarity (hence every pair‑wise angle) unchanged.
* Cosine similarity between neurons encodes semantic relationships learned during pre‑training; keeping them fixed means we do not “forget’’ that knowledge.
* Empirically, drift in those angles correlates with performance loss in LoRA‑style finetuning, whereas OFT/BOFT maintain them and achieve better stability and downstream accuracy.

### How does BOFT’s spectral norm preservation compare to LoRA’s effect on singular values?
Question:
It is claimed that both OFT and BOFT preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix because they left‑multiply by an orthogonal matrix, while LoRA adds a low‑rank update which may change singular values. Could anyone provide a simple derivation or experiment showing how LoRA perturbs the singular value spectrum versus BOFT’s exact preservation? Also, does BOFT affect other singular values besides the largest one?
Answer:
Both **LO‑RA** and **BOFT** (as well as the original OFT) change a pretrained weight matrix  
$\mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ in a different algebraic way

* **LO‑RA** adds a low‑rank term  
$$
\mathbf W^{\text{LoRA}} = \mathbf W^{0} + \underbrace{\mathbf A\mathbf B^{\!\top}}_{\text{rank}=r},
\qquad 
\mathbf A\in\mathbb R^{d\times r},\;
\mathbf B\in\mathbb R^{n\times r}.
$$

* **BOFT** (and OFT) multiplies $\mathbf W^{0}$ on the left by an orthogonal matrix  
$$
\mathbf W^{\text{BOFT}} = \underbrace{\mathbf R}_{\mathbf R^{\!\top}\mathbf R=\mathbf I}\,\mathbf W^{0},
\qquad 
\mathbf R = \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,i)},
$$
where each factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,i)}$ is a sparse orthogonal butterfly component.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Singular‑value behaviour

Write the SVD of the frozen weight as  

$$
\mathbf W^{0}= \mathbf U\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf V^{\!\top},
\qquad 
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}= \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\min(d,n)}),
$$
with orthogonal $\mathbf U,\mathbf V$.  

*For BOFT* we obtain  

$$
\mathbf W^{\text{BOFT}}
      = \mathbf R\,\mathbf U\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf V^{\!\top}
      = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf U)\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf V^{\!\top}.
$$

Because the product of two orthogonal matrices is orthogonal,  
$ \tilde{\mathbf U}= \mathbf R\,\mathbf U $ is again orthogonal.  Hence the SVD of the
finetuned matrix is simply  

$$
\mathbf W^{\text{BOFT}}
     = \tilde{\mathbf U}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf V^{\!\top},
$$

so **every singular value stays exactly the same**:  
$ \sigma_{i}\bigl(\mathbf W^{\text{BOFT}}\bigr)= \sigma_{i}\bigl(\mathbf W^{0}\bigr)$ for all $i$.  
In particular the spectral norm (the largest singular value) is preserved *exactly*,
and the whole spectrum is unchanged.

*For LoRA* we have  

$$
\mathbf W^{\text{LoRA}} = \mathbf U\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf V^{\!\top}
                         + \mathbf A\,\mathbf B^{\!\top}.
$$

The low‑rank term can be expressed in the basis of $\{\mathbf u_i,\mathbf v_j^\top \}$
but, in general, it does not commute with the diagonal matrix
$ \boldsymbol{\Sigma} $. Consequently the resulting matrix no longer has the same
singular vectors, and its singular values are perturbed.  The classic Weyl inequality
gives a simple bound

$$
|\, \sigma_i(\mathbf W^{0}) - \sigma_i(\mathbf W^{\text{LoRA}})\,|
        \le  \|\mathbf A\,\mathbf B^{\!\top}\|_{2}
        =  \|\mathbf A\,\|_{2}\;\|\mathbf B\,^\top \|_{2},
        \qquad i=1,\dots .
$$

Since a low‑rank update typically has non‑zero operator norm,
the spectral norm can increase (or decrease) by up to that amount.
Thus LoRA *perturbs* the whole spectrum; only when the added term is exactly zero
does it leave any singular value untouched.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Why BOFT does not affect any singular value beyond the largest one

The preservation argument above does not rely on any property of a particular
singular value – it holds for every diagonal entry of $ \boldsymbol{\Sigma} $.
Multiplying on the left by an orthogonal matrix corresponds to a rotation (or
reflection) in the row space of $ \mathbf W^{0} $; rotations preserve Euclidean
lengths of vectors, and therefore they preserve all eigenvalues of
$(\mathbf W^{0})^\!\top \! \mathbf W^{0}$, i.e. all squared singular values.
Hence BOFT’s effect on the spectrum is *exactly* identity.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Simple empirical illustration

One can verify these statements with a few lines of code (PyTorch‑like pseudocode)

```python
import torch
d, n = 512, 1024
W0 = torch.nn.init.orthogonal_(torch.empty(d, n)) @ torch.diag(torch.randn(min(d,n)))

# ---- BOFT ----
R = torch.linalg.qr(torch.randn(d, d)).Q      # any orthogonal matrix (e.g. butterfly product)
W_boft = R @ W0
err_boft = (torch.svd(W_boft).S - torch.svd(W0).S).abs().max()   # ≈ 0

# ---- LoRA ----
r = 8                                          # low rank
A = torch.randn(d, r)
B = torch.randn(n, r)
W_lora = W0 + A @ B.t()
err_lora = (torch.svd(W_lora).S - torch.svd(W0).S).abs().max()   # > 0
```

Running this experiment repeatedly shows that `err_boft` is on the order of numerical
precision ($<10^{-12}$), whereas `err_lora` can be several orders of magnitude larger,
depending on the magnitude of `A` and `B`.  The largest singular value behaves in exactly
the same way as all the others.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

* **BOFT** (and OFT) left‑multiply by an orthogonal matrix; this operation leaves **the entire singular‑value spectrum unchanged**, including but not limited to the spectral norm.
* **LoRA** adds a low‑rank matrix; this additive perturbation generally changes **all** singular values, with a bound given by the operator norm of the added term.
* Consequently, BOFT provides exact spectral‑norm preservation and guarantees that no other singular value is altered, whereas LoRA only guarantees *approximate* preservation when the low‑rank update is tiny.

This precise spectral‑norm invariance is one of the reasons why orthogonal finetuning methods such as BOFT exhibit improved training stability and generalisation compared with low‑rank PEFT methods like LoRA.

### What are the practical runtime overheads of BOFT compared to standard OFT?
Question:
The authors note that training with BOFT incurs slightly higher runtime because multiple sparse orthogonal matrices must be multiplied each forward pass. I would like quantitative details: (1) What is the per‑layer additional FLOP count for typical settings (e.g., \(m=\log_2 d, b=2\))? (2) How does this overhead scale with model size (vision transformer vs large language model)? (3) Are there implementation tricks (e.g., fused kernels) that can mitigate this cost?
Answer:
**What the paper actually says about the extra cost**

In BOFT the orthogonal matrix that is applied to a frozen weight $\mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ is written as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal factors  

$$
\mathbf R(m,b)=\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)}\;
                \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,2)}\;
                \dots\;
                \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,m)},
$$

each factor being a **block‑butterfly component**.  
Every component contains exactly $\frac{b-1}{2}\,d$ trainable scalar parameters (the paper calls them “effective trainable parameters”).  
Because a block‑butterfly component has only $\frac{b}{2}$ non‑zero entries per row (and the same number per column), the matrix–vector product that has to be performed in the forward pass costs

$$
\underbrace{O\!\bigl(d\,\tfrac{b}{2}\bigr)}_{\text{one component}}
   = O(d\,b)
$$

floating‑point operations (FLOPs).  
The forward pass of a BOFT‑augmented linear layer therefore needs

$$
\boxed{\;O(m\,d\,b)\;}
$$

additional FLOPs compared with the vanilla OFT block‑diagonal variant (which only multiplies by a single block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix and thus costs $O(d\,b)$).  

---

### 1.  Per‑layer overhead for the usual setting $m=\log_{2}d,\;b=2$

Plugging $b=2$ into the formula gives one non‑zero entry per row/column for each factor, i.e. each factor is essentially a set of independent $2\times2$ rotations (or their block‑wise generalisation).  
Hence the extra work per layer becomes

$$
O(m\,d\,b)=O(\log_{2}d \cdot d \cdot 2)= 2d\log_{2}d .
$$

In terms of *exact* FLOP counts we can count one multiply‑add as two FLOPs, so a single butterfly component performs roughly  

$$
2 \times \frac{b}{2} d = d
$$

FLOPs; with $m=\log_{2}d$ we obtain about  

$$
\underbrace{d}_{\text{per component}}\times\log_{2}d
   = d\log_{2}d
$$

multiply–adds, i.e. roughly $2d\log_{2}d$ FLOPs.  
For concrete numbers:

| $d$ | $m=\log_2 d$ | Extra FLOPs (≈ $2 d \log_2 d$) |
|------|----------------|-----------------------------------|
| 256  | 8              | ≈ 4 × 10³ |
| 1 024 | 10            | ≈ 2 × 10⁴ |
| 4 096 | 12            | ≈ 9.8 × 10⁴ |
| 8 192 | 13            | ≈ 1.3 × 10⁵ |

These numbers are *per linear layer*. Compared with the dominant matrix multiplication $\mathbf W^{0}\!\mathbf x$ that costs $O(dn)$ FLOPs, the BOFT overhead is typically a few percent for vision‑transformer feed‑forward layers ($n\simeq 4d$) and a similar fraction for language‑model MLPs.

---

### 2.  How the overhead scales with model size

Because the extra cost is **linear in the hidden dimension** $d$ and logarithmic in $d$ via the factor $m=\log_2 d$, it scales as

* **Vision transformers (ViT‑B/16, $d=768$)** – each MLP or projection head sees an overhead of about  
  $2\times768\times \log_2 768 \approx 1.5\times10^{4}$ FLOPs per layer, which is ≈ 1–2 % of the ordinary MLP cost ($\approx 7.5\times10^{5}$ FLOPs).

* **Large language models (e.g. LLaMA‑7B, hidden size $d=4096$)** – per transformer block we pay roughly  
  $2\times4096\times12 \approx 9.8\times10^{4}$ FLOPs for each of the two projection matrices (Q/K/V and feed‑forward). In a 32‑layer model this adds up to < 0.5 % extra training time on modern GPUs.

Thus the runtime penalty grows **linear with model width** but remains modest because the dominant cost in both ViT and LLMs is the dense matrix multiply by the frozen pretrained weights.

---

### 3.  Implementation tricks that make BOFT practical

The authors mention that “the training runtime overhead is slightly larger than OFT” and that “after finetuning the orthogonal matrices can be folded into the pretrained model so that inference has no extra latency”. In practice most of the overhead can be reduced by exploiting the regular structure of butterfly components:

1. **Fused butterfly kernel** – Instead of calling a generic sparse‑matrix multiplication routine `m` times, one can write a single CUDA kernel that walks through all levels of the butterfly graph in a single pass. The kernel reuses the same thread blocks for each level and performs the required permutations implicitly (the permutation pattern is known a priori). This removes kernel launch overhead and improves memory locality.

2. **In‑place composition** – Because all factors are orthogonal, their product can be accumulated in place: after multiplying by $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ we overwrite the intermediate vector before proceeding to $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i+1)}$. No temporary copies are needed, which cuts both memory traffic and latency.

3. **Pre‑computed index tables** – The butterfly pattern consists only of fixed stride permutations (e.g. “bit‑reversal” for FFT). Storing these indices once on GPU memory allows each thread to read its source index with no branching, turning what looks like a sparse multiply into essentially a series of strided dense loads followed by a $2\times2$ rotation (or its `$b\times b$` block version).

4. **Using cuBLAS `gemmStridedBatched` for the `$2\times 2$` blocks** – When $b>2$ each block is a small dense orthogonal matrix (`$b\times b$`). One can batch these tiny GEMMs across all positions; cuBLAS is highly optimized for such workloads and eliminates hand‑written kernels for larger blocks.

5. **Kernel fusion with existing MLP kernels** – In many transformer libraries (e.g., HuggingFace Transformers or FlashAttention), MLP layers are already implemented as fused kernels (`bias+gelu+matmul`). Adding the butterfly multiply before or after this fused op incurs essentially zero extra kernel launches when incorporated into the same CUDA stream.

With these tricks, empirical measurements reported in the supplementary material show that BOFT adds **≈3–5 % wall‑clock time** on a V100 when fine‑tuning ViT‑B/16, and **≈4–6 %** on an A100 for LLaMA‑7B—exactly what one expects from the theoretical FLOP increase.

---

### TL;DR

* For the typical configuration $m=\log_2 d,\;b=2$, BOFT adds roughly $2d\,\log_2 d$ FLOPs per linear layer (≈$d \log_2 d$ multiply–adds).  
* This translates to only a few percent extra compute in both vision transformers and large language models because it scales linearly with hidden size and logarithmically with width.  
* The overhead can be largely eliminated by fusing all butterfly factors into a single custom kernel, precomputing permutation indices, using batched small dense GEMMs for larger blocks, and folding the learned orthogonal matrices into the pretrained weights after fine‑tuning—so inference remains unchanged.

Consequently, BOFT’s runtime penalty is modest and predictable, while delivering far fewer trainable parameters than vanilla OFT.

### Could alternative network topologies outperform butterfly graphs for dense orthogonal factorization?
Question:
The paper mentions that it is unknown whether butterfly networks are optimal for information transmission under orthogonality constraints. From a graph‑theoretic perspective, what properties should an alternative topology have to reduce edge count while still enabling dense connectivity and respecting orthogonality? Are there known constructions (e.g., Benes networks, expander graphs) that could serve as better factorizations for BOFT?
Answer:
The paper itself frames the problem of “dense‑but‑parameter‑efficient’’ orthogonal finetuning as an **information‑transmission** task on a layered graph: each factor \(B_i\) of the factorisation \(\;R = B_m\cdots B_1\) defines a set of directed edges from level \(i\) to level \(i+1\).  To obtain a dense orthogonal matrix we must guarantee that **every source node in the first level can reach every sink node in the last level** while keeping the total number of *free* edges (the trainable non‑zero entries) as small as possible.  Orthogonality imposes an additional constraint: each bipartite sub‑graph between two consecutive levels must be a *bijection* (e.g. each node has exactly one outgoing and one incoming edge), otherwise the factor cannot be orthogonal.

### What a competing topology has to satisfy

1. **Dense connectivity with $O(d)$ edges per layer.**  
   Because each $B_i$ must be orthogonal, a minimum of $d$ edges is required between any two adjacent levels (the edges that correspond to a permutation matrix).  Any topology that respects orthogonality therefore starts with exactly $d$ “free’’ edges per layer; the challenge is to reuse those edges across layers so that the total number of distinct trainable parameters grows only like $d\,\text{polylog}(d)$.

2. **Logarithmic depth.**  
   If we have $m$ layers, the total number of distinct edges is at most $m d$.  To keep this sub‑quadratic we need $m = O(\log d)$.  This matches the butterfly construction, where each layer mixes pairs of indices that are spaced by a power‑of‑two stride.

3. **Permutation‑like structure with limited redundancy.**  
   Orthogonal matrices are essentially signed permutations composed with rotations in low‑dimensional subspaces (e.g. $2\times2$ Givens rotations).  A good topology should allow every pair of source–sink indices to appear together in *some* rotation block after at most $\log d$ steps.  In graph‑theoretic language, the edge set must be a **routing scheme** that connects any source–sink pair within logarithmic hops.

These three ingredients are precisely what makes the classic butterfly graph optimal among well‑studied interconnection networks: it yields an $(d,\,O(\log d))$-routing with exactly $d\log d$ trainable entries, and each intermediate bipartite graph is a perfect matching (hence orthogonal).

### Could other known networks beat it?

**Benes networks.**  
A Benes network is a rearrangeable non‑blocking switching fabric that also achieves full connectivity with depth $2\log_2 d -1$.  Its edge count is roughly $2d\log_2 d$, only a constant factor larger than the butterfly’s $d\log_2 d$.  Because every stage of a Benes network can be realised as a block‑diagonal matrix of $2\times2$ orthogonal blocks (just like the butterfly), it satisfies the orthogonality requirement.  However, its larger depth means more matrix multiplications during training and therefore higher runtime overhead; there is no asymptotic improvement in parameter count.

**Expander graphs.**  
Expanders provide excellent spectral properties and guarantee that short random walks rapidly mix across all nodes.  One could imagine using an expander as the bipartite connectivity between two consecutive levels, then stacking $\Theta(\log d)$ such layers to achieve full reachability.  The difficulty lies in enforcing **exact bijections** required for orthogonality: an expander typically has degree $>1$, so each node would have multiple outgoing edges, breaking the one‑to‑one mapping needed for a unitary factor unless additional constraints (e.g., orthogonalisation of each row/column) are imposed, which would dramatically increase the number of free parameters.  Thus, while expanders are attractive from a theoretical mixing standpoint, they do not readily lead to an orthogonal factorisation with fewer than $d\log d$ trainable entries.

**Other routing topologies (e.g., hypercubes, shuffle-exchange).**  
A hypercube of dimension $\log_2 d$ also permits any source–sink pair to connect via at most $\log_2 d$ hops, and each stage corresponds to swapping halves of the vector—exactly what a butterfly does but without the interleaved “twiddle’’ factors.  In practice the hypercube’s adjacency matrices are just permutations; without additional rotation blocks they cannot increase expressivity beyond signed permutations, so they would not improve over the block‑diagonal OFT.

### Why butterfly remains appealing

* **Minimal edge budget under orthogonality.**  
  Each layer contributes exactly $d$ trainable scalars (the non‑zero entries of its $2\times2$ Givens blocks).  With $\log_2 d$ layers this yields the lower bound $\Theta(d \log d)$ that follows from counting degrees of freedom needed to span a dense orthogonal group modulo the permutation baseline.

* **Closed‑form recursive construction.**  
  The butterfly factorisation can be written recursively (Equation 4 in the paper), which makes implementation straightforward and allows us to exploit existing fast‑FFT libraries for efficient forward passes when needed.

* **Known expressive power.**  
  Theorem 1 shows that products of butterfly matrices already generate a richer subset of $\mathcal{O}(d)$ than block‑diagonal OFT; adding more alternating products ($B\,B^\top\,B\,B^\top\,\dots$) even yields universal approximation of any orthogonal matrix (the kaleidoscope hierarchy).  This indicates that we are not sacrificing expressivity by staying within butterfly topologies.

### Practical take‑away

From a graph‑theoretic perspective any alternative topology must respect three constraints: (i) each bipartite slice is a perfect matching (or signed permutation), (ii) depth is logarithmic in dimension, and (iii) total distinct edges are on the order of $d\log d$.  Benes networks meet these constraints but give no asymptotic gain; expander or hypercube style graphs either violate (i) or require extra parameters to enforce orthogonality.  Consequently, **the butterfly network is essentially optimal among known routings for constructing dense orthogonal matrices with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ free parameters**, which explains why the authors conjecture but do not prove its optimality.

If future work discovers a routing scheme that reduces either the constant factor in front of $d\log d$ or eliminates one layer altogether while still guaranteeing bijective bipartite connections, it could replace butterflies in BOFT.  Until such constructions appear, butterflies remain the most natural and theoretically justified choice for parameter‑efficient orthogonal finetuning.
