\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does reinforcement learning mainly improve Maj@K rather than Pass@K according to Figure 8?
Question:
The authors state that RL makes the output distribution more robust, boosting correct responses from TopK but not changing fundamental capability. How is Maj@K computed in this context (majority vote over multiple samples per question?), and why would GRPO’s group‑relative baseline lead to higher probability mass on already good answers rather than generating better new solutions? Is this an artifact of using only outcome supervision with a reward model trained on correctness?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the DeepSeekMath paper the authors evaluate the effect of their reinforcement‑learning (RL) stage by reporting two families of metrics:

* **Pass@K** – the probability that at least one of *K* sampled answers is *exactly* correct.
* **Maj@K** – the accuracy obtained when we take a *majority vote* over the *K* sampled answers (i.e., we pick the answer that appears most often among the *K* samples and check whether that answer is correct).

Both metrics are computed on a **fixed set of test questions**; for each question we generate *K* completions from the model (the same *K* is used for Pass@K and for Maj@K).  Pass@K therefore measures “does the model ever produce a correct solution”, while Maj@K measures “does the model tend to put most of its probability mass on a correct solution”.

---

### Why RL lifts Maj@K but leaves Pass@K almost unchanged

The RL algorithm used in the paper is **Group‑Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**.  Its essential ingredients are:

1. **Outcome‑only reward** – a binary reward (correct / incorrect) that is supplied only at the end of a generated answer.
2. **Group‑relative baseline** – for each question a *group* of $G$ samples (64 in the experiments) is drawn from the current policy; the baseline used to compute advantages is simply the mean reward of that group.
3. **KL regularisation against a fixed reference model** – this prevents the policy from drifting arbitrarily far from the SFT model.

Because the advantage for every token in a sample is proportional to  

$$\hat A_{i,t}= \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)},$$  

all tokens belonging to a *correct* answer receive **a positive, identical signal**, while tokens belonging to an *incorrect* answer receive **a negative, identical signal**.  In other words, GRPO does not distinguish *how much better* one correct answer is compared with another; it only learns to increase the total probability mass assigned to any answer that happens to be correct.

Consequences:

| What GRPO reinforces | Effect on distribution |
|----------------------|------------------------|
| “Any correct answer” | Pushes probability from low‑scoring (incorrect) outputs toward *any* high‑scoring output |
| “Correctness only”   | Does not create new, previously unseen solution strategies; it only makes existing good solutions more likely |

When we look at **Pass@K**, it already saturates fairly early because even the SFT model can produce a correct answer in at least one of 64 samples for many questions.  Adding GRPO does not create *new* correct answers, so the probability that “at least one” sample is correct changes little.

In contrast, **Maj@K** depends on how much of the total probability mass sits on a single correct answer.  Before RL, the distribution over samples may be fairly flat: a question might yield several distinct correct solutions and many incorrect ones, so no single answer dominates and a majority vote often selects an incorrect answer.  After GRPO, because all correct samples receive a uniform positive boost and all incorrect samples are uniformly penalised, the mass concentrates on whichever correct answer appears most frequently in the group.  Hence, when we take a majority vote over the same *K$, we are now much more likely to pick that dominant correct answer, raising Maj@K.

---

### Intuition behind “majority‑vote” vs “any‑correct”

Think of each question as a roulette wheel whose slices correspond to different generated completions.  The SFT wheel has many small slices for both correct and incorrect answers; spinning it 64 times gives you many different colours, so you might hit a green slice (correct) once but you seldom see green dominate.  GRPO rewrites the wheel by merging all green slices into a few larger ones while shrinking red slices.  The chance that at least one spin lands on green (Pass@K) barely changes—the green area was already non‑zero—but now green occupies enough area that most spins land on it, so a majority of spins are green (higher Maj@K).

---

### Relation to “outcome‑only” supervision

Because GRPO’s reward signal comes only from an outcome model trained on binary correctness, it cannot reward intermediate reasoning steps that lead to *novel* solution ideas.  The authors themselves note in §4.3 (“Why RL works?”) that this explains why RL mainly makes the output distribution **more robust** rather than expanding fundamental capability: it redistributes probability towards already‑good solutions instead of discovering new ones.  If one were to employ a richer process‑level reward (as they also experiment with), or use more diverse question distributions during exploration, the RL signal could potentially create new high‑reward regions and thus improve Pass@K as well.

---

### Summary

* **Maj@K** is computed by majority voting over *K* model outputs per test question.
* GRPO uses group‑relative advantages that give every *correct* sample an equal positive push and every *incorrect* sample an equal negative push.
* This pushes probability mass onto existing correct solutions, making them dominate the sampled set—hence Maj@K rises.
* Pass@K measures merely “does any sample hit a correct answer”; because SFT already has non‑negligible chance of doing so, uniformly up‑weighting all correct answers does not markedly increase this chance.
* The effect is therefore an artefact of using only outcome supervision with a binary correctness reward; richer rewards or more diverse exploration could change both metrics.

Thus, Figure 8 reflects that RL as implemented improves *robustness* of the distribution rather than expanding its *capability*.
