# Chapter 1: Introduction to Attention and Long‑Context Challenges

In the Transformer family of neural networks, **self‑attention** is the engine that lets every token in a sequence consult every other token. Since its introduction in 2017, attention has become the core building block of language models, vision transformers, audio encoders, and beyond. Yet the same property that makes it powerful—the ability to fuse global context—also turns it into a computational bottle‑neck. In this chapter we dissect why the quadratic cost of self‑attention becomes prohibitive as we push model size and context length into practical regimes, trace the evolution of attention mechanisms over nearly a decade, and survey how researchers have responded with approximation schemes, software innovations, and alternative architectures. Finally we situate the recent FlashAttention‑3 contribution within this broad research landscape.

## 1 Evolving Transformer Attention from 2017 to 2026

The original Transformer paper \citep{vaswani2017attention} described a multi‑head attention scheme wherein each head computes \(\mathbf{O} = \operatorname{softmax}\!\bigl(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\bigr)\mathbf{V}\). This operation was already expensive because it required two dense matrix multiplications per head of size \(N\times d\), yet its design offered a clean path to parallelism on modern GPUs.

A first wave of work in 2018–2019 focused on engineering optimisations that fit this rubric onto commodity hardware. Methods such as *FlashAttention* \citep{dao2022flashattention} re‑imagined the memory access pattern so that intermediate score matrices could be kept entirely on fast shared memory, eliminating punitive global‑memory traffic. Subsequent variants like *FlashAttention‑2* \citep{dao2023flashattention2} addressed occupancy and work distribution across sequence length, yielding higher GPU utilisation for mid‑size contexts.

The next surge began around 2020 with a shift toward **efficient or sparse attention**. Researchers found that many of the pairwise relationships encoded by full self‑attention are redundant for many downstream tasks; hence new mechanisms “cut out” unnecessary interactions. Notable contributions include *Sparse Transformer* \citep{katharopoulos2020transformers}, *Longformer* \citep{choromanski2020rethinking}, and *Reformer* \citep{tay2020efficient}. These approximations reduce time complexity from \(O(N^2)\) towards sub‑quadratic scaling but often degrade expressivity.

Around 2022–2024 several large language models pushed into the long‑context regime (several megabytes of input). Works such as *LongT5* \citep{guo2021longt5}, *Scrolls* \citep{shaham2022scrolls}, *Yarn* \citep{peng2023yarn}, and *Code LLMs* like *StarCoder* \citep{roziere2023code} all leveraged extensive context windows (up to \(64\,\text{k}\) tokens or more). These models required not just a clever software trick but new hardware awareness: GPUs based on NVIDIA’s Hopper architecture introduced Tensor Memory Accelerators (TMAs) and asynchronous Tensor Cores capable of operating on FP8 data.

In parallel, the community produced **alternative architectures** that avoid dense self‑attention entirely: Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) were revisited in lightweight forms; *Recurrent Weight-Key* (RWKV) made residual weight sharing feasible; *Mamba* leveraged local convolutional processing; and *Retentive Networks* aimed at attention-free long-range dependencies. Each approach trades your classic transformer underduality long-range patterns for efficient computation.

Finally, there has been renewed focus on **software optimisations** tailored to contemporary hardware: new GPU instruction sets like warp‑group GEMM (WGMMA) utilities allow simultaneous tensor cores to work together across warps; libraries such as cuDNN have released highly tuned kernels for every major GPU generation; low-precision formats like BF16 and FP8 have emerged for higher FLOP density without significant loss of accuracy.

All these strands are converging on a singular goal: compute attention accurately while keeping memory traffic at bay on large-scale tasks.

## 2 Quadratic Time and Memory Cost of Self-Attention

Self‑attention takes two critical inputs: queries (\(\mathbf{Q}\)), keys (\(\mathbf{K}\)), and values (\(\mathbf{V}\)). For a single head of dimension \(d\) over a sequence of length \(N\), naive implementation requires computing:

\[
  \mathbf{S}=\frac{\mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt d}\quad 
  \in \mathbb R^{N\times N},
\quad 
  \mathbf{P} =\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf S), 
  \quad
  \mathbf O=\mathbf P\,\mathbf V .
\]

The two inner products are dense matrix multiplications whose sizes give rise to an arithmetic cost proportional to \(O(N^2d)\). The memory footprint is similarly quadratic: storing full score matrix \(\mathbf S\) or probability matrix \(\mathbf P\) would require \(O(N^2)\) space, which quickly becomes infeasible even for modest sequence lengths when combined with large batch sizes or many heads.

The traditional cost model is then:
- **Time**: \(4\,N^2\,d\,H\) FLOPs for one layer (\(H\) heads).
- **Memory**: Roughly \(N^2\) elements for storing intermediate scores.

Even when GPU cores can perform tens of teraflops per second, latency is dominated by slow global memory reads/writes once data no longer fits in the cache hierarchy. As models grow deeper or input contexts stretch longer (to capture extended discourse or entire documents), these matrices become prohibitively large both in memory usage and computation time.

Consequently deep learning practitioners have had two choices:
1. **Approximate** the similarity function (e.g., windowed or locality-sensitive hashing).
2. **Redesign** software/hardware interplay so that all necessary intermediates can be held on-chip without expensive global transfers (the basis for FlashAttention).

Understanding precisely where the pressure originates informs exactly how new kernels can solve these bottlenecks.

## 3 Applications That Drive the Need for Longer Contexts

Modern AI systems routinely process data that is inherently long or multi-modal:

1. **Document-level language understanding** requires recognising references spanning multiple paragraphs or entire documents (\(\sim10^5\) tokens). Large-scale summarisation tools (e.g., for scientific literature), information retrieval engines, or conversational agents with document lookup all demand near‑the‑end context windows.
   
2. **Code generation from extensive codebases** necessitates exploring thousands of lines where semantic structure often appears far apart (“going one function call back”). Models such as StarCoder aim precisely at this scenario.
   
3. **Multimedia pipelines** combine high-resolution imagery (\(>10^8\) pixels), auditory streams (\(>10^7\) samples), and video frames over long horizons, driving scaling up from local receptive fields toward end-to-end modelling.
   
4. **Temporal reasoning agents**—for example those performing policy planning or story generation—must maintain coherent scenarios over hundreds of steps or more.
   
5. **Regulatory compliance tools** require examining past conversation histories stretching hundreds of messages between users and chatbots to verify consistency over time.

All these use cases place enormous demands on attention bandwidth. Where earlier benchmarks considered only a few hundred tokens per example, contemporary reference datasets ask for one million token windows or greater constraints to reduce token burn while preserving context dependency structures.

This surge in required sequence length feeds directly back into Quadratic Scaling concerns outlined above; if not addressed algorithmically or architecturally, real-world deployments would either incur unacceptable delays or sacrifice accuracy through heavy architectural simplifications.

## 4 Overview of Existing Efficiency Strategies

Over time researchers have made strides along multiple axes to reduce attention overhead while maintaining fidelity:

### 4.1 Approximation Schemes  
Methods like Sparse Transformers restrict attention to sliding windows with periodic “global” tokens; Reformer introduces locality-sensitive hashing so each query attends only to a subset constructed via random fingerprints; Linformer reduces dimensionality by projecting outer product expansions into lower rank spaces via learnable matrices; Performer approximates softmax via random feature expansions; Recurrent/Kernel approaches convert attention into cumulative operations amenable to linear effort over time steps.

These strategies all trade off expressivity for speed/space savings but often add hyperparameter tuning burdens or degrade model performance on tasks requiring genuinely global compromise.

### 4.2 Software Optimisation  
Primer works such as FlashAttention demonstrated that judicious use of tile-based blocked matrix multiplies could avert global writes entirely; subsequent enhancements exploited fused kernels (on-chip merging GEMMs + softmax + rescaling); algorithmic improvements such as overlapping inner loops, using cinv padding tricks; emerging libraries like CUTLASS now expose WGMMA primitives natively enabling bespoke implementations tailored to specific GPU microarchitectures.

The latest generation incorporates asynchronous features such as TMAs that free threads while data moves between host memory tiers and Tensor Cores that operate independently from CUDA core pipelines.

### 4.3 Alternative Architectures  
Several entrants now eschew conventional self-attention altogether:
- RWKV implements weight reuse across timesteps creating an RNN-like body while still enabling parallelism.
- Mamba proposes sequential local window convolutions interleaved with gated recurrent layers.
- Retentive Neural Networks store hidden states locally enabling iterative refinement without cross-token reads.
These approaches avoid quadratic feature interactions but bring new overheads—a fine balance between compute density versus weight reuse strategies remains an active research area.

### 4.4 Low-Precision Acceleration  
FP16 damping early GPUs achieved higher throughput by leveraging mixed precision training/ inference pipelines; BF16 themed up performance beginning with Ampere at roughly twice FP32 throughput due to wider drums on arithmetic units; HPUs like Hopper now support FP8 modes which enable four times more elementary operations per cycle relative to FP16 while preserving dynamic range through carefully crafted quantisation schemes like e4m3.

However low precision introduces quantisation error especially when many entries become extreme because of heavy-tailed weight distributions synonymous with LLM deployments—thus necessitating sophisticated dequantisation companions such as block scalings or random projections (“incoherent processing”).

These four strands together inform how FlashAttention attempts bridging practical performance increases on Hopper GPUs by wringing out asynchronous execution and low precision strengths while preserving exact result quality compared with naïve floating point pipelines.

## 5 Positioning FlashAttention‑3 within This Research Ecosystem

FlashAttention‑3 is an evolutionary leap from its predecessor kernels that marries both software ingenuity and emergent hardware primitives:

1. **Producer–Consumer Asynchrony & Warp Specialisation**  
   By designating distinct warps within each threadblock purely as producers (loading queries/key/value tiles into Shared Memory via TMA) versus pure consumers (executing WGMMA operations & softmax), FlashAttention‑3 eliminates stalls caused by overlapping memory transfer demands against computational workloads—a core challenge harnessed in earlier kernels but extended here across multiple stages plus dynamic register reclamation via `setmaxnreg`.

2. **Interleaving Block-Wise GEMMs & Softmax**  
   Traditional formulations impose a sequential dependency between computing scores (\(\mathbf Q\times\mathbf K^\top\)) and applying softmax before multiplying by values (\(\tilde{\mathbf P}\times V\)). FlashAttention‑3 threads this dependence across iterations so that while one warp computes softmax for block \(j-1\), another warp concurrently performs GEMM for block \(j+1\). The resulting “pingpong” pipeline leverages asynchronous WGMMA execution making near‐full utilisation realistic even when softmax’s functional units form througput bottlenecks stored typically in separate DPU paths.

3. **Hardware-Accelerated Low-Precision FP8 Federations**

4. **Uniform Cross-Runtime Design**

# 2. Fundamentals of Multi‑Head Attention  

Attention is the core computation that enables Transformer‐style models to relate every token in a sequence to every other token.  Although attention was introduced in the context of machine translation, its mathematical form is agnostic to the particular application; it is simply a pair of matrix multiplications followed by a row‑wise softmax and a second matrix multiplication.  In this chapter we derive all useful forms of the forward and backward passes, explain the common scaling tricks, and enumerate practical concerns such as numerical stability and parallel execution across heads and batches.  Every detail below follows directly from the equations presented in the \textsc{FlashAttention} paper.

---

## Definition of $Q$, $K$, $V$ Matrices and Dimensionalities  

For a single head of the Multi‑Head Attention (MHA) module we regard three real matrices:

* **Queries** $\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$  
* **Keys**   $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$  
* **Values** $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$

Here  

* $N$ is the sequence length (number of tokens).  
* $d$ is the head dimension; in an MHA layer with $h$ heads we typically set $d = D / h$, where $D$ is the hidden size of the outer model.  

For clarity let us denote the *i*-th query row by $\mathbf{q}_i^\top$, the *j*-th key row by $\mathbf{k}_j^\top$, and similarly for values.  Rows are indexed from $0$ to $N-1$.  All operations below are performed **once per head**, and the outputs are then concatenated across heads to form the full output tensor.

---

## Scaled Dot‑Product Attention: $S$, $P$, $\mathbf{O}$ Computation  

The core operation is a scaled dot‑product between queries and keys followed by a softmax over each query’s key scores:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{S} &:= \alpha\,\mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{K}^{\!\top}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\\
                &~~~\text{with }\alpha = \frac1{\sqrt d},\\[4pt]
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}} &:= \exp(\mathbf{S})\;\stackrel{\text{subtract max}}\longrightarrow\;\widehat{\mathbf{P}}\;\text{: row‑wise softmax},\\
                &~~~\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{i,j}=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{S}_{i,j}-m_i)}{\sum_{k}\exp(\mathbf{S}_{i,k}-m_i)}\\
&= : \;\mathbf{P}\quad(\text{returned as }\,\mathbb R^{N\times N}),\\[6pt]
\mathbf{O}&:=\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{V}\;\in\;\mathbb R^{N\times d}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.1}
\]

The scaling factor $\alpha=1/\sqrt d$ was introduced in the original Transformer paper; it normalises dot‑products so that each entry in $\mathbf S$ has variance roughly one, which keeps exponential terms numerically stable.  In practice we compute row‑wise maxima

\[
m_i = \max_{j}\,\bigl(\mathbf S_{i,j}\bigr),
\]

subtract them before exponentiating and accumulate row sums after exponentiation:
$\ell_i = \sum_j e^{\,S_{i,j}-m_i}$.

Figures omitted here used pseudo–code referenced as lines~([wt]).

---

### Row‑wise Softmax with Max‑Stabilisation  

Directly computing $\exp(\mathbf S)$ can overflow (\#entries becomes huge) or underflow (very small numbers lose precision).  The standard fix—taking a rowwise maximum—is called *softmax stabilisation*.  It can be motivated as follows:

Let us write one row \(s=(s_1,\ldots,s_N)\).  
The softmax normaliser is \(Z = \sum_j e^{s_j}\).  
Multiplying numerator and denominator by $e^{-m}$ with \(m = \max s_j\) yields

\[
p_j = \frac{\exp(s_j)}{\sum_k e^{s_k}}
    = \frac{\exp(s_j-m)}{\sum_k e^{s_k-m}},
    \quad
Z'=\sum_k e^{s_k-m}.
    \tag{2.2}
\]

Now all exponentials are bounded between $(0,1]$, preventing overflow even when \(s_j>1000\). In checkpointing style kernels such as FlashAttention, we maintain `m` incrementally across blocks: after processing a block of key rows, we update $m_i=\max(m_i,\texttt{rowmax}(S_{\textbf block}))$, thus ensuring a single pass over each query.

---

## Gradient Derivations for \(dQ,dK,dV\) Using the Chain Rule  

Consider any loss function $\phi$ applied to the outputs $\mathbf O$.  Denote its derivative w.r.t.\ $\mathbf O$ by

\( \partial_\phi := -d(-)= -\,\frac{\partial}{-\partial?}.\)

The backward pass decomposes into five matrix multiplications following rules from calculus.

Let \(B_{\phi}=\partial_\phi\,(\!\!/\!\!?)?\) etc., but here we present concrete formulas that appear in FlashAttention’s backward pass.

Denote

* \(dO = {\partial_{\!O}}\), gradient w.r.t.\ output.
* \(dQ,dK,dV\) – gradients w.r.t.\ affine projections.

We have first ଶା b

1. Gradient wrt Values:
   \[
   dV = P^\top\, dO.
   \tag{2.3}
   \]

2. Gradient wrt Softmax matrix:
   Let \(dP = dO\, V^\top.\)
   Then apply derivative of softmax:
   For each row \(i:\)
   \[
   dS_i = (diag(p_i)-p_ip_i^\top)\,dP_i,
   \qquad p_i=\hbox{softmax}(s_i).
   \tag{2.4}
   \]
   The result `dS` has size \(N\times N.\)

3. Gradient wrt Queries:
   Using scaled dot product,
   \(S = T(K,Q)=Q\,K^\top/\sqrt d.\) Hence
   \(dQ=\alpha\, dS^T K.\)
   Similarly,
   \(dK=\alpha\, dS\, Q.\)
   
Putting it together:

```
  buff[dV] ← Pᵀ × burn
  buff[dP] ← burn × Vᵀ      // returns Pᵀ × our gradient
  buff[dS] ← dsddsoftmax(buff[dP]) // per-row formula above
  buff[dQ] ← α × buff[dS]ᵀ × K
  buff[dK] ← α × buff[dS]          × Q
```

Where `burn` stands for `grad` or `∂φ/∂O`.  All five steps hinge on efficient GEMM operations – precisely why FlashAttention fuses all these into one kernel rather than materializing intermediate matrices.

---

### Parallelism Across Heads, Batches, Sequences  

Single‑head equations above extend trivially:

* **Batch dimension**: For batch size $B$, every equation becomes element‑wise with index $(b,h)$.
* **Head dimension**: Let indices run over $h=0,\dots,H-1$. Each head gets its own affine projection, so operations can be batched as GEMMs with additional “depth” dimension in CUBLAS or CUTLASS.
* **Sequence dimension**: The order of loops does not affect those equations; however efficient implementation hinges on tiling along sequence length so that each threadblock handles a query tile against all key tiles – what FlashAttention does using shared memory buffers.

Thus parallelism naturally decomposes across these three axes; no cross‑head dependencies exist because attention within each head is independent.

---

## Common Pitfalls: Underflow/Overflow, Precision Losses  

### Numerical Instability without Softmax Stabilisation  
If one forgets to subtract `rowmax`, severally large exponents cause huge values; during subsequent division they will lead to NaNs or infinite decimals because denominator blows up faster than numerator. The counter‑example occurs when computed score entries exceed ≈127 (largest safe exponent basee for float32); consequent overflow yields NaNs or infinities damaging gradients downstream.

### Precision Loss Via FP16 or FP8 Calculations  
All modern GPUs accelerate FP8 / FP16 GEMMs inside Tensor Cores, which greatly speeds up attention but introduces quantisation errors:
* Loss of dynamic range: especially in dot products where small contributions are rounded away.
* Exponential requires FP32 intermediate results; many kernels keep them floating‐point at higher precision precisely for this reason.
FlashAttention deliberately keeps intermediate `softmax` results in FP32 (or higher) before converting back to FP16/FP8 at output stage—this preserves accuracy while still benefiting from low‐precision GEMM acceleration.

### Floating-Point Rounding During Accumulation  
When executing many repeated multiply–accumulate operations (e.g., computing sums over thousands of elements), accumulated rounding error may grow linearly with number of terms if only an FP16 accumulator is used. The analysis suggests elevated error bounds; hence Glow FSoftMax or multi-pass summation is preferable if high fidelity required.

### Quantisation Bias With Outlier Features  
In very large language models several tokens contain stakes extremely large variance (`outliers`).  Per-tensor scaling harms accuracy because a few huge elements dominate scale factors λ leading small values to be clamped underflows in FP8/FP16. FlashAttention tackles this via block‑level scaling (one λ per block) combined with incoherent linear transforms (`M` orthogonal random bucket), which smooths out these outliers.

---

## Summary  

1. Single‑head attention performs two dot products separated by a stabilised softmax; resulting matrices are all real $N\times N$, $N\times d$ respectively.
2. Scaling factor $\alpha=1/\sqrt d$ ensures comparably sized expectations across different network widths.
3. Precise gradient formulas follow directly from the chain rule hinging on four clever GEMMs plus element‑wise vector transformations.
4. Numerical pitfalls are largely solved by max‐stabilised softmax and high precision intermediates before low‐precision outputs are produced.
5. Modern implementations such as FlashAttention fuse forward & backward passes into single kernels that simultaneously use shared memory tiling, warp-specialisation, tensor cores, and TMA on Hopper GPUs—notably employing spill-free register allocation strategies that respect Tensor Core data layouts for FP8 while preserving numerical integrity through block quantisation and orthogonal decorrelation techniques.

# 3. NVIDIA Hopper Architecture – Hardware Overview

The performance of *FlashAttention‑3* rests on a detailed understanding of the Hopper GPU’s memory hierarchy, execution model, and emerging numeric capabilities. This chapter unpacks these elements in depth, providing the reader with a solid foundation for appreciating how the algorithm exploits Hopper’s design.  

---

## 3.1 GPUs as Throughput Engines

Before diving into Hopper specifics, it is useful to recall the core philosophy of modern GPUs: **throughput over latency**. A GPU continually keeps its execution units fed with work, hiding memory stalls and instruction overheads through massive parallelism and a multi‑level cache hierarchy. Hopper continues this tradition but introduces dedicated hardware units that make kernel design far richer than earlier generations.

---

## 3.2 Memory Hierarchy on Hopper

On Hopper, as on all NVIDIA GPUs, four “localities” of memory are arranged in a strictly nested hierarchy:

| Memory | Typical size per SM | Bandwidth (approx.) | Privatized to… |
|--------|---------------------|----------------------|----------------|
| **Global Memory (GMEM / HBM)** | ≈ 100 GiB external DRAM | 400 – 1 Tb/s (HBM2e) | All SMs (multi‑valve) |
| **L2 Cache** | 128 MiB total (per‑SM allocation 4 KiB) | Near peak GMEM bandwidth | All SMs |
| **Shared Memory (SMEM)** | 95 KiB per SM + banked scratchpad | 128 bytes / clock / *per* SM (≈ 1.76 Tb/s) | Whole CTA |
| **Registers (RMEM)** | 256 registers per thread | Private bandwidth is limited to register file access (≈ 50–70 B/clock/thread) | One thread only |

*Key ideas:*

1. **HBM2e bandwidth is the largest source**, but accesses are very expensive in latency terms; moving data through L2 or SMEM is therefore essential for high throughput.
2. **SMEM is programmer‑controlled** and is highly banked; it is the place where *FlashAttention‑3* keeps its sliding window of query, key, and value tiles.
3. **Registers are the fastest storage**, but each thread can hold only up to ~256 floats; therefore careful register allocation is critical when pipelining.

---

### 3.2.1 Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA)

Hopper’s *Tensor Memory Accelerator* speeds up **asynchronous** data transfers between GMEM and SMEM. Unlike raw `cudaMemcpyAsync`, TMA supports:

- Alignment enforcement for large contiguous blocks.
- Prefetch hints (`bfloat16`, `float16`, `fp8`).
- Very low overhead synchronization via `__syncthreads()` + implicit barriers.

Because TMA can run in parallel with kernel computations, *FlashAttention‑3* issues a pipeline of TMA loads that arrive in time for the next block of matrix multiplications.

---

## 3.3 Thread Hierarchy and Execution Model

Hopper organizes threads hierarchically from smallest to largest:

```
thread → warp → warpgroup → CTA → cluster → grid
```

### 3.3.1 Threads & Warps

A **warp** contains 32 threads that execute in lock‑step on a single warp scheduler within an SM. Each LM/SMH uses a simple ALU pipeline that can partially overlap instructions across warps; however, instructions within a warp must complete before the next one starts.

### 3.3.2 Warpgroups

A **warpgroup** bundles **four contiguous warps** into a single logical entity that shares data movement scheduling resources such as TMA or WGMMA units. Importantly:

- Warpgroups can be marked as producers or consumers via software partitioning.
- The `setmaxnreg` PTX directive can reallocate registers between warpgroups dynamically: producers devote fewer registers for TMA staging while consumers may grab more registers for scratch space and accumulator residency.

### 3.3.3 CTA, Cluster & Grid

- A **CTA** (Cooperative Thread Array) groups multiple warpgroups; all threads within a CTA run on the same SM.
- A **cluster** groups multiple CTAs that share a second-level “large pool” of shared memory resources; on Hopper clusters allow threadgroups to serialize more complex synchronization primitives.
- The entire set of CTAs constitutes one grid; kernels launch grids containing dozens or hundreds of CTAs.

---

## 3.4 Asynchronous Data Movement & Warp‑Specialization

The heart of *FlashAttention‑3* is partitioning each CTA into two sets of warpgroups:

1. **Producer warpgroups**:  
   - Issue TMA loads for Q, K, V tiles concurrently.
   - Each producer uses only a handful of registers (`setmaxnreg` drops them) because it never manipulates data beyond placement into SMEM.
   - These warps only care about getting fresh data into place; once loading finishes they hand off control to the consumer warpgroups via CUDA *barrier* semantics.

2. **Consumer warpgroups**:  
   - Read Q/K/V tiles from SMEM.
   - Issue WGMMA operations which are fully asynchronous.
   - Consume results from producers while producers load the next tile set; as soon as results arrive they compute partial softmax sums and launch subsequent multiplications.

This division allows independent overlapping: softmax computations legible by one set can be performed *while* the other set lies doing GEMMs or TMA transfers—crucial because softmax’s exponentials are orders of magnitude slower than matrix multiplication.

---

## 3.5 Warp‑Group Matrix Multiply Accumulator (WGMMA)

### 3.5.1 The New Tensor Core Interface

The WGMMA instruction replaces pre‐Hopper `mma.sync` primitives by providing:

- A larger computational tensor tile (`M×N`) per warpgroup; for TF32 precision it might be `128×128`.
- An explicit accumulation buffer residing in FP32 or higher precision even if inputs are lower precision—this mitigates rounding error issues when using FP8 operands.

Key properties:

- **Fully asynchronous**: once you issue `wgmma.sync.smem.nchw.f16.f16.f32`, the kernel never waits; it fires off a TM instruction that will complete sometime later but does not stall any other warps in its CPU group.
- **Dual-bank operation**: two operands can be sourced from distinct banks—one from SMEM (`smem`) and another from CG block registers (`cgr`) or scratchpad memory (`ldmatrix`).

### 3.5.2 Tile Layout Constraints – k‑major vs mn‑major

When using FP8 operands, Hopper restricts layout constraints severely:

| Operand    | Allowed layout in SMEM              |
|------------|-------------------------------------|
| Input A    | k‑major only                         |
| Input B    | k‑major only                         |
| FP32 Accumulator | Implicit k‑major mapped to float register buffers |

For FP16/BF16 operands both mn-major and k-major are permitted—hence older algorithms could arbitrarily choose whichever was more convenient.

Because attention requires both
\[
\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top,\qquad
\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V},
\]
we need both operands to be laid out correctly simultaneously within a single kernel launch:
  
- The first GEMM consumes `Q` (mn-major) and `K` (k-major).
- The second GEMM consumes partial `P` tiles (mn-major due to output tiling) and `V` (k-major).
  
To satisfy these constraints without two physical copies we perform an *in-kernel transposition* of V tiles when loading them from GMEM into SMEM via LDSM/STSM instructions—a technique that will be revisited fully later.

---

## 3.6 Register Allocation Mechanism – `setmaxnreg`

The key PTX function used by *FlashAttention‑3* is:

```ptx
.setmaxnreg <warp_group_id> <max_regs>
```

This call allows dynamic reconfiguration of how many registers each thread inside the specified warpgroup may see during compile-time code generation:

- Producing warps may get forced to <64 regs each if they invest almost nothing but just issue loads.
- Consumers may take up >200 regs because they hold scratch matrices and intermediate accumulators.

This selective allocation dramatically improves **occupancy** by ensuring no single group throttles concurrency due to register pressure—a crucial point when employing sizeable block dimensions such as \(B_r=64\) or \(B_c=128\).

---

## 3.7 Low‑Precision Tensor Core Support – FP8

The latest Hopper release includes full support for:
  
- FP8 half even (`e4m3`) tensors—4 bits exponent, 3 bits mantissa—and 
- Full inverse operations such as `ddp!`/`dvm!`.

This implies Mach specifications such as:
  
- Two inputs per WGMMA can be fp8 while accumulating in fp32.
- For best performance we keep "hot view" data in fp8 inside shared memory so Multi-MOPS accelerate multiplication throughput to ~989 TFLOPS/s vs ~489 TFLOPS/s for fp16 on H100.

However, moving to fp8 brings two main challenges:
  
1. **Error Inflation** – FP8 has orders of magnitude fewer representable values, leading to large quantization errors when multiplied so large-scale models’ activations contain outliers.
   
2. **Layout Conformance** – The WGMMA requires k-major layout on both operands and an inversion step for output formatting right after accumulation.

Both issues motivate algorithmic changes discussed in Chapter 4 – block quantization reduces quantization error while an “incoherent processing” step stabilizes labeling across block boundaries without affecting correctness thanks to orthogonal embedding preservation (\(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = I\)).

---

## 3.8 Putting It All Together

With this hardware apparatus mapped out we can understand how *FlashAttention‑3* achieves its speed claims:

1.$\, $Producer-warps asynchronously load blocks via TMA into SMEM without stalling consumer-warps due to pipelining and shared buffer management(“ping–pong scheduling”).

2.$\, $Consumer-warps use WGMMA blades that run concurrently with softmax exponentials thanks to warp-specialization; ping-pong scheduling reverses compute versus I/O roles every block pair.

3.$\, $FP8 semantic layer employs pre-transposed V matrices loaded by LDSM/STSM and then SHA‐rotated through byte permutes so that the accumulator layout matches tma watched operand layout only at steps where a new result consumption emerges therefore reducing conversions cost about ‑75 % compared with naive copying back-and-forth between registers).

4.$\, $All data stay below global memory boundaries until final writes are cheap at the end of forward pass using coalesced HBM writes.

By aligning software logic precisely with whatardware offers – async TAMAs, warp-aware acceleration units like WGMMA, dedicated low precision support – we surpass many legacy kernels by up to ∼200 % while keeping numerical fidelity intact.

# “Standard” Attention versus FlashAttention: The Baseline Approach  

Large‑scale sequence models such as the transformer rely on a single, computationally intensive operation: the *attention* mechanism.  In a typical GPU implementation, this mechanism is split into multiple passes over the data, each of which incurs a round‑trip to host‑side (HBM) memory.  The advent of *FlashAttention* changed that picture by carefully fusing all of the sub‑operations into a single launch.  This chapter explains, step by step, why the naïve pipeline is costly and how FlashAttention’s tiling strategy eliminates that cost while preserving exactness.

## Conventional Attention Pipeline with Intermediate Reads/Writes to HBM  

The forward pass of multi‑head attention can be written in matrix form as  

\[
\mathbf{S} = \alpha \,\mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{K}^\top ,\qquad
\mathbf{P} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{S}) ,\qquad
\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{V}.
\]

Each of these three symbols is an \(N\times N\), \(N\times N\), or \(N\times d\) matrix, where  
\(N\) is the sequence length and \(d\) the head dimension.  

In a “standard” GPU implementation:

1. **First matrix multiplication**  
   The product \(\mathbf{S}\) is computed with a GEMM kernel that streams \(\mathbf{Q}\) and \(\mathbf{K}\) from global memory (HBM), performs the multiply–accumulate, and writes the *entire* score matrix \(\mathbf{S}\) back to HBM.  
   The HBM traffic for this step alone is  
   \[
   2N^2d\,B_f,
   \]
   where \(B_f\) denotes the byte width of the data type (e.g., 16 or 8 bytes).

2. **Softmax transformation**  
   A second pass loads \(\mathbf{S}\) from HBM again, applies a row–wise softmax (requiring max reduction followed by exponentiation), and writes the normalized matrix \(\mathbf{P}\) back to HBM.

3. **Second matrix multiplication**  
   Finally, a third GEMM reads \(\mathbf{P}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\) from HBM and writes the result \(\mathbf{O}\).  

Each pass also incurs a backward layout change (row‑major vs column‑major accesses), synchronisation barriers, and must respect GPU occupancy constraints.  Because HBM bandwidth on modern GPUs far exceeds on‑chip shared memory bandwidth, the overall speed is dominated by these three memory traffic bursts.  Empirically one finds that even state‑of‑the‑art GEMM kernels consume only ~35 % of peak throughput on Hopper H100 GPUs when used for attention; the remainder is idle waiting for data to arrive.

In short, conventional attention reasons about *data* rather than computation: it pays for every entry it reads or writes with global memory traffic even if only a small coop­ment of that data is needed in subsequent stages.

## “FlashAttention” Tiling Strategy and Local Softmax Reduction  

FlashAttention replaces this pipeline with a *windowed* approach that eliminates all but one block of global memory traffic per attention block.  The key insights are:

1. **Tiling across sequence length**  
   The sequence dimension is partitioned into tiles of size \(B_r \times B_c\).  Instead of consuming whole matrices at once, each thread block processes only a small window: one block of queries (\(B_r\times d\)) against all blocks of keys/values (\(B_c\times d\)).  This allows all intermediates to live in shared memory or registers.

2. **Local softmax within tiles**  
   Consider a single tile pair: after computing 
   \[
   \tilde{\mathbf{S}} = \alpha\,\mathbf{Q}_i\,\mathbf{K}_j^\top,
   \]
   we can normalise row–wise using only values inside that tile plus an auxiliary scalar per row that aggregates results across all other tiles processed so far.  Mathematically  
   let
   \[
       m_i = \max_{k} S_{ik},\qquad 
       l_i = e^{m_{\text{old}}-m_i}l_{\text{old}} + 
              \sum_j e^{S_{ij}-m_i},
   \]
   where \(m_{\text{old}}\) and \(l_{\text{old}}\) store the maximum and summed exponentials from previous tiles.  These equations exactly preserve the softmax output because they implement *log‑sum–exp* recurrence:
   \[
       S_{ij}' = S_{ij} - m_i,\qquad
       P_{ij} = e^{S_{ij}'} / l_i .
   \]
   Thus after iterating over all key/value tiles we obtain
   \(P_{\text{full}}\) without ever materialising it in global memory.

3. **Fused compute kernel**  
   Within each thread block:
   
   - Load a query tile (\(B_r\times d\)) once from HBM into shared memory.
   - For each key/value tile:
     - Load key tile (\(B_c\times d\)) and value tile (\(B_c\times d\)) from HBM into shared memory.
     - Compute S via GEMM using WGMMA (Tensor Core instruction).
     - Update running maxima \(m_i\) and summed exponentials \(l_i\).
     - Compute *unscaled* probability tensor `tilde_P = exp(S-m_i)` on chip.
     - Update running output `O += tilde_P × V` using another GEMM.
   
   All intermediate tensors—`S`, `tilde_P`, partial `O`—are kept in registers or shared memory; only `O` for the final tile flips out to HBM once per query block.

4. **Avoiding global reductions**  
   Traditional attention would perform an extra reduction for softmax – summing all exponentials across rows – which would require an additional kernel launch or at least an extra write to HBM to gather partial sums from all blocks.  FlashAttention incorporates this reduction inside each tile’s prefix by maintaining two scalars per query row (`m_i` and `l_i`).  At launch exit the entries are rescaded by dividing by `l_i`.  The mathematics shows that combining partial sums in this way results in exactly the same final probabilities as performing softmax on full matrices.

Because every query vector engages with every key/value vector exactly once but never moves the large matrices outside L1/L2 caches or registers, FlashAttention reduces global traffic by roughly an order of magnitude: instead of writing four full-scale tensors (`Q`, `K`, `V`, `S`) it writes only two (`O`, auxiliary scalar vector).  

Operations that are inherently serial—like max reductions within rows—still dominate latency slightly; however they execute on specialized functional units (e.g., `__max()` with atomic combination).  Because these operations involve far fewer elements than GEMMs, they no longer become bottlenecks even on modern GPUs.

## Theoretical Justification That Fused Kernel Yields Exact Same Result as Separate Ops

The central claim behind FlashAttention is that its *windowed* local-softmax algorithm converges to exactly the same output as standard attention when performed in floating point arithmetic *with sufficient precision*.  We sketch why this holds:

1. **Associativity of exponentials in max+sum recurrence**  
   
    Let `row_ip` denote one row of `S` formed by concatenating partial scores from each key block:
    \[
       (\alpha\,q^\top k_0,\dots,\alpha\,q^\top k_m).
    \]
    For any real numbers \(x_k = S_{ik_k}\),
    define recursively
    \[
      m_0 = x_0,\quad l_0 = e^{x_0-m_0}=1,
    \]
    then for \(k>0\):
    \[
      m_k = \max(m_{k-1}, x_k),\\
      l_k = e^{m_{k-1}-m_k} l_{k-1} + e^{x_k-m_k}.
    \]
    By induction,
    if we write
    ```
    M_k := max{x_0,...,x_k}
    L_k := sum_{i=0}^{k} exp(x_i-M_k)
         = (sum_{i=0}^{k-1} exp(x_i-M_{k-1}))·exp(M_{k-1}-M_k)
           + exp(x_k-M_k)
         = L_{k-1}·exp(M_{k-1}-M_k)+exp(x_k-M_k),
    ```
    i.e., precisely our recurrence updates.
   
2. **Softmax invariant**  
   
    For each row,
    ```
        p_j = exp(x_j)/sum(exp(x))
            => p_j ∝ exp(x_j-M)/L,
          where M=max(x), L=sum(exp(x-M)).
    ```
   
    At completion (`k=m`) we have computed:
   
    - `m_m` equal to `M` (overall max).
    - `l_m` equal to `L`.
    
    Then recalculating probabilities using these scalars,
    
    ```
       p̂_j = exp(x_j-m_m)/l_m .
       
       Since m_m=M && l_m=L => p̂_j=p_j .
    ```
   
3. **Matrix form**  
   
    Transcribing back to tensors:
    
    For each query block index \(i\),
    
    - After iterating over all key blocks we have scalar arrays
      \(m_i^{[Nt]}\in\mathbb R^{B_r}\)
      and
      \(l_i^{[Nt]}\in\mathbb R^{B_r}\)
      derived by accumulated updates.
      
      These satisfy 
      \(m_i^{[Nt]}=\max_{\textall k}(s_{ik})=\operatorname{rowmax}(S)\),
      \(l_i^{[Nt]}=\sum_{\textall k}\exp(s_{ik}-m_i^{[Nt]})=e^{-\operatorname{rowmax}(S)}\,\,{\trianglelefteq }\!\!\bigoplus e^{{s}_{ik}}.\)

    - For any visited key/value tile pair we compute intermediate 
      ```
         tilde_P ≔ exp(S−m_cell)
         O_local += tilde_P × V .
      ```
      
      After rescaling at end with replicated\(l_i^{[Nt]}\),
      
      ```
         O_final ≔ O_local / l_i,
      ``$
      
      which recovers 
      
      ```
        O_final[i,:] =
        sum_over(k) softmax(S[i,k]) * V[k,:].
       
       because λ::∀i,j O_final[i,j]
         = sum_over(k)(exp(S[i,k]-m)*V[k,j]) / l
         = sum_over(k)(softmax(S[i,k])V[k,j]).
     ```

Thus every numeric step performed inside a tile follows exactly the rules used by separate attention kernels. No rounding error beyond what standard matrix multiplication already introduces occurs – except possibly due to reduced precision on lower‑precision FP8 modes – because:

- Each scalar update (`m`, `l`) uses exclusive OR operations which are associative if implemented correctly.
- Each exponential and division occurs after all accumulations within a row have been completed.
- Unity scaling factors preserve no additional error.

Consequently FlashAttention produces *exactly* the same tensor as naively executing three distinct kernels: first draw scores, next normalise them globally via row‑wise softmax, finally weight values.

## Summary

A naïve GPU implementation of transformer attention pays an expensive penalty for copying large intermediate matrices between global memory and registers/SME repeatedly.  FlashAttention’s innovation is judicious tiling coupled with a mathematically sound local softmax reduction that allows every intermediate value to remain resident on-chip until it is fully consumed. By fusing, quantizing potential variance across tiles stays minimal – rendering its result numerically identical to separate operations down to machine precision.\n

This baseline understanding not only explains why FlashAttention can match FP32 implementations while operating in FP16/FP8 but also sets up later chapters for more advanced optimisations—such as pipelining across warp groups and exploiting Tensor Core asynchrony—that further squeeze performance out of modern GPU hardware.

# FlashAttention‑2 Revisited: Algorithmic Foundations

Understanding modern GPU‐accelerated attention requires a deep dive into the algorithmic choices that underpin state‑of‑the‑art implementations. Among the most influential was **FlashAttention‑2**, which reimagined the canonical attention kernel by tiling over the sequence length dimension with key/value blocks. In this chapter we examine the forward and backward passes of FlashAttention‑2 in detail, analyze how its design interacts with GPU resource constraints and how it compares with high‑performance GEMM engines, and finally expose the performance bottlenecks that emerge when the algorithm is ported to newer architectures such as NVIDIA’s Hopper H100.

---

## Forward Pass in FlashAttention‑2 with Block Strategy

The conventional formulation of self‑attention involves three matrix multiplications:

1. **Score matrix**: $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$, an $N\times N$ matrix.
2. **Softmax reduction** (row‑wise): $\mathbf{P} = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$.
3. **Output**: $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, producing an $N\times d$ result.

FlashAttention‑2 removes intermediate materialization of $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ by fusing these three operations into a single kernel that operates on *tiles* of the matrices. Let us denote:

- $B_r$ : size of a query block (rows of $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{O}$).
- $B_c$ : size of a key/value block (rows of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$).
- $T_r = \lceil N/B_r\rceil$ : number of query tiles.
- $T_c = \lceil N/B_c\rceil$ : number of key/value tiles.

For a particular query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{B_r\times d}$, FlashAttention‑2 iterates over all key/value tiles:

```text
for j = 0 … T_c-1:
    // Load K_j, V_j into shared memory
    S_i^{(j)}   = Q_i · K_j^T           // MM1 (dot products)
    // Compute softmax statistics for this partial block:
    m_i   = max(m_i, rowmax(S_i^{(j)}))
    exp_S= exp(S_i^{(j)} - m_i)
    l_i   = l_i·exp(m_old - m_i) + row_sum(exp_S)
    // Apply accumulated scaling to O_i
    O_i     = O_i·exp(m_old - m_i) + exp_S · V_j  // MM2
```

The global score matrix never materializes; instead, *rowwise* maximums ($m_i$) and *row sums* ($\ell_i$) are updated on the fly as new blocks arrive. At the end of the loop we rescale $O_i$ by $\ell_i$, obtaining exactly the same result as the naive implementation but without writing or reading an $N\times N$ intermediate.

Because each threadblock processes one query tile ($B_r$ rows) it can be launched in parallel across all query tiles and all heads – a pattern that preserves high parallelism regardless of sequence length.

---

## Backward Pass Considerations

The backward pass entails three additional matrix multiplications derived from the chain rule:

\[
\begin{aligned}
d\!V &= P^\top dO ,\\
dP   &= dO\, V^\top ,\\
dS   &= {\rm dsoftmax}(dP) ,\\
dQ   &= \alpha\, dS\, K ,\\
dK   &= \alpha\, dS^\top Q .
\end{aligned}
\]

In terms of implementation, each derivative requires yet another group of GEMMs that mirror the forward pass structure:

| Derivative | GEMM pair | Memory pattern |
|------------|-----------|----------------|
| $dV$ | MM: $P^\top dO$ | columnwise on $P$, rowwise on $dO$. |
| $dK$ | MM: $dS^\top Q$ | similar to forward MM1 but transposed operands. |
| $dQ$ | MM: $dS K$ | similar to forward MM1 but using “downstream” scores. |

FlashAttention‑2 reuses its blocked layout here as well: for each head and query tile it iterates over key/value tiles again, loading them once into shared memory while streaming gradients through register files or shared space as needed. The backward algorithm thus suffers from similar memory traffic patterns as the forward pass but incurs more GEMMs due to recomputation – typically 2× more FLOPs than forward.

Because backpropagation must keep intermediate values (notably softmax partial sums), FlashAttention‑2 leverages FP32 accumulation for softmax operations even when inputs are FP16 or BF16; this practice preserves numerical accuracy without excessive memory overhead.

---

## Occupancy vs. Utilization: Comparison with H100 GEMM Kernels

GPU kernels compete for several scarce resources per Streaming Multiprocessor (SM):

- **Registers**: Each thread consumes a fixed number; large tile sizes or extra buffering can force spilling to global memory.
- **Shared Memory**: Shared tiling requires setting aside a portion of SMEM; too large buffer reduces threadblock occupancy.
- **Warps**: A kernel’s launch configuration determines how many active warps reside per SM; higher warp occupancy typically yields better latency hiding.

FlashAttention‑2’s design scales well across batch size and head dimension because each CTA is independent. However, when running on **Hopper H100 GPUs**, empirical measurement shows that overall utilization stalls at roughly **35 %**, whereas optimized dense GEMM kernels on the same device achieve **80–90 %** utilization.

This disparity emerges from multiple sources:

1. **Asynchrony Latency**  
   Hopper’s Tensor Cores now issue `WGMMA` asynchronously. In FlashAttention‑2 the kernel still waits for each matrix multiplication before proceeding to the next softmax step (`commit-and-wait` semantics). This serialization prevents overlapping computation with memory transfers (`TMA` copies), forcing parts of the kernel to idle while waiting for operand tiles or accumulator writes.

2. **Register Footprint**  
   The block‐wise approach consumes many registers per thread because each thread must hold:
   - Partial maxima & sums ($m_i$, $\ell_i$).
   - Intermediate partial results ($S^{(j)}$, exponentiated scores).
   - Accumulation buffers for output tiles ($O_i$).  
   On Hopper this exacerbates register pressure so many warps spill, lowering warp occupancy further.

3. **SMEM Usage vs WarpGroup Scheduling**  
   While each CTA can employ shared memory efficiently for its own tile, there is no explicit *warp specialization* – none of the warps are permanently dedicated to either data movement or computation. Consequently procedure calls get serialized inside warps instead using explicit `setmaxnreg` tuning that could allocate more registers to computation-warps and less to load-warps in future designs.

4. **Instruction Throughput Mismatch**
   The kernels for FLASH attention rely heavily on expensive exponential operations (`exp`) which are serviced by CUDA’s multi‐function units (MFU). On Hopper these units have ~16 per SM per cycle front—much less than what Tensor Cores deliver for matmul (~989 TFLOPS FP16). Because FlashAttention‑2 does not overlap MFU usage with pending matrix multiplications, MFU stalls remain idle during critical periods where tensor core pipelines are busy.

Collectively these factors produce an overall resource underutilization that is harder to mitigate once hardware evolves beyond what FlashAttention‑1 was originally engineered for.

---

## Identifying Performance Gaps on Hopper (35 % vs 80–90%) 

The exact percentages emerge from profiling both sets of kernels—standard dense GEMM versus FlashAttention\-2—in RTX/Ada/Hopper series GPUs:

| GPU Series | Average QE % for Dense GEMM | Average QE % for FlashAttention‑2 |
|------------|-----------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Ada         | 88 %                        | 35 %                             |
| Hopper      | 91 %                        | 35 %                             |

The discrete steps responsible for this gap can be enumerated:

1. **Synchronizations in WarpPerTile Loops**  
   Each iteration includes `bar.sync()` and commit points; on Hopper these become costlier because latency between SMEM load (TMA) and WGMMAs is longer due to new dispatch logic.

2. **No Asynchronous Overlap between TMA and WGMMA**  
   Unlike later designs (FlashAttention‐3), FlashAttention‐2 does not allow one warp group to prefetch next blocks while another is doing a MATmul; all warps use both TMA and WGMMAs serially.

3. **Limited Support for FP8 WGMMA in Blocking Layouts**  
   Even though Hopper can run FP8 TMMA directly from shared memory, FlashAttention‑2 only targets FP16/Tensor BF16; therefore it can’t exploit double throughput per core available in newer hardware.

4. **Early Stopping Due to Softmax Projectile**  
   The algorithm needs two consecutive passes over blocks—softmax then accumulation—before moving on; such dependency stalls amortize little unless overlapped.

These observations motivate four specific architectural redesigns in subsequent work:
- Warp specialization with *producer* / *consumer* roles.
- Overlapping softmax operations under asynchronous WGMMA pipelines (“pingpong” scheduling).
- Support for block quantization + incoherent processing enabling optimal FP8 usage.
- Adjustment of buffer reuse patterns in SMEM to reduce register pressure further.

In short, while FlashAttention‑2 pioneered a breakthrough by constructing attention completely within shared memory, its synchronous dataflow model underestimates critical opportunities introduced by Hopper’s advanced hardware features—hence its underwhelming utilization metrics compared with state-of-the-art dense GEMM kernels.*
