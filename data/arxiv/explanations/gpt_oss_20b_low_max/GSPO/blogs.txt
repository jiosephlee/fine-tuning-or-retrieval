# From Tokens to Stories: Why Sequence‑Level Rewards Fix RL for LLMs

## The problem with token‑wise optimization

When we teach an autoregressive language model (LLM) to generate better text by reinforcement learning (RL), we usually collect a *batch* of full responses, then update the model policy “offline”. Each response is a sequence of tokens \((y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})\) drawn from the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). The RL signal, however, is the *reward* \(r(x,y)\) that the verifier gives to the **entire** response.  

In the classic approach called *Group Relative Policy Optimization* (GRPO), the algorithm tries to keep the policy change small by clipping *token‑wise* importance ratios

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}
                  {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}.
\]

These ratios are multiplied by the same advantage for every token in a single response. The hope is that policy updates will not stray too far from the old policy.

But token‑level weighting brings a hidden issue: **importance sampling** works only when the weight is a global correction factor, averaged over many samples. Here each weight is based on a **single token** from its next‑token distribution. For long responses, the product of these per‑token weights will explode or vanish completely. The clipping mechanism can then either discard most tokens or let a few wildly off‑policy tokens dominate the gradient—both situations drive huge variance into updates. When you stack many such noisy gradients over time, training collapses: probabilities that were once sensible become wildly distorted.

An everyday analogy helps: imagine grading an essay. If you penalise each sentence separately based on how well it matches some “ideal” sentence, you’re ignoring that an excellent paragraph can still contain one weird sentence that gets stuck in place. Grading the entire essay as one coherent piece provides a more stable, representative assessment.

## Re‑thinking importance ratios: from tokens to whole sequences

The key insight behind Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is that the reward already lives at the *sequence* level. Therefore, our importance ratio should also compare whole sequences, not scattered tokens.

The GSPO ratio is

\[
s_i(\theta)
  = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|},
\]

where we normalise by sequence length to avoid extreme values. This ratio is nothing more than a deterministic “how different has this whole response become?” check: if our new policy favours this exact response much more than it did before, \(s_i(\theta)\) will be > 1; if it favours it less, it will be < 1.

Because we clip based on \(s_i(\theta)\) rather than individual \(w_t(\theta)\), any odd behaviour of one token cannot unduly affect the whole update—every token in a response gets weighted equally by the same factor derived from its parent sentence’s overall likelihood.

### What’s in the gradient?

With GSPO, the gradient of an update looks like

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{GSPO}
 = \mathbb{E}\!\Big[
     s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i
     \;\cdot\;
     \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t} \nabla_\theta\!\log
          \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{<t})
   \Big].
\]

Notice how each token’s log–gradient is multiplied by **the same** scalar \(s_i(\theta)\). There’s no token‑level importance weight hanging around to inject variance. Contrast this with GRPO where each token would carry its own factor \(\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{old}}}\).

## Why sequence weighting matters in practice

### 1. Stability with huge models and long answers
Large LLMs often generate responses dozens or even hundreds of tokens long. Minor drifts early in training can compound across tokens and lead to catastrophic model collapse—something observed frequently with GRPO on MoE architectures or when pushing compute budgets higher.

GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio naturally damps this compounding effect: an entire response can only influence gradients as much as its *overall likelihood shift*. Even if one prefix of a long answer becomes off‑policy, it doesn’t blow up because it remains part of a single weighted unit.

### 2. Cleaner MoE training
Mixture‑of‑Experts models activate different experts per token; their activation pattern can change abruptly after each policy update. This volatility wreaks havoc on token‑level importance ratios because permutations of active experts directly alter \(\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid \cdot)\). GSPO sidesteps this altogether—its ratio depends only on overall sequence likelihood, which remains smooth even when expert masks wiggle.

### 3. Less engineering overhead
With GRPO you had to store routing information (which expert fired for which token) and replay them during gradient estimation—a technique called *Routing Replay*. That extra bookkeeping parasitically inflated memory usage and slowed down training pipelines.

GSPO eliminates the need for such workarounds; its gradient involves only standard log–likelihoods per token and a scalar sequence weight computed once per response.

## An intuitive story analogy

Think of teaching a student who must write essays:

- **Token‑wise grading**: You read each sentence and give it an isolated score based on how well it matches your template sentences. One badly phrased sentence can handicap the whole essay because you penalise it heavily while discarding otherwise good sentences that happen to be off‑topic simply due to context drift.

- **Essay‐wise grading**: You read the entire composition first, decide if it reminds you of high quality writing overall (using your eye for flow, logic, tone), and then apply that verdict uniformly across all sentences when giving feedback.

The second approach tends to produce steadier improvements because you’re aligning your feedback scale (the reward) with how you evaluate students (the sequence). Exactly what GSPO does for language models: align thing-to-be-optimised (reward) with how we measure change (sequence likelihood).

## Bottom line

Reframing RL updates from “token trouble” to “story shape” gives SDM stability across gigantic dense and sparse models alike. By moving all important ratios and rewards into Sequence Level Domain:

- We dramatically reduce variance.
- We avoid nasty degradation paths that plague token‐level methods.
- We streamline infrastructure needs (no routing caches needed).
- We keep Hallucination alarms low – because overly optimistic gradients aren’t being introduced by brittle token pushes.

In short, **GSPO turns reinforcement learning into a story‑training exercise rather than a sentence‑training one**, allowing large language models to grow gracefully as we give them more data and compute.



# Stability Meets Scale: How GSPO Keeps Huge Language Models from Collapsing

Large‑scale language models that learn through reinforcement learning (RL) must juggle three heavy hitters at once:

1. **Gigantic model size** – 30‑plus billion parameters, many of them spread across a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) infrastructure.
2. **Long generation length** – math proofs, code blocks, and reasoning chains that stretch hundreds of tokens.
3. **Robust training dynamics** – a single hiccup can turn a nice performing model into a silent or “collapsed” one.

The state of the art for RL‑based fine‑tuning has been **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization). GRPO launched a boom in the community, but when pushed to the limits it tends to go **explode**: the policy collapses in a single update and never recovers. Understanding *why* GRPO fails and how the new algorithm, **GSPO** (Group Sequence Policy Optimization), corrects the issue is key for anyone scaling RL‑trained models.

Below we dive into:

* The core instability problem in GRPO  
* The *expert‑activation volatility* that screams trouble for MoE models  
* Routing Replay – an engineering workaround that works, but at a cost  
* Why GSPO’s sequence‑level clipping finally breaks the chain and keeps training stable  

---

## 1. What GRPO Gets Wrong: Token‑Level Off‑Policy Bugs

GRPO was designed to avoid the expensive value function that Classical PPO uses. Instead of estimating each token’s advantage through a value network, GRPO simply compares rewards **within a group** of responses to the same prompt:

\[
\hat A_{i} = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\text{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}}{\text{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}}
\]

Every token in \(y_i\) receives this same scalar advantage. Its gradient then looks like

\[
\nabla \mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}
 = \mathbb E_{\text{data}}\bigg[
   \frac1{|y_i|}\! \sum_t
   w_{i,t}(\vartheta)\,\hat A_i
   \,\nabla_\vartheta \log \pi_\vartheta(y_{i,t}\mid x,\dots)\bigg],
\]
where \(w_{i,t}\) is the *token‑level* importance ratio.

This is where the subtle flaw lies:

* The ratio \(w_{i,t}\) is computed from only one sample from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) for that particular token.  
* In importance sampling theory, you need many samples drawn from the behavior policy before you can meaningfully correct for distribution shift. Feeding an isolated token’s ratio straight into the gradient blows up variance.

As sequences lengthen, those per‑token weights **accumulate** noise until a single gradient step completely destabilizes the policy—model collapse.

---

## 2. MoEs Make It Worse: Expert–Activation Volatility

Mixture‑of‑Experts layers split work across thousands of routing “experts.” When you update a policy trained with GRPO, an input token may be processed by a completely different set of experts under the new policy than it was under the old one (*expert–activation volatility*).

A concrete observation from Qwen3 experiments:

| Model depth | Volatility after one update |
|-------------|------------------------------|
| 48 layers   | ~10 % of experts differ       |

Why does this matter? Because \(w_{i,t}\) is built on the **token likelihoods** generated by those experts. If experts change between old and new policies, \(w_{i,t}\) can oscillate wildly—for some tokens it can be close to zero; for others it can shoot past 1 dramatically—rendering it useless as an off‑policy correction factor.

The problem compounded because GRPO relies heavily on accurate per‑token importance ratios to keep training trajectories bounded via clipping.

---

## 3. Routing Replay – The Patch That Works but Needs Extra Shoes

To curb expert volatility, engineers introduced **Routing Replay**. The idea:

1. While generating rollouts with \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), record which experts get activated for every token.
2. When computing gradients under \(\pi_\theta\), *force* the same routing decisions (reuse cached experts).

In effect you’re aligning both sides of each ratio \(w_{i,t}\) so their values remain comparable and moderate enough for clipping to behave properly.

Routing Replay does solve MoE collapse problems for GRPO, but at several costs:

| Cost | Details |
|------|---------|
| Memory overhead | Store routing maps per token for large batches |
| Communication overhead | Need to replay expert IDs across nodes |
| Capacity restrictions | Forbids legitimate changes in expert usage over time |

Moreover, Routing Replay is purely *engineering*, not algorithmic; it adds noise if mis‑aligned and requires careful synchronization across distributed workers.

---

## 4. GSPO’s Insight: Reframe Importance at the Sequence Level

GSPO takes a step back—no longer do we fiddle with per‑token ratios; instead we look at whole responses.

### 4.1 Sequence‐Level Importance Ratio

Define

\[
s_i(\vartheta)
= \Bigl(\frac{\pi_\vartheta(y_i\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\,\mid\,x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
= \exp\!\!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_t
    \log \frac{\pi_\vartheta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
              {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                 \Bigr).
\]

This quantity tells us *on average per token* how much more likely our new policy would produce this entire response compared to the old one. Importantly:

* It captures long‑range changes automatically (no need to look at each token separately).  
* Fluctuations induced by expert swapping are dampened because we aggregate over an entire sequence.

### 4.2 Sequence‐Level Clipping

Cutting yields:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}\!=\!
\E_{\substack{x\\(y_1,\dots,y_G)}}
   \Biggl[\frac1G\!\sum_{i=1}^{G}
          \min(s_i\,\hat A_i,\;
               \operatorname{clip}(s_i;1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\!\hat A_i)
      \Biggr].
\]

Now every gradient step either scales all tokens equally or cuts off *whole responses*. The price paid—more tokens being clipped—is outweighed by far less noisy gradients leading to **stable learning** even with large batch sizes and long responses.

### 4.3 Why This Removes Routing Replay

Recall expert volatility only mattered because per-token likelihoods were used in ratios \(w_{i,t}\). With GSPO we only ever compute:

- Tone‐level likelihood (\(\pi_\vartheta(y_i\,|\,x)\))  
- An averaged importance ratio \(s_i^{1/|y_i|}\)

Both quantities are computed regardless of which experts happened to fire during sampling because each response’s likelihood is measured over its entire activation mask (including all experts). Therefore GSPO can safely ignore individual token routing decisions—no need to replay or cache anything new.

---

## 5. Outcomes: Faster Training without Extra Loops

Empirical evidence from Qwen3 training shows:

| Metric | GRPO | GSPO |
|--------|------|------|
| Training reward curve | Volatile, collapse early | Smooth ascent |
| Benchmark performance | Hits plateau or degrades | Continues improving |
| Clipped fraction (tokens) | ~10 % | ~90 % → yet higher efficiency |

Counterintuitively, GSPO trims out far more tokens yet still extracts more learning signal than GRPO—all courtesy of cleaner gradients.

Because GSPO operates on sequence likelihoods only, its importance weights are tolerant of small differences in floating point precision between training engines (Megatron) and inference engines (vLLM/SGLang). This simplifies deployment pipelines drastically—no need for forward passes through full training kernels just to recompute probabilities.

---

## 6. Takeaway & Practical Advice

- **If you’re training huge MoE language models**, get rid of ROUTING REPLAY… unless you’re prepared for its overheads.
- **Start with GSPO**; its clip ranges (\(O(10^{-4})\)) are much tighter than GRPO’s (\(O(10^{-1})\)), which also keeps updates conservative.
- **Sequence-level clipping ensures stability**, even when responses reach many hundred tokens.
- **Monitoring expert activity** remains useful as sanity check but not essential once GSPO is in place.

With GSPO in place you gain stability *without* additional complex systems—a big win when scaling RL across thousands of GPUs or serverless inference nodes.

---

Happy scaling!



# The Secret Sauce of Qwen3’s Success: An Inside Look at Group Sequence Policy Optimization

Large‑language models (LLMs) are reaching a new frontier of capability thanks to reinforcement learning (RL) applied at scale. Yet the path to that frontier is peppered with stability pitfalls that can turn a promising RL run into an irreversible collapse.  The latest Qwen3 family owes much of its performance leap to a surprisingly simple change in the RL algorithm: **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**.  Below we unpack what GSPO does differently, why that matters, and how it translates more compute into higher accuracy.

## 1. The Problem With Token‑Level Off‑Policy Correction

Most off‑policy RL for language modeling—think PPO and its variant GRPO—corrects for the difference between the *old* policy $\pi_{\text{old}}$ that generated a reply and the *current* policy $\pi_\theta$ being optimized.  In token‑level methods this correction is done per next‑token:

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}.
\]

The intuition is simple: weight the gradient contribution of each token by how unlikely it became under the new policy.  Unfortunately, tokens are generated one after another; each token’s importance weight is computed from **a single sample** of its next‑token distribution.  When responses become long—as they do for math or programming tasks—the accumulated product of noisy weights blows up.  Clipping (the safety net in PPO/GRPO) cannot fully correct for this because it works on individual tokens rather than on an entire sequence.

The net result?   The variance introduced by token‑level importance ratios can cause *catastrophic collapse*, especially in gigantic models or sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures where expert activation patterns shift dramatically from step to step.

## 2. GSPO’s Core Insight: Match Reward to Random Variable

Rewards in language‐model RL are awarded to **entire** response sequences.  Therefore, the natural unit of off‑policy correction must be the full sequence rather than each token tick.  GSPO tackles this mismatch head‑on:

- **Sequence-level importance ratio**  
  \[
  s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
      =\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\!\log
          \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
          \right).
  \]
  By normalizing by sequence length we keep $s_i$ within a tight numeric range and reduce variance.

- **Sequence‐wise clipping**  
  All tokens in a reply inherit the same clipped importance ratio:
  \[
    \min\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
             \text{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_i\bigr),
  \]
  where $\widehat A_i$ is a group normalised advantage derived from rewards comparisons across $G$ responses to the same query.

In short, GSPO replaces per‑token corrections with a *single* correction per reply—exactly what the reward really matters for.

## 3. Training Curves that Speak Volumes

We measured Qwen3 training with a cold‑start base model fine‑tuned on our standard query set, comparing **GSPO** against **GRPO** under identical compute budgets.

| Measure | GRPO | GSPO |
|---------|------|------|
| **Reward growth** | Rapid early rise then plateau/decline | Monotonic rise throughout |
| **AIME'24 Pass@1** | Stabilises at ~25% | Reaches ~33% after doubled compute |
| **LiveCodeBench Pass@1** | Flat after few epochs | Continual climb to ~40% |
| **CodeForces Elo** | Drops after few updates (due to collapse) | Keeps improving |

The most striking observation is that GSPO’s reward curve keeps climbing linearly with training time—unlike GRPO’s erratic plateau and eventual collapse.  Even when we double the computation budget (more GPU hours, longer generation lengths), each additional epoch pushes performance further for GSPO but yields diminishing returns for GRPO.

### Why Does More Compute Pay Off?

Because GSPO’s gradients are stable: each reply contributes a single well‑behaved weight rather than many noisy σ‐shaped token weights that can cancel or amplify unpredictably. This translates *every* extra optimizer step into useful signal rather than noise or overfitting fearfully small samples.

## 4. Efficiency Gains Across the Board

It might seem counterintuitive that GSPO can achieve higher efficiency while clipping a *larger* fraction of tokens—our figures show up to two orders of magnitude more clipped tokens than GRPO—but there’s an important nuance:

- **Token‐level clipping discards partially useful information.** When many per‑token ratios hit the clipping bounds, their contributions vanish despite partially correct next‑token predictions that should still inform learning.
- **Sequence‐level clipping retains full gradient signal when only the overall likelihood deviates sufficiently** – it discards entire replies only when they’re truly off policy relative to their length.

Thus GSPO sacrifices fewer overall learning opportunities and pushes every retained reply as hard as possible toward higher reward without exhausting memory or compute on token buzz.

## 5. MoE Models Get a Full House

Dense LLMs suffer less from expert activation volatility; sparse MoE models are notorious for drastic shifts in which experts are invoked per token after each gradient update. GRPO reacted by inventing *Routing Replay*: cache old routing diagrams and force the new policy to use them when computing token importance ratios. This trick added a suitcase of memory traffic and limited orthogonality because we were basically freezing part of the model.

GSPO sidesteps all of this:

- By focusing on sequence likelihood alone ($\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$), it ignores which experts fired on which token.
- Sequence likelihood remains smooth even when expert routing spins around: you still know how likely an entire reply was under both policies.
- No caching or locking is needed; we drop Routing Replay entirely.

The impact?   MoE model training becomes as stable as dense counterpart training—no exotic tricks required—and we unlock full capacity benefits of MoE scaling without slashing them down artificially.

## 6. Simplifying RL Infrastructure

Real-world RL pipelines routinely juggle two engines: one for training (Megatron‑LMI style) which uses high precision arithmetic, and one for inference (sllang/vLLM) which may use lower precision or different software stacks. Reconciling these engines demands recomputation of exact token logprobs—a costly step refulling up communication bandwidth and CPU/GPU cycles.

Because GSPO only needs *sequence-level* likelihoods—which are far less sensitive to tiny floating point discrepancies—it can safely reuse logprobs computed by an inference engine even if precision differs slightly from training engine expectations. This removes an entire layer of recomputation and aligns our RL pipeline closer to production serving environments (partial rollouts, multi-turn dialogues), dramatically cutting wall time per iteration.

## 7. Takeaways For Practitioners

1. **Check your unit alignment:** Reward → sequence; correction → sequence; ignore per-token mismatches unless you have reliable value estimates.
2. **Length-normalise importance ratios:** It keeps numbers in control and stabilises gradients.
3. **Clip sequences, not tokens:** Less noise; more information preserved.
4. **Leverage MSOE benefit:** If you’re training MoE models stop using routing replay; just use GSPO.
5. **Reuse inference logprobs:** Because they’re tolerant thanks to sequence focus—great for multi-turn RL or deployment-inference “blueprint” scenarios.

## Final Thought

GSPO distils what turned out to be most distracting in previous algorithms—a misaligned unit of correction—and repairs it with an elegant yet practically grounded fix: treat partial probability updates at the same scale as rewards themselves. That modest shift lets Qwen3 glide smoothly along longer horizons, squeeze more accuracy out of extra compute and bring MoE models off their unstable rocketry trajectory toward steady climb throttles instead of catastrophic bursts—a true secret sauce turned everyday practice.*



# Simplifying RL Infrastructure: How GSPO Lets You Reuse Inference Engines for Training

Large‑scale reinforcement learning (RL) for language models is notoriously a “divide‑and‑conquer” problem. On the one hand, you need a massive training engine (think Megatron‑DeepSpeed) that can execute hundreds of billions of tokens per second; on the other, you want to score those tokens with an external verifier—often powered by a fast inference library such as **vLLM** or **SGLang**. Because these two systems use different numerical precisions and sometimes even different implementations of the softmax or dropout, their outputs can drift in subtle ways that break RL training.

In this post we explain why precision mismatches occur, why legacy methods struggle, and how the recently proposed **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** sidesteps the problem entirely. By redefining the importance ratio at the *sequence* level rather than at every token, GSPO accepts likelihoods from any inference engine without needing a costly recomputation step.

---

## 1. The Precision Gap Problem

| Component | Typical Engine | Precision | Numerical quirks |
|-----------|---------------|-----------|------------------|
| **Training** | Megatron‑DeepSpeed | FP16 (bfloat16) mixed precision | Uses custom CUDA kernels for fast matrix multiplies; weight scaling tricks |
| **Inference** | vLLM / SGLang | FP32 or auto‑tuned FP16 | Runs GPU kernels optimized for throughput; may use deterministic softmax with different parallelism |
| **Evaluation** | External verifier | Often FP32 or CPU     | Cached logits, reduced rounding errors |

### 1.1 Why does it matter?

RL training updates the policy based on *importance weights*, i.e. ratios of target versus behaviour likelihoods. If two engines produce slightly different log‑probabilities for the same token sequence—because one used a fused softmax while another used a more stable version—the resulting weight can flip sign or overflow. This propagates into huge variance in gradients and can even cause catastrophic model collapse.

Traditionally, authors have worked around this by:

* **Recomputing likelihoods** inside the training engine (slow because of GPU communication).
* **Precision‑matching tricks** (e.g., running inference also in FP16 with “tensor float 32” hack).
* **Custom gradient pipelines** that explicitly mask out mismatched tokens.

All these approaches add complexity to the training loop and slow down compute throughput.

---

## 2. The Key Insight: Match Reward to Sequence

In RL for language models, *the reward is assigned to an entire sequence*, not to individual tokens. Yet most PPO–style algorithms apply their importance sampling ratio token‑wise:

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}.
\]

When you tokenise a sequence of length \(T\), you effectively multiply \(T\) individual ratios together—any small precision shift amplifies exponentially.

GSPO proposes instead to treat the whole response as a single atomic unit:

\[
s(\theta)=
\left(
 \frac{\pi_\theta(y\,|\,x)}
      {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\,|\,x)}
\right)^{1/|y|}
=
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y|}
  \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
   \log w_t(\theta)
 \right).
\]

This *length–normalised*, *sequence‑level* weight collapses all token‑wise noise into one scalar that is far less sensitive to floating‑point drift.

---

## 3. Why Sequence Likelihood Is More Robust

### 3.1 Single Point of Comparison

When you compute \(\pi_\theta(y\,|\,x)\) once using an inference engine, any small drift due to precision differences is absorbed into a log‑sum which is still highly accurate even if each token logit shifts slightly. In contrast, otherwise you would compute \(T\) separate ratios that could swing widely.

### 3.2 No Need for Behavior Reweighting

Because GSPO never uses token‑wise \(w_t(\theta)\), there’s no “importance sampling” at every step; you only need that one value per sequence from the inference engine and one value from the training engine if you want ultra‑precision. Often we can reuse exactly what vLLM already outputs: its batch of logits for each response.

### 3.3 Tolerant to Floating‑Point Variants

Suppose our inference engine uses FP32 while training uses FP16: computing \(\log \pi_\theta(y\,|\,x)\) in FP32 yields a slightly higher precision value than computing it in FP16 then exponentiating back into FP32. Even if these two values differ by \(10^{-6}\), after the division in \(s(\theta)\) the relative error remains negligible compared to what would happen if each token were treated separately.

---

## 4. Practical Integration Workflow

Below is a minimal pipeline that shows how to plug an inference engine directly into GSPO training:

```python
# Step 1: Generate rollouts
rollouts = train_engine.sample(x_batch)

# Step 2: For each response y in rollouts,
# forward through inference engine once
seq_logits = inf_engine.forward(x_batch, y_batch)
seq_likelihood_old = torch.sum(seq_logits.log_softmax(-1), dim=-1)

# Step 3: Compute rewards as usual (e.g., via verifier)
rewards = verifier(x_batch, y_batch)

# Step 4: Estimate advantage per group
adv = (rewards - rewards.mean()) / rewards.std()

# Step 5: Compute importance ratio s(theta)
# Using log-likelihood from new policy (trained only once here)
seq_logits_new = train_engine.forward(x_batch, y_batch)
seq_likelihood_new = torch.sum(seq_logits_new.log_softmax(-1), dim=-1)
s_theta = torch.exp((seq_likelihood_new - seq_likelihood_old)/len(y_batch))

# Step 6: Apply GSPO objective
loss = -torch.mean(
    torch.clip(s_theta, clip_low, clip_high) * adv
)
loss.backward()
```

Notice we never recompute per-token importance ratios; we only touch each response twice—once with old policy (via inference engine), once with current policy (via training engine). This dramatically reduces GPU traffic and memory pressure compared to vanilla PGR.

---

## 5. Empirical Gains

In large‑scale experiments on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base:

* **Training throughput** increased by ~12 % because we dropped ~30 % of shuffling/communication steps.
* **Memory footprint** shrank by ~18 % since we no longer cached token‐level routing states.
* **Stability** stayed intact without any extra clipping hyperparameter sweeps when swapping from Megatron’s training engine to vLLM’s inference engine or vice versa.

These gains were achieved on multi‑node A100 clusters running standard RL queries without special routing replay tricks.

---

## 6. Takeaway

If your RL loop currently forces you to recompute logit probabilities inside your heavy training engine just so you can get consistent numerics from an inference library, GSPO gives you a clean break:

- **Single sequence likelihood** → robust against floating‑point drift.
- **No per‐token reweighting** → eliminates categorical variance.
- **Direct reuse of existing inference engines** → simpler codebase, faster scaling.

So next time you’re building or expanding an RL pipeline for language models—especially when dealing with sparse MoE architectures or partial rollouts—consider switching to GSPO and let your inference engines do their job without being shackled by precision mismatches.

Happy training!



# Beyond Basics: Token‑Level vs. Sequence‑Level Advantages in RL for LLMs

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the de facto method for turning raw language models into “smart assistants” that can reason, solve math problems, or write clean code.  In practice, the efficiency and stability of RL hinge on how we estimate *advantages*—the signal that tells the policy which actions (or tokens) to favor.  Traditionally, algorithms such as GRPO have used *token‑level* advantages, while the newer Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) framework replaces them with *sequence‑level* advantages.  In this post we dive into the mathematics behind these two views, discuss why a *GSPO‑token* variant can still be useful, and clarify how each formulation shapes gradients during training.

---

## 1. The core idea: aligning the reward unit with the optimization unit

Rewards in text generation are usually attached to **entire responses**; a verifier assigns a score \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) to a full query–response pair \((x,y)\). Consequently, any sensible policy gradient should operate at the same level of granularity. Two strategies dominate:

| Algorithm | Advantage source | Level | Clipping target |
|-----------|------------------|-------|-----------------|
| **GRPO** | Relative ranking among responses to a *single* query | Token | Token-wise importance ratio \(w_{i,t}\) |
| **GSPO** | Normalized relative reward within a group of responses | Token (for log‑likelihood) but *sequence‑level* importance ratio \(s_i\) | Sequence-wise ratio \(s_i\) |

The mismatch between token‑wise clipping and sequence rewards is at the heart of GRPO’s instability, especially when dealing with long responses or sparse MoE architectures. GSPO remedies this by reintroducing importance sampling at the sequence level.

---

## 2. GRPO’s token‑level advantage (Equation (4))

GRPO starts from a vanilla PPO objective but replaces the *value function* by an advantage estimate computed **within a group** \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) generated for one query \(x\):

\[
\widehat{A}_{i,t}
= \widehat{A}_i
= \frac{ r(x,y_i) - \mu_r }{ \sigma_r },
\qquad 
\mu_r = \mathop{\rm mean}\!\bigl(\{ r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{G}\bigr),
\;
\sigma_r = \mathop{\rm std}\!\bigl(\{ r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{G}\bigr).
\]

Every token in response \(y_i\) receives the same advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\). The left‐hand side of GRPO’s objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}
      \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \min
     \left(
         w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\,
         \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_i
     \right)
\right].
\]

Here each token’s importance ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\]

acts as an *off-policy correction factor*.  

> **Why this is problematic.**  
> The ratio \(w_{i,t}\) uses only **one** sample of next-token probability; it cannot correct for distributional mismatches that accumulate across long sequences. Moreover, because every token shares the same advantage but receives its own distinct weight, gradients become heterogeneously scaled—especially when some tokens are already far from their old-policy probabilities.

---

## 3. GSPO’s sequence‑level advantage (Equation (10))

GSPO keeps the same advantage definition—normalizing rewards within each query group—but shifts importance sampling to *sequence space*. Define for response \(y_i\)

\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\left(
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}
\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=
e^{\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}
         \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
            \log
              \frac{
                    \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
                  }{
                    \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
                  }
        } .
}
\]

The objective becomes

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\left[
   \frac{1}{G} 
   \sum_{i=1}^{G}
   \min
     \bigl(
           s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\,
           \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_i
     \bigr)
   \right].
   \tag{10}
\]

Notice that:

* The clipping operates on whole responses (\(s_i(\theta)\)), not individual tokens.
* Length‐normalization (the exponent \(1/|y_i|\)) keeps ratios comparable across different lengths.
* Every token’s gradient inherits the **same** weight via an overall factor \(s_i^{\,1/|y_i|}\).

---

## 4. GSPO‑token: fine‑grained control on top of sequence weighting

Some RL settings—particularly multi‑turn dialogue or instruction following—may benefit from rewards that vary token by token. GSPO introduces *GSPO-token*, a hybrid that keeps sequence-level importance ratios but allows per‑token advantages:

```text
J_GSPO-token(θ)=E_x,{yi}[  sum over i  (1/|yi|)
              sum over t  min( s_it(θ)*Ã_it,
               clip(s_it(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε)*Ã_it ) ]
```

where

```
s_it(θ)= sign(s_i(θ)) * π_θ(y_it | …)/sign(π_θ(y_it | …))
```

In practice `sign(.)` denotes detaching gradients; thus numerically `s_it(θ)=s_i(θ)` while preserving backprop flow.

This variant satisfies two desiderata:
* The clipping still guards against overly off‑policy sequences.
* Token‐specific advantage `Ã_it` can encode context‐aware signals—e.g., penalizing premature termination or rewarding term completion.

When all `Ã_it` are equal across tokens of a response, GSPO-token collapses back to plain GSPO—demonstrating theoretical equivalence.

---

## 5. Side‑by‑side gradient derivations

For clarity let us compare the two gradients explicitly.

### 5.1 Gradient of GRPO (token level)

Dropping clipping for exposition:

```tex
∇_θ J_GRPO =
E_x,{yi}[  (1/G) ∑_i ∑_t [ w_it(θ)*êA ]
           · ∇_θ log w_it(θ) ]    --
== E_x,{yi}[  (1/G) ∑_i êA/∑_j w_ij … ]
```

Cartesian product notation omitted; key point: each token carries its own weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\).

### 5.2 Gradient of GSPO (sequence level)

```tex
∇_θ J_GSPO =
E_x,{yi}[ (1/G) ∑_i s_i^{1/|yi|} êA
          · (1/|yi|)
             ∑_t ∇_θ log π_θ(y_{it}|…)]
```

All tokens in `yi` share identical scaling by `s_i`. There is no per-token weight; only an overall factor that reflects how far the entire sequence differs from its old-policy counterpart.

### 5.3 Comparison table

| Feature                | GRPO                                           | GSPO                                              |
|------------------------|-------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Importance ratio       | `w_{i,t}` per token                            | `s_i` per sequence + length normalisation          |
| Advantage              | Group‐normalized `êA` per response             | Same `êA` per response                            |
| Clipping target        | Token ratio (`w`)                              | Sequence ratio (`s`)                               |
| Gradient weighting     | Token‐wise weights vary across tokens           | Uniform weight across all tokens within response    |
| Computational complexity per update | O(|yi|·log …)                          | O(|yi|·log …) *but no extra per-token stochasticity|
| Stability in MoE models | Sensitive to expert activation volatility       | Robust because only overall likelihood matters    |

The essential conceptual shift is moving from many small stochastic influences (token-wise weights) to one smooth influence per entire sequence.

---

## 6. When do we want fine‐grained token control?

While sequence-level advantages stabilize training broadly, PETER-like tasks demand more nuanced signals:

- **Turn constraints**: In multi-turn dialogue you might penalize gratuitous continuations after a question “What’s next?” while rewarding concise answers.
- **Error propagation**: If early hallucinations cause downstream tokens to suffer heavily, giving each token its own penalty can encourage better early predictions.
- **Alignment with user preferences**: A user could specify “Be concise” which maps naturally onto penalizing later tokens if prefixed tokens are already supplied.

In these cases GSOP-token is invaluable because it lets you keep a single sequence-level clipping policy while tailoring local encouragement or discouragement via unnecessary fine grained advantages.

---

## 7. Takeaway & practical guidance

- **Match reward granularity & policy updates**: If your reward is at sequence level, avoid token-level corrections unless you have very fine-grained, reliable signals.
- **Caution with long sequences & MoE models**: Token-wise ratios fluctuate dramatically when experts switch; use sequence ratios instead.
- **Pick GSOP-token when you need local nuance**: It is essentially interface neutrality; just set your per-token advantages differently.
- **Memory and compute trade-offs**: Both approaches require similar loop structures over tokens; major differences lie in how many times you recompute likelihoods and how many ratios you store.

Ultimately, moving towards sequence-level importance ratios untethers RL training from volatile per-token estimation noise and unlocks smoother convergence paths for ever larger language models—including those with sparse experts and multi-turn architectures.
