\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why are the clipping ranges in GSPO two orders of magnitude smaller than those in GRPO?
Question:
In §4.1 GSPO uses clipping ranges like $[3	imes10^{-4}, 4	imes10^{-4}]$ whereas GRPO uses [0.2, 0.27]. The paper notes that these numbers are chosen after careful tuning but does not explain why such a drastic difference is necessary. Could someone elaborate on what property of $s_i(	heta)$ (vs. $w_{i,t}(	heta)$) dictates that we must use much tighter clipping? Does this relate to the variance reduction from length normalization or something else?
Answer:
**Why does GSPO use an epsilon that is two orders of magnitude smaller than GRPO’s?**

The clipping range in a PPO‑style algorithm is not a hyperparameter that can be chosen arbitrarily; it must match the *scale* of the importance ratios that the algorithm is actually optimizing.  
In GRPO the importance ratio is defined **per token**

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\;,
$$

so each token receives its own weight.  
These weights can vary wildly: a token that is highly unlikely under the old policy but highly likely under the new policy can push $w_{i,t}$ to *huge* values, whereas a token that is very likely under both policies will have a ratio close to 1. Consequently, when we clip $w_{i,t}$ we must allow a fairly wide interval $[1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon]$ (in practice $\varepsilon\approx 0.2\text{-}0.27$) to avoid discarding too many tokens and to keep the clipped value from saturating.

In contrast, GSPO defines the importance ratio **at the sequence level**

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}=
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr).
$$

Two things happen here:

1. **Length‑normalisation.**  
   Raising the sequence‑level likelihood ratio to the power $1/|y_i|$ forces $s_i(\theta)$ to be close to 1 regardless of the length of the response. A long response whose per‑token ratios are each slightly greater than 1 would otherwise produce an astronomically large product; taking the geometric mean keeps $s_i(\theta)$ in a narrow band around unity.

2. **Smoothing of extreme token ratios.**  
   Because $s_i(\theta)$ is an *average* (log‑average) of all per‑token ratios in a response, extreme outliers are damped out. The typical spread of $s_i(\theta)$ across responses is an order of magnitude smaller than that of any single $w_{i,t}(\theta)$.

As a result, in GSPO all sequences lie roughly in $[0.997,\;1.003]$. To prevent drastic weight changes while still allowing useful policy updates we therefore clip with a tiny epsilon, e.g.

$$
[\,3\times10^{-4},\,4\times10^{-4}\,].
$$

If we were to use a larger epsilon on $s_i(\theta)$, almost every sequence would survive clipping unchanged, which would dramatically reduce bias and increase variance—the very instability that GSPO was designed to eliminate. Conversely, using too small an epsilon would throw away almost every sequence and impede learning.

---

### Connection to variance reduction

Length normalisation was introduced for exactly this reason: it removes one source of high variance caused by different response lengths (a few tokens changing drastically shift the product but not its mean). The tight clipping range therefore complements this stabilization – it keeps every retained sequence’s update magnitude comparable and prevents the “exploding/vanishing” gradients that plague GRPO when tokens with vastly different importance ratios are multiplied together.

---

### Bottom line

- **GRPO**: per‑token ratios with high dynamic range → needs large clipping range ($\varepsilon\approx0.2$–$0.27$).  
- **GSPO**: length‑normalized sequence ratio with small dynamic range → uses tiny clipping range ($\varepsilon\approx3$–$4\times10^{-4}$).

The two‑order‑of‑magnitude difference is thus not arbitrary but dictated by the mathematical properties of the two definitions of importance weights: only GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio stays safely close to one after normalisation, enabling very tight clipping without sacrificing learning signal or stability.
