### What is the theoretical justification for normalizing the sequence‑level importance ratio by sequence length in GSPO?
Question:
$s_i(	heta)=ig(rac{	heta(y_i|x)}{	heta_{	ext{old}}(y_i|x)}ig)^{1/|y_i|}$ is introduced to reduce variance. The paper says varying length would otherwise require different clipping ranges. Can someone explain why the $1/|y_i|$ exponent equalizes the scale across lengths? What would happen to $s_i(	heta)$ if we omitted this normalization – would it still be bounded, and how would that affect clipping stability?
Answer:
### Why the \(1/|y_i|\) exponent is needed

In GSPO the *importance ratio* for a response \(y_i\) is defined as  

\[
s_i(\theta)\;=\;\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
  \;=\;\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
     \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{
           \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
\]

The term inside the exponent is the average log‑ratio of **per‑token** likelihoods.  
Without this averaging, the ratio would be a product over all tokens:

\[
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}
   = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
           {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

A product of many numbers that are each around one can still have a huge dynamic range:
for a sequence of length \(L\) even modest \((1.01)^L\) or \((0.99)^L\) variations explode or vanish exponentially with \(L\).  
Hence, sequences of different lengths would naturally produce importance ratios that differ by orders of magnitude, forcing us to choose *different* clipping bounds for each length in order to keep the clipped portion comparable.

By taking the geometric mean (the \(1/|y_i|\) power), we bring every sequence back to the same scale: each token contributes a factor that is already close to one, and the product becomes a *mean* rather than an *exponential* of length. Variance that scales with length is reduced from \(\mathcal{O}(|y|)\) (product) to \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) (mean). Consequently, \(s_i(\theta)\) stays roughly within a fixed interval, say \([0.5,2]\), regardless of the response length, and a single clipping range works uniformly.

---

### What happens if we omit the normalisation?

If we define

\[
\tilde s_i(\theta)\;=\;\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)},
\]

then

1. **Unboundedness** – The ratio is no longer bounded for long sequences. Even if each per‑token likelihood ratio is close to one, multiplying \(L\) such factors can drift exponentially. In practice this leads to values far outside any reasonable clipping window (e.g., \(10^5\) for a 100‑token reply).

2. **Length‑dependent clipping** – Because \(\tilde s_i(\theta)\) grows with \(|y_i|\), different components of the batch would need different clipping ranges in order to clip “off–policy” samples at comparable levels. Implementing per‑length clips is cumbersome and brittle; small changes in sequence distribution cause large changes in clip behaviour.

3. **Instability** – The extreme ratios make the surrogate loss very sensitive to any single token that deviates slightly from what the new policy would produce. A truncated sample with an unusually high or low likelihood under the old policy can dominate the gradient and drive the policy toward degenerate behaviour—exactly what GRPO suffers from when it uses token‑wise importance weights.

4. **Variance amplification** – Without normalisation, \(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\) has variance that grows roughly linearly with length (because each token contributes its own weight). Although clipping mitigates this to some degree, it still leaves large responses contributing much more noise than short ones.

In short: omitting the length normalisation would leave GSPO’s importance ratios highly variable across sequence lengths, requiring bespoke clipping ranges and making training far more brittle and prone to collapse.

---

### Bottom line: why GSPO works

* GSPO’s ratio is **length‑agnostic** thanks to geometric‑mean normalisation.
* It keeps all sequences within a practical numerical range, enabling a single global clipping bound.
* The resulting surrogate objective is stable: gradients are weighted equally across tokens and sequences of differing lengths.
* Empirically, this design eliminates MoE expert‑activation volatility and removes the need for tricks like Routing Replay.

Thus, the \(1/|y|\) exponent is not just a cosmetic tweak—it is a principled variance‑reduction device that aligns the importance sampling weight with the reward signal at the right granularity.*
