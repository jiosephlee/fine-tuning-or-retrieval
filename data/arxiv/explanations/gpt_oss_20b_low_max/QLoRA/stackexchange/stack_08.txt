\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why does SOTA instruction fine‑tuning datasets differ so much in their impact on MMLU vs chatbot benchmarks?
Question:
"Training LLaMA on FLAN v2 performs well on MMLU but poorly on Vicuna". Can someone explain conceptually why two datasets that both claim to enable instruction following can drive performance trends in such divergent ways? Does this reflect differences in content style, length distribution, or prompt structure?
Answer:
### Why does an “instruction‑fine‑tuned” dataset that looks like it should help make a model good at *chatting* nonetheless leave the model weak on a purely knowledge‑based benchmark like MMLU, and vice‑versa?

**Short answer:** The two benchmarks probe very different aspects of a model, and the training data that best teaches one aspect does not automatically teach the other. The *style* of the data – how instructions are phrased, how many turns a conversation has, how long each example is – strongly shapes what a model learns to do. The paper’s findings show that:

1. **FLAN v2** is built from a wide variety of task prompts that are *short* and *mostly single‑turn*.  
2. **OASST‑1** (the dataset used for Guanaco) is a multi‑turn chat log that includes many conversational turn‑boundaries, natural pauses, and the “follow‑up” logic that a human operator might expect from a chatbot.

Because of these differences, FLAN v2 mainly teaches the model *how to map a prompt to an answer* in a literal, task‑specific way. The model learns to pick up on keywords in the task description and generate the desired output; it pays less attention to conversational nuance such as politeness, context carry‑over, or user intent inferred from previous turns. This makes it excel at MMLU – where each question is essentially a single instructional prompt – but gives it little practice at staying on topic or maintaining conversational flow, which hurts its Vicuna / OASST chat‑benchmarks.

Conversely, OASST‑1 trains on dense dialog passages where each turn can build on the previous ones. The model learns to recognize when something already said needs to be recalled, when it should ask clarifying questions, and how to phrase responses with the right level of detail for a dialogue. That “conversation bias” is exactly what Vicuna evaluates: it asks the model to reply to open‑ended prompts in a chat‑style setting. The learned conversational scaffolding improves chat performance but offers little benefit when each MMLU question stands alone; sometimes even hurts because the model over‑injects discourse markers or “follow‑ups” that are irrelevant for a multiple‑choice quiz.

---

#### 1. Content style matters more than raw size

The paper’s own ablation (“a 9k sample dataset… outperformed a 450k sample dataset”) demonstrates that quality trumps quantity. FLAN v2’s 450 k examples are high in number but largely *task prompts*, whereas OASST‑1’s 9 k examples contain rich dialogue structures. The dense conversational signals buried in OASST 1 train attention not just on *content*, but also on *structural cues* that are invisible to MMLU questions.

#### 2. Prompt length distribution reshapes attention

Longer contexts mean more token budget per input token is devoted to caching earlier tokens’ semantics rather than estimating new parameters. In training, if most sequences are short (as in FLAN), the model tends to stash most embeddings into its *feedforward receptive field*, becoming very good at predicting tokens for slightly larger windows (the kind of window MMLU tests). In contrast, longer conversation sequences force the transformer to develop stronger span–to–span attention patterns and memory usage across turns—exactly what Vicuna expects.

#### 3. Prompt structure shapes what gradients flow

FLAN’s inputs consistently have an “instruction → answer” pairing with minimal interim text; gradients therefore mainly push weight updates that map an instruction phrase directly onto its answer tokens. OASST inputs interleave **user** and **assistant** tokens; gradients backpropagate through “look‑back” attention heads that attend over prior turns. Those heads become better at modeling sequential dependencies rather than isolated instruction mapping.

#### 4. Evaluation metrics reinforce these differences

MMLU treats every prompt as an independent multiple–choice question; forward invariance means context from earlier items has no effect on prediction quality. Vicuna/OA hand‑crafted prompts explicitly measure whether the response is coherent across turns, follows user intent, or shows consistency—properties biased toward training data with conversational turn–by–turn scaffolding.

---

### What does this mean for practitioners?

| Task | Recommended dataset | Reason |
|------|--------------------|--------|
| Knowledge retrieval / large single‐shot QA | FLAN v2 (or other task prompt collections) | Provides concise instruction-answer pairs; few context followers keep focus on factual output |
| Conversation/chatbot | OASST 1 (or any multi-turn dialog) | Gives tone, clarification behavior, and natural turn structure |
| Mixed application | Blend of both | Allows balanced performance; but be aware of potential trade‐off depending on target usage |

If you care about strong performance on *both* regimes you have three options:

1. **Two‐stage finetuning** – first train on FLAN then fine-tune further on OASST (or vice versa) while freezing part of the adapters.
2. **Dataset mixture** during training – sample from both distributions with tuned probabilities.
3. **Adapter branching** – maintain separate small adapters for instruction vs chat tasks; plug them in based on front‑end tagging.

The paper’s findings suggest simple architectural changes (more adapters per layer) were needed anyway because LoRA alone with default hyperparameters couldn't recover full precision for 65B models – regardless of the dataset.

---

### Bottom line

Both datasets claim “instruction following,” but their *instruction formats* differ dramatically: FLAN treats instructions as isolated cues; OASST frames them inside long conversational threads with back‑and‐forth signals. Consequently:

- Models fine‐tuned on FLAN become strong at map‐prompt→answer mappings—a virtue for MMLU’s multiple choice style.
- Models fine‐tuned on OASST develop robust conversational dynamics—a virtue for Vicuna/OA benchmarks.

Thus you’re not seeing contradictory results from two unlabeled instruction sets; you’re seeing two complementary skill sets tailored by different data styles—a phenomenon thoroughly explored and confirmed by QLoRA’s extensive evaluation suite in §4 (“Instruction Fine & GPT-4”), where they note “strong MMLU performance does not imply strong Vicuna performance and vice versa.”
