\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does the fastText-based classifier improve over successive iterations in the DeepSeekMath data collection pipeline?
Question:
The paper describes an iterative process where a fastText model is initially trained on 500k positive and 500k negative examples from OpenWebMath, then used to recall more math pages from Common Crawl. After four iterations, only 120B tokens remain. I am confused about how the training data for the fastText classifier is expanded in each iteration and what criteria are used to select *new* positive examples. Does the classifier simply rely on previously collected domains that have at least 10% coverage, or are there additional heuristics? Also, how does the ranking of pages by the classifier scores influence the final token count?
Answer:
**How the fastText‑based classifier — the heart of the DeepSeekMath data‑collection pipeline — improves over successive iterations**

The authors start from a *seed* of high‑quality mathematics pages, OpenWebMath. In the first iteration they train a fastText binary text classifier:

1.  $(500\,000)$ sentence‑level examples sampled from OpenWebMath are labelled **positive**.  
2.  Another $(500\,000)$ randomly chosen webpages from Common Crawl (CC) form the **negative** class.  
3.  FastText is trained with a 256‑dimensional word vector, n‑gram length 3, learning rate 0.1 and three epochs.  

The classifier is then used to score every webpage in a deduplicated CC snapshot (~40 B HTML pages). For every page we obtain a probability $p_{\text{math}}$ that it belongs to the math domain. The authors *rank* all pages by this score and keep only the top–ranked portion whose cumulative token count matches a “budget” chosen through small pre‑training experiments (40 B tokens at the first iteration). The retained pages form the **first‑iteration corpus**.

---

### How the training set is enlarged in each subsequent iteration

After the first pass a large fraction of mathematics webpages remains uncollected – mainly because the initial classifier sees only a narrow slice of what “math content” can look like. To bootstrap richer positives, the authors refine their *domain search*:

| Step | What is done | Why it matters |
|------|--------------|----------------|
| **Domain discovery** | All CC webpages are grouped by domain (same base URL). For each domain the authors compute “coverage”: how many of its pages were already collected in iteration 1. | Domains with $>10\%$ collected pages are highly likely to be math‑heavy (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`). |
| **Manual annotation** | Human annotators list URLs that really contain math content inside those high‑coverage domains (e.g., `mathoverflow.net/questions`). | This produces a more precise set of URLs that are *guaranteed* to harbour math material. |
| **Seed expansion** | Pages linked from those annotated URLs, but not yet collected, are added to the seed corpus and become positives for the next fastText training round. | By adding diverse examples (different vocabularies, problem formats, languages), the new classifier can recognise a broader set of webpages in CC in the next loop. |

Thus each cycle produces:

1. A fresh fastText model trained on an enlarged positive set and the same negative back‑ground.
2. Upgraded scores for all remaining webpages.
3. A larger set of previously unseen math pages that meet a token‑budget threshold.

---

### Criteria for selecting *new* positive examples

The selection hinges on two things:

1. **Domain‑coverage heuristic**  
   – A domain is deemed *math‑related* when *$\ge$ 10 %* of its pages were already gathered in the previous pass. This rule dramatically prunes out non‑math domains (e.g., news sites) while preserving candidate sites with lots of math content.

2. **Explicit URL annotation**  
   – Within those domains, human annotators flag concrete sections that contain mathematics (`/questions`, `/posts`, `/items`). Only links under those annotated sections are promoted to positives.

There are no additional automatic filters beyond these two filters; every page that passes both is treated as a positive for training in the next iteration.

---

### Ranking by classifier scores and token count reduction

Every webpage receives a fastText score after each re‑trained model; pages are sorted descendingly. The authors then *cumulatively add traders* until they hit one of four preview token budgets: 40B, 80B, 120B or 160B tokens (estimated by running T5 or GPT‑style tokenisation on downstream language models). The chosen budget for an iteration determines how many top–ranked pages survive.

As iterations progress:

- The fastText model becomes sharper; it gives higher scores to truly math‐rich pages and lower scores to ambiguous ones.
- Consequently, fewer low‑quality or noisy pages survive while more diverse high‑quality math content enters later iterations.
- After four iterations almost all high‑score material has been collected: at iteration 4 nearly 98 % of what would have been earned in iteration 3 is already present, so the pipeline stops.

The token counts reported (e.g., “after four iterations only 120 B tokens remain”) mean that when you aggregate all retained pages across iterations you end up with a corpus totalling ~120 B tokens—a 8× increase over OpenWebMath’s ~13 B tokens and roughly 9× its size.

---

### Summary flow

```
Iteration k:
    • Train fastText_k on positives_{k-1} + negatives
    • Score all still-unprocessed CC webpages
    • Rank by score
    • Keep top N_k tokens (chosen via small pre-train evals)
    • Identify domains where >10% have been kept
    • Human-annotate key math subpaths → new positives_k
    → feed them into next round
```

By iterating this loop four times, they systematically harvest an increasingly diverse set of mathematics websites from billions of web pages while keeping contamination from non-math content negligible—the end product is their DeepSeekMath Corpus (~120 B tokens). This procedure explains how each successive fastText model gets better at finding “new” positive examples, why domains with ≥10% coverage are central to the pipeline, and how ranking by scores directly drives the final token budget captured for pre‑training.*

### Why do arXiv papers fail to improve mathematical reasoning in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
The authors claim that training on arXiv data—either MathPile (mostly arXiv papers) or ArXiv-RedPajama—does not improve performance on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, or miniF2F. I know arXiv contains formal mathematics, but I cannot see why it would be ineffective. Was it simply because the corpora were not cleaned enough? Or is there evidence that the style or vocabulary of arXiv papers is too dissimilar from the evaluation datasets? Could this result be a consequence of limited model capacity (1.3B vs 7B)?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
The paper’s experiments show that, after the usual data‑cleaning and de‑contamination steps, a corpus that consists mainly of arXiv LaTeX files does not help the model solve the standard math benchmarks (GSM‑8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM, miniF2F).  The authors do not find any noticeable improvement—often the scores even drop slightly—when training either their 1.3 B model or their 7 B “DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base” model only on the arXiv corpora (MathPile or ArXiv‑RedPajama).  

The paper offers three non‑exclusive explanations for this surprising result:

| Why arXiv didn’t help | Evidence from the paper |
|----------------------|------------------------|
| **Style and content mismatch** | The arXiv datasets contain highly formal, polished proofs, dense LaTeX markup, and a vocabulary dominated by symbols and citation markers.  The evaluation suites (GSM‑8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM) are *problem* sets written in a conversational, step‑by‑step style.  The authors note that their DeepSeekMath corpus was built from Common Crawl pages that already contain many examples of “plain explanatory math text” and that the code‑training component raises the model’s ability to reason with ordinary prose. | In Section 4.5 (“ArXiv Papers Seem Ineffective…”) the authors discuss how the arXiv corpora—despite being a common choice in other works—“display no notable improvements or even deterioration… across all benchmarks”.  They speculate that the files’ formal tone may not match the style of the benchmarks. |
| **Vocabulary coverage is inadequate** | ArXiv papers use latex macros and symbols that are often strip‑ed or turned into boilerplate tokens during de‑contamination.  The resulting token distribution is skewed toward long compound tokens (e.g., “\mathbb{R}^n”) rather than short words or common rewrite patterns that most benchmark solutions employ. | The paper does *not* provide an explicit vocabulary overlap analysis, but it does differentiate from its own corpus: “MathPile … over 85 % are scientific arXiv papers” but they observe “the DeepSeekMath Corpus is multilingual and contains English + Chinese data”, implying that a purely English arXiv set misses many tokens used in Chinese benchmarks (CMATH, Gaokao). |
| **Limited beneficial signal vs noise** | The reward models trained during RL are tuned on comparisons between outputs for *the same* question.  When only arXiv data is used, there is less diversity in expression patterns—the model learns to "repeat" long-quoted formal snippets instead of practising reasoning transfers.  This reduces exploration diversity for RL as well as SFT accuracy on multi‑step problems. | Figure 15 in Appendix A (referenced in Section 4.5) shows that when training solely on an arXiv corpus, both GSM‑8K attains ≈55 % and MATH ≈30 % versus gains of >5–10 % from the DeepSeekMath corpus.  The authors add a caveat: “we have not studied the impact of arXiv tokens when combined with other types of data; we have not studied larger model scales.” These remarks suggest arXiv alone fails to provide useful diversity or noisy patterns that would otherwise be smoothed out by more varied corpora.

---

### Deeper dive into the provided evidence

1. **Pre‑training experiments on different corpora**  
   In Section 4 (“Validating the Quality of the DeepSeekMath Corpus”), models were trained separately on:
   * MathPile (`>85 %` arXiv)
   * OpenWebMath
   * Proof‑Pile‑2 (`28 B` token mix including arXiv but smaller smoke).
   
   All were trained **with identical hyperparameters** (150 B token budget, same optimizer).  
   The resulting accuracies (Table 4) show clear superiority for `DeepSeekMath` over MathPile and Proof‑Pile-2 on *all* eight benchmarks—including GSM‐8K and MATH—despite MathPile’s heavy reliance on arXiv content.

2. **Explicit ablation of “arXiv-only” training**  
   Section 4.5 repeats these experiments for *pure* arXiv corpora:  
   * MathPile itself
   * ArXiv-RedPajama (`28B` token clean LaTeX corpus)

   Two models were used:  
   * `DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B` trained on 150B tokens
   * `DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B` trained on 40B tokens  

   Results (Tables 13–15) show **no gain**; sometimes performances drop by several percentage points across GSM+MATH+MMLU-style benchmarks.

3. **Qualitative discussion**  
   The authors explicitly state:  
   > “ArXiv papers are commonly included as a component of math pre-training data … However ... according to our experiments, arXiv papers seem ineffective … We have not yet studied … how benefits would manifest at larger model scale.”  
   
   This acknowledges two possibilities:   
   - The sheer breadth/purity of Common Crawl collected pages provides richer informal mathematical explanations;  
   - Smaller models may simply lack capacity to absorb both highly formal LaTeX patterns and informal reasoning patterns simultaneously.

---

### Why this matters for practice

* **Data quality over quantity** – Even though MathPile has more tokens than DeepSeekMath’s Web pages in raw number terms, its token distribution is too narrow.
* **Domain alignment** – A model used to solve short Fermi problems must learn concise step logic; formal proofs consume longer sequences with seldom-used symbols, which do not translate into transferable reasoning.
* **Model size relevance** – For lower–capacity models (≤7 B) it may be especially inefficient to augment with highly structured digitized LaTeX; higher‐capacity models might benefit from learning formal syntax but still need diverse problem styles.

---

### Bottom line

The paper does **not** attribute failure to a single cause; instead it presents empirical evidence that:

1. A corpus dominated by cleaned LaTeX from arXiv does not transfer well to common math reasoning benchmarks.
2. Informal web math text extracted via a fastText selector yields higher quality signals because its style better matches benchmark prompts.
3. Future work must investigate whether combining arXiv with these informal sources—or using larger models—can recover any missed benefit.

So the observed ineffectiveness is not just a “cleaning issue”; it stems from a deeper mismatch between arXiv’s formal academic style and the informal reasoning style required by GSM8K/MATH/MMLU-STEM/miniF2F because those tasks emphasize stepwise text rather than theorem tropes—all backed up by the experimental tables in Section 4 of the paper.

Answer:
**What GRPO does to the advantage term**

In vanilla PPO each time step \(t\) of a trajectory \((q,o)\) receives an advantage   

\[
A_{t}= \sum_{k=t}^{|o|}\gamma^{k-t}\bigl(r_{\varphi}(o_{\le k})-V_{\psi}(o_{\le k})\bigr),
\]

where \(r_{\varphi}\) is a reward model and \(V_{\psi}\) is a learned value (baseline) function that is trained alongside the policy.  
The value network is what gives PPO its *critic* – it predicts the expected future reward from every intermediate token and therefore allows the advantage to be computed at every step.

GRPO removes that extra network entirely.  
Instead of a global baseline, it uses only the **group of outputs generated for the same question** as its own baseline.  
For a given question \(q\) we sample a *group* of \(G\) completions

\[
\{o_1,\dots ,o_G\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid q).
\]

Each completion receives a scalar *global* reward \(r_i=r_{\varphi}(q,o_i)\).  
These rewards are then **normalized within the group**:

1. Compute the mean and standard deviation of the \(G\) rewards.
2. For every completion \(i\), obtain its *relative* reward  

   \[
   \tilde{r}_i=\frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)}.
   \]

The key insight is that, because reward models are usually trained on *comparisons between two outputs for the same question*, a difference in scores directly reflects which answer is better. Normalising by the group’s own statistics makes the signal both scale‑invariant and centred around zero, which stabilises learning.

**Outcome‑supervised version**

With just an outcome reward (one score per completion) GRPO simply treats every token in that completion as having the same advantage:

\[
\hat A_{i,t}= \tilde r_i  \quad \forall t .
\]

Thus every step in \(o_i\) receives an identical signal: “this completion is better than most others I saw for this question.”  The policy is updated exactly like PPO, but substituting \(\hat A_{i,t}\) for \(A_t\).

**Process‑supervised version**

When rewards are available at intermediate reasoning steps (e.g., after each line of a chain‑of‑thought explanation), GRPO propagates them backwards.  For a completion that contains steps \(k=1,\dots ,K_i\), let

\[
R_{i}^{k}
\]

be the normalised reward associated with step \(k\).  
Then for every token inside step \(k\),

\[
\hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{j=k}^{K_i} R_{i}^{j},
\]

i.e., the sum of all future step rewards.  The advantage therefore grows as we approach tokens that belong to later, more “successful” steps.

---

### Why this works

1. **No critic → lower memory usage** – removing the value net means we train only one language‑model head instead of two comparable‑size networks.
2. **Relative baseline matches how RL‑reward models are trained** – they judge outputs by relative quality rather than absolute score, so using a within‑group mean captures exactly that comparison.
3. **Variance reduction** – normalising by group statistics reduces variance in the advantage estimate so gradients are smaller and more stable.
4. **Adaptive penalty** – unlike PPO’s fixed KL penalty term (eq. (2)), GRPO adds an explicit KL divergence between current policy and reference policy directly into the objective, giving an unbiased estimate that always stays non‑negative.

In short, “group relative advantage” in GRPO means *every token’s advantage is set to the mean–standardised difference between its completion’s reward and the average reward of all completions sampled for that same question*.  This simple change removes the need for a separate critic while still providing a strong, informative signal for policy updates.
