Chapter 3: # 3. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

Reinforcement learning from human feedback has become the cornerstone of modern instruction‑tuned and alignment‑focused language models. In this chapter, we provide a self‑contained exposition of the RLHF workflow, taking the reader from the formal objective that drives policy optimization, through the construction of a reward estimator from noisy human judgments, to the practicalities and pitfalls of actually training a large language model with modern policy‑gradient methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Throughout we preserve the notation used in the seminal papers, so that readers can directly translate the discussion into code or paper analysis.

---

## Formulating the KL‑regularized reward maximization problem

The ultimate goal of RLHF is to obtain a language model \(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\) that, given a prompt \(x\), produces a completion \(y\) which humans judge to be better according to some latent reward function \(r^{*}(x,y)\). Two intertwined requirements make this problem difficult:

1. **Reward uncertainty** – we never see \(r^{*}\) directly; we only observe **relative** preferences over pairs of completions.
2. **Distribution drift** – any policy update that diverges too far from its starting point can result in catastrophic failure—either loss of useful knowledge or mode collapse.

To address these constraints, RLHF casts inference as a *constrained* reinforcement learning problem:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\pi^{*}_{\theta} = \arg\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\;
& \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}}\!\bigl[r^{*}(x,y)\bigr] \\
& \quad - \beta\, \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\;\Vert\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
\end{aligned}\tag{3.1}
\]
Here:

- \(\mathcal{D}\) is a corpus of prompts drawn from deployment.
- \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is the *reference policy*: essentially the starting language model (often an SFT model). It embodies prior knowledge and serves as a safety net.
- The **KL term** penalizes deviation from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), limiting how much we are allowed to change probability mass.
- The scalar \(\beta > 0\) balances exploitation (maximizing reward) against safety (controlling drift).

Equation (3.1) is an example of *policy regularisation*. Its form can be derived by acknowledging that LLMs produce sequences by sampling tokens one after another; however, for brevity we treat each completion \(y\) as an atomic outcome.

## Translating human preferences into a reward function

Human labelers play an essential role: they compare two completions \(y_{1}\) and \(y_{2}\) for a given prefix \(x\), and indicate which one they prefer. This data is denoted \(y_{w}\succ y_{l} \mid x\), where “\(w\)” means winner and “\(l\)” means loser.

### The Bradley–Terry model

A principled way to encode preferential feedback is via the Bradley–Terry (BT) model:
\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{\exp(r^{*}(x,y_{1}))}
     {\exp(r^{*}(x,y_{1})) + \exp(r^{*}(x,y_{2}))}. 
\tag{3.2}
\]
If two completions have higher rewards under \(r^{*}\), they are more likely to win. The BT formulation reduces preference learning to binary classification: we treat the pair as two classes and predict which one should obtain higher logit.

### Parametric reward estimation

Because we cannot observe \(r^{*}\), we fit a parametric surrogate \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) – typically a neural network that accepts a prompt/completion pair, tokenises it, and outputs a scalar reward. The negative log‑likelihood objective over all preference pairs \(\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y_{w},y_{l})_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\) is:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi};\, \mathcal{D}) = - 
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})}[\, 
   \log\sigma(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l}))\,],
   \tag{3.3}
\]
where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\). Several practical tricks help stabilise training:

- **Reward anchoring** – one often normalises rewards so their mean is zero under \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).
- **Filter on temperature** – keeping per‑token likelihoods bounded avoids log‑divergence when logits diverge.

Once trained, \(r_{\phi}\) is interpreted as *the reward function that explains human preferences*, up to additive constants per prefix (the BT family is underdetermined by a constant shift).

## From reward to policy: KL‐bounded optimality

Equation (3.1) cannot be optimized directly because it requires gradient signals through sampling from an LM policy over all token sequences—a combinatorial nightmare. Classical RL solutions discretise using likelihood ratios or advantage estimates; RLHF routinely employs **PPO**, an actor‑critic method that samples trajectories from the current policy and adjusts parameters via clipped surrogate objectives.

Nevertheless, prior work has shown that when the reward function is *logistic*-like, Eq. (3.1)’s optimal solution admits an analytic form:
\[
    \pi_r(y|x)
    = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
            e^{\, r(x,y)/\beta}}
           {Z(x)},     
    \qquad
    Z(x)=
         \sum_{y'} 
           \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)
           e^{\, r(x,y')/\beta}.     
    \tag{3.4}
\]
This equation emerges from treating RLHF as *variational inference*: maximizing expected reward minus KL is equivalent to minimizing relative entropy between the new and reference distributions conditioned on weighted exponentiated rewards.

Key observations:

- **Proportional update rule** – if two completions swap order in terms of their exponentiated rewards, their relative likelihoods under the updated policy swap accordingly.
- **Partition function** – likewise to softmax normalization, \(Z(x)\) ensures probabilities sum to one; computing it exactly would necessitate summation over all possible completions—impossible in practice.

Large‑scale LMs therefore approximate this operator either via Monte‑Carlo sampling (counting how many times each token occurs across many trajectories) or via learning separate value functions as peractor baselines.

## Practical RLHF training with PPO

While Eq (3.4) reveals closed form for an *optimal* policy under fixed rewards, real training proceeds incrementally: every iteration samples new trajectories with current parameters, critiques them using on‑policy critics or baselines, and performs gradient updates on both actor and critic networks.

### PPO surrogate objective

PPO introduces an importance–sampling ratio
\(r_t = {\exp(\theta-\theta_\text{old})}\)
and clamps it to stay within [\(1-\epsilon,\; 1+\epsilon]\).
The PPO loss aimed at increasing expected reward while controlling policy shifts becomes:
```python
loss = -torch.mean(
            torch.minimum(
                r_t * adv_t,
                torch.clamp(r_t, 1-epsilon, 1+epsilon)*adv_t
            )
        )
```
where `adv_t` is an advantage estimate (reward difference shifted by learned value).

### Advantages of PPO in practice

- **Sample efficiency** – Each trajectory can be re-used several times.
- **Stability** – Clipping reduces variance due to large ratios.
- **Flexibility** – Can incorporate novel tricks like KL loss or action padding.

### Hyperparameter profile

Typical configurations for replacing SFT LMs in RLHF tasks:

| Hyperparameter | Setting |
|----------------|----------|
| batch size | 8192 |
| clip ratio ε | 0.2 |
| entropy bonus | 0 (often omitted due deterministic LMs?) |
| KL coefficient β | tuned per task |
| rollout length | full generated sequence |

Large models exacerbate sampling costs: generating sampled completions takes seconds per prompt; thus practitioners often cap rollout horizons or use prompt‑caching strategies.

## Challenges that motivate Direct Preference Optimization

Despite PPO’s ingenuity, several systematic difficulties persist:

1. **High variance gradient estimates** – Because trajectories are long sequences of discrete tokens, estimating advantages reliably requires careful baselining or variance reduction techniques.
2. **Reward estimator inaccuracies** – Training `r_phi` may misinterpret preferences if data lacks diversity; any systematic bias propagates into the learned policy.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity** – The balancing coefficient β must be tuned per task and per seed; small changes can tip training towards mode collapse or high-divergence behaviours.
4. **Computational burden** – Sampling thousands of completions at each step for large models severely limits scaling.

The DPO method presented later bypasses many of these pitfalls by directly modelling policies consistent with human preferences through a closed form reparameterization of rewards (§4). Nonetheless, understanding conventional RLHF remains essential because DPO inherits much of its intuition: it still optimises for maximizing expected latent reward while shrinking distance from a reference LM through KL regularisation.

### Summary

In this chapter we have walked through the entire pipeline of RLHF:

1. Collect human preference pairs between two completions.
2. Fit an implicit reward network via Bradley–Terry binary cross‑entropy.
3. Convert latent rewards into constrained optimisation over policies with KL penalties.
4. Train using advanced actor–critic methods such as PPO.
5. Grapple with practical difficulties including sample inefficiency and tuning burdens.

While each step appears straightforward at first glance—"train a stackable classifier", "run trust‐region updates"—the combinatorial explosion inherent in discrete text generation turns them into challenging engineering puzzles. Recognising these challenges sets the stage for more principled alternatives like Direct Preference Optimization, which will explore exactly why some manipulations peppered across RLHF may be unnecessary if we change how we formulate our objective in hindsight.\quad_endblockquote
