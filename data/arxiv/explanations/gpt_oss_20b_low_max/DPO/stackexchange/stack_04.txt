Question:

Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes, the two coefficients play very similar roles: both are slack variables that trade off reward against deviation from a reference policy. In practice, a “reasonable” value for $\eta$ in DPO can be chosen by translating an existing PPO target‑KL (or β) to the DPO scale. Because the two objectives are mathematically equivalent up to a deterministic mapping of $\beta$ to $\eta$, you can use the same numerical range that works for β ≈ 0.05–5 and simply pick $\eta$ in that range. The exact conversion is not needed as long as you keep $\eta$ in the same order of magnitude, since DPO’s performance is only weakly sensitive to $\eta$ compared with PPO’s sensitivity to β.

---

### 1.  What the two terms actually do

In RLHF the constrained optimisation problem (Eq. (5) in the paper)

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y}\Big[r_{\phi}(x,y)\Big] - 
        \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big]
\]

uses a *KL penalty* with weight $\beta>0$.  
A small β forces the new policy to stay close to the reference; a large β lets the policy chase higher reward but risks mode‑collapse or drift into regions where the reward model is poorly calibrated.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) replaces the KL penalty by *implicit rewards* that depend on the ratio of model to reference logits:

\[
r_{\theta}(x,y)=\eta\,\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)} .
\]

The weighted cross‑entropy loss used in DPO is

\[
L_{\text{DPO}}
  =-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[
      \log\sigma
      \bigl(
          \eta\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w|x)}
          - \eta\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l|x)}
     \bigr)
   \Big].
\]

The factor $ \eta $ therefore controls how strongly preference data influence parameter updates: big $\eta$ gives weight to poor‑ranked pairs and pushes the policy further away from π_ref, while small $\eta$ keeps updates gentle.

Because DPO was derived directly from τ‑constrained RL (Eq. (4) → (6)), **the mapping between χ and η is bijective** – changing one of them changes exactly which family of policies maximises the objective. Thus, apart from numerical scaling, they *are* comparable hyperparameters.

### 2.  Practical guidance for choosing η if you already have PPO

Suppose your PPO implementation uses a target–KL setting: you optimise β such that  

\[
\mathbb{E}_{x}\ [\,\mathbb{D}_{KL}\!\big[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big]\,]
           \;\approx\;\texttt{target-kl}.
\]

A convenient recipe is:

1. **Run one PPO trial** with several β values and record the achieved average KL after convergence (say, β = 0.05 → KL≈3, β = 0.5 → KL≈7, etc.).  
2. **Pick an η that reproduces a similar KL** by observing how changes in η affect divergence when running DPO on a *validation* set of preferences. Because both coefficients determine how much probability mass is shifted from ref to new policy, you normally find that η ≈ β works, but you may need a 10–20% adjustment depending on your reward scale and temperature used during sampling.

In practice:

* **Start with η in {0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1}.**  
  These are exactly the ranges used in Section 3 of the paper; they produce stable training and reasonable KL–reward trade‑offs across tasks.

* **Monitor KL during training**: compute  
  \(K(x)= -\,H[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)] + H[\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x)] + \mathbb{E}_{y}\!\left[\log r_{\theta}(x,y)\right]\).  
  If your KL drifts far above your target, reduce η; if it’s too low, increase η.

* **Transferability**: Because DPO exploits an exact analytic mapping between reward and policy (Eq. (8)), you rarely need to fine‑tune η after switching tasks or model sizes; simply re‑compute η via step 1 for each new setting.

---

### 3.  Why they are not identical numerically

While we can say “η ≈ β”, several implementation details make them differ:

* **Reward scaling** – In PPO you typically normalise $r_\phi(x,y)$ (mean = 0, variance = 1). In DPO no explicit normalisation occurs; instead all scaling comes from η itself.
* **Policy entropy/temperature** – If you use temperature when generating samples for preference labels (common practice), this effectively rescales rθ by $1/\text{temperature}$; equivalently rescale η.
* **Sampling distribution** – PPO rolls out trajectories under πθ while gathering rewards; DPO trains on static pairs generated by π_ref, so your “effective” KL will be different unless you adjust sampling temperatures accordingly.

Because these factors shift how sharply πθ deviates from π_ref for a given coefficient value, it's safest to start with small values (e.g., 0.1) and empirically adjust rather than directly copy β values across algorithms.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

* **Conceptual equivalence:** both coefficients are slack variables balancing reward interest vs obedience to a reference distribution.
* **Practical equivalence:** starting with an η close to your PPO β typically gives acceptable performance; then tune slightly based on observed KL or validation loss.
* **Empirical stability:** The authors report no need for heavy hyper‑parameter tuning when moving from Adam→AdamW or TF→JAX; a modest range around {0.05–1} usually suffices across palatable settings.

So if you’re switching from PPO with target‑KL = 6 to DPO, set η ≈ 0.5–1 and let training guide fine‑tuning—this will give you comparable behaviour without having to redo extensive hyperparameter sweeps
