# From Preferences to Policies: The Theory Behind DPO’s Closed‑Form Solution  

Large language models (LLMs) can write poetry, answer questions, and even debug code—all through autoregressive probability models that learn from the web.  Yet pinning down *how* an LLM should behave when asked to obey a user‑defined preference is trickier.  The standard route has been **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**: first learn a reward function that predicts whether humans prefer one generated text over another, then fine‑tune the LLM with policy‑gradient methods such as PPO until it maximises that reward while staying close to its original distribution.

Whether or not you love PPO, you’ll agree that RL agents need a lot of careful engineering: set up a reward signal, bootstrap a baseline, tune variance reduction tricks, and—most painfully—sample trajectories from a gigantic transformer during every gradient step.  The new algorithm *Direct Preference Optimization* (DPO) sidesteps all that by re‑expressing the reward model directly in terms of the policy itself.  What follows is a walkthrough of the mathematical ideas that make this possible.

---

## 1.  The RLHF objective we care about

The RLHF pipeline usually optimises

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}}\Big[
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)]}_{\text{expected reward}}
- \beta\,\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\!\big[\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big]}_{\text{staying close to the reference}}
\Big].
\tag{1}
\]

- **$x$** is a prompt or context.  
- **$\pi_{\text{ref}}$** is usually an instruction‑tuned or “SFT” model; it maps $x$ to a distribution over completions $y$.  
- **$r(x,y)$** is the human‑aligned reward we have to learn from pairwise preferences $y_w \succ y_l$.  
- **$\beta$** balances exploitation vs. keeping the new policy in the “safe” region near $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  

Equation (1) is perfectly nice in theory but horrible in practice because we still need the sampler for $\pi_\theta$ during every update.

---

## 2. Solution of the KL‑constrained problem

If we vacuum out the expectation over prompts and treat each $x$ independently, the objective reduces to a classic constrained optimisation problem:

\[
\max_{\pi}\; \sum_{y} \pi(y|x)\, r(x,y)
-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\big[\pi(\cdot|x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big].
\]

A straight‑forward application of Lagrange multipliers (see any control‑as‑inference lecture) yields the optimal policy for that fixed $x$:

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
\pi_{r}(y|x)=
\frac{
  \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
  e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta}
}{
  Z(x)
}
}
\tag{2}
\]
where $Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)e^{r(x,y)/\beta}$ guarantees normalization.

Equation (2) tells us something very powerful: *given any scalar reward function* $r$, *the best possible policy is an exponential tilt of the reference distribution*.

---

## 3. Rephrasing “reward” as “policy”

What if we solved for $r$ instead of $\pi$?  Taking logs of (2):

\[
r(x,y)=
{\color{teal}\beta} \log \frac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
+ {\color{teal}\beta} \log Z(x).
\tag{3}
\]

The terms have intuitive interpretations:

- The ratio $\frac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ says how much more (or less) likely we want $y$ under our *new* policy than under the old one.
- The partition term $\log Z(x)$ is a constant depending only on $x$, so it cancels out if we only care about *differences* in rewards between two completions.

Now suppose human annotators give us pairwise preferences modelled by a **Bradley–Terry** distribution:

\[
p^{*}(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)
= 
\sigma \!\big(r^{*}(x,y_1)-r^{*}(x,y_2)\big),
    \quad
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\ .
    \tag{4}
\]

Because $\sigma$ depends only on reward differences, plugging (3) into (4) eliminates the partition term entirely!  After some algebra we get a preference model expressed *purely in terms of policies*:

\[
p^{*}(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)
= 
\sigma\, 
   {\color{orange}\beta} 
   \log
   \frac{
     \dfrac{\pi^{*}(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)}
   }{
     \dfrac{\pi^{*}(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}
   }.
   \tag{5}
\]

Equation (5) says: *if I had an optimal policy $\pi^{*}$, these would be exactly its Bradley–Terry likelihoods for human preferences*. Crucially, it may be inverted: **we can fit $\pi_\theta$ directly by maximizing these likelihoods**.

---

## 4. The Direct Preference Optimization loss

Take logarithms of Eq (5) and sum over all labeled pairs $(x,y_w,y_l)$ in our dataset $\mathcal D$.  The maximum likelihood estimator becomes

\[
J(\theta)
=
-\underbrace{
\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}
    [
      \log\sigma 
       ({\,{\color{orange}\beta}\,\log
          {\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
          {\atop /\; }
          {\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}}})
    ]
}_{=:J_{\mathrm{cross-entropy}}\ .
     }                        \tag{6}
     % %  

The loss is nothing more exotic than a binary cross‑entropy between human labels and a *softmax probability that depends on log‑ratio terms*.  
Let’s unpack what happens inside:

1. **Log‐ratio of probabilities** – for each token generated in $y$, we compute  
   $$ r_\theta(x,y)= {\color{orange}\beta}\;\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}.$$
   This quantity acts exactly like an implicit reward distilled from the current policy.
2. **Dynamic weighting** – because this log‐ratio appears inside a sigmoid function, examples where the current policy already orders prefered over dispreferred completions get down–weighted automatically, whereas badly ordered pairs receive larger gradients.
    
Thus training reduces to ordinary backpropagation through a transformer and no sampling or variance estimate are required.

---

## 5. Why does this work? – Intuition from equivalence classes

From any reward function $r$, there are infinitely many equivalent rewards differing by an additive function of $x$: \( r'(x,y)= r(x,y)+f(x)\). In Bradley–Terry models such “shifted” rewards generate identical preference distributions because only differences matter.

Likewise, if two reward functions fall into the same equivalence class they induce **the same optimal policy** under constraint (1), i.e., necessarily they differ only by a global shift that does not affect probabilities in Eq (2).  

This observation guarantees that by reparameterising our reward as \( r = {\beta}\log (\frac{\tilde{\pi}(y|x)}{\tilde{\pi}_{ref}(y|x)}) + const(x)\)—equivalently enforcing Eq (3)—we lose **no expressive power**: every class contains at least one member of this form. Therefore DPO can recover any optimal‐policy shaped by some latent reward consistent with human data.

---

## 6. Connecting to actor–critic instability

Standard RLHF optimises (1) via PPO or REINFORCE. Those methods need unbiased estimates of returns—typically obtained by discounted sums of rewards—plus explicit baselines for variance reduction. DPO sidesteps all that because it never requires sampling trajectories; its loss uses deterministic probability ratios computed from two completions per prompt.

The same derivation also shows why having a proper normalising term helps actor–critic methods: when you drop $\log Z(x)$ you bias gradients toward high‐reward areas but lose stability—just like unnormalised advantage estimates lead to exploding gradients. With DPO there’s no such intermediate scalar; gradients derive directly from data.

---

## 7. Putting it together – A quick algorithmic recipe

```python
# Preliminaries ----------------------------------------------------
# Refine Base LM : pi_ref (often SFT or Preferred‑FT)
# Dataset : {(prompt x_i, chosen y_w_i , rejected y_l_i)}

for epoch in range(num_epochs):
    for batch in data_loader:
        # Log probabilities under current model θ and ref model:
        logp_theta_w = pi_theta.log_probs(batch.x, batch.y_w)
        logp_ref_w   = pi_ref.log_probs(batch.x, batch.y_w)

        logp_theta_l = pi_theta.log_probs(batch.x, batch.y_l)
        logp_ref_l   = pi_ref.log_probs(batch.x, batch.y_l)

        # Implicit "Reward"
        z_w = beta * (logp_theta_w - logp_ref_w)
        z_l = beta * (logp_theta_l - logp_ref_l)

        # Cross‑entropy loss:
        logits = z_w - z_l                # difference appears automatically
        loss = torch.nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
                       logits , torch.ones_like(logits), reduction='mean')

        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
```

That’s it—single forward pass through two completions per prompt per step; no trajectory sampling, no KL surrogate clipping, no extra networks for baselines.

---

## 8. What have experiments shown?

On controlled sentiment generation where an exact sentiment classifier supplies ground truth rewards—an otherwise impossible toy test—the frontier plotted by DPO consistently beats PPO even with PPO trained on real ground truth rewards: higher reward at lower KL divergence.

On real datasets such as Reddit TL;DR summarisation or Anthropic’s Helpful & Harmless dialogue set:

- DPO achieves equivalent or better win rates versus baseline policies compared with PPO-trained models.
- It retains robustness across sampling temperatures while basic PPO collapses if temperature is too high.
- Its computational profile is dramatic: far less GPU time per epoch because every gradient step processes only two completions instead of dozens hidden within RL rollouts.

---

## 9. Takeaway

DPO turns an optimization problem about expectations over trajectories into an elegant closed-form expression involving only pairwise log-ratio comparisons between two completions per prompt:

1. The **branchless KL constraint** becomes an exponentiated weight ($e^{\,/β}$) inside a policy update.
2. A **reward model is folded into the policy itself**, guaranteeing optimality without extra parameters or baselines.
3. Training reduces to standard transformer backpropagation with a binary cross‑entropy loss anchored in human preference data.

In short: *your language model already secretly knows how to score itself when you ask for relative quality*, so you simply let it update its own log-likelihood ratios until you match people’s pairwise judgements—no more monkeying around with samplers or estimating baselines!
