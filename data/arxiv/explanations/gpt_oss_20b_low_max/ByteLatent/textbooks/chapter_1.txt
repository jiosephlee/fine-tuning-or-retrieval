\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 1: # 5 Scaling Laws for Byte Models

The study of scaling laws—how performance changes with model size, data quantity, or compute budget—has become a central tool for guiding the design of modern large language models (LLMs). When models operate on **bytes** rather than discrete tokens, a key challenge emerges: how can we meaningfully quantify performance across different tokenization schemes and vocabulary sizes? Traditional language‑model evaluation relies on *perplexity*, which presupposes a fixed token set. For byte‑level models there is no such fixed vocabulary; consequently, perplexity is ill‑defined or inapplicable.  

To overcome this limitation the authors introduce **Bits‑Per‑Byte (BPB)** as a universal metric that can be applied to both byte‑level and token‑level models in a consistent manner. This chapter expounds on BPB: its definition, mathematical derivation, normalization rationale, and its role as a bridge between raw cross‑entropy loss and comparable performance metrics across tokenized and tokenizer‑free architectures.

---

## 5.1 BPB Definition and Normalization by \(\ln 2\)

Let \(\mathcal{V}\) denote the set of possible bytes (256 values) and let
\[
\mathbf{x} = (x_1,x_2,\dots,x_N)
\]
be a sequence of bytes produced by the training data distribution.
A language model supplies an estimate of the conditional distribution
\(p_\theta(x_i \mid x_{<i})\), parameterized by \(\theta\).  
During training, we compute the standard one–step cross‑entropy loss
\[
\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})
= - \sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_\theta(x_i \mid x_{<i}),
\]
where logs are taken in natural base \(e\).  
The numerator of BPB is thus precisely this cross‑entropy sum; it measures how many *nats* of uncertainty remain after observing each byte.

To express this quantity in **bits**, we divide by \(\ln 2\), because
\(1~{\rm nat}=1/\ln(2)~{\rm bits}\).  Moreover we must average over the **number of bytes** in the sequence to obtain an *expected* loss per byte.  Formally,
\[
\boxed{
{\rm BPB}(\mathbf{x})
= \frac{1}{N\,\ln 2}
   \sum_{i=1}^{N} -\,\log p_\theta(x_i \mid x_{<i})
}
= \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})}{N\,\ln 2}.
\]
Thus **BPB** is simply the cross‑entropy divided by \(\log_2 e \approx 1.4427\) and further conditioned on one byte per observation.

### Intuition

Think of a perfectly predictive model that never errs: for every byte it assigns probability 1 to the correct next byte and 0 to all others. Its cross‑entropy becomes zero, yielding BPB = 0 bits per byte—an ideal limit. Conversely, a naïve uniform guess over \(256\) possible bytes records
\(p_\theta(x_i)=1/256\) for all \(i\); then each term contributes
\(-\log(1/256)=8\,{\rm nats}\), leading to
\[ {\rm BPB}=8/\ln 2 \approx 11.6\,{\rm bits}, \]
which reflects maximal uncertainty (since all \(256^N\) sequences are equally likely).  

Because BPB measures *average information per symbol*, it mirrors the classic notion from information theory: the entropy rate of the source if our estimator were perfectly optimal.

---

## 5.2 Relationship to Cross‑Entropy Loss

The **cross‑entropy loss** (\(\mathcal{L}_{CE}\)) is often reported during training because it directly optimizes model parameters via stochastic gradient descent. However, its raw magnitude depends on:

1. **Length of the sequence** (\(N\)), because longer batches accumulate more negative log probabilities.
2. The base of logs used (natural vs base 10 vs base 2).

In contrast, BPB controls for these factors by:

- **Normalizing by length**: dividing by \(N=\#\,{\rm bytes}\) guarantees comparability among batches of different sizes.
- **Changing logarithm base**: conversion from nats to bits ensures that closer alignment with human-meaningful units (bits correspond directly to binary representation).

Mathematically:
\[
{\rm BPB}
= \frac{1}{N}
  \underbrace{-\,E_{\mathbf{x}}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_\theta(x_i | x_{<i})/\ln 2 \right]}_{\text{Expectation over data}}
= E_{\mathbf{x}}\!\left[ -\,E_{\text{next}}[\log_2 p_\theta]\right].
\]

This shows that BPB is essentially the *expected* per-byte entropy under the model’s distribution.

#### Example

Suppose we have two models \(A,B\):
- Model A yields an average cross‑entropy loss of \(37~{\rm nats/byte}\).
- Model B yields \(36~{\rm nats/byte}\).

Converting:
- \( {\rm BPB}_A = 37 / \ln 2 \approx 25.6~{\rm bits/byte}. \)
- \( {\rm BPB}_B = 36 / \ln 2 \approx 24.9~{\rm bits/byte}. \)

Although both metrics are close numerically, converting to bits clarifies that model B reduces uncertainty by roughly \(0.7~{\rm bits/byte}\), which translates to about a halving in the number of candidate completions at each step.

## 5.3 Comparability Across Token versus Byte Models

When evaluating diverse modeling architectures—say a traditional BPE tokenizer followed by an LLM versus a “tokenizer‑free” byte encoder—direct comparison using perplexity defeats us:

- **Perplexity** always depends on vocabulary size; larger vocabularies yield lower perplexities even if underlying prediction quality is identical.
- Different tokenization strategies produce varying length distributions; comparing raw sequence lengths biases even more heavily.

BPB removes these artifacts:

| Model                      | Tokenizer? | Vocabulary Size |
|----------------------------|------------|-----------------|
| Llama 3                     | YES        | ~128K tokens    |
| BLT (entropy patching)     | NO         | emergent patches|
| BLT (space patching)       | NO         | emergent patches|

Because each byte is treated atomically under both regimes, *any* difference in performance captured by BPB necessarily arises from statistical modeling power rather than preprocessing quirks.

### Practical Calculation Flow

During evaluation on held-out text:

1. Pass each input through the entire pipeline (tokenizer or patch encoder).
2. For every downstream prediction step produce \(p_\theta(x_i|x_{<i})\).
3. Accumulate un-normalized log probabilities.
4. After processing all samples, compute:
   - Total bytes processed: \(N_{\text{tot}}\).
   - Total un-normalized log probability sum: \(S_{\text{ce}}\).
5. Output:
   \[
   {\rm BPB} = -\,S_{\text{ce}}/(N_{\text{tot}}\,\ln 2).
   \]

Because no explicit vocabulary lookup is required for bytes—their embedding indices range over only $256$ items—this procedure scales linearly with input size and verifies that predictions were performed on raw atomic symbols whenever possible.

---

## Conclusion

Bits‑Per‑Byte offers an elegant bridge between theoretically grounded information theory and pragmatic large‑scale model evaluation:

- It normalizes against sequence length and logarithmic base so that short vs long texts do not skew results.
- It seamlessly applies to any decoding procedure—including those without fixed units—making high‐level scaling laws independent from token semantics.
- Because it is essentially an entropy rate estimate, it lets researchers reason about *how much* uncertainty remains per byte or token irrespective of how those symbols were constructed.

As LLM research ventures further into block-free or hierarchical schemes where tokens no longer dictate computational granularity—as witnessed in the Byte Latent Transformer—the need for such homogeneous metrics becomes indispensable for fair model comparison and evidence‐based architecture design.’
