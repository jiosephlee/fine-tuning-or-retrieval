\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# From Sketches and Segmentation Maps to Finished Images: How OFT Improves Controllable Generation

## When text alone is not enough

Text-to-image models are great at turning prompts into images, but text is often too vague for precise control. If you want a building to follow a sketch, a scene to match a segmentation map, or a face to respect landmark positions, you need the model to obey more than words. That is the problem this paper tackles with controllable generation.

The core idea is simple: finetune a pretrained diffusion model so it can follow an extra structural signal without losing the visual quality it already knows how to produce. The paper’s answer is Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT. Instead of updating weights in a way that can distort the pretrained model, OFT learns orthogonal transformations that preserve the pairwise angular relationships between neurons. In the paper’s terms, this preserves hyperspherical energy, which the authors argue is important for keeping the model’s original semantic generation ability intact.

For controllable generation, that matters a lot. You want the model to become more obedient to the input control signal, but not at the cost of making images collapse, drift, or lose realism.

## What controllable generation looks like in practice

The paper studies three concrete control settings.

The first is canny edge to image, or C2I, on the COCO dataset. Here the input is a rough edge map, and the model must turn that structure into a coherent image.

The second is segmentation map to image, or S2I, on ADE20K. In this case, the model gets a semantic layout and must render an image that respects those object regions.

The third is landmark to face, or L2F, on CelebA-HQ. This is a particularly strict test of structural control, because the model has to generate a face that matches a set of facial landmarks and therefore a specific pose and geometry.

These tasks are useful because they separate “looks realistic” from “actually follows the conditioning signal.” A model can produce a beautiful image while ignoring the sketch or map. The paper argues that OFT helps close that gap.

## Why OFT is a good fit for structural control

OFT’s main property is that it updates the model by applying learned orthogonal transformations to neuron weights. That sounds abstract, but the practical effect is straightforward: the model changes in a controlled way.

Orthogonal updates preserve the angles between neurons in a layer. The authors connect this to the idea that angular relationships carry semantic structure. So if you want to modify how the model responds to control signals, changing those directions can be effective, but doing it while preserving the pretrained geometry helps keep the original generative ability intact.

The paper also proposes Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning, or COFT. COFT adds an explicit constraint that keeps the finetuned model close to the pretrained one. In practice, this gives an additional stability boost. For controllable generation, that stability is important because the model has to learn a new input pathway without becoming brittle.

Another practical advantage is that OFT does not add test-time inference overhead. After finetuning, the learned orthogonal transform is folded into the pretrained weights, so the model runs at the same speed as before.

## How the paper measures whether control really works

One of the most useful parts of the paper is its evaluation approach. For controllable generation, standard image quality metrics are not enough. A model can generate a sharp image and still ignore the conditioning signal. So the paper introduces a control consistency metric.

The idea is to estimate the control signal back from the generated image and compare it to the original input control signal. In other words, instead of only asking “does this image look good?”, the method asks “did the model actually preserve the intended structure?”

The exact metric depends on the task:

For C2I, the paper uses IoU and F1.

For S2I, it uses mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy.

For L2F, it measures the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the control landmarks and the landmarks predicted from the generated face.

This is a sensible way to evaluate controllability because it directly checks alignment between the output and the structural input.

## What the experiments show

Across the three control tasks, OFT and COFT outperform the paper’s baselines, which include ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. The gains show up in both quantitative metrics and visual quality.

On the landmark-to-face task, OFT and COFT converge faster than the baselines. The paper reports that LoRA takes much longer to reach similar performance, and that the OFT variants train more smoothly. The authors also note that COFT is especially stable because the explicit deviation constraint makes optimization easier to manage.

On segmentation-to-image, OFT and COFT produce images that follow the segmentation layout more faithfully while also looking more realistic. In the paper’s examples, LoRA struggles to follow the segmentation map, while ControlNet, OFT, and COFT perform much better. OFT and COFT also produce more vivid details and more reasonable geometry with fewer trainable parameters than ControlNet.

On canny edge to image, OFT and COFT can hallucinate semantically similar images from rough edges more effectively than T2I-Adapter and LoRA. This is a useful result because edge maps are often incomplete by design; the model has to infer plausible visual details while staying aligned with the outline.

The broader pattern is that OFT gives the model enough flexibility to learn control, but not so much freedom that it destroys the pretrained image prior.

## Why this matters for designers and creators

For designers, illustrators, and other creators, controllable generation is valuable because it turns a generative model from a prompt machine into a structural rendering tool. A sketch becomes a scene. A layout becomes an image. A face pose becomes a concrete portrait.

The challenge is reliability. If a model only sometimes follows the conditioning signal, it is hard to use in a real workflow. The paper’s results suggest that OFT improves that reliability by making finetuning more stable and more sample-efficient. The model converges with fewer training images and fewer epochs, and it does so without adding inference cost.

That combination is important in practice. Better controllability is not just about higher benchmark scores; it is about making the model easier to steer and easier to trust.

## The main takeaway

The paper shows that controllable generation is not just a matter of adding more parameters or training longer. The way you finetune matters. OFT works because it changes the model in a structurally restrained way, preserving the pretrained representation while still adapting to new control signals.

For tasks like canny edge to image, segmentation-to-image, and face landmark control, that leads to stronger adherence to the input structure, faster convergence, and better stability. And by introducing control consistency metrics, the paper also gives us a more meaningful way to check whether a model truly obeys its conditioning input.

In short, OFT makes diffusion models better at listening.
