\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Why AI Image Models Forget: A Simple Guide to Orthogonal Finetuning

Text-to-image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion can generate impressive images from prompts, but they are not always easy to adapt. If you finetune them on a narrow task, such as learning a specific subject or following an extra control signal, they can start to forget the broad visual knowledge they had before. The result is a familiar kind of drift: the model becomes better at the new task, but worse at general image quality, prompt following, or subject identity.

The paper *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning* proposes a neat way around this problem. Instead of letting finetuning freely change the model weights, it constrains the update to be an orthogonal transformation. In plain language, the model is allowed to rotate or reflect its internal feature space, but not distort it arbitrarily. That simple restriction turns out to preserve much of the pretrained model’s structure while still giving it room to adapt.

## The problem: finetuning can damage what the model already knows

The paper focuses on two common text-to-image adaptation tasks.

In subject-driven generation, the model gets a few images of a subject and must generate that same subject in new contexts from a text prompt. Think of teaching the model a specific dog, chair, or toy.

In controllable generation, the model gets an additional signal such as canny edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks, and must generate an image that follows both the text prompt and the control input.

Both tasks require the model to learn something new without ruining what it already knows. Standard finetuning often does this with small learning rates, or with methods like LoRA that add a constrained update. But the paper argues that simply keeping the weight update small is not enough. A model can stay numerically close to its pretrained weights and still lose important semantic structure.

That is the key motivation for Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT.

## The core idea: rotate the neurons instead of rewriting them

OFT applies a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $R$ to pretrained weights $W^0$:

$$
W = R W^0, \qquad R^\top R = I.
$$

If you have not seen this kind of update before, the important part is the constraint $R^\top R = I$. It means the transform is orthogonal, so it preserves angles between vectors. This is why the paper describes OFT as preserving the pairwise geometric relationships among neurons in a layer.

Why does that matter? The paper uses hyperspherical energy as a way to summarize the geometry of a layer after normalizing its neurons onto the unit sphere. You do not need the full formula to understand the intuition: it measures how neuron directions relate to each other. Because orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles, they preserve this geometry exactly.

The authors’ main claim is that this structural preservation is important for keeping the semantic generation ability of the pretrained model intact. In other words, if the model already knows how to generate good images, you want finetuning to change the model in a way that respects that internal organization.

A useful analogy from the paper is that of changing coordinates rather than redrawing the whole map. The information is still there, but viewed through a new orientation.

## Why angles matter more than you might expect

The paper’s intuition is that neuron directions carry much of the semantic content of the model. This is consistent with prior observations that angular similarity is often a strong signal for meaning in neural representations.

The paper even includes a toy autoencoder example suggesting that angular information can reconstruct images much better than magnitude information alone. That is not the same as saying magnitude is useless in every setting, but it does support the broader point: when you want to preserve semantic structure, the geometry of vectors matters a lot.

This is also why OFT is different from just applying a small learning rate. Small updates in Euclidean distance do not necessarily preserve the relational structure among neurons. OFT explicitly preserves that structure by restricting the update to an orthogonal transformation.

## Making it practical: COFT and efficient parameterization

Pure orthogonal finetuning is already useful, but the paper goes one step further with Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning, or COFT. COFT adds an explicit radius constraint:

$$
\|R - I\| \leq \epsilon.
$$

This keeps the finetuned model within a controlled neighborhood of the pretrained one. In the paper, this improves stability even further. A smaller $\epsilon$ means the model stays closer to the original pretrained behavior; a larger $\epsilon$ gives it more freedom to adapt.

The paper also addresses efficiency. Enforcing orthogonality directly can be expensive, so OFT uses Cayley parameterization and block-diagonal structure to reduce overhead. The result is a finetuning method that stays parameter-efficient while still preserving the orthogonal constraint.

One practical advantage is that OFT adds no extra inference cost. At test time, the learned orthogonal transform can be folded into the pretrained weights, so the final model runs like a normal diffusion model.

## What the experiments show

The paper evaluates OFT on both subject-driven and controllable generation using Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the base model.

For subject-driven generation, OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA in subject preservation metrics such as DINO and CLIP-I, while keeping prompt fidelity and diversity competitive. More importantly, they are more stable during training. DreamBooth can collapse after long finetuning, and LoRA can lose subject identity, but OFT remains consistent.

For controllable generation, the paper tests tasks like canny edge to image, segmentation map to image, and landmark to face. OFT and COFT achieve stronger control and faster convergence than ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA in the reported experiments. The paper also introduces a control consistency metric that checks whether the generated image actually preserves the original control signal, and OFT performs well there too.

A recurring theme in the results is that OFT is not only effective, but also easier to train. It converges faster, is less sensitive to training iteration count, and works well with fewer training images.

## The big takeaway

The main lesson from this paper is simple but useful: when adapting a powerful generative model, preserving its internal geometry can matter more than just keeping weight changes small.

Orthogonal Finetuning does this by learning rotations and reflections of neuron space instead of unconstrained updates. That preserves pairwise angles, preserves hyperspherical energy, and seems to preserve the pretrained model’s semantic structure much better than naive finetuning.

If you think of finetuning as editing a model, OFT is a careful edit that keeps the original layout intact. That is exactly what you want when the goal is to customize an image model without making it forget how to generate good images in the first place.
