\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# How to Teach an Image Model a New Subject with Just a Few Photos

Teaching a text-to-image model a new subject is trickier than it sounds. Give it a handful of photos of a person, pet, or object, and you want two things at once: the model should keep that subject recognizable, and it should still follow new prompts like “in a snowy forest” or “wearing sunglasses.” In practice, those goals often fight each other. If finetuning is too aggressive, the model can drift, overfit, or collapse. If it is too conservative, it never really learns the subject.

The paper *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning* tackles this problem with a surprisingly simple idea: instead of freely rewriting the model’s weights, update them by an orthogonal transformation. In plain terms, the method tries to rotate the model’s internal geometry without breaking the structure that already knows how to generate good images.

## Why few-shot subject learning is hard

The subject-driven generation setting is straightforward to state and hard to solve. You provide a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model with a few images of a specific subject, plus a text prompt describing how you want that subject to appear in a new scene. The model must preserve identity while also respecting the prompt.

This is exactly the kind of finetuning where standard approaches can struggle. DreamBooth-style finetuning updates the pretrained model directly, usually with a small learning rate and a prior-preservation loss. LoRA, another popular method, adds a low-rank update instead of changing all the weights. Both help, but neither gives a principled guarantee that the pretrained generation ability will survive the finetuning process.

The paper shows two common failure modes. One is loss of identity: the model no longer clearly looks like the subject. The other is instability over training time: if you keep finetuning, the outputs can degrade or collapse. In the paper’s examples, DreamBooth starts producing collapsed images after more iterations, and LoRA can lose key identity details entirely.

The core challenge is not just “learn the new subject.” It is “learn the new subject without damaging the old model.”

## The key idea behind OFT

OFT stands for Orthogonal Finetuning. The basic insight is that the *direction* of neurons matters more than their raw magnitude for preserving semantics. The paper motivates this with an analysis of hyperspherical similarity, which captures the angles between neurons in a layer. If those angles stay the same, the layer keeps much of its original structure.

That is where orthogonal transformations come in. An orthogonal matrix is essentially a rotation or reflection. If you apply the same orthogonal transform to all neurons in a layer, you preserve pairwise angles exactly. The paper uses this property to define OFT: finetune the model by learning a layer-shared orthogonal transformation instead of making unrestricted weight updates.

A useful way to think about it is that OFT changes the coordinate system of a layer rather than rewriting the knowledge inside it. The model can still adapt to the new subject, but it does so while keeping the geometric relationships among neurons intact. The authors argue that this preserves the pretrained model’s semantic structure, which is crucial for good image generation.

They also give an intuitive motivation from a toy experiment with an autoencoder trained on flower images. In that setup, angular information in the neurons was enough to almost perfectly reconstruct the input, while magnitude alone was not very informative. That supports the paper’s claim that angles carry a lot of semantic signal.

## COFT adds a leash

OFT already helps with stability, but the paper goes one step further with Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning, or COFT.

COFT uses the same orthogonal update, but adds an explicit constraint that keeps the learned transform close to the identity matrix. In practical terms, that means the finetuned model is not allowed to wander too far from the pretrained one. This is useful because it gives a cleaner balance between flexibility and stability.

The paper’s interpretation is simple: OFT preserves the layer geometry, while COFT preserves the geometry and also limits how far the update can move. That extra constraint makes training even more predictable. In the experiments, the value of the constraint controls how much the model adapts. A larger constraint gives behavior closer to OFT, while a smaller one keeps the model more similar to the original pretrained diffusion model.

## What the paper shows on subject-driven generation

For subject-driven generation, the authors use Stable Diffusion v1.5 and compare OFT and COFT against DreamBooth and LoRA. All methods use the same DreamBooth loss, so the comparison is about the finetuning strategy itself rather than a different training objective.

The results are strongest in two places: identity preservation and training stability.

In qualitative examples, OFT and COFT keep the subject recognizable while still following the prompt well. LoRA often fails to preserve identity, and DreamBooth can preserve identity but struggle more with prompt following. The paper also shows that OFT and COFT remain stable even after many more training iterations. That matters in practice, because it means the method is less sensitive to the exact number of finetuning steps. You do not need to carefully baby-sit the optimization process for each new subject.

The quantitative results tell the same story. The paper measures subject fidelity with DINO and CLIP-I, prompt fidelity with CLIP-T, and diversity with LPIPS. Across these metrics, OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on subject fidelity by a wide margin, while matching or slightly improving prompt fidelity and diversity.

The authors also repeat finetuning across 30 random seeds, which makes the result more convincing: this is not just a lucky run. OFT and COFT are consistently better behaved.

## Why this approach is appealing

There are two practical reasons this method stands out.

First, it is parameter-efficient. OFT uses a structured orthogonal transform rather than a full unconstrained update, so it can adapt the model without modifying as many parameters. The paper also notes that OFT does not add inference overhead. At test time, the learned transform can simply be absorbed into the pretrained weights, so generation speed stays the same as the original model.

Second, it gives a cleaner story about why finetuning works. Instead of relying on a very small learning rate and hoping the model stays close enough to the original solution, OFT explicitly preserves an internal geometric property of the layer. That makes the method less like “fine-tune carefully and hope for the best” and more like “adapt while preserving the model’s structure.”

## Takeaway

If you want to teach a diffusion model a new subject from just a few photos, the main difficulty is not merely learning the subject. It is learning it without breaking the pretrained model’s ability to generate high-quality images in response to text.

That is the problem OFT tries to solve. By finetuning with orthogonal transformations, it preserves the angular structure of each layer, which the paper argues is closely tied to semantic information. COFT tightens the approach further by keeping the update close to the original model. In the subject-driven generation experiments, both methods produce more stable finetuning, better identity preservation, and better overall consistency than DreamBooth and LoRA.

In short: OFT is a neat example of how a geometric constraint can make finetuning not just more efficient, but also much more reliable.
