\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet… and Now OFT: What’s the Difference?

Finetuning text-to-image diffusion models is a balancing act. You want the model to learn something new, like a specific subject or a new control signal, without breaking the photorealism and semantic richness it already knows.

That is the problem this paper tackles with Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT. Instead of updating weights in the usual way, OFT learns a layer-wise orthogonal transformation of the pretrained weights. In plain terms, it changes the model by rotating or reflecting neuron directions, which preserves the internal geometry of the layer. The paper argues that this geometry matters because it helps preserve the pretrained model’s generation ability.

## The basic trade-off: adaptation vs. preservation

Every finetuning method has to answer the same question: how much should the model change?

If you change too little, the model does not learn the new task well. If you change too much, it may lose the broad generative ability that made the pretrained model useful in the first place.

The paper looks at two common text-to-image settings:

- **Subject-driven generation**: learn a specific subject from only a few images, then generate that subject in new scenes.
- **Controllable generation**: follow an extra signal such as canny edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks.

OFT is designed to do both while preserving the pretrained model’s behavior more reliably than standard finetuning.

## What makes OFT different?

Most finetuning methods think in terms of Euclidean distance: keep the updated model close to the original model.

OFT takes a different view. It focuses on preserving **pairwise angles between neurons** in a layer. The paper uses the notion of **hyperspherical energy** to describe this structure. You do not need the full math to get the intuition: if the relationships among neurons stay the same, the model is more likely to keep its pretrained semantics.

OFT enforces this by learning an orthogonal matrix $R$ and applying it to the pretrained weights $W^0$:

$W = RW^0$

Because $R$ is orthogonal, it preserves angles and norms. That means OFT can adapt the model without tearing apart the layer geometry.

The paper also introduces **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)**, which adds a radius constraint so the learned transform stays near the identity matrix. This makes finetuning even more stable.

## OFT vs. DreamBooth

DreamBooth is the classic subject-personalization baseline. It finetunes the diffusion model directly with a small set of subject images, usually with a prior-preservation loss to avoid forgetting.

In practice, DreamBooth works, but the paper highlights two weaknesses:

- It can be sensitive to the number of training iterations.
- It may eventually drift or collapse if finetuning goes too far.

OFT uses the DreamBooth training setup, but replaces naive weight updates with orthogonal transformations. The result, according to the paper, is more stable training and better preservation of the subject’s identity, while still following the text prompt well.

A useful way to think about the difference is this: DreamBooth nudges the model directly, while OFT changes the model through a geometry-preserving transformation.

## OFT vs. LoRA

LoRA is popular because it is parameter-efficient. Instead of updating the full weight matrix, it learns a low-rank additive update. That makes it light, easy to fine-tune, and practical for many workflows.

OFT is also parameter-efficient, but in a different way. It does not rely on a low-rank additive patch. Instead, it parameterizes an orthogonal transform, often with block structure to keep the number of trainable parameters small.

The paper’s practical comparison is interesting:

- LoRA is efficient, but in the experiments it has a lower ceiling on subject fidelity and controllability.
- OFT converges faster and is more stable.
- OFT preserves pretrained semantics better, which seems especially important when the new task is sensitive to identity or control accuracy.

So if LoRA is “small additive change,” OFT is “structured geometric change.”

## OFT vs. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter

ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are designed for controllable generation. They make the model obey spatial or structural signals such as edges, masks, or landmarks.

These methods are strong because they inject explicit control pathways into the generation process. But they also come with trade-offs: more machinery, more training burden, and in the paper’s comparison, slower convergence than OFT.

OFT takes a different route. It does not add a separate control branch. Instead, it finetunes the pretrained diffusion model itself using orthogonal transforms. In the experiments, that gives three practical advantages:

- stronger controllability than the baselines in the tested tasks,
- fewer trainable parameters than ControlNet,
- no extra inference overhead.

That last point matters. Since the learned transform can be folded into the pretrained weights after training, OFT runs at the same inference speed as the original model.

## What the experiments show

The paper evaluates OFT on Stable Diffusion v1.5 across two settings.

For **subject-driven generation**, OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on identity preservation metrics such as DINO and CLIP-I, while keeping prompt fidelity and diversity competitive. The authors also show that OFT is much less sensitive to the exact number of finetuning steps.

For **controllable generation**, they test canny edge to image, segmentation map to image, and landmark to face tasks. OFT and COFT converge faster than ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA, and they achieve stronger control consistency. The paper even introduces a new control-consistency metric: generate an image, estimate the control signal back from that image, and compare it to the original input signal.

That is a nice practical idea, because it checks whether the model truly followed the control input rather than just producing a plausible-looking image.

## So when does OFT make sense?

If you want a quick mental model, here is the short version:

- **DreamBooth**: good for straightforward subject finetuning, but can be unstable and needs careful tuning.
- **LoRA**: great when you want lightweight updates, but it may not preserve semantics or controllability as well as you want.
- **ControlNet / T2I-Adapter**: strong choices for explicit control signals, but with extra model complexity.
- **OFT / COFT**: a geometry-preserving finetuning strategy that aims for a better balance of stability, efficiency, and controllability.

The main idea behind OFT is simple, even if the math is not: do not just make the weights “close” to the pretrained model; preserve the structure that makes the pretrained model good in the first place.

That is why orthogonal finetuning is an interesting addition to the diffusion toolbox. It offers a different answer to the finetuning problem: instead of patching the model or attaching a controller, rotate the model’s internal representation in a way that keeps its original knowledge intact.
