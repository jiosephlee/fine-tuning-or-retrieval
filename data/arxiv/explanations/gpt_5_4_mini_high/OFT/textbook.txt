\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

# What text-to-image diffusion models do well

Large text-to-image diffusion models have made it possible to generate photorealistic images from text prompts with striking quality. The paper emphasizes that these models already have impressive text-guided control and can produce high-fidelity images, which is why they are such attractive backbones for downstream adaptation. In other words, they are not being introduced as weak models that need to be replaced; they are powerful pretrained generators whose semantic knowledge is already rich.

At the same time, the paper’s motivation is that the very strength of these models creates a new challenge. Once a model has learned broad visual semantics from large-scale pretraining, the question is no longer simply whether it can generate images, but how to steer it toward a specific downstream task without destroying what it already knows. This is the core problem behind the paper’s focus on controllability. The model must remain a good general image generator even after being adapted to a more specialized use case.

# Pixel-space versus latent-space diffusion models

The paper situates text-to-image diffusion models in two broad families: pixel-space models and latent-space models. GLIDE and Imagen are examples of pixel-space diffusion systems. In that setting, the diffusion process operates directly on image pixels. The paper notes that GLIDE trains the text encoder jointly with a diffusion prior using paired text-image data, while Imagen uses a frozen pretrained text encoder.

A second family is latent-space diffusion, where the model works in a compressed representation rather than directly in pixel space. Stable Diffusion and DALL-E2 are given as examples. Stable Diffusion uses VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook that serves as its latent space, while DALL-E2 uses CLIP to build a joint latent embedding space for images and text. The paper’s point is not that one family is universally better than the other, but that modern text-to-image generation has advanced through both routes, and the proposed finetuning framework is intended to be broadly usable.

This distinction matters for the paper because the downstream adaptation problem is not tied to a particular generation space. Whether the model is operating in pixels or latent variables, the central issue is still how to control the generator in a way that preserves the pretrained model’s semantic capabilities. The paper therefore frames its method as model-agnostic, even though its experiments use a specific latent-space backbone.

# Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained backbone

In the experiments, the paper uses Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained text-to-image model. This choice is important because Stable Diffusion is a strong, widely used latent-space generator, and the paper uses it as the base model to study finetuning for control tasks. The backbone is not being trained from scratch; instead, the goal is to adapt a capable pretrained model.

This experimental setup reflects the paper’s broader philosophy. The key question is not how to make a text-to-image model work at all, but how to alter its behavior with minimal disruption to the generative knowledge it already contains. By starting from Stable Diffusion v1.5, the paper can directly test whether a finetuning strategy preserves image quality, semantic richness, and stability while adding new controllable behavior.

# Why text-only guidance is often too weak for fine-grained control

Although text prompts are powerful, the paper argues that text-only guidance is often ambiguous and insufficient for precise control. A prompt can describe the broad content of an image, but it frequently leaves many details underspecified. This is especially problematic when the desired output must match a specific identity, pose, spatial arrangement, or structural constraint.

The paper’s motivation is that many downstream tasks need more than a free-form caption. For example, if a user wants a particular subject to appear in new contexts, the text prompt alone does not tell the model which exact subject to preserve. Likewise, if the goal is to follow a specific edge map or segmentation layout, text alone cannot encode those spatial instructions. In such cases, the model needs extra guidance beyond language.

This is why the paper frames the problem as one of controllability rather than just text conditioning. Text gives a useful semantic direction, but it does not fully determine the image. The downstream task is therefore to introduce stronger control signals while keeping the pretrained generator faithful and visually coherent.

# Subject-driven generation: personalizing a model from a few images

One of the two main downstream settings studied in the paper is subject-driven generation. The task is simple to state: given only a few images of a subject and a text prompt, the model should generate new images of that same subject in different contexts. The subject could be a specific object, person, or other instance, and the text prompt is used to place that subject into a new scene or action.

The difficulty is that the model must learn identity-specific details from very little data. It is not enough to generate something merely similar in category; the output must preserve the subject’s distinctive appearance. At the same time, the prompt still has to control the context. So subject-driven generation asks the model to balance two forms of information: identity from the few reference images and semantic composition from the text prompt.

The paper discusses DreamBooth as a representative framework for this task. DreamBooth finetunes a text-to-image diffusion model using a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior preservation loss, together with a customized token representing the subject. The paper’s contribution is not to redefine the task, but to improve how the model is adapted to it. Subject-driven generation is a strong test of whether finetuning can personalize a model without erasing its general ability to generate realistic images.

# Controllable generation: conditioning on signals such as edges, segmentation maps, and landmarks

The second downstream setting is controllable generation, where the model receives an additional control signal alongside the text prompt. The paper gives examples such as canny edges, segmentation maps, and landmarks. The purpose of these signals is to constrain the structure of the output image more explicitly than text can.

The paper studies three concrete tasks in this category. In canny edge to image generation, the model must produce an image that follows a boundary sketch. In segmentation map to image generation, the model must respect the semantic layout encoded by the map. In landmark to face generation, the model must generate faces whose geometry matches facial landmarks. All of these tasks ask the same basic question: can a pretrained generator be adapted so that it obeys an external structural guide while still producing realistic images?

This setting makes the control problem especially visible. A model that generates attractive images is not necessarily a controlled model. It may ignore the extra signal, follow it only loosely, or respond in an unstable way. The paper’s framing is that controllable generation requires a finetuning strategy that can inject new conditioning information without causing the model to collapse or forget how to generate high-quality images.

# The two main goals of downstream adaptation: training efficiency and preservation of generalizable generation

The paper summarizes effective finetuning with two main desiderata. The first is training efficiency, meaning that the adaptation method should use fewer trainable parameters and fewer training epochs. The second is preservation of generalizable generation, meaning that the finetuned model should keep the pretrained model’s ability to generate high-fidelity, diverse, and semantically coherent images.

These two goals are closely related. If adaptation is too heavy-handed, the model may become specialized but lose the broad generative competence learned during pretraining. If adaptation is too weak or inefficient, it may fail to learn the new task well. The paper emphasizes that a successful finetuning method needs to strike a balance between flexibility and stability.

This balance becomes especially important for large text-to-image diffusion models. Because these models are already very capable, the finetuning method should not treat them as blank slates. Instead, the adaptation should be economical and controlled, preserving the structure of the pretrained generator while steering it toward the desired downstream behavior.

# Why finetuning large generative models is harder than standard supervised finetuning

Finetuning large generative models is difficult for a reason that is different from ordinary supervised learning. In the paper’s discussion, existing methods often update the pretrained weights with a small learning rate or add a small reparameterized component. These strategies are simple and intuitive because they try to keep the finetuned model close to the pretrained one. However, the paper stresses that such closeness is not enough to guarantee preservation of generative performance.

The challenge is that for generation tasks, the model’s internal structure matters. A small change in Euclidean distance between weights does not necessarily correspond to a small change in semantic behavior. The paper argues that Euclidean difference alone is not a sufficiently informative measure of how much the pretrained generative ability has been preserved. This is why the authors see a need for a more structural way to think about adaptation.

Another complication is that there is no easy principled rule for choosing finetuning hyperparameters such as the number of training epochs. Standard finetuning can be fragile: if it is too conservative, the model may not adapt; if it is too aggressive, it may lose the pretrained generative structure. The paper describes this as a key difficulty in adapting large diffusion models, and it is exactly what motivates the search for a more principled finetuning framework.

From the paper’s perspective, the hard part is not just learning a downstream task. It is learning that task while respecting the pretrained model’s internal organization. That is why the paper treats controllability and preservation as a coupled problem rather than separate ones. The method must provide enough freedom to learn new behavior, but not so much freedom that the original generative capabilities are destroyed.

Existing Finetuning Approaches and Their Limitations

# Standard finetuning with small learning rates

The most direct way to adapt a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model is to keep training it on the downstream task with gradient descent, usually with a small learning rate. In the paper, this is described as the conventional finetuning strategy: one updates the model itself, or only certain layers, while trying to avoid moving too far from the pretrained solution. The basic intuition is simple. If the pretrained model already knows how to generate realistic images, then only a modest amount of adjustment should be needed to specialize it for a new task such as subject-driven generation or controllable generation.

This strategy is appealing because it is easy to implement and, at first glance, it seems to respect the pretrained model. A small learning rate implicitly encourages the finetuned parameters to stay close to the original parameters, which is often taken as a sign that the model’s prior knowledge is being preserved. In the paper’s notation, this amounts to treating finetuning as if it were approximately minimizing a distance such as $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0 \|$, where $\bm{M}$ denotes the finetuned weights and $\bm{M}^0$ denotes the pretrained weights. However, the paper emphasizes that this is only a heuristic. It is not a principled guarantee that the model will keep the same generative behavior, and in large diffusion models even a small parameter change can eventually accumulate into a meaningful semantic shift.

# Why small Euclidean weight changes are commonly assumed to preserve pretrained knowledge

The common belief behind small-learning-rate finetuning is that if the weights do not move very far in Euclidean space, then the model should not change much functionally. This is a natural first guess, and it explains why many finetuning methods are designed to constrain the magnitude of the update rather than its structure. The paper notes that existing methods often implicitly rely on exactly this assumption: smaller Euclidean distance from the pretrained model is treated as a proxy for better preservation of pretrained knowledge.

The limitation, according to the paper, is that Euclidean distance is too coarse to capture what actually matters for preserving semantics in a neural network. Two models can be close in parameter space while still differing in how their neurons relate to one another, and those relational changes can alter the model’s generation behavior. The paper argues that semantic preservation is not only about how far the weights move, but about how the internal structure of the representation changes. That is why the authors say Euclidean difference alone cannot fully capture the degree of semantic preservation. In their view, a more structural notion of similarity is needed, one that reflects the organization of neurons rather than only the size of the parameter update.

# DreamBooth for subject-driven generation

DreamBooth is the main subject-driven generation baseline discussed in the paper. The task is to take only a few images of a subject and finetune a text-to-image diffusion model so that it can generate that same subject in new contexts described by text prompts. DreamBooth does this by introducing a customized token for the subject and finetuning with a reconstruction loss together with a class-specific prior preservation loss. In the paper’s setup, this makes DreamBooth a strong and natural baseline for personalized generation.

At the same time, the paper shows that DreamBooth can be fragile. Because it directly updates the pretrained model with a standard finetuning procedure, it may preserve identity reasonably well for a while but still become unstable with more training. The paper reports that after enough iterations, DreamBooth can begin to generate collapsed images. It can also maintain the subject identity while failing to follow the text prompt accurately, which means that the model may remember the person, object, or instance but not the requested context. This tension between identity preservation and prompt adherence is one of the main limitations of naive finetuning in the subject-driven setting.

# LoRA as a low-rank additive update method

LoRA is presented in the paper as a parameter-efficient alternative to full finetuning. Its basic idea is to represent the weight update as a low-rank addition, rather than updating all of the weights freely. This means that the model is adapted through a restricted additive update, which reduces the number of trainable parameters. In the paper’s comparison, LoRA is a useful reference point because it embodies the general strategy of making finetuning cheaper while trying to preserve the behavior of the pretrained model.

The paper also makes clear why LoRA is only a partial solution. Low-rank structure is a useful form of constraint, but it is not the same as preserving the relational geometry of the neurons. In the subject-driven experiments, LoRA can fail to preserve the subject identity, and in the controllable generation experiments it shows a relatively low performance ceiling even after careful hyperparameter tuning. In the paper’s interpretation, LoRA prevents the pretrained weights from shifting too dramatically, but it does not directly preserve the semantic structure that underlies the pretrained model’s generative ability. As a result, LoRA can be efficient, yet still insufficiently stable for preserving the fine details of a pretrained diffusion model.

# Adapter-style finetuning and parameter-efficient transfer

The paper places LoRA within a broader family of parameter-efficient adaptation methods, including adapter-style approaches that have been widely studied in natural language processing. These methods aim to make finetuning practical by adding a small number of trainable components or by reparameterizing the update, rather than retraining the full network. The motivation is straightforward: if the pretrained model is already very powerful, then perhaps only a small amount of additional capacity is needed to specialize it for a downstream task.

What the paper stresses, however, is that parameter efficiency alone does not solve the harder problem of preserving pretrained generative performance. Whether the extra parameters are added as adapters, low-rank updates, or other reparameterizations, the central issue remains the same: the method must adapt the model without destroying the structure that supports its original semantics. The paper’s critique is not that these methods are useless, but that they do not come with a principled mechanism for protecting semantic preservation. In practice, they can still require careful tuning of learning rate, rank, or training duration, and they may still show instability when the downstream task pushes the model too far from its pretrained state.

# ControlNet and T2I-Adapter for controllable generation

For controllable generation, the paper focuses on methods that adapt a pretrained diffusion model to accept extra control signals such as canny edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks. ControlNet is highlighted as a prominent example. It finetunes a pretrained diffusion model so that it can follow additional control inputs, and the paper treats it as one of the strongest prior baselines in this setting. T2I-Adapter is another closely related approach, also designed to improve controllability by finetuning a pretrained diffusion model.

The paper’s discussion makes two limitations of these methods especially clear. First, they can be relatively parameter-heavy and, in the case of ControlNet, introduce additional computational overhead at inference time. Second, their optimization behavior may be less stable than desired. In the experiments, the paper reports that controllability in ControlNet can emerge rather suddenly after several epochs, rather than improving in a smooth and predictable way. T2I-Adapter and ControlNet are therefore effective baselines, but they do not fully solve the problem of getting strong controllability while also preserving pretrained generation quality, keeping inference lightweight, and converging reliably.

# Typical failure modes: overfitting, identity drift, prompt mismatch, and model collapse

The paper’s examples make it clear that standard finetuning methods often fail in predictable ways. One common failure mode is overfitting. In subject-driven generation, a model may initially improve at reproducing the subject, but if training continues too long it can collapse. The paper gives a concrete example in which DreamBooth begins to generate collapsed images after many iterations. This shows that more training is not always better; beyond a point, finetuning can damage the model rather than improve it.

A second failure mode is identity drift. This occurs when the generated output no longer resembles the intended subject, even if the model still produces plausible images. The paper notes that LoRA can lose the subject identity in the subject-driven setting. A third failure mode is prompt mismatch: the model may preserve the subject but fail to obey the text prompt describing the desired context. DreamBooth is explicitly described in this way in the paper’s qualitative comparison. In controllable generation, there is a related problem of weak or delayed control, where the generated image does not reliably follow the input signal, or where the control effect appears only after long training. Together, these failure modes show that preserving image realism is not enough; finetuning must preserve the right kind of structure.

# The missing ingredient: a structural measure of semantic preservation beyond Euclidean distance

The paper argues that the central missing ingredient in existing finetuning approaches is a structural measure of semantic preservation. Euclidean distance between pretrained and finetuned weights is too blunt because it only measures how far the parameters move, not how the internal relationships among neurons change. What matters for the semantics of a diffusion model, the paper suggests, is the geometry of those relationships. This is why the authors turn to hyperspherical similarity and hyperspherical energy, which describe pairwise relationships among neurons on the unit hypersphere.

In this view, preserving pretrained knowledge means preserving the relational structure encoded by neuron angles, not merely keeping the weights numerically close. The paper proposes that a good finetuning method should therefore minimize changes in hyperspherical energy, because that quantity captures how uniformly or non-uniformly the neurons are arranged on the sphere. Existing methods such as small-learning-rate finetuning, low-rank adaptation, and adapter-style updates do not explicitly enforce this kind of structural preservation. That omission is what motivates the paper’s later orthogonal finetuning framework. Orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles, and therefore preserve hyperspherical energy exactly, giving the paper a principled way to adapt the model while keeping its pretrained semantic structure intact.

A Geometric View of Neural Semantics

# Viewing neurons as vectors in weight space

The paper begins from a simple but powerful geometric picture: a neuron, or more precisely the weights associated with a neuron in a layer, can be viewed as a vector in a high-dimensional space. In a fully connected layer, the weight matrix is written as $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is one neuron. This viewpoint matters because it turns questions about learning into questions about the relationships among vectors. Instead of thinking only about individual scalar weights, the paper asks how the collection of neuron vectors is arranged relative to one another.

That geometric arrangement is not just a bookkeeping device. The paper argues that the way neurons are positioned in weight space reflects semantic structure in the model. When a model processes an input $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, the output of a fully connected layer is $\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}$. So each neuron contributes through its interaction with the input vector, and the full layer output depends on the entire set of neuron directions and magnitudes. This makes the layer naturally interpretable as a geometric object whose behavior is governed by vector relationships.

The chapter’s central intuition is that, for finetuning a text-to-image diffusion model, preserving the useful arrangement of these vectors may be more important than preserving the exact raw coordinates. In other words, the paper treats the pretrained neuron configuration as a structured geometric object that already encodes generative knowledge, and finetuning should modify that object carefully rather than scramble it.

# Direction versus magnitude: what each part of a neuron represents

A key idea in the paper is that a neuron vector has two conceptually different components: direction and magnitude. The direction describes where the vector points, while the magnitude describes how long it is. The paper’s argument is that, for semantic information, direction is often more important than magnitude. This is why the paper emphasizes neuron angles and orthogonal transformations rather than unconstrained weight changes.

The intuition comes from the fact that the paper studies semantic preservation in a pretrained model. If the goal is to adapt the model to a new task while keeping its generative ability intact, then the critical question is which aspects of the neuron geometry encode that ability. The paper argues that directions carry the main semantic content, while magnitudes can be less informative. This is why the authors focus on angular information and pairwise relationships rather than simply on Euclidean distance between weight tensors.

This distinction also explains why the paper thinks standard finetuning can be fragile. A small Euclidean change in the weights does not necessarily mean that the semantic structure of the neuron configuration has been preserved. A model can move only a little in Euclidean distance and still alter the relative directions of neurons in a way that damages generation quality. Conversely, a transformation that changes coordinates but preserves pairwise angles may retain the important relational structure. The paper builds its finetuning method around exactly this geometric insight.

# Cosine similarity and angular information

To make the notion of direction mathematically meaningful, the paper uses hyperspherical similarity, with cosine similarity as the standard example. Two vectors are similar in this sense if the angle between them is small, and dissimilar if the angle is large. The paper points to prior empirical work showing that angular feature difference can characterize semantic difference well. In the geometric picture adopted here, angles become a proxy for meaning.

This is important because the paper is not just talking about individual vectors in isolation. It is concerned with pairwise relations among neurons in the same layer. Cosine similarity captures those relations by comparing directions after removing magnitude. If $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes a normalized neuron, then the relevant comparison is between $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$. Once vectors are normalized, the remaining information is purely angular, which is exactly the kind of structure the paper wants to preserve.

The paper’s broader claim is that these angular relationships help encode the knowledge of the neural network. That is why preserving the pairwise angles among neurons is treated as a principled finetuning objective. When OFT later learns an orthogonal transformation, it does so precisely because orthogonal transformations preserve these angles. The use of cosine similarity is therefore not incidental; it is the mathematical expression of the paper’s semantic hypothesis.

# The unit hypersphere as a space of normalized neurons

Normalization plays a central role in the paper’s geometric framework. Once each neuron is normalized to unit length, it lies on the unit hypersphere. The set of normalized neurons therefore becomes a configuration of points on a spherical surface rather than arbitrary vectors in Euclidean space. This shift is crucial because it separates direction from magnitude and makes angular geometry explicit.

The paper uses the unit hypersphere to define and reason about hyperspherical energy. In this setting, the arrangement of neurons is judged by how the normalized vectors are distributed on the sphere and by how their pairwise relationships behave. The paper describes hyperspherical energy as characterizing the pairwise relational structure among neurons and capturing their uniformity on the unit hypersphere. In its formal expression for a fully connected layer, it is written as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1},
\]
where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$.

The importance of the hypersphere is that it provides a natural geometric stage on which semantic structure can be compared and preserved. If two models have neuron sets that differ only by an orthogonal transformation, then their points on the hypersphere have the same pairwise angular relationships. In the paper’s view, this means the intrinsic relational structure has been preserved even if the coordinate system has changed. That is why the hypersphere is not just a mathematical convenience, but the key space in which semantic preservation is analyzed.

# Empirical evidence that angular differences correlate with semantic differences

The paper does not rely only on abstract geometry; it also appeals to prior empirical findings. It cites earlier work showing that angular feature difference is a strong indicator of semantic difference. The idea is that changes in direction often reflect changes in meaning more faithfully than changes in raw scale. This motivates the paper’s focus on angles as the main carrier of semantic information.

The evidence is strengthened by the paper’s own toy experiment. There, the authors train a standard convolutional autoencoder on flower images and then examine three ways of generating the feature map in testing: the inner product used during training, the magnitude information, and the angular information. The result is striking: the angular information alone can almost perfectly recover the input images, while magnitude information contains no useful information. The point of the experiment is not that magnitude never matters in any setting, but that, in this example, direction is the part of the representation that preserves the image’s semantic structure.

This empirical result supports the paper’s central thesis in a very direct way. If angular information alone can preserve and reconstruct the image content so effectively, then modifying neuron directions is a plausible way to semantically alter a generative model. At the same time, because the model’s learned structure is encoded in these angles, any finetuning strategy must be careful not to disrupt them arbitrarily. This is exactly the balance OFT seeks.

# The autoencoder toy experiment: angular information versus magnitude information

The toy autoencoder experiment deserves special emphasis because it makes the abstract geometry tangible. The paper trains a convolutional autoencoder on flower images using the standard inner product during training. Then, at test time, it replaces the usual feature computation with different alternatives to see which component carries useful information. The three cases are the original inner product, magnitude information, and angular information.

The outcome shows that angular information can reconstruct the input images almost perfectly, whereas magnitude information fails to provide useful reconstruction. This observation is used by the paper as concrete evidence that the directions of neurons encode essential semantic content. In other words, the experiment suggests that the meaning of the image is stored in how vectors point, not merely in how large they are.

This matters for finetuning because it indicates what should be preserved and what may be safely adjusted. If direction is the semantic carrier, then changing directions is a direct way to modify the model’s behavior. However, because those same directions also store pretrained knowledge, the change must be structured. The toy experiment therefore serves as a bridge between intuition and method: it shows why the paper later turns to orthogonal transformations, which can alter coordinates while preserving the angular relations that the experiment suggests are semantically important.

# The phase-versus-magnitude analogy from Fourier analysis

To motivate direction-based editing further, the paper draws an analogy from the $2$D Fourier transform. In that setting, an image can be decomposed into magnitude and phase spectra. The paper emphasizes that phase contains angular information between the input and the basis, and that this phase information preserves the major part of the semantics. Indeed, the paper notes that the phase spectrum of an image, even with a random magnitude spectrum, can still reconstruct the original image without losing its semantics.

The reason this analogy is useful is that it offers a familiar example of how angular information can dominate meaning. If phase in Fourier analysis preserves the essential structure of an image, then perhaps neuron directions in a neural network play an analogous role. The paper uses this comparison to reinforce the idea that semantic manipulation should focus on directional changes rather than unrestricted coordinate changes.

This analogy does not claim that neurons are literally Fourier coefficients. Rather, it serves as an intuition pump. It suggests that semantic content can be robustly encoded in angular relationships, and that changing those relationships is a controlled way to modify output behavior. The paper’s finetuning method is built on this same belief: if one wants to steer a model’s generation, adjusting the geometry of directions is more meaningful than freely perturbing all weights.

# Why semantic editing may be better achieved by changing neuron directions rather than unrestricted weights

The final step in the paper’s geometric intuition is to connect these ideas to editing and finetuning. In both subject-driven generation and controllable generation, the task is not to retrain the model from scratch, but to adapt it in a way that introduces new control while preserving pretrained generative ability. The paper argues that unrestricted weight updates are too flexible for this purpose. They may change the model enough to damage its original semantics, even if the Euclidean distance from the pretrained weights remains small.

By contrast, changing neuron directions in a controlled way directly targets the part of the representation that appears to carry semantic information. The paper’s geometric claim is that the relationships among neurons, especially their pairwise angles, are what encode the knowledge we want to preserve. If these angles are maintained, the model can be adapted without destroying its intrinsic structure. That is why the paper later designs Orthogonal Finetuning around transformations that preserve pairwise hyperspherical similarity.

The orthogonal viewpoint also explains why the paper sees finetuning as adjusting a coordinate system rather than rewriting the model from scratch. An orthogonal transformation rotates or reflects the neuron vectors while preserving the angles between them. In the paper’s language, this lets the model change its semantic behavior in a controlled way while leaving the hyperspherical energy unchanged. Thus the geometric view of neural semantics is not merely descriptive; it directly motivates the finetuning strategy that follows.

Hyperspherical Energy as a Measure of Structure Preservation

# Definition of hyperspherical similarity between neurons

In the paper, each layer is viewed as a collection of neuron weight vectors. For a fully connected layer, the weights are written as $\mathbf{W}=\{\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each column $\mathbf{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ can be thought of as one neuron. To compare neurons in a way that focuses on their directions rather than their magnitudes, the paper normalizes each vector onto the unit hypersphere by defining $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i:=\mathbf{w}_i/\|\mathbf{w}_i\|$.

Once the neurons are normalized, their relationship is measured by hyperspherical similarity, which the paper describes through angular notions such as cosine similarity. The central idea is simple: two neurons are considered similar if they point in nearly the same direction on the unit sphere, and dissimilar if their directions differ substantially. This is a geometric way of looking at a layer, because it ignores the raw scale of each weight vector and instead concentrates on the relative orientation of the neurons. The paper emphasizes that this angular information is the part that most closely reflects semantic structure.

# Definition of hyperspherical energy for a layer

Hyperspherical energy is the layer-level quantity built from these pairwise directional relationships. For a weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$, the paper defines the hyperspherical energy as
\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Here, the sum runs over all distinct pairs of normalized neurons in the same layer. Because the vectors lie on the unit sphere, the Euclidean distance $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|$ is itself a function of the angle between the two neurons, so the energy is ultimately an angular, similarity-based quantity.

The intuition is that hyperspherical energy compresses the geometry of the whole layer into one scalar. If many neurons point in similar directions, their pairwise distances become small, and the inverse-distance terms become large. If the neurons are more spread out on the hypersphere, the pairwise distances are larger and the resulting energy is different. The paper uses this quantity not as a generic regularizer, but as a structural summary of how a layer’s neurons are arranged relative to one another.

# Interpreting hyperspherical energy as pairwise relational structure among neurons

The main reason the paper cares about hyperspherical energy is that it captures relational structure rather than isolated parameter values. A single neuron does not tell the whole story; what matters is how every neuron in a layer is positioned relative to every other neuron. Because $\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})$ aggregates all pairwise comparisons, it reflects the collective geometry of the layer. In this sense, hyperspherical energy is not just a property of individual weights, but a property of the layer as an organized system.

This is why the paper describes hyperspherical energy as characterizing the pairwise neuron relationship on the unit hypersphere. The model’s internal structure is viewed through the angles among neurons, and those angles are treated as part of the knowledge stored in the pretrained network. If finetuning disrupts these relationships too much, the pretrained semantic structure can be damaged. So hyperspherical energy is used as a structural criterion: it measures whether the finetuned layer still resembles the pretrained layer in terms of its internal geometry.

# Why preserving pairwise angles may preserve pretrained semantics

The paper’s motivation for focusing on angular structure is that directions appear to carry semantic information. It cites prior observations that angular feature differences can characterize semantic gaps well, and it points to SphereNet as evidence that learning on the unit hypersphere can preserve or improve representational capacity. The paper also gives a toy autoencoder experiment on flower images, where angular information at test time can almost perfectly recover the input images, while magnitude information alone is not useful. The lesson is that the direction of a feature vector can be far more informative than its length.

This matters for text-to-image diffusion models because the downstream tasks in the paper are semantic tasks. Subject-driven generation requires the same subject to appear in new contexts, and controllable generation requires the output image to follow an extra control signal. In both cases, the model needs to change the generated content while retaining useful pretrained knowledge. The paper argues that modifying neuron directions is a natural way to alter semantics, but that such modification should be constrained. Preserving pairwise angles is therefore treated as a way of preserving the semantic organization already learned during pretraining.

# The OFT hypothesis: good finetuning should keep hyperspherical energy close to the pretrained model

From this perspective, the paper proposes a very direct hypothesis: a good finetuned model should have hyperspherical energy that stays close to the pretrained model’s hyperspherical energy. In its formal objective, OFT aims to minimize
\[
\left\|\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\right\|,
\]
where $\mathbf{W}^0$ denotes the pretrained weights. The goal is not to make the energy small in an absolute sense, but to preserve the pretrained layerwise structure as much as possible while still adapting to the downstream task.

This is an important distinction. The pretrained diffusion model already contains a useful arrangement of neuron directions, and that arrangement is part of why it can generate high-quality images. OFT assumes that this structure should not be discarded during finetuning. Instead, the model should be adapted in a way that respects the original geometry. In other words, the paper treats the pretrained hyperspherical energy as a structural template that finetuning should follow rather than overwrite.

# Why adding a regularizer alone does not guarantee preservation

A natural first idea would be to add a regularizer that penalizes changes in hyperspherical energy during finetuning. The paper explicitly discusses this possibility, but it argues that such a penalty does not guarantee that the energy difference will actually be well minimized. The issue is that a regularizer is still only an indirect encouragement. It can be outweighed by the main task objective, and it does not inherently force the model to stay within the same geometric structure as the pretrained network.

The paper’s point is not that regularization is useless, but that it is not principled enough for preserving structure. If the finetuning objective is only to keep the energy from drifting, optimization may still find solutions that satisfy the task while subtly damaging the geometry that supports pretrained semantic generation. OFT instead uses a construction that preserves the relevant pairwise relationships by design. That shift from “encouraging” preservation to “guaranteeing” preservation is one of the paper’s core contributions.

# Invariance of pairwise angular relationships under a shared orthogonal transform

The key mathematical fact behind OFT is that pairwise angular relationships are preserved if every neuron in a layer is transformed by the same orthogonal matrix. The paper writes the finetuned weight matrix as $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$, where $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$. Because $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal, it preserves inner products and lengths. Consequently, if the normalized neurons in the pretrained layer are transformed in this way, the distances between any pair of neurons remain the same.

This is easy to see geometrically. An orthogonal matrix acts like a rotation or reflection of the entire coordinate system. It does not distort angles; it only changes how the same geometric arrangement is represented in space. So if the same $\mathbf{R}$ is applied to all neurons in a layer, then the pairwise geometry on the hypersphere is preserved exactly, and so is the hyperspherical energy. The paper uses this invariance to motivate OFT: instead of directly optimizing a fragile similarity penalty, it learns a layer-shared orthogonal transformation that keeps the internal structure intact.

# Why minimizing hyperspherical energy is appropriate in some classification settings but not generative finetuning

The paper also makes a careful comparison with prior work on orthogonal over-parameterized training for classification. In that setting, the goal is to train a classifier from scratch while keeping hyperspherical energy small. This makes sense there because randomly initialized networks already have small hyperspherical energy in expectation, and lower energy is associated with better generalization. In a classification model, one often wants neurons to be less redundant and more evenly spread on the hypersphere.

Generative finetuning is a different problem. Here the model is not being trained from scratch; it is being adapted from a pretrained diffusion model that already encodes useful semantic structure. For such a model, driving the energy toward a new minimum is not desirable, because the pretrained arrangement itself is meaningful. The paper argues that minimizing hyperspherical energy could actually destroy the semantics that make the pretrained generator work well. That is why OFT does not try to make the energy small. It tries to keep the finetuned energy aligned with the pretrained energy, preserving structure instead of replacing it.

Orthogonal Finetuning: Core Method and Theory

# From hyperspherical energy preservation to shared orthogonal transformations

Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, begins with a simple but powerful idea: if a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model already has useful semantic structure, then a good finetuning method should adapt the model without destroying that structure. The paper argues that a better way to measure whether the structure is preserved is not just to look at Euclidean distance between pretrained and finetuned weights, but to look at the relational geometry among neurons. In particular, the paper focuses on hyperspherical similarity, such as cosine similarity, because this kind of angular information has been observed to encode semantic information well.

To formalize this, the paper uses hyperspherical energy, which measures how uniformly neurons are arranged on the unit hypersphere. If a layer has neuron vectors $\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n$, and $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the normalized version of the $i$-th neuron, then the hyperspherical energy is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
The key point is that this quantity depends on the pairwise angular relationships among neurons. The paper’s hypothesis is that if finetuning keeps this energy close to the pretrained model’s energy, then the model is more likely to preserve its original semantic generation ability.

A naive strategy would be to add a regularizer that directly penalizes changes in hyperspherical energy. The paper argues that this is not enough, because simply encouraging the energy to stay the same does not guarantee that the difference will be minimized in a structurally meaningful way. Instead, OFT uses a more principled route: exploit the fact that hyperspherical energy is invariant under a shared orthogonal transformation. This leads naturally to the central update rule of the method.

# Fully connected layer notation and the OFT formulation

The paper explains OFT first using a fully connected layer. Let
\[
\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}
\]
be the weight matrix of a layer, where each column $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents one neuron, and let $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be the input. The layer output is
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x}.
\]
If $\bm{W}^0$ denotes the pretrained weights, then OFT does not replace them with an arbitrary new matrix. Instead, it learns an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and sets
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0,
\]
with the orthogonality constraint
\[
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}.
\]
This means that finetuning is carried out by transforming the pretrained neurons with a shared orthogonal matrix. The paper emphasizes that the attainable minimum of the hyperspherical-energy difference objective is zero, and that this minimum is reached when the finetuned weights differ from the pretrained ones only by rotation or reflection.

This formulation is the core OFT principle:
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\|
\]
is achieved exactly by transformations of the form $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with orthogonal $\bm{R}$. In other words, OFT does not ask the model to forget its old representation and learn a brand-new one. It asks the model to keep the same internal geometry while changing its orientation in weight space in a controlled way.

# The forward pass $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$

With OFT, the forward pass of the layer becomes
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
subject to the orthogonality constraint on $\bm{R}$. This is the operational form of the method: during training, the model learns $\bm{R}$, and during inference the transformed matrix can simply be folded into the pretrained weights as an equivalent weight matrix.

A crucial design choice is that OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ to the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. This makes the finetuned model start exactly from the pretrained model, rather than from a perturbed version of it. The paper treats this as an important stability property, because finetuning should begin with no change in behavior and then move gradually toward the downstream task.

This identity initialization also makes the method conceptually clean. The pretrained model is not replaced; it is only re-expressed through a learned orthogonal transform. That is why the method can be interpreted as a controlled adaptation rather than a wholesale rewrite of the layer’s parameters.

# Why an orthogonal matrix preserves pairwise neuron angles and hyperspherical energy

The reason orthogonality is so central is that orthogonal transformations preserve geometry. In the paper’s setting, the important geometry is not the absolute length of a neuron vector, but the pairwise relationships among neuron directions. Since hyperspherical energy is built from normalized neurons, preserving those directions means preserving the energy.

The paper’s key claim is that if the same orthogonal transformation is applied to all neurons in a layer, then the pairwise hyperspherical similarity is preserved. Intuitively, an orthogonal matrix does not distort angles: it can rotate or reflect a set of vectors, but it cannot change the relative angles between them. Therefore, when all neurons are transformed by the same $\bm{R}$, the pairwise distances on the unit hypersphere remain unchanged, and so does hyperspherical energy.

This is why OFT is stronger than a generic small update. A small Euclidean change in the weights does not necessarily mean that the semantic geometry has been preserved. OFT instead constrains the update to preserve the angular structure that the paper argues is tied to semantic generation ability. In this sense, OFT uses a geometric invariant, not just a magnitude constraint, as the basis for finetuning.

# Rotation and reflection as allowable transformations

The paper notes that the orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ may represent either a rotation or a reflection. In matrix terms, this corresponds to the determinant being $1$ or $-1$, respectively. Both are allowed because both preserve pairwise angles and therefore preserve hyperspherical energy.

This matters for understanding the objective’s zero minimum. If the finetuned layer differs from the pretrained one only by such a transformation, then the layer’s internal relational structure is unchanged. The neurons have been moved in the ambient space, but the geometry among them remains the same. The paper uses this fact to justify learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix rather than a more general transform.

The allowable transformations are therefore not arbitrary. OFT permits only those transformations that can change the coordinate representation of the layer without distorting its internal semantic organization. That is the core compromise between adaptability and preservation.

# Layer-shared transformation as a change of coordinate system for neurons

One of the most useful intuitions in the paper is that OFT can be understood as changing the canonical coordinate system for the neurons in a layer. Because the same orthogonal matrix is shared across all neurons in the layer, the transformation does not treat individual neurons independently. Instead, it changes the entire layer as a group.

This perspective explains why OFT can modify semantics while preserving structure. The layer is not being broken apart and rebuilt neuron by neuron. It is being viewed in a different orthonormal basis. As a result, the layer can respond differently to inputs even though the internal pairwise geometry among its neurons remains intact. The paper argues that this kind of coordinated reorientation is enough to support downstream control tasks such as subject-driven generation and controllable generation.

The layer-shared design is also what gives OFT its structural regularity. If each neuron could be transformed independently, the geometry of the layer could drift in a way that destroys the pretrained organization. By tying all neurons in a layer to the same orthogonal transform, OFT keeps the update coherent and semantically meaningful.

# Identity initialization so finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained model

The paper places strong emphasis on starting from the pretrained model exactly, and identity initialization is how OFT does that. When $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, the OFT layer reduces to the original pretrained layer:
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0.
\]
So before training begins, the model behaves exactly as it did at pretraining time.

This is more than a convenience. It is part of the method’s stability story. Finetuning should not force the model to begin from a modified state that already risks losing the pretrained generative behavior. Instead, OFT begins at the pretrained solution and then searches for a better downstream task adaptation through orthogonal movement away from that point. In the paper’s framing, this respects the original knowledge while still allowing controlled change.

Identity initialization also makes OFT conceptually aligned with the paper’s broader goal of preserving semantic generation. The method does not gamble on a large unconstrained shift at the start of training. It begins from a known good model and only moves in ways that the orthogonality constraint permits.

# Why orthogonality acts as a useful regularizer

The paper presents orthogonality as a strong form of regularization. Standard finetuning is very flexible, but that flexibility can be a liability: if the model changes too freely, it can lose the pretrained semantic structure and become unstable. OFT deliberately restricts that freedom. By forcing updates to be orthogonal, it preserves pairwise neuron angles and thereby preserves the relational geometry that the paper treats as essential.

At the same time, orthogonality is not so restrictive that the model becomes unable to adapt. This is why the paper describes OFT as a sweet spot between flexibility and regularity. The method can still change the orientation of the representation, which is enough to support task-specific control, but it does so on a smooth hypersphere manifold rather than through arbitrary parameter drift. The paper argues that this leads to better optimization behavior and faster convergence.

It is also important that OFT does not try to minimize hyperspherical energy the way some classification-oriented orthogonal training methods do. For finetuning a pretrained diffusion model, the goal is not to spread neurons out uniformly on the hypersphere. The goal is to keep the pretrained semantic structure intact. That is why preserving the pretrained energy, rather than reducing it, is the relevant objective here.

# What goes wrong when the orthogonality constraint is removed

The paper includes an explicit experiment showing that the orthogonality constraint is not just a theoretical nicety. When OFT is run without the orthogonality constraint, the method fails in practice. In the controllable generation setting, the unconstrained variant fails to generate realistic images and does not achieve the desired control effect.

This result is important because it shows that the success of OFT is not simply due to having additional trainable parameters or a different finetuning setup. The structured constraint itself is doing meaningful work. Without it, the transform becomes too free, and the pretrained semantic organization is no longer protected. The model loses the stability that orthogonality provides.

So the failure case clarifies the point of the method. OFT is not merely about learning a new transformation; it is about learning a transformation that is powerful enough to adapt the model and disciplined enough to preserve what matters. The orthogonality constraint is exactly what keeps those two goals in balance.

Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization for Large Models

# Why Naive Orthogonalization Methods Are Too Expensive in Practice

Orthogonal Finetuning is built around a simple idea: instead of changing a pretrained layer arbitrarily, learn a transformation that preserves pairwise neuron angles. The challenge is that, for a large diffusion model, an orthogonal transformation must still be trainable at scale. A direct approach would be to optimize a dense matrix and then enforce orthogonality with a standard procedure such as Gram-Schmidt. The paper notes that although such orthogonalization methods are differentiable, they are often too expensive to use in practice.

The underlying issue is that a large layer already contains many parameters, and an unconstrained orthogonal matrix of size $d \times d$ still has substantial complexity. OFT therefore needs a parameterization that does two things at once: it must guarantee orthogonality by construction, and it must do so with a manageable number of trainable parameters. This is the motivation for using a skew-symmetric parameterization together with the Cayley transform, and then further reducing the burden with a block-diagonal structure.

# Skew-Symmetric Matrices and Their Role in Parameterizing Orthogonal Transforms

The paper introduces a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as the actual learnable object behind the orthogonal transform. A matrix is skew-symmetric when it satisfies $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. Instead of learning the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ directly, OFT learns $\bm{Q}$ and maps it into an orthogonal matrix through a deterministic transformation.

This matters because the orthogonality requirement is built into the parameterization rather than enforced after the fact. In other words, the optimization happens over $\bm{Q}$, but the resulting transformation automatically satisfies the constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$. The paper also points out that identity initialization is straightforward: if $\bm{Q}$ is initialized to the zero matrix, then the resulting transform starts from the identity, so the finetuned model begins exactly at the pretrained weights.

# The Cayley Transform $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$

The key mechanism used by OFT is the Cayley transform,
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. This transform converts the learned skew-symmetric matrix into an orthogonal matrix. The paper uses this to define the finetuned weight matrix as
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0,
\]
so the pretrained layer is not replaced, but transformed by a learned orthogonal map.

Intuitively, the Cayley transform is attractive because it allows OFT to optimize a relatively unconstrained matrix $\bm{Q}$ while guaranteeing the structural property OFT needs. The transform is also compatible with the paper’s finetuning philosophy: the pretrained model is preserved at initialization, and training then learns how to reorient the neuron space without breaking the orthogonal relation among neurons in the same layer.

# What the Cayley Transform Can and Cannot Represent

The paper is explicit about one limitation of the Cayley parameterization: it can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$. In the paper’s terminology, this means that it covers a restricted subset of orthogonal transforms, not the full set of all orthogonal matrices. Even so, the authors report that this restriction does not hurt performance in practice.

From a practical perspective, this limitation is acceptable because the goal is not to enumerate every possible orthogonal matrix. Instead, the goal is to find a transformation that is expressive enough to adapt the model while still protecting the pretrained structure. The paper also notes, in its concluding discussion, that the matrix inverse in the Cayley transform slightly limits scalability, which is part of why additional efficiency tricks are needed for large diffusion networks.

# Block-Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices for Parameter Reduction

To make OFT practical for large layers, the paper does not always use one dense orthogonal matrix. Instead, it proposes a block-diagonal form,
\[
\bm{R} = \mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots, \bm{R}_r),
\]
where each block $\bm{R}_i \in \mathrm{O}(d/r)$ is itself orthogonal. This means that the full transform is still orthogonal, but it is built from smaller orthogonal pieces. The transform remains able to reorient the layer, but it does so in a more structured and parameter-efficient way.

The main effect of the block-diagonal design is to reduce the number of free parameters. Rather than learning a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix at once, OFT learns several smaller orthogonal blocks. The paper emphasizes that this does not sacrifice the key property OFT cares about: because the full matrix is still orthogonal, the pairwise angles among neurons are preserved, and therefore the hyperspherical energy is preserved as well.

# The Block Count $r$ as a Trade-Off Between Flexibility and Efficiency

The number of blocks, $r$, controls how fine-grained the orthogonal transform is. When $r$ is small, each block is larger, so the transform has more flexibility but also more parameters. When $r$ is larger, each block is smaller, so the model becomes more parameter-efficient and more strongly regularized.

The paper frames this as an important regularization mechanism. A block-diagonal structure is not just a convenience for reducing memory; it also restricts the space of allowed transforms. This restriction can be helpful because it keeps the finetuned model closer to the pretrained geometry while still allowing enough freedom to adapt to a downstream task. In the paper’s terms, the block-diagonal parameterization is a stronger regularization of the orthogonal matrix.

In the extreme case $r = 1$, there is only a single block, so the parameterization reduces to an ordinary orthogonal matrix of size $d \times d$. As $r$ grows, the transform becomes more structured and more compact. The paper also notes that for convolution layers, the same idea has useful interpretations: different blocks can act on different channels, or the same block can be shared across channels.

# Optional Block Sharing and Its Effect on Parameter Complexity

The paper further proposes an optional block-sharing strategy, where the blocks are tied so that
\[
\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j \quad \forall i \neq j.
\]
This means that instead of learning $r$ distinct orthogonal blocks, the same block is reused across the diagonal structure. The transform remains orthogonal, but the number of distinct parameters drops even further.

This sharing is a stronger form of compression than the basic block-diagonal design. Since all blocks are identical, the model uses fewer degrees of freedom while still preserving the orthogonal structure. The paper states that this reduces the parameter complexity to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$, compared with $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ without sharing. In the context of OFT, this is another way to control the balance between efficiency and flexibility without giving up the core hyperspherical-energy preservation property.

# Parameter Counts and Asymptotic Complexity of OFT

The paper provides explicit parameter-count formulas to show why OFT is lightweight. A full orthogonal matrix of size $d \times d$ has $d(d-1)/2$ parameters, which gives a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. By contrast, an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix has
\[
\frac{d(d/r - 1)}{2}
\]
parameters, which yields complexity $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$.

The paper also considers a scaling regime where the block count grows with the width, for example $r = \alpha d$ with $0 < \alpha \leq 1$. In that case, the block-diagonal OFT parameterization becomes $\mathcal{O}(d)$, which is much smaller than the cost of a dense orthogonal transform. If block sharing is used as well, the cost drops further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$.

The important point is that these reductions do not come at the expense of the orthogonality constraint. Regardless of whether the transform is dense, block-diagonal, or block-shared, the resulting matrix is still orthogonal, so the hyperspherical energy preservation property remains intact.

# A Concrete Comparison of OFT and LoRA Parameter Efficiency

The paper compares OFT to LoRA because LoRA is one of the most relevant parameter-efficient finetuning baselines. LoRA adds a low-rank update to a weight matrix, and its trainable parameter count is
\[
r'(d+n)
\]
for a weight matrix of size $d \times n$, where $r'$ is the LoRA rank. This is an additive update mechanism, whereas OFT uses a multiplicative orthogonal transform.

The paper argues that these two methods represent different kinds of efficiency. LoRA relies on a low-rank structure in the update itself, while OFT exploits the sparsity structure of a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix. If both methods use rank-like hyperparameters that scale with width, the paper notes that LoRA has complexity $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$, while OFT has complexity $\mathcal{O}(d)$. This makes OFT especially attractive when large layers need to be adapted without introducing a large number of new parameters.

The paper gives a concrete example with a $128 \times 128$ weight matrix. With $r' = 8$, LoRA uses $2{,}048$ trainable parameters. With $r = 8$ and no block sharing, OFT uses $960$ trainable parameters. This example makes the efficiency gain easy to see: OFT is not only structurally different from LoRA, but also more compact in this setting. Just as importantly, the orthogonal parameterization preserves the layer’s hyperspherical energy, which the paper argues is crucial for maintaining the pretrained generative ability of the diffusion model.

Together, these design choices make OFT practical for large diffusion networks: the Cayley transform guarantees orthogonality, the block-diagonal structure keeps the parameter count small, and the optional block-sharing variant pushes efficiency even further.

Constrained and Extended Variants: COFT and Re-scaled OFT

# Why an Explicit Deviation Constraint Helps

Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, is built on a simple geometric idea: instead of changing a pretrained text-to-image model in an arbitrary way, it learns an orthogonal transformation that rotates or reflects the neurons in a layer while preserving their pairwise angles. In the paper’s language, this keeps the hyperspherical energy unchanged, and that matters because the authors argue that the angular relationships among neurons are closely tied to semantic structure. So OFT is already more disciplined than ordinary finetuning: it changes the model enough to adapt to a downstream task, but it does so without destroying the angular organization that the pretrained model has learned.

Even so, the paper makes a crucial observation: preserving hyperspherical energy alone does not automatically guarantee that the finetuned model stays close to the pretrained one in parameter space. Orthogonality controls the form of the update, but it does not explicitly bound how far the learned transformation can move from the identity. That is why the authors introduce an additional constraint in COFT. The idea is to keep the benefits of OFT’s angular preservation while also making the finetuning process less free to drift, so that stability improves. In other words, OFT gives a good geometric update rule, and COFT adds an explicit notion of proximity to the original model.

# COFT and the Constraint $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\leq \epsilon$

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning, or COFT, uses the same basic forward pass as OFT:
\[
\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x},
\]
but it adds a second condition on the orthogonal matrix:
\[
\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^\top = \mathbf{I}, \qquad \|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\| \leq \epsilon.
\]
Here $\mathbf{W}^0$ is the pretrained weight matrix, and $\mathbf{R}$ is the learned orthogonal transformation. The first constraint says the layer update must remain orthogonal, so the pairwise angles among neurons are preserved. The second says the transformation itself must not stray too far from the identity. Taken together, these two constraints make COFT a more restrictive version of OFT: it still adapts the model, but it does so within a controlled neighborhood of the pretrained solution.

This is exactly what makes COFT different from the usual “small learning rate” intuition in standard finetuning. Ordinary finetuning often tries to stay close to the pretrained weights only implicitly, through optimization settings that discourage large updates. COFT turns that implicit preference into an explicit condition. The paper emphasizes that the second constraint is usually assumed by existing finetuning methods, but COFT states it directly. That explicitness is one reason COFT is presented as a more principled way to trade off adaptation and preservation.

# Interpreting $\epsilon$ as a Radius Around the Pretrained Model

The bound $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\leq \epsilon$ can be read as defining a radius around the pretrained model. Since the finetuned weight matrix is $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$, the identity transformation corresponds to leaving the pretrained model unchanged, and deviations from identity correspond to actual finetuning movement. A small $\epsilon$ means the model is only allowed to move a little away from the pretrained weights; a larger $\epsilon$ gives the optimizer more room to adapt. So $\epsilon$ is not just a technical constant. It is the knob that tells COFT how much change is allowed.

The paper’s discussion makes this interpretation very concrete. With a larger $\epsilon$, COFT behaves more like OFT, because the orthogonal transformation is freer to change the layer. With a smaller $\epsilon$, COFT behaves increasingly like the pretrained diffusion model, because the allowed deviation from identity shrinks. This is why the authors describe COFT as making the flexibility of finetuning explicit. The model is not simply told to “learn carefully”; instead, it is placed inside a neighborhood whose size is controlled by $\epsilon$.

# Using the Neumann-Series Approximation to Move the Constraint into $Q$-Space

To implement the orthogonal matrix efficiently, the paper uses the Cayley parameterization:
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\mathbf{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, meaning $\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^\top$. This parameterization is useful because it guarantees orthogonality, but it makes the direct $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|$ constraint awkward to enforce. The paper’s trick is to examine what happens when $\mathbf{Q}$ is small. Using a Neumann-series approximation, the authors write
\[
\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2),
\]
assuming the series converges in the operator norm. This approximation reveals that, near the identity, controlling $\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}$ is closely related to controlling $\mathbf{Q}$ itself.

That is the key move behind COFT’s constrained optimization. Instead of trying to enforce the deviation bound directly on $\mathbf{R}$, the paper moves the constraint into the space of the skew-symmetric parameter $\mathbf{Q}$. In this reparameterized view, the condition becomes a bound of the form
\[
\|\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{0}\| \leq \epsilon',
\]
for another tolerance parameter $\epsilon'$. The point is not that $\epsilon'$ must equal $\epsilon$, but that the constraint is now expressed in the variable that is actually being optimized. This makes the optimization problem much more manageable while still keeping the learned orthogonal transform near the identity.

# Projected Gradient Descent for Enforcing the Constraint on $Q$

Once the constraint is expressed in terms of $\mathbf{Q}$, the paper says it can be enforced with projected gradient descent. The idea is straightforward: after a gradient update proposes a new value of $\mathbf{Q}$, the algorithm projects it back into the set of admissible matrices if it has wandered outside the allowed norm ball. The projection step keeps the parameter inside the constraint region, so the orthogonal matrix derived from it remains close to the pretrained model’s identity transformation.

The important point is that this projected optimization is not a separate objective from the orthogonality requirement; it is the mechanism that makes the constraint practical. The Cayley parameterization handles orthogonality, and the projection handles closeness to the origin in $\mathbf{Q}$-space. Together, they turn COFT into a controlled version of OFT rather than a completely free orthogonal update. In the paper’s framing, this is what makes COFT more stable: it does not merely rely on the optimizer to behave well, but explicitly restricts the space in which it can move.

# How $\epsilon$ Controls the Stability-Flexibility Trade-Off

The paper repeatedly returns to the same design tension: finetuning needs enough flexibility to adapt, but not so much that it destroys the pretrained model’s useful structure. COFT addresses this tension through $\epsilon$. If $\epsilon$ is large, the orthogonal transformation can move farther from the identity, so the model becomes more adaptable but also less constrained. If $\epsilon$ is small, the model stays closer to the pretrained weights, which makes training more conservative and stable but also less expressive.

This is why the authors describe $\epsilon$ as controlling the balance between flexibility and regularity. A larger radius gives COFT more freedom, so it can resemble the behavior of OFT more closely. A smaller radius makes the learned model behave more like the original text-to-image diffusion model. The paper’s empirical discussion also suggests that this explicit constraint improves stability beyond OFT itself. So $\epsilon$ is not just a hyperparameter to tune; it is the mechanism that lets COFT tune how much the pretrained semantics should be preserved versus how much the downstream task should be allowed to reshape the model.

# Initialization of COFT Through a Zero Skew-Symmetric Matrix

COFT starts from the pretrained model by initializing the skew-symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ to the all-zero matrix. This is an especially clean initialization because the zero matrix is skew-symmetric, and when $\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}$ the Cayley transform gives
\[
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}.
\]
So at the beginning of training, the finetuned model is exactly the pretrained model. The transformation does not yet change any neuron directions, and the optimization begins from the same point as the original network.

This identity initialization is important for the spirit of COFT. The method is meant to refine a pretrained diffusion model, not to replace it with something unrelated. Starting from $\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}$ makes the optimization path continuous with the pretrained solution and ensures that any change is introduced gradually. In the paper’s view, that is another ingredient in COFT’s improved stability: the model begins at the original weights and is only allowed to depart from them within the constrained orthogonal family.

# Re-scaled OFT with a Learnable Diagonal Scaling Matrix $\mathbf{D}$

The paper also proposes a simple extension called re-scaled OFT. Here, the model does not only learn an orthogonal transformation $\mathbf{R}$; it also learns a diagonal scaling matrix
\[
\mathbf{D} = \text{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n),
\]
with all $s_i>0$. The forward pass becomes
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D})^\top \mathbf{x}.
\]
Relative to OFT, the new ingredient is $\mathbf{D}$, which gives each neuron its own learnable magnitude scale. The paper motivates this extension by noting that scaling does not alter hyperspherical energy, since that energy depends only on normalized neuron directions. Because $\mathbf{D}$ is diagonal and positive, it adds a small amount of extra flexibility without changing the angular geometry that OFT is designed to preserve.

This is why the authors describe re-scaled OFT as a lightweight extension rather than a new framework. It preserves the orthogonal backbone of OFT while allowing the model to adjust magnitudes in addition to directions. The extra parameters are negligible, but the added freedom can make the finetuned model more expressive. In the paper, this variant is not the main focus of the experiments, because the authors want to isolate the effect of orthogonal transformation itself. Even so, they report that re-scaled OFT is generally better.

# Why Positive Rescaling Preserves Hyperspherical Energy and When Extra Magnitude Flexibility May Improve Performance

The reason positive rescaling preserves hyperspherical energy is built into the definition of that energy. For a weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$, the paper defines hyperspherical energy using normalized neurons $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i=\mathbf{w}_i/\|\mathbf{w}_i\|$. If a neuron is multiplied by a positive scalar $s_i$, then
\[
\widehat{s_i\mathbf{w}_i} = \frac{s_i\mathbf{w}_i}{\|s_i\mathbf{w}_i\|} = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i.
\]
So the normalization removes the magnitude change completely. That is why the paper can add $\mathbf{D}$ without breaking the hyperspherical preservation property: the pairwise angular structure remains the same, and the hyperspherical energy is unchanged.

This additional degree of freedom can help because not every downstream task is solved purely by rotating neuron directions. Sometimes a model needs a little more expressive power than orthogonality alone provides. Re-scaled OFT supplies that power by letting each neuron’s magnitude adjust while leaving the directional geometry intact. The paper says this improves flexibility with very little parameter cost, and it finds that the re-scaled version is generally better. At the same time, the authors still emphasize the original OFT in their main experiments so that the benefits of orthogonal transformation itself remain clear.

OFT in Context: Relation to LoRA, Architectures, and Optimization

# Multiplicative updates in OFT versus additive updates in LoRA

The main conceptual difference between Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and LoRA is the way each method changes a pretrained layer. LoRA uses an additive update: it modifies the pretrained weight matrix by adding a learned low-rank matrix. OFT, in contrast, uses a multiplicative update: it applies a learned orthogonal transformation to the pretrained weight matrix. This difference matters because the paper’s central claim is not merely that the update should be small, but that it should preserve the semantic structure already present in the pretrained model.

In OFT, the learned transform is constrained to be orthogonal, so the transformation preserves pairwise angles between neurons in the same layer. The paper argues that these angles are a key part of what stores semantic information in the model. This is why OFT is designed as a structural update rather than a simple weight perturbation. LoRA’s low-rank update is meant to be parameter-efficient and to avoid large shifts in the pretrained weights, but it does not explicitly preserve the relational geometry among neurons. OFT is built specifically around that geometry.

This distinction also helps explain why OFT is paired with the notion of hyperspherical energy. The paper uses hyperspherical energy to describe the pairwise similarity structure of neurons on the unit hypersphere. Because orthogonal transformations preserve angles, OFT preserves this energy exactly. So while both LoRA and OFT aim to adapt large pretrained models efficiently, OFT does so by respecting a stronger structural constraint on the layer representation.

# Rewriting OFT as $W^0 + (R - I)W^0$

A useful way to connect OFT to more familiar update rules is to rewrite its forward pass algebraically. If a pretrained layer has weights $\bm{W}^0$ and OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, then the finetuned weights are $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Expanding this expression gives
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0.
\]
This makes OFT look superficially similar to an additive update. The difference is that the update is not learned directly as an unconstrained matrix; instead, it is induced by an orthogonal transform.

The paper uses this rewriting to show that OFT can be viewed as a controlled way of modifying the pretrained matrix. The term $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ is analogous to an update term, but the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ keeps the transformation geometrically well-behaved. In other words, OFT does not change the model by freely adding arbitrary directions to the weights. It changes the model by rotating or reflecting the layer representation while preserving the pairwise angular structure of the neurons.

This perspective also explains the identity initialization used in OFT. By setting $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ at the start, the model begins exactly at the pretrained solution. Training then learns a deviation from identity, but that deviation is constrained to remain orthogonal. This is the OFT analogue of starting from the pretrained model and adapting it gradually, except the adaptation is now carried out through a structured transform rather than a free-form additive update.

# Low-rank and sparsity perspectives on parameter-efficient finetuning

The paper emphasizes that OFT and LoRA represent two different routes to parameter efficiency. LoRA relies on a low-rank assumption for the update, which reduces the number of trainable parameters by restricting the rank of the added matrix. OFT instead relies on a sparsity-like structure in the parameterization of the orthogonal matrix, especially through a block-diagonal form. Both methods are efficient, but they are efficient in different ways and for different reasons.

For a dense orthogonal matrix of size $d \times d$, the paper notes that the number of parameters is $d(d-1)/2$, which is $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. To make OFT more practical, the paper introduces a block-diagonal parameterization with $r$ blocks. This reduces the parameter complexity to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. If the blocks are shared across positions, the complexity drops further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. The important point is that these reductions do not weaken the orthogonality constraint itself, so the preservation of hyperspherical energy is retained.

The paper compares this directly with LoRA. If LoRA uses a low-rank parameter $r'$, then its trainable parameter count is $r'(d+n)$ for a weight matrix with shape $d \times n$. Under the scaling choice described in the paper, OFT can become very parameter-efficient as well. But the parameter savings come from a different structural assumption: LoRA compresses the update by low rank, whereas OFT compresses the transformation by block structure. The paper frames this as a contrast between low-rank structure and sparsity structure.

# Applying OFT to transformer attention weights

Although the paper presents OFT as a model-agnostic method, it is especially natural to compare it with LoRA in transformers because LoRA is commonly applied to attention weights. To make a fair comparison, the paper follows this practice and applies OFT to the same attention layers. In a transformer, the relevant layers are still just weight matrices, so OFT can be inserted as an orthogonal transform of those matrices without changing the basic architecture.

This is important because it shows that OFT is not tied to a special diffusion-model-specific mechanism. Instead, it acts on the linear components already present in the model. In the transformer setting, the learned orthogonal matrix modifies how the attention-related projections behave while keeping the pretrained structure geometrically coherent. The paper’s main claim is that this kind of constrained transformation can improve controllability without destroying the semantic capability of the pretrained generator.

For the experiments in the paper, applying OFT only to attention weights also makes the comparison with LoRA cleaner. Since both methods operate on the same layers, improvements in generation quality, stability, and convergence are easier to interpret as coming from the update rule itself rather than from different layer choices. This is one reason the paper emphasizes that OFT is a finetuning framework rather than a new model architecture.

# Interpreting OFT in convolutional layers and channel-wise blocks

The paper also explains how OFT can be interpreted in convolutional layers. This is where the block-diagonal parameterization becomes especially intuitive. A convolution layer can be seen as acting on channel-wise representations, and the block structure of $\bm{R}$ can be aligned with those channels. In the extreme case where the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique channel. The paper notes that this resembles learning depth-wise convolution kernels.

The block structure therefore gives OFT a natural channel-wise interpretation. Rather than mixing all neurons in a layer at once, the transformation can be organized so that different groups of neurons are handled separately. When the blocks are shared, the same orthogonal transform is applied across channels. This offers a way to adapt convolutional layers while preserving the orthogonal geometry that OFT depends on.

The paper does not present convolutional finetuning as the main experimental focus, but it does emphasize that OFT is well suited for it. The reason is that the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix is flexible enough to interact with channel-based structure, yet constrained enough to preserve the pairwise angular relations that the method is built around. This makes OFT more than a transformer-specific trick; it is a general way of organizing structured finetuning in layers that are naturally represented by matrices.

# Why OFT is model-agnostic in principle

The paper states that OFT is model-agnostic in principle because it operates on weight matrices rather than on a specific generation pipeline. As long as a model contains linear transformations, an orthogonal update can be applied to those transformations. This is why the method can be used on different text-to-image diffusion architectures, and why the authors describe it as applicable to any neural network in principle.

In practice, the paper applies OFT to Stable Diffusion v1.5 and evaluates it on two types of downstream tasks: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. But the method itself does not depend on those tasks. Its logic is tied to how weights are updated, not to the exact task label or the exact modality of the control signal. The same orthogonal transformation principle can therefore be transferred across settings where preserving pretrained structure matters.

The model-agnostic property is also reflected in the deployment story. Because the learned transform can be merged into the pretrained weights, the architecture used during inference is effectively the same as the original one. So OFT does not require a separate control branch or a special inference-time module. That makes the method broadly compatible with existing diffusion systems while remaining structurally simple.

# Why OFT may converge faster: semantic alignment and smoother optimization on constrained manifolds

The paper offers two main reasons that OFT may converge faster than standard finetuning methods. The first is semantic alignment. The authors argue that the directions of neurons carry much of the semantic information in the model. They support this intuition with a toy autoencoder experiment: angular information can almost perfectly recover the input images, while magnitude information alone does not. From this perspective, changing neuron directions is a direct way to change semantics, which is exactly what downstream controllability tasks require.

The second reason is optimization geometry. By constraining updates to orthogonal transformations, OFT effectively performs finetuning on a smooth hypersphere manifold. The paper argues that this gives a better optimization landscape than unconstrained finetuning. The orthogonality condition acts as a strong regularizer, preventing the model from wandering into unstable regions where pretrained knowledge is quickly destroyed. This is also consistent with the paper’s empirical observations: without the orthogonality constraint, the method fails to generate realistic images or achieve control, whereas with the constraint it trains stably.

These two explanations work together. If semantics live largely in angular relationships, then a method that directly adjusts angles should be efficient. And if the updates are restricted to orthogonal transforms, then the optimization path is regular enough to be stable. The paper’s experiments on both subject-driven generation and controllable generation show that this combination can lead to faster and smoother convergence than methods like DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, or T2I-Adapter.

# The trade-off between flexibility and regularity in finetuning

A central theme of the paper is the trade-off between flexibility and regularity. Standard finetuning is highly flexible because it allows the weights to move freely, but the paper argues that this freedom often comes at the cost of stability and preservation of pretrained performance. In the extreme, the model can collapse or lose the semantic structure that made the pretrained diffusion model strong in the first place.

OFT is designed to sit in the middle of this trade-off. It is more flexible than simply freezing the pretrained model, because it still allows the layer to change. But it is more regular than unconstrained finetuning, because the update must be orthogonal and therefore preserve the relational geometry of the neurons. The paper presents this as the key reason OFT can adapt to downstream tasks without severely damaging the pretrained generative ability.

The block-diagonal parameterization adds another layer of regularity. The paper explicitly says that this structure can be viewed as a stronger regularization of the orthogonal matrix. In that sense, the method’s design is not just about reducing parameter count; it is also about controlling how much freedom the finetuning process has. The constrained variant, COFT, makes this even more explicit by limiting deviation from the pretrained model. Together, these ideas clarify the paper’s broader optimization message: successful finetuning requires enough freedom to adapt, but not so much freedom that the pretrained semantics are lost.

# Merging the learned transform into pretrained weights for zero extra inference cost

One of the practical advantages of OFT is that it introduces no additional inference overhead. This is possible because the learned orthogonal matrix can be merged into the pretrained weights after finetuning. If the adapted weight matrix is $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, then at inference time one can simply use $\bm{W}$ as an ordinary weight matrix. There is no need to keep a separate finetuning module active during generation.

This is a significant deployment advantage over methods that require extra branches or adapters at test time. Since the orthogonal transform is folded into the pretrained weights, the inference graph remains as simple as the original model. The paper explicitly notes that the inference speed is therefore the same as that of the pretrained model.

In practical terms, this means OFT gets many of the benefits of finetuning without creating a runtime penalty. That matters especially for text-to-image diffusion, where inference is already computationally expensive. The method is thus attractive not only because it improves controllability and stability, but also because it preserves deployability. The pretrained model can be adapted offline, and the resulting model can be used exactly like the original one at test time.

Subject-Driven Generation with OFT

# Task definition: preserving subject identity while changing context

Subject-driven generation asks a very specific question: if a model is shown only a few reference images of a subject, can it later generate that same subject in new scenes, poses, or styles when given a text prompt? The goal is not simply to reproduce the reference images. Instead, the model must keep the subject recognizable while also obeying the new prompt, which means it has to separate “what the subject is” from “what context the subject appears in.”

This task is a natural test for finetuning methods because it exposes the central tension in adaptation: if the finetuning is too weak, the model will not learn the subject; if it is too aggressive, it may overwrite the pretrained generative ability and produce unstable or distorted images. In the paper, this tension is exactly what OFT and COFT are designed to address. The method is meant to change the model just enough to learn the new subject-specific behavior, while preserving the semantic structure that makes the pretrained diffusion model useful in the first place.

# Using the DreamBooth training framework with OFT

For subject-driven generation, the paper adopts the DreamBooth framework as the training setting. DreamBooth uses a customized token and a few subject images, and finetunes a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model using a reconstruction loss together with a class-specific prior preservation loss. OFT does not replace this overall training objective. Instead, it changes how the model parameters are adapted inside that framework.

This distinction is important. If two methods use the same loss function, then differences in performance can be attributed much more cleanly to the finetuning strategy itself. In this paper, OFT keeps the DreamBooth supervision but updates the model through orthogonal transformations rather than through standard small-step weight updates. The constrained variant, COFT, adds an explicit bound on how far the finetuned model may move from the pretrained one. So the DreamBooth setup remains the same, but the geometry of finetuning is redesigned.

# Baselines: DreamBooth and LoRA

The paper compares OFT and COFT mainly against DreamBooth and LoRA. DreamBooth is the most direct baseline because it is the standard subject-driven finetuning method in this setting. It adapts the diffusion model using the same basic training supervision, but it relies on conventional finetuning with a small learning rate. LoRA is the other key baseline. It is a parameter-efficient adaptation method that updates the model through a low-rank weight modification.

These two baselines represent two common finetuning philosophies. DreamBooth is flexible but can be unstable if the finetuning runs too long or becomes too aggressive. LoRA is more structured and parameter-efficient, but the paper shows that its ability to preserve identity and follow prompts is weaker in this task. OFT is positioned between these extremes: it is parameter-efficient like LoRA, but it preserves the pretrained structure through an orthogonal update, which the paper argues leads to better stability and better retention of semantic knowledge.

# Training setup, iteration schedules, and fairness of comparison

The experiments use Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained text-to-image model. For subject-driven generation, the paper generally follows the DreamBooth setup. For fairness, all methods use the same loss function as in DreamBooth, and OFT or COFT is applied to the same layer where LoRA is used. This is an important design choice because it keeps the comparison focused on the adaptation method rather than on differences in architecture placement.

The paper also follows the original papers for DreamBooth and LoRA and uses their best hyperparameter setups. That means the comparison is not meant to disadvantage the baselines by giving them weak training settings. In the qualitative analysis, the paper also avoids cherry-picking by using the same finetuned model for each method when generating examples. These choices matter because subject-driven generation can be sensitive to training details, so a fair benchmark must control as many factors as possible.

# Convergence and stability across short and long finetuning runs

One of the clearest findings in the subject-driven generation experiments is that OFT and COFT converge stably and quickly. After 400 iterations, both OFT variants and DreamBooth achieve good control, but LoRA fails to preserve the subject identity. The short-run result already suggests that OFT is learning the target subject more reliably than the low-rank baseline.

The longer-run behavior makes the contrast even sharper. After 2000 iterations, DreamBooth starts to generate collapsed images, and LoRA still fails to track the intended subject content correctly. In contrast, both OFT and COFT remain stable and continue to produce consistent subject control. The paper emphasizes that this insensitivity to the exact number of finetuning iterations is practically valuable, because it reduces the burden of tuning the training schedule separately for different subjects. In particular, COFT with a proper $\epsilon$ makes both the learning rate and the iteration number easier to set.

# Failure modes of baselines: identity loss, prompt mismatch, and collapsed images

The subject-driven generation results expose three different failure modes. The first is identity loss, where the model no longer produces the same subject after finetuning. The paper shows that LoRA is especially prone to this problem. In one example, LoRA completely loses the subject identity in the bowl case, and in another it turns a yellow shirt into yellow fur. These are not minor visual imperfections; they are signs that the model has failed to keep the subject-specific information intact.

The second failure mode is prompt mismatch. DreamBooth can preserve the subject more faithfully than LoRA, but it often fails to follow the text prompt accurately. In the bowl example, the paper notes that DreamBooth keeps the subject identity but does not generate the image according to the prompt. The third failure mode is collapse under long training, where the images deteriorate as finetuning continues. DreamBooth begins to produce collapsed images after enough iterations. OFT and COFT avoid these problems much better, which is why the paper presents them as a more stable route to subject-driven adaptation.

# Quantitative metrics: DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, and LPIPS

To evaluate subject-driven generation quantitatively, the paper uses four metrics that each capture a different aspect of quality. DINO and CLIP-I measure subject fidelity. CLIP-I is defined as the average pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between generated and real images, while DINO is similar except that it uses ViT S/16 DINO embeddings. These metrics ask whether the generated image still looks like the reference subject.

The other two metrics assess different properties. CLIP-T measures text prompt fidelity by computing the average cosine similarity between the text prompt and the generated image in CLIP space. LPIPS is used to evaluate sample diversity by measuring the average LPIPS cosine similarity between generated images of the same subject with the same prompt. Together, these metrics let the paper check whether a method preserves identity, follows the prompt, and still allows varied outputs rather than producing one fixed template.

# Why repeated runs over multiple random seeds matter

The paper repeats finetuning of the same pretrained model across 30 different random seeds. The purpose is to minimize randomness in the comparison. This is a sensible choice because finetuning methods can be affected by stochastic effects in initialization, optimization, and sampling, and a single run might not tell the whole story.

By averaging over many seeds, the paper makes its quantitative comparison more trustworthy. If one method is truly more stable, that should show up not just in one fortunate run but across repeated trials. This is especially relevant for subject-driven generation, where the training objective is relatively narrow and a small change in optimization behavior can make a visible difference in whether the subject identity is preserved or lost.

# Qualitative comparison: subject fidelity, prompt following, and diversity

The qualitative results reinforce the quantitative story. The paper shows randomly selected examples from each method, and for each method it chooses the model with the best validation CLIP metrics. Importantly, the prompts are not separately optimized for the final images. This means the examples reflect the actual general behavior of each finetuned model rather than hand-tuned prompt engineering.

In these comparisons, OFT and COFT consistently preserve the subject while also following the prompt more accurately. DreamBooth is often better at maintaining the subject than LoRA, but it can miss the intended prompt. LoRA, by contrast, often fails to preserve identity at all. The paper concludes that OFT and COFT achieve both subject preservation and controllability at the same time, which is exactly the combination that subject-driven generation requires.

# Practical lessons on hyperparameter robustness and sample efficiency

A key practical lesson from the subject-driven generation experiments is that OFT is much less sensitive to the number of training iterations than the baselines. The paper notes that one can directly choose a relatively large iteration number without carefully tuning it, and OFT still performs well. COFT goes one step further by making the deviation from the pretrained model explicit through the $\epsilon$ constraint, which makes the training dynamics even easier to control. This robustness is valuable in practice because it reduces the amount of trial-and-error required to adapt the model to a new subject.

The paper also reports better sample efficiency: OFT converges well with a much smaller number of training images and epochs. That matters because subject-driven generation is often intended for low-data settings, where the user may only have a handful of reference photos. In that regime, a finetuning method is useful only if it can learn quickly without destroying the pretrained model. The paper’s results suggest that OFT, and especially COFT, strike that balance better than the standard alternatives.

Controllable Generation, Evaluation, and Future Directions

# Task definition and motivation

This chapter focuses on the paper’s second major application: controllable generation for text-to-image diffusion models. The setting extends ordinary text prompting by adding an extra control signal that specifies some structure the generated image should follow. The goal is therefore not just to produce a realistic image from text, but to produce an image that is both faithful to the prompt and consistent with the provided control input.

The paper emphasizes that text guidance alone is often too ambiguous for fine-grained control. A prompt may describe a scene or object, but it does not always specify layout, shape, pose, or identity details. A control signal fills in that gap. In the paper, the control signal can take forms such as canny edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks. The model must use that signal as a stronger guide while still preserving the rich generative ability learned during pretraining.

This is exactly why controllable generation is a difficult finetuning problem. If finetuning is too weak, the model ignores the control signal. If finetuning is too aggressive, the model may follow the signal but lose realism or semantic richness. The paper’s central claim is that Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, and its constrained variant, COFT, provide a better balance between these two requirements than standard finetuning methods.

# Baselines: ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA

To evaluate controllable generation, the paper compares OFT and COFT against three representative baselines. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are methods designed specifically to add control to pretrained diffusion models. LoRA is a more general finetuning method that is also relevant because it is parameter-efficient and widely used for adapting large models.

The paper uses these baselines in a fairly direct way. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter serve as the main controllable-generation comparisons because they are designed to inject control signals into diffusion models. LoRA is included because it offers a simple low-rank alternative for finetuning, and it helps establish whether OFT’s gains come from the particular orthogonal parameterization rather than from parameter-efficient adaptation in general.

A key experimental point is fairness. The paper applies OFT or COFT to the same layers where LoRA is used, so that the comparison is not confounded by different adaptation locations. The experiments also use Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained backbone, and the controllable-generation tasks are finetuned for 20 epochs.

# Datasets and tasks: Canny-to-image, segmentation-to-image, and landmark-to-face

The paper studies three challenging controllable-generation tasks. The first is canny edge to image, or C2I, on the COCO dataset. Here the control signal is an edge map, and the model must generate a plausible image that respects those edges. This task tests whether the model can convert sparse structural information into a realistic image.

The second task is segmentation map to image, or S2I, on the ADE20K dataset. In this setting, the control signal is a semantic segmentation map, which specifies the class of each region in the image. This is a more detailed and semantically rich form of control, because the output must match the spatial organization implied by the map.

The third task is landmark to face, or L2F, on the CelebA-HQ dataset. Here the control signal consists of facial landmarks, and the generated face should follow the prescribed pose and structure. This task is especially useful for evaluating geometric control, because even small deviations in landmark alignment are easy to detect.

These three tasks together cover different forms of conditioning. Canny edges provide coarse shape information, segmentation maps provide dense semantic layout, and landmarks provide precise spatial structure for faces. The paper uses this mix to show that the method is not specialized to one type of control signal.

# Why controllable generation needs both fidelity and strict control

Controllable generation is not simply about making images look good. The generated output must satisfy two requirements at once. First, it must be visually faithful: the image should be realistic, detailed, and coherent. Second, it must obey the control signal with high accuracy. A beautiful image that ignores the input signal is a failure, just as a technically obedient but low-quality image is a failure.

This dual requirement is what makes evaluation difficult. Ordinary perceptual quality alone does not tell us whether the control signal was respected. At the same time, a model could score well on a control metric but still generate unnatural images. The paper’s experimental design reflects this tension by evaluating both the visual quality and the consistency between the input control and the output image.

The paper argues that OFT and COFT improve this balance. By preserving the pretrained model’s hyperspherical structure while adapting it through orthogonal transformations, they aim to keep the semantic generation ability intact while still enabling the model to respond to new control signals.

# The control consistency metric

A central contribution of the paper is a new evaluation measure for controllable generation called the control consistency metric. The idea is simple and intuitive: if a generated image truly follows the intended control signal, then one should be able to recover a compatible control signal from that image and compare it to the original input.

In other words, the metric asks whether the generated image contains the right structure in a form that an external estimator can recognize. The paper presents this as a way to more directly evaluate controllability than relying only on image quality or prompt alignment. It is particularly useful because the “correctness” of controllable generation is inherently tied to structure, not just appearance.

The paper also notes an important caveat. The control consistency evaluation depends on the accuracy of the off-the-shelf segmentation or detection model used to recover the control signal from the generated image. That means the metric is powerful, but it is also only as reliable as the auxiliary predictor that measures consistency. Even so, it gives a much more direct assessment of control behavior than visual inspection alone.

# Task-specific metrics

Because the three controllable-generation tasks involve different kinds of control, the paper uses different metrics for each one. For C2I, it computes intersection-over-union, or IoU, and F1 score. These metrics compare the recovered edge structure to the original edge signal and summarize how well the generated image preserves the intended shape.

For S2I, the paper uses mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy. These are standard segmentation-based measures that compare the semantic regions inferred from the generated image with the original segmentation map. They are especially appropriate here because the input itself is a dense semantic layout.

For L2F, the paper measures the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the control landmarks and the predicted landmarks. This is a direct geometric measure: smaller distance means the generated face better matches the target pose and structure. Because this task is about precise alignment, the landmark error is a natural choice.

The paper’s choice of metrics is important conceptually. It shows that controllable generation should be evaluated in a task-aware way. Different control signals require different measures, but the common theme is the same: the output should make the intended control recoverable.

# Convergence analysis and evidence of faster learning

One of the strongest empirical claims in the paper is that OFT and COFT converge faster than the baselines. The clearest convergence study is reported on the landmark-to-face task. There, OFT and COFT exhibit stable learning dynamics and reach strong control performance significantly earlier than the competing methods.

The paper gives a particularly striking comparison with LoRA. It reports that LoRA needs 20 epochs to converge to the level that OFT reaches by epoch 8. This suggests not only that OFT is more sample-efficient, but also that it is easier to optimize. The training process is more predictable, which matters in practice because it reduces the need for careful tuning of the number of epochs.

The comparison with ControlNet is also revealing. The paper observes that ControlNet’s controllability suddenly appears after the 8th epoch, matching the sudden convergence behavior previously reported for that method. In contrast, OFT and COFT improve more smoothly. That smoother trajectory is practically valuable because it makes training behavior easier to interpret and hyperparameters easier to choose.

The paper also points out that OFT can be data-efficient. In one example from the teaser, OFT converges well with only $5\%$ of the ADE20K dataset. This is consistent with the broader theme that the method can adapt effectively even when data is limited.

# Quantitative results and data efficiency

Across the controllable-generation tasks, the paper reports that OFT and COFT outperform the baselines in terms of controllability. The quantitative results show stronger agreement between the generated image and the input control signal, while still maintaining high image quality.

A particularly important finding is that OFT and COFT use a similar number of trainable parameters to LoRA, but far fewer than ControlNet. Despite this small parameter budget, they produce better or more stable control performance. This is a central part of the paper’s argument: parameter efficiency does not have to come at the cost of control quality.

The paper also observes that the performance of OFT has not saturated. In their experiments, the method continues to improve as the number of trainable parameters increases. This suggests that the orthogonal parameterization remains expressive, even though it is constrained. At the same time, the paper emphasizes that OFT has a lower effective cost than much larger control modules.

The comparison with LoRA is nuanced. LoRA can do relatively well in some settings, but the paper reports that its performance ceiling is lower overall. In particular, LoRA performs better than ControlNet on segmentation-to-image, but worse on canny-to-image and landmark-to-face. In the paper’s view, this reinforces the idea that OFT’s orthogonal updates preserve more useful pretrained structure while still enabling adaptation.

# Qualitative results across edges, segmentation maps, and facial landmarks

The qualitative results mirror the quantitative trends. In the segmentation-to-image task, LoRA often fails to follow the segmentation map correctly, whereas ControlNet, OFT, and COFT can. The paper reports that OFT and COFT often produce images with more vivid details and more reasonable geometric structure than ControlNet, while using far fewer parameters.

In the canny-to-image task, OFT and COFT can generate semantically plausible images from relatively rough edge information. This matters because edge maps are sparse and ambiguous; the model must fill in a great deal of content while respecting the constraints encoded in the contours. The paper finds that T2I-Adapter and LoRA perform worse here.

In the landmark-to-face task, OFT and COFT achieve the most accurate pose control, even for challenging face configurations. The generated faces track the input landmarks closely, which indicates that the model is not merely producing visually believable faces but is also obeying the geometric conditioning signal.

The overall qualitative message is consistent across tasks. OFT and COFT preserve realism while increasing control fidelity. This combination is exactly what controllable generation needs, and it is one of the strongest pieces of evidence in favor of the orthogonal finetuning approach.

# Main empirical takeaways

The paper’s main empirical takeaway is that OFT and COFT improve controllable generation along several dimensions at once. They produce higher-quality images, stronger adherence to control, faster convergence, and better finetuning stability than the baselines. They also do so with fewer trainable parameters than ControlNet and with parameter budgets comparable to LoRA.

Another important conclusion is that COFT improves stability even further by making the deviation from the pretrained model explicit. The paper presents the constraint parameter $\epsilon$ as a way to control the balance between flexibility and conservativeness: larger $\epsilon$ makes COFT behave more like OFT, while smaller $\epsilon$ keeps it closer to the pretrained model. This gives the user a direct knob for stability.

The paper also stresses that OFT adds no additional inference overhead. At test time, the learned orthogonal matrix can simply be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix, yielding an equivalent adapted model. So the gains in controllability do not come at the cost of slower deployment.

# Limitations and future directions

Although the results are strong, the paper is explicit about several limitations. The first is computational. OFT uses Cayley parameterization to guarantee orthogonality, but this involves a matrix inverse. That inverse makes the method less scalable than one might like, especially for large layers. The paper partially addresses this with block-diagonal parameterization, but the underlying cost remains an issue.

The second limitation is representational bias. The block-diagonal structure reduces the number of parameters and makes optimization easier, but it also introduces additional structural assumptions. In the paper’s own words, this stronger regularization improves efficiency, yet it also limits flexibility. That trade-off is central to the current form of OFT.

The third limitation concerns scalability and expressiveness more broadly. The paper notes that parameter efficiency currently depends heavily on the block-diagonal design, and it asks how one might build more expressive orthogonal structures that remain efficient without becoming too biased.

These limitations lead to several open problems. One is how to make differentiable orthogonalization faster, so that the orthogonality constraint can be enforced without a costly matrix inverse. Another is compositionality: if multiple downstream tasks each produce an orthogonal matrix, those matrices can be multiplied and the result is still orthogonal, but it is not yet clear whether such combinations preserve the knowledge from all tasks in a useful way. A final open question is how to design more effective parameter-efficient orthogonal structures that keep the benefits of OFT while reducing the bias introduced by block diagonalization.

Taken together, these open problems show that controllable generation is not only an application area for OFT, but also a test bed for future research on structured finetuning. The paper establishes that orthogonal finetuning is a strong and practical direction; the remaining challenge is to make it even more scalable, expressive, and compositional.
