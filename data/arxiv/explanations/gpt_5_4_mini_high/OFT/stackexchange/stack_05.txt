\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why is hyperspherical energy defined as $\sum_{i \ne j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ instead of a direct cosine-similarity quantity?
Question:
The paper discusses hyperspherical similarity using cosine similarity, but then defines the hyperspherical energy of a layer as $\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \ne j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$. I am confused about why this particular inverse-distance form is used. Since for unit vectors the Euclidean distance and cosine angle are related, is this just a monotone reparameterization, or does it have a special property that matters for the theory? Also, what happens numerically if two neurons become very close and a term becomes large?
Answer:
The short answer is that the paper is not using $\sum_{i \neq j} \langle \hat{\bm w}_i,\hat{\bm w}_j\rangle$ as a generic “similarity score”; it is using the standard hyperspherical energy from the cited hyperspherical-energy literature, where the natural quantity is a repulsive potential on the unit sphere. For unit vectors, these two viewpoints are closely related, but not identical.

If $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between $\hat{\bm w}_i$ and $\hat{\bm w}_j$, then
$$
\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\|^2
= 2 - 2\cos\theta_{ij},
$$
so
$$
\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\|^{-1}
= \bigl(2(1-\cos\theta_{ij})\bigr)^{-1/2}.
$$
So yes, on the unit sphere it is a monotone function of cosine similarity: smaller angle means larger energy. But it is not just a harmless reparameterization. The inverse-distance form is nonlinear and heavily emphasizes very close pairs. That matters because hyperspherical energy is meant to measure how “crowded” the neurons are on the sphere, not just their average correlation. A direct sum of cosines can cancel positive and negative terms and is much less sensitive to a pair of neurons nearly collapsing onto each other.

This is exactly why the inverse-distance form is useful in the paper’s theory. OFT does not try to minimize the raw hyperspherical energy; it tries to preserve the pretrained layer’s energy exactly by applying a shared orthogonal transform, and orthogonal maps preserve all pairwise Euclidean distances and angles. So the key property is invariance under the same orthogonal transformation, not merely monotonicity in cosine. The inverse-distance form is the canonical “energy” that goes with the hypersphere geometry, and it matches the intuition that nearby neurons should contribute a very large penalty.

On your numerical question: if two normalized neurons become extremely close, the corresponding term becomes very large, and if they coincide exactly, it diverges. That is not a problem for OFT as presented, because the method is not optimizing $\mathrm{HE}(\bm W)$ directly; it is preserving $\mathrm{HE}(\bm W)$ by construction through orthogonal parameterization. So the singularity is more of a conceptual feature of the measure than a training instability in this paper. If one were to use this quantity as an actual loss, one would typically need an $\varepsilon$-clamp in the denominator to avoid division by zero.

So the right way to read the paper is: hyperspherical energy is a geometric descriptor of the layer’s pairwise angular structure, inverse-distance is the standard choice for that descriptor, and the orthogonal finetuning result works because orthogonal transforms leave that structure unchanged.
