\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 5: Orthogonal Finetuning: Core Method and Theory

# From hyperspherical energy preservation to shared orthogonal transformations

Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, begins with a simple but powerful idea: if a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model already has useful semantic structure, then a good finetuning method should adapt the model without destroying that structure. The paper argues that a better way to measure whether the structure is preserved is not just to look at Euclidean distance between pretrained and finetuned weights, but to look at the relational geometry among neurons. In particular, the paper focuses on hyperspherical similarity, such as cosine similarity, because this kind of angular information has been observed to encode semantic information well.

To formalize this, the paper uses hyperspherical energy, which measures how uniformly neurons are arranged on the unit hypersphere. If a layer has neuron vectors $\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n$, and $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the normalized version of the $i$-th neuron, then the hyperspherical energy is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
The key point is that this quantity depends on the pairwise angular relationships among neurons. The paper’s hypothesis is that if finetuning keeps this energy close to the pretrained model’s energy, then the model is more likely to preserve its original semantic generation ability.

A naive strategy would be to add a regularizer that directly penalizes changes in hyperspherical energy. The paper argues that this is not enough, because simply encouraging the energy to stay the same does not guarantee that the difference will be minimized in a structurally meaningful way. Instead, OFT uses a more principled route: exploit the fact that hyperspherical energy is invariant under a shared orthogonal transformation. This leads naturally to the central update rule of the method.

# Fully connected layer notation and the OFT formulation

The paper explains OFT first using a fully connected layer. Let
\[
\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}
\]
be the weight matrix of a layer, where each column $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents one neuron, and let $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be the input. The layer output is
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x}.
\]
If $\bm{W}^0$ denotes the pretrained weights, then OFT does not replace them with an arbitrary new matrix. Instead, it learns an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and sets
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0,
\]
with the orthogonality constraint
\[
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}.
\]
This means that finetuning is carried out by transforming the pretrained neurons with a shared orthogonal matrix. The paper emphasizes that the attainable minimum of the hyperspherical-energy difference objective is zero, and that this minimum is reached when the finetuned weights differ from the pretrained ones only by rotation or reflection.

This formulation is the core OFT principle:
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\|
\]
is achieved exactly by transformations of the form $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with orthogonal $\bm{R}$. In other words, OFT does not ask the model to forget its old representation and learn a brand-new one. It asks the model to keep the same internal geometry while changing its orientation in weight space in a controlled way.

# The forward pass $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$

With OFT, the forward pass of the layer becomes
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
subject to the orthogonality constraint on $\bm{R}$. This is the operational form of the method: during training, the model learns $\bm{R}$, and during inference the transformed matrix can simply be folded into the pretrained weights as an equivalent weight matrix.

A crucial design choice is that OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ to the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. This makes the finetuned model start exactly from the pretrained model, rather than from a perturbed version of it. The paper treats this as an important stability property, because finetuning should begin with no change in behavior and then move gradually toward the downstream task.

This identity initialization also makes the method conceptually clean. The pretrained model is not replaced; it is only re-expressed through a learned orthogonal transform. That is why the method can be interpreted as a controlled adaptation rather than a wholesale rewrite of the layer’s parameters.

# Why an orthogonal matrix preserves pairwise neuron angles and hyperspherical energy

The reason orthogonality is so central is that orthogonal transformations preserve geometry. In the paper’s setting, the important geometry is not the absolute length of a neuron vector, but the pairwise relationships among neuron directions. Since hyperspherical energy is built from normalized neurons, preserving those directions means preserving the energy.

The paper’s key claim is that if the same orthogonal transformation is applied to all neurons in a layer, then the pairwise hyperspherical similarity is preserved. Intuitively, an orthogonal matrix does not distort angles: it can rotate or reflect a set of vectors, but it cannot change the relative angles between them. Therefore, when all neurons are transformed by the same $\bm{R}$, the pairwise distances on the unit hypersphere remain unchanged, and so does hyperspherical energy.

This is why OFT is stronger than a generic small update. A small Euclidean change in the weights does not necessarily mean that the semantic geometry has been preserved. OFT instead constrains the update to preserve the angular structure that the paper argues is tied to semantic generation ability. In this sense, OFT uses a geometric invariant, not just a magnitude constraint, as the basis for finetuning.

# Rotation and reflection as allowable transformations

The paper notes that the orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ may represent either a rotation or a reflection. In matrix terms, this corresponds to the determinant being $1$ or $-1$, respectively. Both are allowed because both preserve pairwise angles and therefore preserve hyperspherical energy.

This matters for understanding the objective’s zero minimum. If the finetuned layer differs from the pretrained one only by such a transformation, then the layer’s internal relational structure is unchanged. The neurons have been moved in the ambient space, but the geometry among them remains the same. The paper uses this fact to justify learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix rather than a more general transform.

The allowable transformations are therefore not arbitrary. OFT permits only those transformations that can change the coordinate representation of the layer without distorting its internal semantic organization. That is the core compromise between adaptability and preservation.

# Layer-shared transformation as a change of coordinate system for neurons

One of the most useful intuitions in the paper is that OFT can be understood as changing the canonical coordinate system for the neurons in a layer. Because the same orthogonal matrix is shared across all neurons in the layer, the transformation does not treat individual neurons independently. Instead, it changes the entire layer as a group.

This perspective explains why OFT can modify semantics while preserving structure. The layer is not being broken apart and rebuilt neuron by neuron. It is being viewed in a different orthonormal basis. As a result, the layer can respond differently to inputs even though the internal pairwise geometry among its neurons remains intact. The paper argues that this kind of coordinated reorientation is enough to support downstream control tasks such as subject-driven generation and controllable generation.

The layer-shared design is also what gives OFT its structural regularity. If each neuron could be transformed independently, the geometry of the layer could drift in a way that destroys the pretrained organization. By tying all neurons in a layer to the same orthogonal transform, OFT keeps the update coherent and semantically meaningful.

# Identity initialization so finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained model

The paper places strong emphasis on starting from the pretrained model exactly, and identity initialization is how OFT does that. When $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, the OFT layer reduces to the original pretrained layer:
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0.
\]
So before training begins, the model behaves exactly as it did at pretraining time.

This is more than a convenience. It is part of the method’s stability story. Finetuning should not force the model to begin from a modified state that already risks losing the pretrained generative behavior. Instead, OFT begins at the pretrained solution and then searches for a better downstream task adaptation through orthogonal movement away from that point. In the paper’s framing, this respects the original knowledge while still allowing controlled change.

Identity initialization also makes OFT conceptually aligned with the paper’s broader goal of preserving semantic generation. The method does not gamble on a large unconstrained shift at the start of training. It begins from a known good model and only moves in ways that the orthogonality constraint permits.

# Why orthogonality acts as a useful regularizer

The paper presents orthogonality as a strong form of regularization. Standard finetuning is very flexible, but that flexibility can be a liability: if the model changes too freely, it can lose the pretrained semantic structure and become unstable. OFT deliberately restricts that freedom. By forcing updates to be orthogonal, it preserves pairwise neuron angles and thereby preserves the relational geometry that the paper treats as essential.

At the same time, orthogonality is not so restrictive that the model becomes unable to adapt. This is why the paper describes OFT as a sweet spot between flexibility and regularity. The method can still change the orientation of the representation, which is enough to support task-specific control, but it does so on a smooth hypersphere manifold rather than through arbitrary parameter drift. The paper argues that this leads to better optimization behavior and faster convergence.

It is also important that OFT does not try to minimize hyperspherical energy the way some classification-oriented orthogonal training methods do. For finetuning a pretrained diffusion model, the goal is not to spread neurons out uniformly on the hypersphere. The goal is to keep the pretrained semantic structure intact. That is why preserving the pretrained energy, rather than reducing it, is the relevant objective here.

# What goes wrong when the orthogonality constraint is removed

The paper includes an explicit experiment showing that the orthogonality constraint is not just a theoretical nicety. When OFT is run without the orthogonality constraint, the method fails in practice. In the controllable generation setting, the unconstrained variant fails to generate realistic images and does not achieve the desired control effect.

This result is important because it shows that the success of OFT is not simply due to having additional trainable parameters or a different finetuning setup. The structured constraint itself is doing meaningful work. Without it, the transform becomes too free, and the pretrained semantic organization is no longer protected. The model loses the stability that orthogonality provides.

So the failure case clarifies the point of the method. OFT is not merely about learning a new transformation; it is about learning a transformation that is powerful enough to adapt the model and disciplined enough to preserve what matters. The orthogonality constraint is exactly what keeps those two goals in balance.
