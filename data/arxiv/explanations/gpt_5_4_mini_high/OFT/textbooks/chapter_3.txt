\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 3: A Geometric View of Neural Semantics

# Viewing neurons as vectors in weight space

The paper begins from a simple but powerful geometric picture: a neuron, or more precisely the weights associated with a neuron in a layer, can be viewed as a vector in a high-dimensional space. In a fully connected layer, the weight matrix is written as $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is one neuron. This viewpoint matters because it turns questions about learning into questions about the relationships among vectors. Instead of thinking only about individual scalar weights, the paper asks how the collection of neuron vectors is arranged relative to one another.

That geometric arrangement is not just a bookkeeping device. The paper argues that the way neurons are positioned in weight space reflects semantic structure in the model. When a model processes an input $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, the output of a fully connected layer is $\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}$. So each neuron contributes through its interaction with the input vector, and the full layer output depends on the entire set of neuron directions and magnitudes. This makes the layer naturally interpretable as a geometric object whose behavior is governed by vector relationships.

The chapter’s central intuition is that, for finetuning a text-to-image diffusion model, preserving the useful arrangement of these vectors may be more important than preserving the exact raw coordinates. In other words, the paper treats the pretrained neuron configuration as a structured geometric object that already encodes generative knowledge, and finetuning should modify that object carefully rather than scramble it.

# Direction versus magnitude: what each part of a neuron represents

A key idea in the paper is that a neuron vector has two conceptually different components: direction and magnitude. The direction describes where the vector points, while the magnitude describes how long it is. The paper’s argument is that, for semantic information, direction is often more important than magnitude. This is why the paper emphasizes neuron angles and orthogonal transformations rather than unconstrained weight changes.

The intuition comes from the fact that the paper studies semantic preservation in a pretrained model. If the goal is to adapt the model to a new task while keeping its generative ability intact, then the critical question is which aspects of the neuron geometry encode that ability. The paper argues that directions carry the main semantic content, while magnitudes can be less informative. This is why the authors focus on angular information and pairwise relationships rather than simply on Euclidean distance between weight tensors.

This distinction also explains why the paper thinks standard finetuning can be fragile. A small Euclidean change in the weights does not necessarily mean that the semantic structure of the neuron configuration has been preserved. A model can move only a little in Euclidean distance and still alter the relative directions of neurons in a way that damages generation quality. Conversely, a transformation that changes coordinates but preserves pairwise angles may retain the important relational structure. The paper builds its finetuning method around exactly this geometric insight.

# Cosine similarity and angular information

To make the notion of direction mathematically meaningful, the paper uses hyperspherical similarity, with cosine similarity as the standard example. Two vectors are similar in this sense if the angle between them is small, and dissimilar if the angle is large. The paper points to prior empirical work showing that angular feature difference can characterize semantic difference well. In the geometric picture adopted here, angles become a proxy for meaning.

This is important because the paper is not just talking about individual vectors in isolation. It is concerned with pairwise relations among neurons in the same layer. Cosine similarity captures those relations by comparing directions after removing magnitude. If $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes a normalized neuron, then the relevant comparison is between $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$. Once vectors are normalized, the remaining information is purely angular, which is exactly the kind of structure the paper wants to preserve.

The paper’s broader claim is that these angular relationships help encode the knowledge of the neural network. That is why preserving the pairwise angles among neurons is treated as a principled finetuning objective. When OFT later learns an orthogonal transformation, it does so precisely because orthogonal transformations preserve these angles. The use of cosine similarity is therefore not incidental; it is the mathematical expression of the paper’s semantic hypothesis.

# The unit hypersphere as a space of normalized neurons

Normalization plays a central role in the paper’s geometric framework. Once each neuron is normalized to unit length, it lies on the unit hypersphere. The set of normalized neurons therefore becomes a configuration of points on a spherical surface rather than arbitrary vectors in Euclidean space. This shift is crucial because it separates direction from magnitude and makes angular geometry explicit.

The paper uses the unit hypersphere to define and reason about hyperspherical energy. In this setting, the arrangement of neurons is judged by how the normalized vectors are distributed on the sphere and by how their pairwise relationships behave. The paper describes hyperspherical energy as characterizing the pairwise relational structure among neurons and capturing their uniformity on the unit hypersphere. In its formal expression for a fully connected layer, it is written as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1},
\]
where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$.

The importance of the hypersphere is that it provides a natural geometric stage on which semantic structure can be compared and preserved. If two models have neuron sets that differ only by an orthogonal transformation, then their points on the hypersphere have the same pairwise angular relationships. In the paper’s view, this means the intrinsic relational structure has been preserved even if the coordinate system has changed. That is why the hypersphere is not just a mathematical convenience, but the key space in which semantic preservation is analyzed.

# Empirical evidence that angular differences correlate with semantic differences

The paper does not rely only on abstract geometry; it also appeals to prior empirical findings. It cites earlier work showing that angular feature difference is a strong indicator of semantic difference. The idea is that changes in direction often reflect changes in meaning more faithfully than changes in raw scale. This motivates the paper’s focus on angles as the main carrier of semantic information.

The evidence is strengthened by the paper’s own toy experiment. There, the authors train a standard convolutional autoencoder on flower images and then examine three ways of generating the feature map in testing: the inner product used during training, the magnitude information, and the angular information. The result is striking: the angular information alone can almost perfectly recover the input images, while magnitude information contains no useful information. The point of the experiment is not that magnitude never matters in any setting, but that, in this example, direction is the part of the representation that preserves the image’s semantic structure.

This empirical result supports the paper’s central thesis in a very direct way. If angular information alone can preserve and reconstruct the image content so effectively, then modifying neuron directions is a plausible way to semantically alter a generative model. At the same time, because the model’s learned structure is encoded in these angles, any finetuning strategy must be careful not to disrupt them arbitrarily. This is exactly the balance OFT seeks.

# The autoencoder toy experiment: angular information versus magnitude information

The toy autoencoder experiment deserves special emphasis because it makes the abstract geometry tangible. The paper trains a convolutional autoencoder on flower images using the standard inner product during training. Then, at test time, it replaces the usual feature computation with different alternatives to see which component carries useful information. The three cases are the original inner product, magnitude information, and angular information.

The outcome shows that angular information can reconstruct the input images almost perfectly, whereas magnitude information fails to provide useful reconstruction. This observation is used by the paper as concrete evidence that the directions of neurons encode essential semantic content. In other words, the experiment suggests that the meaning of the image is stored in how vectors point, not merely in how large they are.

This matters for finetuning because it indicates what should be preserved and what may be safely adjusted. If direction is the semantic carrier, then changing directions is a direct way to modify the model’s behavior. However, because those same directions also store pretrained knowledge, the change must be structured. The toy experiment therefore serves as a bridge between intuition and method: it shows why the paper later turns to orthogonal transformations, which can alter coordinates while preserving the angular relations that the experiment suggests are semantically important.

# The phase-versus-magnitude analogy from Fourier analysis

To motivate direction-based editing further, the paper draws an analogy from the $2$D Fourier transform. In that setting, an image can be decomposed into magnitude and phase spectra. The paper emphasizes that phase contains angular information between the input and the basis, and that this phase information preserves the major part of the semantics. Indeed, the paper notes that the phase spectrum of an image, even with a random magnitude spectrum, can still reconstruct the original image without losing its semantics.

The reason this analogy is useful is that it offers a familiar example of how angular information can dominate meaning. If phase in Fourier analysis preserves the essential structure of an image, then perhaps neuron directions in a neural network play an analogous role. The paper uses this comparison to reinforce the idea that semantic manipulation should focus on directional changes rather than unrestricted coordinate changes.

This analogy does not claim that neurons are literally Fourier coefficients. Rather, it serves as an intuition pump. It suggests that semantic content can be robustly encoded in angular relationships, and that changing those relationships is a controlled way to modify output behavior. The paper’s finetuning method is built on this same belief: if one wants to steer a model’s generation, adjusting the geometry of directions is more meaningful than freely perturbing all weights.

# Why semantic editing may be better achieved by changing neuron directions rather than unrestricted weights

The final step in the paper’s geometric intuition is to connect these ideas to editing and finetuning. In both subject-driven generation and controllable generation, the task is not to retrain the model from scratch, but to adapt it in a way that introduces new control while preserving pretrained generative ability. The paper argues that unrestricted weight updates are too flexible for this purpose. They may change the model enough to damage its original semantics, even if the Euclidean distance from the pretrained weights remains small.

By contrast, changing neuron directions in a controlled way directly targets the part of the representation that appears to carry semantic information. The paper’s geometric claim is that the relationships among neurons, especially their pairwise angles, are what encode the knowledge we want to preserve. If these angles are maintained, the model can be adapted without destroying its intrinsic structure. That is why the paper later designs Orthogonal Finetuning around transformations that preserve pairwise hyperspherical similarity.

The orthogonal viewpoint also explains why the paper sees finetuning as adjusting a coordinate system rather than rewriting the model from scratch. An orthogonal transformation rotates or reflects the neuron vectors while preserving the angles between them. In the paper’s language, this lets the model change its semantic behavior in a controlled way while leaving the hyperspherical energy unchanged. Thus the geometric view of neural semantics is not merely descriptive; it directly motivates the finetuning strategy that follows.
