\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 10: Controllable Generation, Evaluation, and Future Directions

# Task definition and motivation

This chapter focuses on the paper’s second major application: controllable generation for text-to-image diffusion models. The setting extends ordinary text prompting by adding an extra control signal that specifies some structure the generated image should follow. The goal is therefore not just to produce a realistic image from text, but to produce an image that is both faithful to the prompt and consistent with the provided control input.

The paper emphasizes that text guidance alone is often too ambiguous for fine-grained control. A prompt may describe a scene or object, but it does not always specify layout, shape, pose, or identity details. A control signal fills in that gap. In the paper, the control signal can take forms such as canny edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks. The model must use that signal as a stronger guide while still preserving the rich generative ability learned during pretraining.

This is exactly why controllable generation is a difficult finetuning problem. If finetuning is too weak, the model ignores the control signal. If finetuning is too aggressive, the model may follow the signal but lose realism or semantic richness. The paper’s central claim is that Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, and its constrained variant, COFT, provide a better balance between these two requirements than standard finetuning methods.

# Baselines: ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA

To evaluate controllable generation, the paper compares OFT and COFT against three representative baselines. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are methods designed specifically to add control to pretrained diffusion models. LoRA is a more general finetuning method that is also relevant because it is parameter-efficient and widely used for adapting large models.

The paper uses these baselines in a fairly direct way. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter serve as the main controllable-generation comparisons because they are designed to inject control signals into diffusion models. LoRA is included because it offers a simple low-rank alternative for finetuning, and it helps establish whether OFT’s gains come from the particular orthogonal parameterization rather than from parameter-efficient adaptation in general.

A key experimental point is fairness. The paper applies OFT or COFT to the same layers where LoRA is used, so that the comparison is not confounded by different adaptation locations. The experiments also use Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained backbone, and the controllable-generation tasks are finetuned for 20 epochs.

# Datasets and tasks: Canny-to-image, segmentation-to-image, and landmark-to-face

The paper studies three challenging controllable-generation tasks. The first is canny edge to image, or C2I, on the COCO dataset. Here the control signal is an edge map, and the model must generate a plausible image that respects those edges. This task tests whether the model can convert sparse structural information into a realistic image.

The second task is segmentation map to image, or S2I, on the ADE20K dataset. In this setting, the control signal is a semantic segmentation map, which specifies the class of each region in the image. This is a more detailed and semantically rich form of control, because the output must match the spatial organization implied by the map.

The third task is landmark to face, or L2F, on the CelebA-HQ dataset. Here the control signal consists of facial landmarks, and the generated face should follow the prescribed pose and structure. This task is especially useful for evaluating geometric control, because even small deviations in landmark alignment are easy to detect.

These three tasks together cover different forms of conditioning. Canny edges provide coarse shape information, segmentation maps provide dense semantic layout, and landmarks provide precise spatial structure for faces. The paper uses this mix to show that the method is not specialized to one type of control signal.

# Why controllable generation needs both fidelity and strict control

Controllable generation is not simply about making images look good. The generated output must satisfy two requirements at once. First, it must be visually faithful: the image should be realistic, detailed, and coherent. Second, it must obey the control signal with high accuracy. A beautiful image that ignores the input signal is a failure, just as a technically obedient but low-quality image is a failure.

This dual requirement is what makes evaluation difficult. Ordinary perceptual quality alone does not tell us whether the control signal was respected. At the same time, a model could score well on a control metric but still generate unnatural images. The paper’s experimental design reflects this tension by evaluating both the visual quality and the consistency between the input control and the output image.

The paper argues that OFT and COFT improve this balance. By preserving the pretrained model’s hyperspherical structure while adapting it through orthogonal transformations, they aim to keep the semantic generation ability intact while still enabling the model to respond to new control signals.

# The control consistency metric

A central contribution of the paper is a new evaluation measure for controllable generation called the control consistency metric. The idea is simple and intuitive: if a generated image truly follows the intended control signal, then one should be able to recover a compatible control signal from that image and compare it to the original input.

In other words, the metric asks whether the generated image contains the right structure in a form that an external estimator can recognize. The paper presents this as a way to more directly evaluate controllability than relying only on image quality or prompt alignment. It is particularly useful because the “correctness” of controllable generation is inherently tied to structure, not just appearance.

The paper also notes an important caveat. The control consistency evaluation depends on the accuracy of the off-the-shelf segmentation or detection model used to recover the control signal from the generated image. That means the metric is powerful, but it is also only as reliable as the auxiliary predictor that measures consistency. Even so, it gives a much more direct assessment of control behavior than visual inspection alone.

# Task-specific metrics

Because the three controllable-generation tasks involve different kinds of control, the paper uses different metrics for each one. For C2I, it computes intersection-over-union, or IoU, and F1 score. These metrics compare the recovered edge structure to the original edge signal and summarize how well the generated image preserves the intended shape.

For S2I, the paper uses mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy. These are standard segmentation-based measures that compare the semantic regions inferred from the generated image with the original segmentation map. They are especially appropriate here because the input itself is a dense semantic layout.

For L2F, the paper measures the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the control landmarks and the predicted landmarks. This is a direct geometric measure: smaller distance means the generated face better matches the target pose and structure. Because this task is about precise alignment, the landmark error is a natural choice.

The paper’s choice of metrics is important conceptually. It shows that controllable generation should be evaluated in a task-aware way. Different control signals require different measures, but the common theme is the same: the output should make the intended control recoverable.

# Convergence analysis and evidence of faster learning

One of the strongest empirical claims in the paper is that OFT and COFT converge faster than the baselines. The clearest convergence study is reported on the landmark-to-face task. There, OFT and COFT exhibit stable learning dynamics and reach strong control performance significantly earlier than the competing methods.

The paper gives a particularly striking comparison with LoRA. It reports that LoRA needs 20 epochs to converge to the level that OFT reaches by epoch 8. This suggests not only that OFT is more sample-efficient, but also that it is easier to optimize. The training process is more predictable, which matters in practice because it reduces the need for careful tuning of the number of epochs.

The comparison with ControlNet is also revealing. The paper observes that ControlNet’s controllability suddenly appears after the 8th epoch, matching the sudden convergence behavior previously reported for that method. In contrast, OFT and COFT improve more smoothly. That smoother trajectory is practically valuable because it makes training behavior easier to interpret and hyperparameters easier to choose.

The paper also points out that OFT can be data-efficient. In one example from the teaser, OFT converges well with only $5\%$ of the ADE20K dataset. This is consistent with the broader theme that the method can adapt effectively even when data is limited.

# Quantitative results and data efficiency

Across the controllable-generation tasks, the paper reports that OFT and COFT outperform the baselines in terms of controllability. The quantitative results show stronger agreement between the generated image and the input control signal, while still maintaining high image quality.

A particularly important finding is that OFT and COFT use a similar number of trainable parameters to LoRA, but far fewer than ControlNet. Despite this small parameter budget, they produce better or more stable control performance. This is a central part of the paper’s argument: parameter efficiency does not have to come at the cost of control quality.

The paper also observes that the performance of OFT has not saturated. In their experiments, the method continues to improve as the number of trainable parameters increases. This suggests that the orthogonal parameterization remains expressive, even though it is constrained. At the same time, the paper emphasizes that OFT has a lower effective cost than much larger control modules.

The comparison with LoRA is nuanced. LoRA can do relatively well in some settings, but the paper reports that its performance ceiling is lower overall. In particular, LoRA performs better than ControlNet on segmentation-to-image, but worse on canny-to-image and landmark-to-face. In the paper’s view, this reinforces the idea that OFT’s orthogonal updates preserve more useful pretrained structure while still enabling adaptation.

# Qualitative results across edges, segmentation maps, and facial landmarks

The qualitative results mirror the quantitative trends. In the segmentation-to-image task, LoRA often fails to follow the segmentation map correctly, whereas ControlNet, OFT, and COFT can. The paper reports that OFT and COFT often produce images with more vivid details and more reasonable geometric structure than ControlNet, while using far fewer parameters.

In the canny-to-image task, OFT and COFT can generate semantically plausible images from relatively rough edge information. This matters because edge maps are sparse and ambiguous; the model must fill in a great deal of content while respecting the constraints encoded in the contours. The paper finds that T2I-Adapter and LoRA perform worse here.

In the landmark-to-face task, OFT and COFT achieve the most accurate pose control, even for challenging face configurations. The generated faces track the input landmarks closely, which indicates that the model is not merely producing visually believable faces but is also obeying the geometric conditioning signal.

The overall qualitative message is consistent across tasks. OFT and COFT preserve realism while increasing control fidelity. This combination is exactly what controllable generation needs, and it is one of the strongest pieces of evidence in favor of the orthogonal finetuning approach.

# Main empirical takeaways

The paper’s main empirical takeaway is that OFT and COFT improve controllable generation along several dimensions at once. They produce higher-quality images, stronger adherence to control, faster convergence, and better finetuning stability than the baselines. They also do so with fewer trainable parameters than ControlNet and with parameter budgets comparable to LoRA.

Another important conclusion is that COFT improves stability even further by making the deviation from the pretrained model explicit. The paper presents the constraint parameter $\epsilon$ as a way to control the balance between flexibility and conservativeness: larger $\epsilon$ makes COFT behave more like OFT, while smaller $\epsilon$ keeps it closer to the pretrained model. This gives the user a direct knob for stability.

The paper also stresses that OFT adds no additional inference overhead. At test time, the learned orthogonal matrix can simply be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix, yielding an equivalent adapted model. So the gains in controllability do not come at the cost of slower deployment.

# Limitations and future directions

Although the results are strong, the paper is explicit about several limitations. The first is computational. OFT uses Cayley parameterization to guarantee orthogonality, but this involves a matrix inverse. That inverse makes the method less scalable than one might like, especially for large layers. The paper partially addresses this with block-diagonal parameterization, but the underlying cost remains an issue.

The second limitation is representational bias. The block-diagonal structure reduces the number of parameters and makes optimization easier, but it also introduces additional structural assumptions. In the paper’s own words, this stronger regularization improves efficiency, yet it also limits flexibility. That trade-off is central to the current form of OFT.

The third limitation concerns scalability and expressiveness more broadly. The paper notes that parameter efficiency currently depends heavily on the block-diagonal design, and it asks how one might build more expressive orthogonal structures that remain efficient without becoming too biased.

These limitations lead to several open problems. One is how to make differentiable orthogonalization faster, so that the orthogonality constraint can be enforced without a costly matrix inverse. Another is compositionality: if multiple downstream tasks each produce an orthogonal matrix, those matrices can be multiplied and the result is still orthogonal, but it is not yet clear whether such combinations preserve the knowledge from all tasks in a useful way. A final open question is how to design more effective parameter-efficient orthogonal structures that keep the benefits of OFT while reducing the bias introduced by block diagonalization.

Taken together, these open problems show that controllable generation is not only an application area for OFT, but also a test bed for future research on structured finetuning. The paper establishes that orthogonal finetuning is a strong and practical direction; the remaining challenge is to make it even more scalable, expressive, and compositional.
