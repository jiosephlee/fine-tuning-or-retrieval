\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 2: Existing Finetuning Approaches and Their Limitations

# Standard finetuning with small learning rates

The most direct way to adapt a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model is to keep training it on the downstream task with gradient descent, usually with a small learning rate. In the paper, this is described as the conventional finetuning strategy: one updates the model itself, or only certain layers, while trying to avoid moving too far from the pretrained solution. The basic intuition is simple. If the pretrained model already knows how to generate realistic images, then only a modest amount of adjustment should be needed to specialize it for a new task such as subject-driven generation or controllable generation.

This strategy is appealing because it is easy to implement and, at first glance, it seems to respect the pretrained model. A small learning rate implicitly encourages the finetuned parameters to stay close to the original parameters, which is often taken as a sign that the model’s prior knowledge is being preserved. In the paper’s notation, this amounts to treating finetuning as if it were approximately minimizing a distance such as $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0 \|$, where $\bm{M}$ denotes the finetuned weights and $\bm{M}^0$ denotes the pretrained weights. However, the paper emphasizes that this is only a heuristic. It is not a principled guarantee that the model will keep the same generative behavior, and in large diffusion models even a small parameter change can eventually accumulate into a meaningful semantic shift.

# Why small Euclidean weight changes are commonly assumed to preserve pretrained knowledge

The common belief behind small-learning-rate finetuning is that if the weights do not move very far in Euclidean space, then the model should not change much functionally. This is a natural first guess, and it explains why many finetuning methods are designed to constrain the magnitude of the update rather than its structure. The paper notes that existing methods often implicitly rely on exactly this assumption: smaller Euclidean distance from the pretrained model is treated as a proxy for better preservation of pretrained knowledge.

The limitation, according to the paper, is that Euclidean distance is too coarse to capture what actually matters for preserving semantics in a neural network. Two models can be close in parameter space while still differing in how their neurons relate to one another, and those relational changes can alter the model’s generation behavior. The paper argues that semantic preservation is not only about how far the weights move, but about how the internal structure of the representation changes. That is why the authors say Euclidean difference alone cannot fully capture the degree of semantic preservation. In their view, a more structural notion of similarity is needed, one that reflects the organization of neurons rather than only the size of the parameter update.

# DreamBooth for subject-driven generation

DreamBooth is the main subject-driven generation baseline discussed in the paper. The task is to take only a few images of a subject and finetune a text-to-image diffusion model so that it can generate that same subject in new contexts described by text prompts. DreamBooth does this by introducing a customized token for the subject and finetuning with a reconstruction loss together with a class-specific prior preservation loss. In the paper’s setup, this makes DreamBooth a strong and natural baseline for personalized generation.

At the same time, the paper shows that DreamBooth can be fragile. Because it directly updates the pretrained model with a standard finetuning procedure, it may preserve identity reasonably well for a while but still become unstable with more training. The paper reports that after enough iterations, DreamBooth can begin to generate collapsed images. It can also maintain the subject identity while failing to follow the text prompt accurately, which means that the model may remember the person, object, or instance but not the requested context. This tension between identity preservation and prompt adherence is one of the main limitations of naive finetuning in the subject-driven setting.

# LoRA as a low-rank additive update method

LoRA is presented in the paper as a parameter-efficient alternative to full finetuning. Its basic idea is to represent the weight update as a low-rank addition, rather than updating all of the weights freely. This means that the model is adapted through a restricted additive update, which reduces the number of trainable parameters. In the paper’s comparison, LoRA is a useful reference point because it embodies the general strategy of making finetuning cheaper while trying to preserve the behavior of the pretrained model.

The paper also makes clear why LoRA is only a partial solution. Low-rank structure is a useful form of constraint, but it is not the same as preserving the relational geometry of the neurons. In the subject-driven experiments, LoRA can fail to preserve the subject identity, and in the controllable generation experiments it shows a relatively low performance ceiling even after careful hyperparameter tuning. In the paper’s interpretation, LoRA prevents the pretrained weights from shifting too dramatically, but it does not directly preserve the semantic structure that underlies the pretrained model’s generative ability. As a result, LoRA can be efficient, yet still insufficiently stable for preserving the fine details of a pretrained diffusion model.

# Adapter-style finetuning and parameter-efficient transfer

The paper places LoRA within a broader family of parameter-efficient adaptation methods, including adapter-style approaches that have been widely studied in natural language processing. These methods aim to make finetuning practical by adding a small number of trainable components or by reparameterizing the update, rather than retraining the full network. The motivation is straightforward: if the pretrained model is already very powerful, then perhaps only a small amount of additional capacity is needed to specialize it for a downstream task.

What the paper stresses, however, is that parameter efficiency alone does not solve the harder problem of preserving pretrained generative performance. Whether the extra parameters are added as adapters, low-rank updates, or other reparameterizations, the central issue remains the same: the method must adapt the model without destroying the structure that supports its original semantics. The paper’s critique is not that these methods are useless, but that they do not come with a principled mechanism for protecting semantic preservation. In practice, they can still require careful tuning of learning rate, rank, or training duration, and they may still show instability when the downstream task pushes the model too far from its pretrained state.

# ControlNet and T2I-Adapter for controllable generation

For controllable generation, the paper focuses on methods that adapt a pretrained diffusion model to accept extra control signals such as canny edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks. ControlNet is highlighted as a prominent example. It finetunes a pretrained diffusion model so that it can follow additional control inputs, and the paper treats it as one of the strongest prior baselines in this setting. T2I-Adapter is another closely related approach, also designed to improve controllability by finetuning a pretrained diffusion model.

The paper’s discussion makes two limitations of these methods especially clear. First, they can be relatively parameter-heavy and, in the case of ControlNet, introduce additional computational overhead at inference time. Second, their optimization behavior may be less stable than desired. In the experiments, the paper reports that controllability in ControlNet can emerge rather suddenly after several epochs, rather than improving in a smooth and predictable way. T2I-Adapter and ControlNet are therefore effective baselines, but they do not fully solve the problem of getting strong controllability while also preserving pretrained generation quality, keeping inference lightweight, and converging reliably.

# Typical failure modes: overfitting, identity drift, prompt mismatch, and model collapse

The paper’s examples make it clear that standard finetuning methods often fail in predictable ways. One common failure mode is overfitting. In subject-driven generation, a model may initially improve at reproducing the subject, but if training continues too long it can collapse. The paper gives a concrete example in which DreamBooth begins to generate collapsed images after many iterations. This shows that more training is not always better; beyond a point, finetuning can damage the model rather than improve it.

A second failure mode is identity drift. This occurs when the generated output no longer resembles the intended subject, even if the model still produces plausible images. The paper notes that LoRA can lose the subject identity in the subject-driven setting. A third failure mode is prompt mismatch: the model may preserve the subject but fail to obey the text prompt describing the desired context. DreamBooth is explicitly described in this way in the paper’s qualitative comparison. In controllable generation, there is a related problem of weak or delayed control, where the generated image does not reliably follow the input signal, or where the control effect appears only after long training. Together, these failure modes show that preserving image realism is not enough; finetuning must preserve the right kind of structure.

# The missing ingredient: a structural measure of semantic preservation beyond Euclidean distance

The paper argues that the central missing ingredient in existing finetuning approaches is a structural measure of semantic preservation. Euclidean distance between pretrained and finetuned weights is too blunt because it only measures how far the parameters move, not how the internal relationships among neurons change. What matters for the semantics of a diffusion model, the paper suggests, is the geometry of those relationships. This is why the authors turn to hyperspherical similarity and hyperspherical energy, which describe pairwise relationships among neurons on the unit hypersphere.

In this view, preserving pretrained knowledge means preserving the relational structure encoded by neuron angles, not merely keeping the weights numerically close. The paper proposes that a good finetuning method should therefore minimize changes in hyperspherical energy, because that quantity captures how uniformly or non-uniformly the neurons are arranged on the sphere. Existing methods such as small-learning-rate finetuning, low-rank adaptation, and adapter-style updates do not explicitly enforce this kind of structural preservation. That omission is what motivates the paper’s later orthogonal finetuning framework. Orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles, and therefore preserve hyperspherical energy exactly, giving the paper a principled way to adapt the model while keeping its pretrained semantic structure intact.
