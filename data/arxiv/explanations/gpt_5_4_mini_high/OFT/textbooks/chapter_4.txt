\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 4: Hyperspherical Energy as a Measure of Structure Preservation

# Definition of hyperspherical similarity between neurons

In the paper, each layer is viewed as a collection of neuron weight vectors. For a fully connected layer, the weights are written as $\mathbf{W}=\{\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each column $\mathbf{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ can be thought of as one neuron. To compare neurons in a way that focuses on their directions rather than their magnitudes, the paper normalizes each vector onto the unit hypersphere by defining $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i:=\mathbf{w}_i/\|\mathbf{w}_i\|$.

Once the neurons are normalized, their relationship is measured by hyperspherical similarity, which the paper describes through angular notions such as cosine similarity. The central idea is simple: two neurons are considered similar if they point in nearly the same direction on the unit sphere, and dissimilar if their directions differ substantially. This is a geometric way of looking at a layer, because it ignores the raw scale of each weight vector and instead concentrates on the relative orientation of the neurons. The paper emphasizes that this angular information is the part that most closely reflects semantic structure.

# Definition of hyperspherical energy for a layer

Hyperspherical energy is the layer-level quantity built from these pairwise directional relationships. For a weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$, the paper defines the hyperspherical energy as
\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Here, the sum runs over all distinct pairs of normalized neurons in the same layer. Because the vectors lie on the unit sphere, the Euclidean distance $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|$ is itself a function of the angle between the two neurons, so the energy is ultimately an angular, similarity-based quantity.

The intuition is that hyperspherical energy compresses the geometry of the whole layer into one scalar. If many neurons point in similar directions, their pairwise distances become small, and the inverse-distance terms become large. If the neurons are more spread out on the hypersphere, the pairwise distances are larger and the resulting energy is different. The paper uses this quantity not as a generic regularizer, but as a structural summary of how a layer’s neurons are arranged relative to one another.

# Interpreting hyperspherical energy as pairwise relational structure among neurons

The main reason the paper cares about hyperspherical energy is that it captures relational structure rather than isolated parameter values. A single neuron does not tell the whole story; what matters is how every neuron in a layer is positioned relative to every other neuron. Because $\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})$ aggregates all pairwise comparisons, it reflects the collective geometry of the layer. In this sense, hyperspherical energy is not just a property of individual weights, but a property of the layer as an organized system.

This is why the paper describes hyperspherical energy as characterizing the pairwise neuron relationship on the unit hypersphere. The model’s internal structure is viewed through the angles among neurons, and those angles are treated as part of the knowledge stored in the pretrained network. If finetuning disrupts these relationships too much, the pretrained semantic structure can be damaged. So hyperspherical energy is used as a structural criterion: it measures whether the finetuned layer still resembles the pretrained layer in terms of its internal geometry.

# Why preserving pairwise angles may preserve pretrained semantics

The paper’s motivation for focusing on angular structure is that directions appear to carry semantic information. It cites prior observations that angular feature differences can characterize semantic gaps well, and it points to SphereNet as evidence that learning on the unit hypersphere can preserve or improve representational capacity. The paper also gives a toy autoencoder experiment on flower images, where angular information at test time can almost perfectly recover the input images, while magnitude information alone is not useful. The lesson is that the direction of a feature vector can be far more informative than its length.

This matters for text-to-image diffusion models because the downstream tasks in the paper are semantic tasks. Subject-driven generation requires the same subject to appear in new contexts, and controllable generation requires the output image to follow an extra control signal. In both cases, the model needs to change the generated content while retaining useful pretrained knowledge. The paper argues that modifying neuron directions is a natural way to alter semantics, but that such modification should be constrained. Preserving pairwise angles is therefore treated as a way of preserving the semantic organization already learned during pretraining.

# The OFT hypothesis: good finetuning should keep hyperspherical energy close to the pretrained model

From this perspective, the paper proposes a very direct hypothesis: a good finetuned model should have hyperspherical energy that stays close to the pretrained model’s hyperspherical energy. In its formal objective, OFT aims to minimize
\[
\left\|\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\right\|,
\]
where $\mathbf{W}^0$ denotes the pretrained weights. The goal is not to make the energy small in an absolute sense, but to preserve the pretrained layerwise structure as much as possible while still adapting to the downstream task.

This is an important distinction. The pretrained diffusion model already contains a useful arrangement of neuron directions, and that arrangement is part of why it can generate high-quality images. OFT assumes that this structure should not be discarded during finetuning. Instead, the model should be adapted in a way that respects the original geometry. In other words, the paper treats the pretrained hyperspherical energy as a structural template that finetuning should follow rather than overwrite.

# Why adding a regularizer alone does not guarantee preservation

A natural first idea would be to add a regularizer that penalizes changes in hyperspherical energy during finetuning. The paper explicitly discusses this possibility, but it argues that such a penalty does not guarantee that the energy difference will actually be well minimized. The issue is that a regularizer is still only an indirect encouragement. It can be outweighed by the main task objective, and it does not inherently force the model to stay within the same geometric structure as the pretrained network.

The paper’s point is not that regularization is useless, but that it is not principled enough for preserving structure. If the finetuning objective is only to keep the energy from drifting, optimization may still find solutions that satisfy the task while subtly damaging the geometry that supports pretrained semantic generation. OFT instead uses a construction that preserves the relevant pairwise relationships by design. That shift from “encouraging” preservation to “guaranteeing” preservation is one of the paper’s core contributions.

# Invariance of pairwise angular relationships under a shared orthogonal transform

The key mathematical fact behind OFT is that pairwise angular relationships are preserved if every neuron in a layer is transformed by the same orthogonal matrix. The paper writes the finetuned weight matrix as $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$, where $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$. Because $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal, it preserves inner products and lengths. Consequently, if the normalized neurons in the pretrained layer are transformed in this way, the distances between any pair of neurons remain the same.

This is easy to see geometrically. An orthogonal matrix acts like a rotation or reflection of the entire coordinate system. It does not distort angles; it only changes how the same geometric arrangement is represented in space. So if the same $\mathbf{R}$ is applied to all neurons in a layer, then the pairwise geometry on the hypersphere is preserved exactly, and so is the hyperspherical energy. The paper uses this invariance to motivate OFT: instead of directly optimizing a fragile similarity penalty, it learns a layer-shared orthogonal transformation that keeps the internal structure intact.

# Why minimizing hyperspherical energy is appropriate in some classification settings but not generative finetuning

The paper also makes a careful comparison with prior work on orthogonal over-parameterized training for classification. In that setting, the goal is to train a classifier from scratch while keeping hyperspherical energy small. This makes sense there because randomly initialized networks already have small hyperspherical energy in expectation, and lower energy is associated with better generalization. In a classification model, one often wants neurons to be less redundant and more evenly spread on the hypersphere.

Generative finetuning is a different problem. Here the model is not being trained from scratch; it is being adapted from a pretrained diffusion model that already encodes useful semantic structure. For such a model, driving the energy toward a new minimum is not desirable, because the pretrained arrangement itself is meaningful. The paper argues that minimizing hyperspherical energy could actually destroy the semantics that make the pretrained generator work well. That is why OFT does not try to make the energy small. It tries to keep the finetuned energy aligned with the pretrained energy, preserving structure instead of replacing it.
