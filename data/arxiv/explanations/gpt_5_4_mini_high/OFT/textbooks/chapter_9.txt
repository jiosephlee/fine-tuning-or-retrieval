\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 9: Subject-Driven Generation with OFT

# Task definition: preserving subject identity while changing context

Subject-driven generation asks a very specific question: if a model is shown only a few reference images of a subject, can it later generate that same subject in new scenes, poses, or styles when given a text prompt? The goal is not simply to reproduce the reference images. Instead, the model must keep the subject recognizable while also obeying the new prompt, which means it has to separate “what the subject is” from “what context the subject appears in.”

This task is a natural test for finetuning methods because it exposes the central tension in adaptation: if the finetuning is too weak, the model will not learn the subject; if it is too aggressive, it may overwrite the pretrained generative ability and produce unstable or distorted images. In the paper, this tension is exactly what OFT and COFT are designed to address. The method is meant to change the model just enough to learn the new subject-specific behavior, while preserving the semantic structure that makes the pretrained diffusion model useful in the first place.

# Using the DreamBooth training framework with OFT

For subject-driven generation, the paper adopts the DreamBooth framework as the training setting. DreamBooth uses a customized token and a few subject images, and finetunes a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model using a reconstruction loss together with a class-specific prior preservation loss. OFT does not replace this overall training objective. Instead, it changes how the model parameters are adapted inside that framework.

This distinction is important. If two methods use the same loss function, then differences in performance can be attributed much more cleanly to the finetuning strategy itself. In this paper, OFT keeps the DreamBooth supervision but updates the model through orthogonal transformations rather than through standard small-step weight updates. The constrained variant, COFT, adds an explicit bound on how far the finetuned model may move from the pretrained one. So the DreamBooth setup remains the same, but the geometry of finetuning is redesigned.

# Baselines: DreamBooth and LoRA

The paper compares OFT and COFT mainly against DreamBooth and LoRA. DreamBooth is the most direct baseline because it is the standard subject-driven finetuning method in this setting. It adapts the diffusion model using the same basic training supervision, but it relies on conventional finetuning with a small learning rate. LoRA is the other key baseline. It is a parameter-efficient adaptation method that updates the model through a low-rank weight modification.

These two baselines represent two common finetuning philosophies. DreamBooth is flexible but can be unstable if the finetuning runs too long or becomes too aggressive. LoRA is more structured and parameter-efficient, but the paper shows that its ability to preserve identity and follow prompts is weaker in this task. OFT is positioned between these extremes: it is parameter-efficient like LoRA, but it preserves the pretrained structure through an orthogonal update, which the paper argues leads to better stability and better retention of semantic knowledge.

# Training setup, iteration schedules, and fairness of comparison

The experiments use Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained text-to-image model. For subject-driven generation, the paper generally follows the DreamBooth setup. For fairness, all methods use the same loss function as in DreamBooth, and OFT or COFT is applied to the same layer where LoRA is used. This is an important design choice because it keeps the comparison focused on the adaptation method rather than on differences in architecture placement.

The paper also follows the original papers for DreamBooth and LoRA and uses their best hyperparameter setups. That means the comparison is not meant to disadvantage the baselines by giving them weak training settings. In the qualitative analysis, the paper also avoids cherry-picking by using the same finetuned model for each method when generating examples. These choices matter because subject-driven generation can be sensitive to training details, so a fair benchmark must control as many factors as possible.

# Convergence and stability across short and long finetuning runs

One of the clearest findings in the subject-driven generation experiments is that OFT and COFT converge stably and quickly. After 400 iterations, both OFT variants and DreamBooth achieve good control, but LoRA fails to preserve the subject identity. The short-run result already suggests that OFT is learning the target subject more reliably than the low-rank baseline.

The longer-run behavior makes the contrast even sharper. After 2000 iterations, DreamBooth starts to generate collapsed images, and LoRA still fails to track the intended subject content correctly. In contrast, both OFT and COFT remain stable and continue to produce consistent subject control. The paper emphasizes that this insensitivity to the exact number of finetuning iterations is practically valuable, because it reduces the burden of tuning the training schedule separately for different subjects. In particular, COFT with a proper $\epsilon$ makes both the learning rate and the iteration number easier to set.

# Failure modes of baselines: identity loss, prompt mismatch, and collapsed images

The subject-driven generation results expose three different failure modes. The first is identity loss, where the model no longer produces the same subject after finetuning. The paper shows that LoRA is especially prone to this problem. In one example, LoRA completely loses the subject identity in the bowl case, and in another it turns a yellow shirt into yellow fur. These are not minor visual imperfections; they are signs that the model has failed to keep the subject-specific information intact.

The second failure mode is prompt mismatch. DreamBooth can preserve the subject more faithfully than LoRA, but it often fails to follow the text prompt accurately. In the bowl example, the paper notes that DreamBooth keeps the subject identity but does not generate the image according to the prompt. The third failure mode is collapse under long training, where the images deteriorate as finetuning continues. DreamBooth begins to produce collapsed images after enough iterations. OFT and COFT avoid these problems much better, which is why the paper presents them as a more stable route to subject-driven adaptation.

# Quantitative metrics: DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, and LPIPS

To evaluate subject-driven generation quantitatively, the paper uses four metrics that each capture a different aspect of quality. DINO and CLIP-I measure subject fidelity. CLIP-I is defined as the average pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between generated and real images, while DINO is similar except that it uses ViT S/16 DINO embeddings. These metrics ask whether the generated image still looks like the reference subject.

The other two metrics assess different properties. CLIP-T measures text prompt fidelity by computing the average cosine similarity between the text prompt and the generated image in CLIP space. LPIPS is used to evaluate sample diversity by measuring the average LPIPS cosine similarity between generated images of the same subject with the same prompt. Together, these metrics let the paper check whether a method preserves identity, follows the prompt, and still allows varied outputs rather than producing one fixed template.

# Why repeated runs over multiple random seeds matter

The paper repeats finetuning of the same pretrained model across 30 different random seeds. The purpose is to minimize randomness in the comparison. This is a sensible choice because finetuning methods can be affected by stochastic effects in initialization, optimization, and sampling, and a single run might not tell the whole story.

By averaging over many seeds, the paper makes its quantitative comparison more trustworthy. If one method is truly more stable, that should show up not just in one fortunate run but across repeated trials. This is especially relevant for subject-driven generation, where the training objective is relatively narrow and a small change in optimization behavior can make a visible difference in whether the subject identity is preserved or lost.

# Qualitative comparison: subject fidelity, prompt following, and diversity

The qualitative results reinforce the quantitative story. The paper shows randomly selected examples from each method, and for each method it chooses the model with the best validation CLIP metrics. Importantly, the prompts are not separately optimized for the final images. This means the examples reflect the actual general behavior of each finetuned model rather than hand-tuned prompt engineering.

In these comparisons, OFT and COFT consistently preserve the subject while also following the prompt more accurately. DreamBooth is often better at maintaining the subject than LoRA, but it can miss the intended prompt. LoRA, by contrast, often fails to preserve identity at all. The paper concludes that OFT and COFT achieve both subject preservation and controllability at the same time, which is exactly the combination that subject-driven generation requires.

# Practical lessons on hyperparameter robustness and sample efficiency

A key practical lesson from the subject-driven generation experiments is that OFT is much less sensitive to the number of training iterations than the baselines. The paper notes that one can directly choose a relatively large iteration number without carefully tuning it, and OFT still performs well. COFT goes one step further by making the deviation from the pretrained model explicit through the $\epsilon$ constraint, which makes the training dynamics even easier to control. This robustness is valuable in practice because it reduces the amount of trial-and-error required to adapt the model to a new subject.

The paper also reports better sample efficiency: OFT converges well with a much smaller number of training images and epochs. That matters because subject-driven generation is often intended for low-data settings, where the user may only have a handful of reference photos. In that regime, a finetuning method is useful only if it can learn quickly without destroying the pretrained model. The paper’s results suggest that OFT, and especially COFT, strike that balance better than the standard alternatives.
