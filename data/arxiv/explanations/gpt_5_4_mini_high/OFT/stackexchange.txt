\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### In OFT, what exactly counts as a "neuron" in the weight matrix $\bm{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$?
Question:
I'm reading the OFT paper, and for a fully connected layer they write $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \ldots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ with output $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x}$. So it seems each column $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is called a neuron. My confusion is that many implementations store linear weights in the opposite shape, and for convolution or attention layers it is even less obvious what the "neuron" object is. When the paper says it preserves pairwise relationships among neurons, should I interpret that as preserving angles between output-channel weight vectors, attention projection columns, or something else?
Answer:
In the paper, “neuron” means the weight vector associated with one output unit of a layer, not an activation value. In their fully connected example they write $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and $\bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top \bm{x}$, so the $i$-th output coordinate is $z_i=\bm{w}_i^\top \bm{x}$. Under that convention, each column $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is one “neuron,” and the hyperspherical energy is computed from the normalized versions of those column vectors. The key point is that OFT preserves pairwise angles because if $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, then for any two neurons
$$
(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i)^\top(\bm{R}\bm{w}_j)=\bm{w}_i^\top \bm{w}_j,
$$
so all cosine similarities within the layer stay unchanged.

If your implementation stores linear weights in the usual PyTorch shape $\text{[out\_features, in\_features]}$, then it is just the transpose of the paper’s notation. In that case, the “neurons” are the rows of the stored tensor rather than the columns. So the terminology is about the mathematical object, not the storage layout.

For convolution, the same idea applies after reshaping: each output channel’s kernel, flattened into a vector, plays the role of a neuron. That is also consistent with the paper’s remark that OFT is suited to convolution layers and that different block-diagonal choices correspond to transforming channels in different ways. For attention layers, the paper treats the attention projection weights as linear layers and applies OFT to those weights; again, the relevant “neurons” are the per-output weight vectors of that projection matrix after whatever flattening/reshaping is needed.

So the safest interpretation is: when the paper says it preserves pairwise relationships among neurons, read that as preserving pairwise angles/cosine similarities among the weight vectors of a layer, one vector per output unit or output channel, in the paper’s matrix convention. It is not referring to activations, and it is not a special notion unique to attention.

### Why does left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserve pairwise neuron angles and hyperspherical energy?
Question:
The main OFT update is $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. The paper says this provably preserves the pairwise hyperspherical similarity between neurons in the same layer, and therefore preserves the hyperspherical energy. I think this should follow from orthogonal matrices preserving inner products and norms, but I am not fully seeing the derivation when the energy is defined as $\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \ne j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$. Can someone show explicitly why $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0$ implies each pairwise term stays unchanged?
Answer:
Yes — the preservation is basically a consequence of the fact that an orthogonal matrix is an isometry of $\mathbb{R}^d$. In the paper’s setting, the weight matrix has columns $\bm{w}_i$ (the neurons), and OFT replaces each column by the same left multiplication $\bm{w}_i = \bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0$ with $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. Since $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, it preserves lengths and inner products.

The first step is to check what happens after normalization. Because orthogonal matrices preserve norms, we have
$$
\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0\| = \|\bm{w}_i^0\|.
$$
So the normalized neuron becomes
$$
\hat{\bm{w}}_i
= \frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}
= \frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0\|}
= \frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0}{\|\bm{w}_i^0\|}
= \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0.
$$
This is the identity you wrote down.

Now look at a pair of normalized neurons. Their distance after the transform is
$$
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|
= \|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|
= \|\bm{R}(\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \hat{\bm{w}}_j^0)\|.
$$
Since $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, it preserves Euclidean norm:
$$
\|\bm{R}\bm{x}\|^2
= (\bm{R}\bm{x})^\top (\bm{R}\bm{x})
= \bm{x}^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}\bm{x}
= \bm{x}^\top \bm{x}
= \|\bm{x}\|^2.
$$
Applying this with $\bm{x} = \hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \hat{\bm{w}}_j^0$ gives
$$
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|
= \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|.
$$
Therefore each summand in the hyperspherical energy is unchanged:
$$
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}
=
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|^{-1}.
$$
Summing over all $i \neq j$ immediately gives
$$
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) = \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}^0).
$$

If you want the angle viewpoint, it is the same statement in cosine form. For unit vectors,
$$
\|\hat{\bm{u}} - \hat{\bm{v}}\|^2 = 2 - 2\hat{\bm{u}}^\top \hat{\bm{v}},
$$
so preserving distances is equivalent to preserving inner products, hence angles. Orthogonal matrices preserve inner products because
$$
(\bm{R}\bm{u})^\top(\bm{R}\bm{v}) = \bm{u}^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}\bm{v} = \bm{u}^\top \bm{v}.
$$
That is why the paper can say OFT “provably preserves pairwise hyperspherical similarity” and therefore preserves hyperspherical energy: the whole energy is built only from pairwise distances/angles on the sphere, and orthogonal transforms do not change those quantities.

So the short version is: left-multiplication by $\bm{R}$ rotates or reflects the entire set of neurons together, and rigid motions do not change pairwise angles or distances. Because hyperspherical energy depends only on those pairwise distances, it stays exactly the same.

### If OFT applies the same orthogonal transform to all neurons in a layer, why is it not just a change of basis?
Question:
I may be misunderstanding something basic. If every neuron in a layer is transformed by the same orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, then it feels like we are just rotating the coordinate system. In linear algebra, a pure change of basis usually should not change the represented function if the rest of the network is adjusted consistently. But OFT only changes one layer to $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and the paper claims this meaningfully changes generation behavior. What is the right intuition here? Is OFT functionally equivalent to rotating the input features to that layer by $\bm{R}^\top$, or is there a better way to think about why the model output changes?
Answer:
Your intuition is partly right, but only at the level of one isolated linear map. For a single layer, if $W = R W^0$ with $R^\top R = I$, then

$$
z = W^\top x = (R W^0)^\top x = (W^0)^\top R^\top x.
$$

So yes, algebraically, applying $R$ to the weights is equivalent to rotating the layer input by $R^\top$ before using the pretrained weights. But that is not a “mere change of basis” for the network as a whole, because the rest of the network is not rotated consistently. A true change of basis would require transforming the adjacent representations and compensating in neighboring layers so that the overall input-output function stays exactly the same. OFT does not do that; it changes only the selected layer’s weights, so the network function changes.

That distinction is exactly why OFT is useful. The paper’s point is not that orthogonal finetuning preserves the model’s function, but that it preserves the *geometry* of the pretrained neurons in that layer: pairwise angles, and therefore hyperspherical energy, stay unchanged because orthogonal transforms preserve inner products. In other words, OFT moves the whole set of neuron vectors as a rigid body on the hypersphere. This keeps the pretrained relational structure intact while still changing how the layer responds to the incoming features.

A good way to think about it is: the layer is learning a new orientation of the same “semantic axes,” not just renaming coordinates. Since the input activations $x$ coming from earlier layers are fixed, changing the orientation of the weight vectors changes the dot products $w_i^\top x$, hence the activations, hence the generated image. So the model output changes meaningfully even though the internal angle structure of that layer is preserved.

So the short answer is: OFT is not functionally equivalent to a harmless basis change of the whole network. It is an orthogonally constrained reparameterization of one layer that preserves the pretrained layer geometry but intentionally alters the layer’s interaction with its input, which is why generation behavior changes.

### How strong is the connection between "angular information" and semantics in this paper?
Question:
The paper motivates OFT by arguing that neuron directions carry semantic information, and it supports this with a toy autoencoder experiment where angular information can almost reconstruct the image while magnitude information cannot. I understand the experiment qualitatively, but I am not sure how much of that transfers to pretrained diffusion model weights. Is the claim that semantics are literally encoded mainly in the angles of weight vectors, or only that angular changes are a good low-level proxy for semantic change during finetuning? I am confused about how strong this assumption is supposed to be.
Answer:
The paper is **not** claiming that semantics are literally stored only in the angles of weight vectors. The stronger, literal claim is much weaker than that: it says that **preserving the angular geometry of neurons is a good proxy for preserving the pretrained model’s generative semantics during finetuning**.

The distinction matters. The toy autoencoder and the Fourier-phase analogy are used as **motivation**, not as a proof about diffusion-model weights. The authors are essentially arguing that if you change a layer too freely, you risk destroying the internal geometric relationships the pretrained model learned. Since an orthogonal transform preserves all pairwise angles between neurons, OFT keeps that relational structure intact while still allowing the model to adapt. In other words, the method is built around the idea that the **relative direction pattern** of neurons is an important structural carrier of meaning, not that all meaning is mathematically encoded only in direction.

You can see this careful wording throughout the paper. They say things like “we **hypothesize** that a good finetuned model should have a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy,” and “this phenomenon **suggests** that changing the neuron directions is the key.” That is a heuristic supported by empirical evidence, not a theoretical identity. Their actual guarantee is only that orthogonal finetuning preserves hyperspherical energy, i.e. the pairwise angular relationships among neurons. The claim that this preserves “semantic generation ability” is then validated experimentally on subject-driven and controllable generation.

So the right way to read the paper is: **angular information is treated as a useful low-level structural proxy for semantics, and preserving it helps avoid catastrophic drift during finetuning**. It is not saying that semantics are exclusively or literally encoded in angles, and the paper does not prove such a statement for pretrained diffusion models. In fact, the existence of COFT and the re-scaled OFT variant shows the authors do not think angle alone is the whole story; magnitude and distance from the pretrained weights still matter for stability.

In short, the paper’s assumption is **moderately strong as an empirical design principle, but not a strong theoretical claim about where semantics live in the weights**.

### Why is hyperspherical energy defined as $\sum_{i \ne j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ instead of a direct cosine-similarity quantity?
Question:
The paper discusses hyperspherical similarity using cosine similarity, but then defines the hyperspherical energy of a layer as $\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \ne j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$. I am confused about why this particular inverse-distance form is used. Since for unit vectors the Euclidean distance and cosine angle are related, is this just a monotone reparameterization, or does it have a special property that matters for the theory? Also, what happens numerically if two neurons become very close and a term becomes large?
Answer:
The short answer is that the paper is not using $\sum_{i \neq j} \langle \hat{\bm w}_i,\hat{\bm w}_j\rangle$ as a generic “similarity score”; it is using the standard hyperspherical energy from the cited hyperspherical-energy literature, where the natural quantity is a repulsive potential on the unit sphere. For unit vectors, these two viewpoints are closely related, but not identical.

If $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between $\hat{\bm w}_i$ and $\hat{\bm w}_j$, then
$$
\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\|^2
= 2 - 2\cos\theta_{ij},
$$
so
$$
\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\|^{-1}
= \bigl(2(1-\cos\theta_{ij})\bigr)^{-1/2}.
$$
So yes, on the unit sphere it is a monotone function of cosine similarity: smaller angle means larger energy. But it is not just a harmless reparameterization. The inverse-distance form is nonlinear and heavily emphasizes very close pairs. That matters because hyperspherical energy is meant to measure how “crowded” the neurons are on the sphere, not just their average correlation. A direct sum of cosines can cancel positive and negative terms and is much less sensitive to a pair of neurons nearly collapsing onto each other.

This is exactly why the inverse-distance form is useful in the paper’s theory. OFT does not try to minimize the raw hyperspherical energy; it tries to preserve the pretrained layer’s energy exactly by applying a shared orthogonal transform, and orthogonal maps preserve all pairwise Euclidean distances and angles. So the key property is invariance under the same orthogonal transformation, not merely monotonicity in cosine. The inverse-distance form is the canonical “energy” that goes with the hypersphere geometry, and it matches the intuition that nearby neurons should contribute a very large penalty.

On your numerical question: if two normalized neurons become extremely close, the corresponding term becomes very large, and if they coincide exactly, it diverges. That is not a problem for OFT as presented, because the method is not optimizing $\mathrm{HE}(\bm W)$ directly; it is preserving $\mathrm{HE}(\bm W)$ by construction through orthogonal parameterization. So the singularity is more of a conceptual feature of the measure than a training instability in this paper. If one were to use this quantity as an actual loss, one would typically need an $\varepsilon$-clamp in the denominator to avoid division by zero.

So the right way to read the paper is: hyperspherical energy is a geometric descriptor of the layer’s pairwise angular structure, inverse-distance is the standard choice for that descriptor, and the orthogonal finetuning result works because orthogonal transforms leave that structure unchanged.

### Does preserving hyperspherical energy really preserve semantics, or is it only preserving one scalar summary?
Question:
One part of the paper says a good finetuned model should have minimal difference in hyperspherical energy from the pretrained model. But hyperspherical energy, as defined here, is just one scalar sum over all neuron pairs. Many different neuron configurations could share the same energy. At the same time, the actual OFT parameterization $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ is much stronger, because it preserves every pairwise angle exactly. So I am confused about what the real hypothesis is: does the paper rely on preserving the scalar $\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W})$, or on preserving the full pairwise angular geometry induced by a shared orthogonal transform?
Answer:
Your intuition is right that $\mathrm{HE}(\bm W)$ by itself is only a scalar, so it cannot uniquely determine the neuron configuration. Many different weight matrices can share the same hyperspherical energy. The paper is not really claiming that this scalar, taken in isolation, “preserves semantics.” Rather, $\mathrm{HE}$ is being used as a convenient summary of a much stronger geometric property: the pairwise angular structure of the neurons.

The key point is that OFT does not merely regularize $\mathrm{HE}(\bm W)$ directly. The actual parameterization is $\bm W=\bm R\bm W^0$ with $\bm R^\top \bm R=\bm I$. That is much stronger than “same energy,” because an orthogonal transform preserves every pairwise inner product between normalized neurons:
$$
(R\hat{\bm w}_i)^\top(R\hat{\bm w}_j)=\hat{\bm w}_i^\top \hat{\bm w}_j.
$$
So all pairwise angles are unchanged, and therefore the hyperspherical energy, which is just a function of those pairwise distances/similarities, is unchanged as a consequence. In other words, the paper’s real hypothesis is not “a good model is characterized by one scalar,” but “the semantic content of a pretrained layer is largely encoded in its pairwise angular geometry, and preserving that geometry helps preserve pretrained generative ability.” The scalar energy is just a compact way to express that geometry at the layer level.

The paper actually acknowledges the weakness of a scalar regularizer: it says a naive penalty to keep hyperspherical energy unchanged does not guarantee the energy difference can be minimized well. That is exactly why they introduce the orthogonal reparameterization instead. So the logic is: semantics are believed to live in the angular relationships; $\mathrm{HE}$ is a proxy for that structure; and the orthogonal constraint is the mechanism that preserves the full structure, not just the scalar summary.

So the short answer is: no, the paper is not relying on preserving only one scalar summary. It uses $\mathrm{HE}$ as a diagnostic and motivation, but the substantive assumption is preservation of the full pairwise angular geometry via a shared orthogonal transform. The semantic claim is empirical and hypothesis-driven, not a theorem that “equal hyperspherical energy implies equal semantics.”

### What expressivity is lost when OFT restricts updates to the form $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$?
Question:
Compared with standard finetuning, OFT only allows multiplicative updates through an orthogonal matrix. This seems much more restrictive than arbitrary additive updates, and even more structured than LoRA. I understand why that may help preserve pretrained behavior, but I cannot tell what kinds of downstream changes are impossible under this constraint. For example, if the target task requires changing the relative angles between neurons or moving weights outside the span implied by $\bm{W}^0$, does OFT fundamentally fail? Is there a clean way to describe the function class reachable by OFT versus LoRA?
Answer:
The cleanest way to think about OFT in this paper is that it does **not** learn an arbitrary new weight matrix; it learns a new **coordinate system** for the pretrained one. If a layer has pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ and OFT uses $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I}$, then the entire layer stays in the orthogonal orbit of $\bm{W}^0$. That has a very concrete consequence: all pairwise inner products between neurons are preserved, because

$$
\bm{W}^\top \bm{W} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{W}^0.
$$

So OFT preserves the whole Gram matrix of the layer, which means it preserves neuron norms, pairwise angles, hyperspherical energy, rank, and singular values. In the paper’s language, it is literally preventing the layer’s hyperspherical geometry from changing, and only allowing a global rotation or reflection of the neuron vectors.

So yes: if the downstream task truly requires changing those relationships, plain OFT cannot represent it. For example, it cannot selectively make two neurons more correlated or less correlated, cannot rescale one neuron relative to another, and cannot move to a weight matrix with a different Gram matrix. More broadly, it cannot leave the equivalence class of $\bm{W}^0$ under rigid orthogonal transformations. That is the expressivity it gives up.

At the same time, that restriction is also the point of the method. The paper argues that for these diffusion finetuning tasks, much of the useful semantic adaptation comes from changing **directions** rather than destroying the pretrained angular structure. So OFT is not trying to be universal; it is trying to make a controlled change that preserves the pretrained model’s internal geometry.

Compared with LoRA, the difference is really “geometry-preserving versus low-rank perturbing.” LoRA uses $\bm{W}=\bm{W}^0+\Delta \bm{W}$ with $\operatorname{rank}(\Delta \bm{W})$ small, so it can alter angles and norms in a much more unconstrained way, but only through a low-rank additive update. OFT, by contrast, can be full-rank as a matrix transform, but it is constrained to be orthogonal, so it cannot change the layer’s internal pairwise geometry at all. That means the two methods are not nested: LoRA is less geometrically rigid, while OFT is less free in the kinds of deformations it can express.

One small nuance from the paper is that their practical parameterization can be even more restrictive than full OFT, because they use block-diagonal orthogonal matrices for efficiency. And when they want a bit more flexibility, they introduce re-scaled OFT, which adds diagonal magnitude parameters; that can change norms but still leaves the angular structure intact.

So the short answer is: **plain OFT can only realize weight matrices orthogonally equivalent to $\bm{W}^0$, which means it preserves all pairwise neuron angles and norms. Any downstream change that needs altering that geometry is outside its function class.**

### Why does the paper say the Cayley parameterization only gives determinant $1$, and does excluding reflections matter?
Question:
The paper parameterizes the orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ for a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$, and then says this only produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$. My confusion is twofold. First, why exactly does this parameterization exclude determinant $-1$ matrices? Second, since the earlier discussion allowed both rotations and reflections as valid ways to preserve hyperspherical energy, what is lost by restricting to the special orthogonal group? Could there be finetuning directions that require a reflection?
Answer:
The short answer is that the Cayley map is a parameterization of the connected component of the orthogonal group containing the identity, namely the special orthogonal group $SO(d)$. That is why it produces $\det = 1$ but never $\det = -1$.

If $\bm Q^\top=-\bm Q$ and
$$
\bm R = (\bm I+\bm Q)(\bm I-\bm Q)^{-1},
$$
then $\bm R$ is orthogonal, and in fact
$$
\det(\bm R)=\frac{\det(\bm I+\bm Q)}{\det(\bm I-\bm Q)}.
$$
Because $\bm Q$ is skew-symmetric, $\det(\bm I+\bm Q)=\det((\bm I+\bm Q)^\top)=\det(\bm I-\bm Q)$, so the ratio is $1$. Intuitively, the Cayley transform is a continuous map starting at $\bm Q=\bm 0 \mapsto \bm R=\bm I$. Since determinant is continuous and cannot jump from $1$ to $-1$ without leaving the orthogonal group, this construction stays entirely in $SO(d)$ and never reaches the $\det = -1$ component of $O(d)$.

As for whether excluding reflections matters, in the context of this paper the answer is basically “no.” The paper’s key invariant is hyperspherical energy, and that is preserved by any orthogonal matrix, whether $\det(\bm R)=1$ or $\det(\bm R)=-1$. So from the standpoint of preserving pairwise angles, both rotations and reflections are equally valid. The paper explicitly notes that the minimum of the energy difference can be achieved if $\bm W=\bm R\bm W^0$ for an orthogonal $\bm R$, and it parenthetically interprets $\det(\bm R)=\pm 1$ as rotation or reflection.

What the Cayley parameterization loses is only the “improper” orthogonal transformations, i.e. the reflections/orientation-reversing part of $O(d)$. Geometrically, that means it cannot flip the handedness of the coordinate system. But for OFT that does not seem to be a useful degree of freedom: the method is initialized at the identity and is meant to make a smooth, stable finetuning from the pretrained model. In that setting, staying in the identity component is natural, and the authors state that this limitation “does not affect the performance in practice.” So yes, in principle there are orthogonal directions outside $SO(d)$, but the paper’s empirical conclusion is that they are not needed for the downstream tasks they study.

If you wanted reflections anyway, you would need a different parameterization or an extra discrete reflection factor. But the paper’s point is that the benefit comes from orthogonality itself, not from access to the $\det = -1$ component.

### How justified is the COFT step that replaces $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon$ with a constraint on $\bm{Q}$?
Question:
In COFT, the paper adds a deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon$ and then argues that, using the Cayley transform and a Neumann-series approximation, this can be turned into a constraint $\|\bm{Q}\| \le \epsilon'$ on the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$. I am struggling with this step. Since $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ is nonlinear in $\bm{Q}$, is the new constraint only approximate? Under what norm and under what conditions on $\bm{Q}$ does this really guarantee that $\bm{R}$ stays within the desired radius of the identity?
Answer:
Strictly speaking, that step in COFT is a surrogate, not an exact equivalence as written in the paper.

What the paper uses is the Cayley parametrization
$$
R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1},
$$
with $Q^\top=-Q$, and then the Neumann-series expansion
$$
(I-Q)^{-1}=I+Q+Q^2+\cdots,
$$
which is valid when $\|Q\|<1$ in a submultiplicative operator norm, typically the spectral norm $\|\cdot\|_2$. Plugging this in gives
$$
R = I + 2Q + O(Q^2),
$$
so near $Q=0$ the map $Q\mapsto R$ is locally linear and a small ball around $0$ in $Q$-space corresponds to a small neighborhood of $I$ in $R$-space. That is the justification the authors are relying on.

If you want a rigorous bound, the key identity is
$$
R-I = 2Q(I-Q)^{-1}.
$$
Hence, for any operator norm,
$$
\|R-I\| \le 2\|Q\|\cdot \|(I-Q)^{-1}\|.
$$
If $\|Q\|<1$, then Neumann gives $\|(I-Q)^{-1}\|\le \frac{1}{1-\|Q\|}$, so
$$
\|R-I\| \le \frac{2\|Q\|}{1-\|Q\|}.
$$
Therefore a sufficient condition for $\|R-I\|\le \epsilon$ is
$$
\|Q\| \le \frac{\epsilon}{2+\epsilon}.
$$
So the replacement $\|R-I\|\le \epsilon \Rightarrow \|Q\|\le \epsilon'$ is indeed justified as a sufficient, local control condition, but not an exact algebraic equivalence unless you specify the norm and the mapping between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon'$.

Because $Q$ is skew-symmetric, you can say a bit more in the spectral norm. In that case the Cayley transform is exact on each $2\times 2$ block, and one gets
$$
\|R-I\|_2 = \frac{2\|Q\|_2}{\sqrt{1+\|Q\|_2^2}}.
$$
So in the $\|\cdot\|_2$ norm, small $\|Q\|_2$ really does mean $R$ stays close to $I$, and the exact threshold corresponding to $\|R-I\|_2\le \epsilon$ is
$$
\|Q\|_2 \le \frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{4-\epsilon^2}}, \qquad 0\le \epsilon<2.
$$

So the short answer is: the paper’s step is an approximation motivated by the local behavior of the Cayley map, and the clean rigorous version is in the operator/spectral norm with $\|Q\|<1$. The authors are not claiming a global exact equivalence; they are using a practical constrained parameterization that keeps $R$ near the identity when $Q$ is projected to be small.

### Why does a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix still preserve hyperspherical energy of the full neurons?
Question:
To reduce parameters, the paper replaces a full orthogonal matrix with a block-diagonal one, $\bm{R} = \mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \ldots, \bm{R}_r)$. I understand that such an $\bm{R}$ is still orthogonal if each block is orthogonal, but intuitively it feels like it only rotates coordinates inside each block and never mixes features across blocks. So why does this still preserve the hyperspherical energy of the whole neuron vectors exactly? Is the only effect of block structure to reduce expressivity, while the pairwise-angle preservation remains mathematically exact?
Answer:
Yes — in the paper, the hyperspherical energy is preserved exactly as long as the transformation applied to the whole layer is orthogonal, and a block-diagonal matrix with orthogonal blocks is still orthogonal as a matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$.

The key point is that OFT does not rotate each neuron independently; it applies the same left-multiplication $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ to every neuron vector in the layer, where each neuron is a column of $\bm{W}^0$. If $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, then for any two neurons $\bm{w}_i$ and $\bm{w}_j$ we have
$$
(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i)^\top(\bm{R}\bm{w}_j)=\bm{w}_i^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}\bm{w}_j=\bm{w}_i^\top \bm{w}_j,
$$
and also $\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i\|=\|\bm{w}_i\|$. So the normalized vectors satisfy
$$
\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}_i}=\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i,
$$
and therefore pairwise angular relationships are unchanged:
$$
\|\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}_i}-\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}_j}\|
=\|\bm{R}(\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j)\|
=\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|.
$$
Since the paper defines hyperspherical energy as a sum over terms of the form $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$, every term in the sum is unchanged, so the whole energy is unchanged exactly.

The block-diagonal structure only restricts which orthogonal transformations you can represent. It reduces expressivity and parameter count, because you are only allowed to rotate within each block and not mix coordinates across blocks, but it does not weaken the geometric invariance. In other words, “block-diagonal” changes the set of admissible orthogonal maps; it does not stop an admissible map from being an isometry. That is why the paper says that “the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal, so there is no sacrifice in preserving hyperspherical energy.”

So the short answer is: the preservation is exact because hyperspherical energy is invariant under any orthogonal transformation, and a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix is still an orthogonal transformation. The only thing the block structure sacrifices is flexibility, not the invariance itself.

### Is the OFT-versus-LoRA parameter-efficiency comparison in the paper using a fair scaling regime?
Question:
The paper argues that if $r = r' = \alpha d$, then OFT can have parameter complexity $\mathcal{O}(d)$ while LoRA has $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$. I am having trouble interpreting this comparison, because in practice LoRA ranks are often kept as small constants rather than scaling linearly with $d$. On the other hand, OFT's block count $r$ is also a structural hyperparameter rather than a direct analogue of rank. Is there a principled way to compare the two methods at equal expressive capacity, or is this complexity argument mainly illustrative?
Answer:
The short answer is: the paper’s $\mathcal{O}(d)$ versus $\mathcal{O}(d^2+dn)$ comparison is mainly an illustrative scaling argument, not a rigorous “equal expressive capacity” equivalence.

What the authors do is pick a regime where both structural hyperparameters grow with width, namely $r=r'=\alpha d$, and then compare the resulting parameter counts. For LoRA, the update has the form $BA$ with rank at most $r'$, so the number of trainable parameters is $r'(d+n)$. If $r'=\alpha d$, this becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2+dn)$. For OFT, the trainable object is an orthogonal transform $R$ parameterized in block-diagonal form, and the number of parameters is roughly $d^2/r$; if $r=\alpha d$, that becomes $\mathcal{O}(d)$. So the point is that, under that particular scaling choice, OFT’s parameter count grows much more slowly.

But that is not the same thing as saying the two methods have comparable expressive capacity under the same hyperparameter values. In fact, they are quite different families of functions: LoRA changes the weight matrix by a low-rank additive update, whereas OFT applies a multiplicative orthogonal transform that preserves pairwise angles and hyperspherical energy. Because one is a rank-constrained additive perturbation and the other is a geometry-preserving reparameterization, there is no canonical one-to-one mapping between “block count” $r$ and LoRA rank $r'$.

So if your concern is fairness, you are right to be cautious. In practice, LoRA is usually used with small constant ranks, and in that regime the asymptotic story can look very different. The paper does not claim that the scaling regime $r=r'=\alpha d$ is the only fair way to compare them; rather, it uses it to make a simple complexity comparison and then backs this up empirically by applying OFT and LoRA to the same layers and reporting concrete parameter counts and performance. For example, they explicitly give a $128 \times 128$ case where LoRA with $r'=8$ has $2048$ trainable parameters, while OFT with $r=8$ has $960$.

So the principled takeaway is not “OFT always dominates LoRA at equal capacity,” but rather “these are two different parameter-efficient adaptation mechanisms, and their efficiency should be compared either at a matched parameter budget or by downstream performance.” The paper’s asymptotic complexity statement is best read as a heuristic illustrating that OFT can be very compact when the block structure is chosen appropriately, not as a theorem of equal expressive power.

### Why should OFT converge faster than LoRA if both are parameter-efficient finetuning methods?
Question:
The paper repeatedly claims that OFT and COFT converge faster and more smoothly than LoRA and other baselines. The explanation given is that changing neuron directions is more semantically effective, and that optimization on a smooth hypersphere manifold may be easier. I can follow this intuitively, but I do not see a concrete optimization reason. Is there a more formal argument for why orthogonal updates should improve conditioning or gradient flow here, or is this mostly an empirical observation from the experiments?
Answer:
The short answer is that the paper does **not** give a formal convergence-rate theorem showing that OFT must converge faster than LoRA. The claim is mainly a **geometric argument plus empirical evidence**.

What the paper does say is that OFT constrains each adapted layer to have the form $W = RW^0$ with $R^\top R = I$. That means the finetuning step is an **orthogonal transformation**, so it preserves norms and pairwise angles. In the language of the paper, it preserves hyperspherical energy, which they use as a proxy for the semantic structure of the pretrained model. The idea is that, if the important information is already encoded in the directions and angular relations of neurons, then rotating those directions is a more direct and less destructive way to adapt the model than adding an arbitrary low-rank update as in LoRA.

From an optimization standpoint, the closest formal statement you can make from the paper is that orthogonal maps are **isometries**. They do not introduce extra scaling or anisotropy, so they do not worsen the layer’s conditioning in the way a general unconstrained update might. In that sense, OFT keeps the pretrained representation on a smooth, geometry-preserving manifold, which is why the authors say it has a better optimization landscape. But the paper does **not** prove a sharper gradient-flow or conditioning bound, so the “faster convergence” claim should be read as an **empirical consequence of the orthogonal inductive bias**, not as a rigorous theorem.

This is also why COFT is even more stable: by adding $\|R-I\| \leq \epsilon$, it explicitly limits how far the orthogonal update can move away from the pretrained model, which the paper argues makes training smoother still. So the formal part is the invariance under orthogonal transforms; the faster convergence itself is mostly supported by the experiments and by the intuition that preserving pretrained geometry makes finetuning easier.

### Why does re-scaled OFT still count as preserving the important structure if it allows arbitrary positive per-neuron scaling?
Question:
The paper proposes a re-scaled OFT variant with forward pass $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{D}$ is a positive diagonal matrix. I understand that scaling each neuron does not change hyperspherical energy because normalization removes magnitudes. But from the network's forward-pass perspective, changing magnitudes can still strongly affect activations and therefore generation. So I am confused about the interpretation: if the key preservation property is unchanged hyperspherical energy, why does adding unrestricted scaling not undermine the same stability argument?
Answer:
In the paper’s terminology, the “important structure” that OFT is trying to preserve is not the exact forward map of the network, but the angular geometry of the neurons, as measured by hyperspherical energy. That is why the authors can say that a positive diagonal scaling $\bm{D}$ does not break the preservation claim: hyperspherical energy is defined after normalizing each neuron, so the magnitudes introduced by $\bm{D}$ disappear when you form $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$. In other words, if you replace $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$ with $s_i>0$, then the directions of the neuron vectors are unchanged, only their radii are changed, and the pairwise cosine similarities remain the same.

That said, your concern is exactly right in a broader functional sense: changing magnitudes can absolutely change activations and therefore generation. The paper does not claim otherwise. Re-scaled OFT is presented as a more flexible extension, not as something with the same strict stability guarantee as vanilla OFT. The rigorous invariance result in the paper is about preserving the hyperspherical structure under orthogonal transformations; once you add arbitrary positive scaling, you are preserving only that angular structure, not the layer’s exact output distribution.

So the right way to read the paper is: orthogonal finetuning preserves the semantic structure they care about because they hypothesize that semantics live primarily in neuron directions, while magnitude is less important. The re-scaled variant keeps that angular geometry intact but gives the optimizer extra freedom to adjust gain. That extra freedom can indeed affect the function more strongly, which is why the authors treat it as an optional, more flexible variant rather than the core theoretical method. In fact, the paper even introduces COFT precisely to restore extra stability by constraining deviation from the pretrained weights. So the stability argument is not “scaling is harmless”; it is “scaling does not destroy the specific geometric invariant we use to proxy semantic preservation, but it may reduce functional stability, so use it as a flexibility knob rather than a guarantee.”
