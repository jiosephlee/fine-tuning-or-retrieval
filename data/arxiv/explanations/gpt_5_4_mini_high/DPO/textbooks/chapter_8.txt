\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 8: Experimental Design, Baselines, and Evaluation

# The Goals of the Empirical Study

The empirical section of the paper is designed to answer three closely related questions. First, can Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO, solve the same preference-learning problem as standard RLHF methods while producing a good trade-off between reward and staying close to the reference model? Second, does it continue to work when moved from a carefully controlled setting to large, real preference datasets and larger language models? Third, is the evaluation procedure itself trustworthy, especially when the paper relies on GPT-4 as a proxy for human judgment in some settings?

These goals shape the structure of the experiments. The authors begin with a controlled sentiment task, where the reward is known by construction, so they can directly measure the reward-versus-KL trade-off that RLHF is supposed to optimize. They then move to two realistic preference-learning tasks, summarization and single-turn dialogue, where the true reward is not available and success must be estimated through win rates against a baseline completion. Finally, they include a human validation study to check whether GPT-4 judgments are a reasonable stand-in for human evaluation.

A key theme across all experiments is that the paper is not only testing final output quality. It is also testing whether DPO behaves like a stable and efficient optimizer of the underlying RLHF objective. That is why the experiments include both controlled and realistic tasks, both automated and human evaluation, and both direct optimization baselines and stronger but more expensive baselines such as Best of $N$.

# Controlled Sentiment Generation on IMDb

The first task is controlled sentiment generation. Here, the prompt $x$ is a prefix of a movie review from the IMDb dataset, and the model must generate a completion $y$ with positive sentiment. The task is not simply to imitate human-written reviews; rather, it asks whether a language model can be steered toward a specific attribute of the output while still generating fluent text. This makes it a useful toy setting for studying alignment and control.

For this task, the paper fine-tunes GPT-2-large as the supervised fine-tuning, or SFT, model. The SFT model is trained on reviews from the IMDb training split until convergence. This model serves as the starting point for preference-based methods and as the reference point against which KL divergence is measured. Because the task is controlled, the authors do not need a human preference dataset for evaluation; instead, they can generate preference pairs synthetically and use a sentiment classifier as the reward signal.

The importance of this task is that it gives the cleanest possible view of the optimization problem. Since the reward is known, the paper can directly examine whether a method is making the right trade-off between increasing reward and remaining close to the reference policy. That is exactly the kind of trade-off RLHF methods are supposed to manage, so this setting lets the authors test the core algorithmic claim of DPO under ideal measurement conditions.

# Summarization on the Reddit TL;DR Dataset with Human Preferences

The second task is summarization. In this setting, the prompt $x$ is a forum post from Reddit, and the desired response $y$ is a summary of the main points in the post. The paper uses the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset, together with human preference labels collected by Stiennon et al. This is a more realistic preference-learning benchmark than the controlled sentiment task because the quality of a summary is not captured by a simple classifier and must be judged by humans.

The summarization setup is also important because it reflects a common pattern in preference-based language model training: the preference data do not come from the exact same model that is later fine-tuned. The paper notes that the human preference dataset was gathered on samples from a different, but similarly trained, SFT model. This matters because the evaluation is not just asking whether the model can memorize a narrow set of annotated examples. It is asking whether the learned preference optimization method can generalize across a realistic data pipeline.

The authors use a GPT-J model fine-tuned on human-written forum post summaries as the SFT model for this task. DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT are all trained from the same GPT-J SFT starting point. Evaluation is then performed by sampling completions on the test split and comparing them against reference summaries from the test set using win rates judged by GPT-4. This makes summarization a key test of scalability: the method must work on a real preference dataset and with a larger model than the controlled sentiment experiment.

# Single-Turn Dialogue on the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless Dataset

The third task is single-turn dialogue. Here, the prompt $x$ is a human query, and the model must produce a response $y$ that is both helpful and engaging. The queries can range widely, from technical questions to requests for advice, so the task tests whether a preference-learning method can produce broadly useful assistant behavior rather than just controlling a single stylistic attribute.

The paper uses the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dataset, which contains 170k dialogues between a human and an automated assistant. Each transcript ends with a pair of responses generated by a large language model and a human preference label indicating which response was preferred. Unlike summarization, there is no standard pre-trained SFT model already available for this setting, so the paper builds one by fine-tuning an off-the-shelf language model on the preferred completions only.

This setup is especially useful because it closely resembles how preference data are often used in practice. The model must learn from offline comparisons rather than from direct demonstrations of ideal responses. It also tests whether DPO can operate in a setting where the model is initialized from a generic base model rather than from a task-specific SFT model. That makes dialogue a useful stress test of the full preference-learning pipeline.

# Model Choices Across the Experiments

The paper deliberately uses different base models across the tasks to match the scale and structure of the data. In the controlled sentiment experiment, the SFT model is GPT-2-large. In summarization, the starting point is a GPT-J model already fine-tuned for summarization. In dialogue, the base model is Pythia-2.8B, and the paper also reports a PPO model trained on Anthropic Helpful and Harmless data from a 6B-parameter Pythia-based system.

These model choices matter because the paper is not only trying to show that DPO works in one narrow setting. It is trying to show that the method applies across model families and task scales. The experiments therefore span models from GPT-2-large up to systems with 6B parameters, showing that the preference-learning formulation is not tied to a single architecture or dataset.

The task-specific model choices also affect the reference model used by DPO and the other comparison methods. In some settings, a task-specific SFT model is available and serves naturally as the reference. In others, such as dialogue, the paper builds a reference model by supervised learning on preferred completions. This is part of the experimental design because it ensures that the comparison methods start from a reasonable baseline before preference optimization begins.

# Synthetic Preferences in the Sentiment Setting

In the controlled sentiment task, the paper does not rely on human annotators to create the preference labels. Instead, it generates preference pairs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier. The construction is simple: for a given prompt $x$ and two generated completions $y_w$ and $y_l$, the completion with higher predicted positive sentiment is labeled as preferred, so that $p(\text{positive} \mid x, y_w) > p(\text{positive} \mid x, y_l)$.

This synthetic preference construction is important because it creates a controlled environment in which the reward is effectively known. Since the classifier defines the reward, the authors can measure exactly how much positive sentiment the policy produces and how far it moves from the reference policy. That makes the sentiment task ideal for studying optimization behavior, rather than only final response quality.

At the same time, the task still uses the same preference-learning machinery as the real human-feedback tasks. The model sees pairs of completions and learns which one should be preferred. The difference is that the source of the labels is artificial rather than human. This lets the paper isolate the algorithmic question: if the preference objective is well defined, can DPO optimize it directly and stably?

# Why the Sentiment Task Exposes the True Reward-KL Trade-Off

The controlled sentiment setting is the only experiment in the paper where the true reward is available, and this is why it can be evaluated with a reward-versus-KL frontier. The RLHF objective used by the paper is not just about maximizing reward. It also includes a KL-divergence penalty that keeps the policy from drifting too far from the reference model. A method that obtains slightly more reward but at a much larger KL may actually be worse, because it may be overfitting the reward or producing unrealistic generations.

This is why the paper treats sentiment generation as a two-objective comparison. The authors compute the average reward under the known sentiment classifier and the average sequence-level KL divergence from the reference policy. The sequence-level KL is the sum of the per-timestep KL divergences. By looking at the frontier traced out by different hyperparameter settings, they can see which method gives the best reward for a given amount of deviation from the reference model.

That kind of analysis is only possible because the reward is known. In the later summarization and dialogue experiments, the reward is latent, so the paper cannot compute the same frontier directly. Instead, it must rely on win rates against a baseline completion. The controlled sentiment task therefore serves as a kind of microscope for the optimization problem that RLHF methods are supposed to solve.

# Baselines: Prompting, Supervised Fine-Tuning, Preference Fine-Tuning, Unlikelihood, PPO, PPO-GT, and Best of $N$

The paper compares DPO against a broad set of baselines, and the mix of baselines reflects the different ways one might try to improve a language model from preferences. A simple starting point is zero-shot or few-shot prompting. The paper includes zero-shot prompting with GPT-J for summarization and 2-shot prompting with Pythia-2.8B for dialogue. These baselines show what the base models can do without any task-specific preference optimization.

The supervised-learning baselines are SFT and Preferred-FT. SFT is the initial fine-tuned language model trained on demonstrations or task-specific supervision. Preferred-FT goes one step further by training on the chosen completion $y_w$ only, using supervised learning on preferred examples. This is a natural baseline because it uses the same preference data, but in a much simpler way than RLHF.

The paper also includes Unlikelihood, which tries to increase the probability of the preferred completion while explicitly decreasing the probability of the dispreferred one. An optional coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ weights the unlikelihood term. This baseline is useful because it is closer in spirit to DPO than ordinary supervised learning, but it still does not use the same objective.

Finally, the paper compares against PPO, PPO-GT, and Best of $N$. PPO is the standard RLHF optimizer that fine-tunes the model using a learned reward model. PPO-GT is the same style of optimization but uses the ground-truth reward in the controlled sentiment setting, making it an oracle baseline. Best of $N$ samples multiple responses from the SFT or Preferred-FT model and returns the one with the highest score according to the learned reward model. It is a strong baseline, but it is computationally expensive because it requires multiple sampled completions for every query at test time.

# Hyperparameter Sweeps and Sampling Choices

The paper does not compare methods using a single setting of each hyperparameter. Instead, it performs sweeps designed to explore the relevant trade-offs. In the controlled sentiment experiment, PPO is run with target KL values in $\{3,6,9,12\}$, DPO is run with $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, and Unlikelihood is run with $\alpha \in \{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$. Preferred-FT is also run with different random seeds. Altogether, the sentiment study includes 22 runs.

This kind of sweep is essential because these methods are sensitive to how strongly they are pushed toward the preference signal. The KL target in PPO controls how far the policy is allowed to move from the reference, while $\beta$ plays the corresponding role in DPO. The unlikelihood coefficient $\alpha$ governs the strength of the penalty on the rejected completion. By sweeping these values, the paper can compare frontiers rather than isolated points.

Sampling choices also matter in the real-task evaluations. For summarization and dialogue, the paper samples completions at temperatures ranging from $0.0$ to $1.0$ and compares methods at the temperatures that work best for them. This is important because a model’s apparent quality can change substantially with the sampling temperature. The evaluation therefore treats temperature as part of the experimental design rather than as a fixed afterthought.

# Evaluating Controlled Settings with Reward-Versus-KL Frontiers

In the controlled sentiment task, the evaluation criterion is the reward-KL frontier. Each method is trained under a particular hyperparameter setting, and after every 100 training steps until convergence, the authors evaluate the policy on test prompts. For each policy they compute the average reward under the true reward function, which is the sentiment classifier, and the average sequence-level KL divergence from the reference policy.

The reason this evaluation is so informative is that it matches the structure of the RLHF objective itself. RLHF is not supposed to maximize reward without limit. It is supposed to find a policy that improves behavior while staying near the base model. The frontier tells you whether a method is getting a better trade-off than its competitors, not just whether it can push reward upward by brute force.

This evaluation style is especially suited to comparing DPO with PPO, because both are intended to optimize the same constrained objective. The paper uses this setting to ask whether DPO can reach the same or better trade-offs without needing a separate reward model and reinforcement-learning loop. The frontier-based analysis is therefore the most direct test of the paper’s central claim about optimization quality.

# Evaluating Real Tasks with GPT-4 Win Rates

In summarization and dialogue, the paper cannot directly compute the true reward, so it uses win rates. A win rate is computed by comparing a model’s completion against a baseline completion and asking GPT-4 which one is better. For summarization, the baseline is the reference summary in the test set. For dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response in the test data.

This choice reflects the fact that real preference-learning tasks are not easily reduced to a simple automatic score. The goal is not merely to match a string overlap metric. It is to produce outputs that humans would prefer. A win-rate evaluation therefore compares two candidate completions and asks which one is better according to a judge that approximates human preference.

The paper uses this procedure on the test split of each dataset. In summarization, the generated completions are compared against ground-truth reference summaries. In dialogue, they are compared against the preferred test responses. The resulting win rates give a task-level picture of how useful the fine-tuned policies are, while still being compatible with the offline preference-learning setup.

# Why Automatic Metrics Such as ROUGE Are Not Enough

The paper explicitly motivates its use of win-rate evaluation by noting that automatic metrics can be poorly correlated with human preferences. In particular, prior work has found that ROUGE may not reflect what people actually consider a good summary. That matters because ROUGE measures n-gram overlap, which can reward surface similarity even when the resulting summary is not especially helpful, concise, or faithful in the way humans care about.

This is why the summarization experiments do not rely on ROUGE as the main criterion. The paper’s objective is to compare methods according to the preferences that matter in alignment settings, not simply according to lexical overlap with a reference. A method that produces a summary more preferred by humans is more relevant to the paper’s goals than one that merely scores higher on a surface metric.

The same logic applies to dialogue. A good assistant response is not defined by overlap with a reference response, but by whether it is helpful and appropriate to the user’s query. The win-rate evaluation is therefore more aligned with the preference data the models are trained on, and more informative about whether DPO is actually learning the kind of behavior the authors want.

# Human Validation of GPT-4 as an Evaluator

Because GPT-4 is used as an evaluator in the main experiments, the paper includes a human study to check whether GPT-4 judgments are reliable. The study is performed on results from the TL;DR summarization experiment, and it uses two different GPT-4 prompts. The simple prompt, GPT-4 (S), asks which summary better summarizes the important information in the post. The concise prompt, GPT-4 (C), adds an explicit request to favor conciseness as well.

The reason for testing two prompts is that evaluator behavior can depend on prompt wording. The paper reports that GPT-4 with the simple prompt tends to prefer summaries that are longer and more repetitive than humans prefer. The concise prompt is meant to correct for that tendency and produce judgments that better reflect human preferences. This is a good example of evaluation design mattering as much as model design: if the judge is biased, the measured win rates can be misleading.

To validate the judge, the paper compares several systems spanning a range of quality, including a strong DPO model, a weaker PPO model, and an intermediate SFT model, all against greedily sampled PPO. The paper finds that GPT-4 tends to agree with humans about as often as humans agree with each other, suggesting that it is a reasonable proxy for human evaluation. The concise prompt is ultimately used for the main summarization results because it is more representative of human judgments.
