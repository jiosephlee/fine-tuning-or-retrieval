\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 6: Understanding the DPO Update in Practice

# The implicit reward defined by the policy

The central idea behind Direct Preference Optimization is that the language model itself can be viewed as defining an implicit reward function. In the paper, this reward is written as
\[
\hat{r}_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
This expression compares the probability the current policy assigns to a completion \(y\) with the probability assigned by a fixed reference policy \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), which is typically the supervised fine-tuned model used as the starting point for RLHF. If the current model prefers a response more strongly than the reference model does, then the implicit reward is higher.

This reparameterization is not just a convenient definition. It is the key move that makes DPO possible. Earlier in the paper, the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem is shown to have the form
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
\]
Rearranging this relation gives a reward in terms of the policy and the reference policy, up to an additive term depending only on \(x\). Because the Bradley-Terry preference model depends only on reward differences between two completions, that additive term cancels out. DPO exploits exactly this fact: instead of learning a separate reward model and then optimizing a policy against it, it directly parameterizes the preference model through the policy itself. In this sense, the policy network is simultaneously the language model and the implicit reward model.

# The gradient of the DPO loss

DPO trains the policy by minimizing a binary cross-entropy loss over preference pairs \((x,y_w,y_l)\), where \(y_w\) is the preferred completion and \(y_l\) is the dispreferred completion. The paper gives the gradient of this loss as
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
=
-\beta \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}
\left[
\sigma\!\left(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\right)
\left(
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)
-
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)
\right)
\right].
\]
This formula makes the update rule easy to interpret. The term in parentheses says that training pushes up the log probability of the preferred completion and pushes down the log probability of the dispreferred completion. The outer negative sign means that gradient descent on the loss produces exactly that effect.

The more subtle part is the sigmoid factor multiplying the update. It depends on the difference between the implicit rewards assigned to the dispreferred and preferred completions. When the model currently ranks the pair correctly and confidently, this difference is negative and the sigmoid is small, so the update is gentle. When the model ranks the pair incorrectly, the difference is positive and the sigmoid is large, so the update becomes stronger. In other words, the gradient is not a uniform push over all examples. It is a weighted correction that focuses more on pairs that the current policy is getting wrong.

# Why DPO increases preferred responses and decreases dispreferred ones

The direction of the DPO update follows directly from the pairwise preference objective. The loss is built from
\[
\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
-
\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right),
\]
so the model is rewarded when the preferred completion has a larger relative probability under \(\pi_\theta\) than the dispreferred one. This is the same logic as in the reward-modeling objective earlier in the paper, except that the reward differences have been rewritten in terms of policy log-ratios.

Practically, this means DPO does not try to make every completion more likely. It tries to increase the relative preference margin between the two candidates in each labeled pair. If the model already places the preferred response above the dispreferred one, the update is small. If the ordering is wrong, the update pushes the model to reverse it. That makes DPO a direct preference-fitting method rather than a generic likelihood maximization procedure.

# The dynamic weighting term \( \sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)) \)

A distinctive feature of DPO is the weighting term
\[
\sigma\!\left(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\right),
\]
which appears inside the gradient. The paper emphasizes that this term acts like a dynamic, per-example importance weight. It is larger when the current implicit reward model assigns a higher score to the dispreferred completion than to the preferred one, and smaller when the model already orders the pair correctly.

This weighting matters because it makes the update adaptive. The model does not spend equal effort on every labeled comparison. Instead, it places more emphasis on comparisons where the current policy is misaligned with the preference data or where the margin is weak. The paper notes that this weighting is scaled by \(\beta\), so it also reflects how strongly the KL constraint is being enforced. The result is a training signal that is both preference-sensitive and conservatism-aware.

# Why hard or incorrectly ranked examples receive larger updates

The sigmoid weight can be understood as a difficulty-sensitive correction term. If the current model already strongly prefers the winning completion, then \(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\) is very negative and the sigmoid is close to zero. Such examples already agree with the current policy, so they contribute little to the gradient. If the current model prefers the losing completion, then the difference becomes positive and the sigmoid approaches one, giving that example a much larger influence on the update.

This behavior is especially important for the kinds of preference data used in DPO, where some pairs are likely easy and others are ambiguous or difficult. The update is therefore not just “make preferred answers more probable.” It is “make the biggest corrections where the model most badly disagrees with the data.” That is why the paper describes the term as higher weight when the reward estimate is wrong. Hard or incorrectly ranked examples are exactly the ones that trigger the strongest adjustments.

# How the weighting term helps avoid degeneration compared with naive objectives

The paper contrasts DPO with a naive probability-ratio style objective and with unlikelihood-style training. A naive approach that simply increases the preferred completion and decreases the dispreferred one, without the dynamic weighting term, can cause the model to degenerate. The paper reports that the weighting coefficient is important for preventing that failure mode. The key point is that not all pairwise updates should be equally aggressive; the model needs a correction rule that depends on how wrong its current ordering is.

This is one reason DPO is more stable than a simple unlikelihood objective. Unlikelihood training directly tries to maximize the preferred completion and minimize the dispreferred one, but DPO adds a structured importance weight derived from the implicit reward interpretation. That weight keeps the update aligned with the KL-constrained RLHF objective and reduces the risk of overcorrecting examples that are already well handled. In the paper’s framing, that makes DPO a faithful optimization of the RLHF problem rather than a blunt alternative to it.

# The end-to-end DPO training pipeline on an offline preference dataset

The DPO pipeline is simple once the preference data are available. First, one has a dataset of prompt-response comparisons
\[
\mathcal{D}=\{x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N,
\]
where each prompt \(x\) comes with a preferred completion \(y_w\) and a dispreferred completion \(y_l\). Then the policy \(\pi_\theta\) is optimized by minimizing the DPO loss over this fixed offline dataset. There is no separate reward model to fit and no reinforcement learning loop that samples from the policy during training.

In the paper’s formulation, the data themselves can come from sampling two completions from a reference policy and having humans choose the better one. But once the preference dataset is collected, DPO trains exactly like a supervised classification problem over pairs. That is what makes the procedure computationally lightweight and straightforward to implement. The learning signal comes entirely from the offline preference annotations and the comparison of the policy with the reference policy.

# How to construct or reuse preference datasets collected from a reference model

The paper notes that preference datasets are often collected from samples drawn from a supervised fine-tuned reference model \(\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}\). In that case, the natural choice is to set the DPO reference policy \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) equal to \(\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}\). This matches the distribution from which the comparison data were drawn and keeps the optimization aligned with the data-generating process assumed by the theory.

The paper also emphasizes that in practice one may want to reuse preference datasets that are already publicly available rather than generating new samples and new human labels. That makes DPO especially practical, since it can train from existing offline preference data. The important requirement is that the reference policy used in the objective should be chosen to reflect, as closely as possible, the distribution used to obtain the comparisons.

# What to do when the original SFT reference model is unavailable

Sometimes the original supervised fine-tuned reference model is not available. The paper gives a practical remedy for that case: initialize the reference policy by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred completions in the dataset. Concretely, the reference is chosen as
\[
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\arg\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x,y_w\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)\bigr].
\]
This does not recreate the original reference model exactly, but it provides a usable substitute.

The purpose of this step is to reduce distribution shift between the true, unavailable reference distribution and the reference policy actually used inside DPO. The paper presents it as a practical way to make the preference data compatible with the DPO objective when the original SFT checkpoint is missing. In other words, if the true reference policy cannot be recovered, one builds a reasonable approximation from the preferred responses already present in the data.

# Choosing a reference policy by fine-tuning on preferred completions

The previous point has an immediate practical interpretation: a good fallback reference policy can be obtained by fine-tuning on the preferred completions themselves. The paper uses this idea in settings where a standard SFT model is not available. The resulting reference model is trained so that its completions stay within the distribution of the model while being anchored to the responses that humans selected.

This choice matters because DPO compares the learned policy against \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) at every update. If the reference policy is badly mismatched to the data, the implicit reward signal becomes less meaningful. By fine-tuning on preferred completions, the reference policy becomes a useful baseline for the DPO ratio \(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\). The paper’s practical message is that DPO does not require a perfect reference checkpoint, but it does benefit from a reference policy that is close to the preference data distribution.

# The practical meaning of \( \beta \) as a conservativeness parameter

The parameter \(\beta\) plays the same conceptual role in DPO that it does in the KL-constrained RLHF objective. In the RL formulation,
\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\ \mathbb{E}[r_\phi(x,y)]-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\right],
\]
larger \(\beta\) means stronger pressure to stay close to the reference policy. In DPO, \(\beta\) scales the log-ratio inside the classifier, and therefore controls how strongly each preference comparison pushes the model away from the reference.

This makes \(\beta\) a conservativeness parameter. A larger \(\beta\) corresponds to a more conservative update that places more weight on maintaining proximity to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), while a smaller \(\beta\) allows the model to move more aggressively. The paper’s experiments treat \(\beta\) as the main knob controlling the reward-KL tradeoff, which is exactly the tradeoff that standard RLHF tries to manage through its KL penalty.

# Why DPO avoids online sampling, value estimation, and extensive RL-style tuning

DPO’s main practical advantage is that it removes the machinery that makes RLHF difficult to train. Standard RLHF pipelines require training a reward model, then running an RL algorithm such as PPO, often with online sampling from the policy, reward normalization, and additional tuning to keep the optimization stable. The paper stresses that this process is computationally expensive and sensitive to hyperparameters.

DPO replaces that entire sequence with a single offline optimization objective. There is no need to sample from the language model during fine-tuning, no separate value function is required to estimate the normalization term, and no RL-specific training loop is needed. Because the preference objective is written directly in terms of the policy and the reference policy, the optimization can be carried out with a simple binary cross-entropy loss. That is why the paper describes DPO as stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, and why it argues that DPO meaningfully lowers the barrier to training language models from human preferences.
