\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 2: Background: From Pretraining to Preference Learning

# Self-Supervised Language Modeling and Emergent Zero-Shot and Few-Shot Abilities

Modern language models begin with large-scale unsupervised, or more precisely self-supervised, training on massive text corpora. The paper emphasizes that models trained this way acquire broad world knowledge and even some reasoning skills, despite never being explicitly taught downstream tasks in the way a supervised classifier would be. This is why such models can sometimes perform zero-shot or few-shot: they can complete tasks from prompts alone, or with only a small number of examples, because pretraining has already exposed them to many patterns of language and knowledge.

At the same time, the paper stresses an important limitation of this stage. The training data comes from humans with many different goals, priorities, and skill levels, so the model learns a mixture of behaviors. That mixture is powerful, but it is not aligned by default. A model may know about a common misconception without being meant to repeat it, or it may have seen rare high-quality examples of code without reliably preferring those examples when asked to generate code. So pretraining gives the model capability, but not control. It teaches what the model can say, not which behavior it should choose when multiple plausible responses are available.

This distinction between capability and control is the background motivation for the rest of the paper. The model’s knowledge base is broad, but users care about selecting the right slice of that knowledge for a given application. That is the core alignment problem that preference learning tries to solve.

# Instruction Tuning and Supervised Fine-Tuning as Early Alignment Stages

The paper describes supervised fine-tuning, or SFT, as the first alignment stage that typically follows pretraining. In SFT, a pretrained language model is trained on high-quality examples for the downstream task of interest, such as dialogue or summarization. The goal is to move the model from a general-purpose language predictor to a model that already behaves sensibly in a target setting. In the paper’s notation, this produces an initial policy $\pi^\text{SFT}$.

Instruction tuning is a closely related idea. Here the model is fine-tuned on datasets of instructions paired with human-written completions. The paper notes that this procedure helps models generalize to instructions outside the instruction-tuning set and generally improves usability. The intuition is straightforward: rather than only learning to continue text, the model learns to respond to user requests in the form of instructions. That makes the model much more usable as an assistant, because it learns the conversational structure of “ask, answer, help.”

SFT is important not just as a standalone method, but because it establishes a starting point for later alignment methods. In the RLHF pipeline reviewed in the paper, the SFT model becomes the reference policy. It is the base distribution from which preference samples are drawn and against which later changes are measured. So SFT is not merely an optional warm-up; it is the scaffold on which preference learning is built.

# How Supervised Fine-Tuning Produces an Initial Policy for Downstream Tasks

The paper presents SFT as the mechanism that turns a general pretrained model into a task-specific policy. In dialogue, summarization, and related settings, the model is first trained on human demonstrations of high-quality responses so that it learns what “reasonable” outputs look like in that domain. This produces $\pi^\text{SFT}$, which is not yet perfectly aligned to human preferences, but is already a much better starting point than the raw pretrained model.

This initial policy matters because downstream alignment methods are not usually asked to solve the entire problem from scratch. Instead, they refine the behavior of a model that already knows the task format. In the paper’s RLHF pipeline, prompts $x$ are fed to the SFT model, and the resulting answers are compared by human labelers. In other words, the SFT model is the source of candidate behavior that preference learning will later sculpt. It reduces the search space from “all possible language” to “reasonable task-following outputs.”

There is also a practical reason SFT is used. A policy that starts closer to the desired behavior is easier to optimize and evaluate. The paper’s broader message is that alignment is a staged process: pretraining provides language competence, SFT provides task competence, and preference learning then adjusts the model toward the kinds of responses humans actually prefer.

# Why Preference Data Can Complement or Outperform Pure Supervised Fine-Tuning

The paper argues that relative human judgments are often easier to collect than expert demonstrations. Instead of asking a labeler to produce the perfect answer from scratch, we can ask which of two model outputs is better. That is a smaller and often more natural task for humans. This is one reason preference data can complement supervised fine-tuning: it captures human judgments about quality without requiring full target responses.

Preference data is also useful because many language tasks do not have a single correct answer. A response can be more helpful, more concise, more fluent, safer, or more aligned with the user’s intent without being uniquely specified. The paper gives examples through its broader discussion of alignment: we may want a coding assistant to prefer high-quality code, or a model to avoid repeating a misconception as fact. Relative preferences are well suited to these kinds of goals because they directly encode what humans would rather see, not just what a dataset happened to contain.

The paper further notes that preference learning has improved translation, summarization, story generation, and instruction-following. The common thread is that the output quality is often judged holistically. Supervised fine-tuning can show the model one acceptable answer, but preferences can tell the model which of two or more plausible answers is better. That additional signal can be especially valuable when the desired behavior is subtle, subjective, or dependent on human priorities rather than on exact string matching.

# RLHF and RLAIF as Dominant Paradigms for Aligning Language Models

According to the paper, reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, became the dominant way to turn preference data into aligned behavior. The basic idea is simple: first fit a reward model to human comparisons, then use reinforcement learning to train the language model to maximize that learned reward while staying close to the reference policy. The paper reviews the standard pipeline as having three phases: supervised fine-tuning, reward modeling, and reinforcement learning optimization.

The reward modeling phase usually uses a preference model such as Bradley-Terry. Human labelers compare two completions and indicate which one they prefer. A reward network is then trained so that the preferred completion receives a higher score than the dispreferred one. That learned reward is only an approximation to human judgment, but it gives the RL stage a numeric target that can be optimized. The paper emphasizes that a KL divergence penalty is added to prevent the policy from drifting too far from the original model, which helps preserve language quality and maintain diversity.

The paper also discusses RLAIF, or reinforcement learning from AI feedback, as a close relative of RLHF. In that setup, the feedback comes from another model rather than directly from humans, often with only weak human supervision in the form of a rubric or text instructions for the annotating model. The paper places both RLHF and RLAIF in a broader family of preference-based alignment methods. What unites them is the idea that humans, or systems acting on their behalf, can judge relative quality more easily than they can specify a single explicit reward function.

# Applications of Preference Learning in Summarization, Dialogue, Translation, and Story Generation

The paper situates preference learning as a general approach that has already been applied to several open-ended natural language tasks. In translation, summarization, story generation, and instruction-following, outputs are often judged by how well they satisfy human expectations rather than by a single reference answer. Preference data fits these settings well because it can rank competing responses by quality instead of forcing every example into a single supervised target.

Summarization is a particularly clear example. A summary should capture the important points, remain concise, and read naturally. There are many acceptable summaries of the same source text, so human preference comparisons are often more informative than exact-match metrics. The paper notes that prior work found reinforcement learning with human preferences to be effective in summarization. Similarly, in dialogue, the right response depends on helpfulness, relevance, and tone, not just factual correctness. Preference data can encode those qualities directly.

The paper also points to translation and story generation as other domains where relative judgments help. In such tasks, a model may produce several linguistically fluent outputs, but one may be more faithful, more engaging, or more aligned with user goals. The advantage of preference learning is that it can capture those distinctions even when a conventional supervised label would be too crude.

# Offline Preference Datasets Versus Online Interaction Settings

A central distinction in the paper is between offline and online preference learning. In language model alignment, the usual setting is offline: a fixed batch of preference-annotated pairs is collected in advance, and the model is trained on that static dataset. The paper’s RLHF setup assumes a dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, where each example is a prompt and a preferred versus dispreferred completion. The training loop then reuses those recorded comparisons rather than asking for new labels at every step.

This differs from contextual dueling bandits, where preferences are typically obtained online as part of the interaction process. In that setting, the learner can choose actions and observe pairwise preferences during learning. That online feedback loop changes both the theory and the algorithmic design. The paper highlights this contrast to show that language model alignment is usually not an interactive online control problem in the classical sense; it is more often an offline learning problem with a fixed dataset of human judgments.

The offline nature of preference learning for language has practical advantages. It makes annotation reusable and allows training to proceed without continuously involving humans. But it also creates a challenge: the model is optimized on a fixed distribution of annotated examples, even though the deployed model may encounter new prompts and new response styles. This is one reason the paper pays attention to how preference-based methods generalize beyond the exact training distribution.

# Related Settings Outside Language: Contextual Dueling Bandits and Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning

The paper places its contribution in the context of two related bodies of work outside language modeling. The first is contextual dueling bandits, where instead of observing numeric rewards, the learner sees preferences between actions or rankings of actions. In that literature, the theoretical notion of an optimal policy is often replaced by a von Neumann winner, a policy whose expected win rate against any other policy is at least $50\%$. This is a useful abstraction when absolute rewards are unavailable.

The second related area is preference-based reinforcement learning. In that setting, the learner observes binary preferences generated by an unknown scoring function rather than explicit reward values. Many algorithms in this area estimate that latent scoring function and then optimize it. The paper explicitly notes that these methods often follow a two-stage structure: first infer the hidden reward-like quantity, then use it to improve the policy. Some methods can reuse off-policy preference data, but the overall logic remains to reconstruct reward before policy optimization.

The paper’s point in mentioning these areas is not just historical. It uses them to show that preference learning has a mature theoretical foundation, but also that language modeling introduces a distinct combination of scale, discreteness, and implementation difficulty. That combination motivates a new approach.

# What Makes Preference Optimization for Language Models Unique at Scale

The paper argues that language model alignment is unusually challenging because the policy acts over discrete token sequences and must be trained at very large scale. Once the model is generating text, the objective of directly optimizing preferences becomes hard to differentiate through in the usual way. That is why RLHF historically relied on reinforcement learning algorithms such as REINFORCE and PPO. But those methods are not only mathematically indirect; they are also complex and often unstable in practice.

The standard RLHF pipeline requires training multiple models: the initial supervised policy, a reward model, and then a final policy trained with reinforcement learning. It also requires sampling from the model during training, which makes the process computationally expensive. The paper emphasizes that the added KL constraint is essential because it keeps the policy close to the region where the reward model is reliable and helps prevent collapse to a narrow set of high-reward outputs. But that same constraint adds another layer of tuning and optimization difficulty.

This is what makes preference optimization for language models qualitatively different from smaller-scale or more classical preference learning problems. The central challenge is not merely learning from comparisons, but doing so in a way that is stable, efficient, and compatible with very large language models. The paper’s broader background narrative prepares the reader for its main proposal: a method that keeps the benefits of preference learning while avoiding the complexity of an explicit reward model and a reinforcement learning loop.
