\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 4: The Conventional RLHF Pipeline

# Phase 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning to Obtain $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$

The conventional RLHF pipeline begins with a language model that has already learned broad linguistic and world knowledge from large-scale unsupervised pre-training. That initial model is not yet shaped to follow a particular task or to reflect human preferences in a reliable way, so the first alignment step is supervised fine-tuning on high-quality data for the downstream task of interest. In the paper, this produces an initial model denoted $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$.

This stage matters because it gives the later preference-learning steps a strong starting point. Rather than asking the model to learn task behavior and human preference at the same time, the pipeline first teaches the model to produce generally good task-relevant responses, such as summaries, dialogue responses, or other domain-specific completions. In the paper’s framing, RLHF is a post-training stage that comes after this supervised step. The supervised model then becomes the reference point from which preferences are collected and against which later policy updates are measured.

# Phase 2: Sampling Response Pairs and Collecting Human Preferences

Once $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ has been trained, the next stage is to generate candidate responses and ask humans which one is better. Concretely, prompts $x$ are fed to the SFT model, which produces pairs of answers $(y_1,y_2)\sim \pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}(y\mid x)$. Human labelers then examine the two completions and indicate a preference, written as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the preferred completion and $y_l$ is the dispreferred one.

This kind of data is attractive because relative judgments are often easier to collect than absolute scores or expert demonstrations. The paper emphasizes that human preference annotations provide a practical way to encode what people consider helpful, safe, or high quality. The result is a static offline dataset of comparisons,
\[
\mathcal{D}=\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N,
\]
which becomes the core training signal for the reward model. The important idea is that the human is not asked to write the perfect answer from scratch; instead, they only need to say which of two sampled answers they prefer.

# Constructing a Reward Model $r_\phi$ from Pairwise Comparisons

The conventional RLHF pipeline assumes that these pairwise preferences are generated by some latent reward function $r^*(x,y)$ that we cannot observe directly. The reward model is a learned approximation to this hidden scoring rule. Its job is to explain why one completion is preferred over another, and then to generalize that explanation to new model outputs.

The paper discusses preference modeling through the Bradley-Terry framework, which is a standard way to turn reward differences into preference probabilities. In that model, the probability that completion $y_1$ is preferred over $y_2$ given prompt $x$ is
\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x,y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x,y_1)\right)+\exp\left(r^*(x,y_2)\right)}.
\]
This formulation captures a simple intuition: the more the reward model values one response relative to another, the more likely that response is to be preferred. The framework is also compatible with more general Plackett-Luce ranking models if several ranked answers are available, but the pairwise Bradley-Terry version is the one emphasized in the paper.

# The Bradley-Terry Reward-Model Loss

Given a dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D}$, the reward model parameters $\phi$ are estimated by maximum likelihood. In practice, this becomes a binary classification problem: the model must predict which of two responses the human preferred. The loss used in the paper is
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr],
\]
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function.

The structure of this loss is important. It does not ask the reward model to assign an absolute score that is correct in some independent sense; instead, it only asks for the preferred response to have a higher score than the dispreferred one. That matches the nature of the data, since the labels are relative judgments. The logistic form turns the reward difference into a probability, so if the reward model puts much higher value on $y_w$ than on $y_l$, the loss is small; if it ranks them incorrectly, the loss becomes large. This is the standard reward-learning step used before RL optimization in RLHF.

# Initializing Reward Models from Language Models with a Scalar Reward Head

In the paper, the reward model is typically initialized from the SFT language model itself. More precisely, one takes the SFT model and adds a linear layer on top of the final transformer representation that outputs a single scalar reward value. This design lets the reward model reuse the language understanding already present in the fine-tuned model, rather than learning from scratch.

The paper also notes a practical normalization step: prior work often normalizes rewards so that they have lower variance, with the effect that the rewards have zero mean for each prompt. The intuition is that a reward model used for downstream optimization should be stable enough to compare responses consistently. By initializing from a language model and then learning only a scalar reward head, the pipeline tries to produce a scoring function that is both expressive and relatively easy to train from preference data.

# Phase 3: Reinforcement Learning with the Learned Reward

After the reward model is fit, the final phase of conventional RLHF uses that learned reward as feedback to optimize a policy. The policy being trained is parameterized by $\pi_\theta$, and the goal is to make it generate responses that score highly under $r_\phi$. Because the outputs are discrete text sequences, this objective cannot be optimized by ordinary gradient descent directly; instead, reinforcement learning is used.

The basic optimization problem in the paper is
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid\mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr].
\]
The first term encourages the model to produce high-reward completions. The second term penalizes the policy for drifting too far from a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, which in practice is the initial SFT model. The scalar $\beta$ controls the strength of this penalty.

This stage is the heart of RLHF as it is usually implemented. The learned reward model gives a notion of what counts as good behavior, and the policy update tries to move the language model toward that behavior while keeping it close to the original model. In the paper’s discussion, the standard approach is to construct a reward signal of the form
\[
r(x,y)=r_\phi(x,y)-\beta \bigl(\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)-\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr),
\]
and then maximize it with PPO. That is the usual route from preference data to a new aligned policy.

# The KL-Constrained RLHF Objective

The KL term in the RLHF objective is not just a technical detail. It is the mechanism that keeps optimization under control. Without it, the policy could move too aggressively toward responses that happen to receive high reward according to the learned model, even if those responses are far from the kinds of outputs the reward model was trained to evaluate well. The paper emphasizes that the constraint helps prevent the model from drifting too far from the distribution on which the reward model is accurate.

The KL penalty also helps preserve generation diversity. If the policy were allowed to optimize reward without restraint, it could collapse toward a narrow set of high-scoring answers. The paper explicitly notes that this kind of mode collapse is something the reference constraint is meant to prevent. So the objective is really balancing two desires at once: maximize reward, but do so conservatively enough that the policy remains usable and well-behaved.

# Why the Reference Policy Matters for Stability and Diversity

The reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is usually set to $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$, and the policy being optimized is also initialized from that model. This is a central feature of the conventional pipeline. The reference policy acts as an anchor: it defines what it means to stay close to the original language model and provides the baseline distribution against which change is measured.

In the paper’s description, the reference policy matters for two reasons. First, it preserves stability by constraining updates to the region where the reward model was trained to be meaningful. Second, it maintains diversity by discouraging the policy from collapsing onto a small number of responses with artificially high reward. The need for this anchor is one reason RLHF is more complicated than ordinary supervised learning. The policy is not merely being taught a target response; it is being adjusted carefully so that improvement does not come at the cost of losing the behavior of the underlying language model.

# How PPO Is Typically Used to Optimize RLHF

Because the objective includes sampling from $\pi_\theta$ and a KL constraint, conventional RLHF typically uses reinforcement learning algorithms to carry out the policy optimization. The paper names PPO as the standard choice in prior RLHF systems. In this setup, the learned reward model supplies the signal, and PPO updates the policy to increase expected reward while respecting the KL penalty.

The important practical point is that PPO is being used to optimize a text-generation model, which means the policy must be sampled from during training. The paper stresses that this is a much more complicated procedure than supervised fine-tuning. Instead of simply comparing model outputs to labels in a fixed dataset, training now involves generating responses, evaluating them with a learned reward, and repeatedly updating the policy based on those samples. That makes the overall pipeline much more elaborate.

# The Computational Cost of Sampling in the Training Loop

One of the paper’s main criticisms of conventional RLHF is that it is computationally heavy. The policy has to be sampled during training, which means generation is part of the optimization loop itself. For language models, generation is expensive, especially at large scale. The training process therefore has to manage not just model updates but also the cost of repeatedly producing text from the current policy.

The pipeline also involves multiple models. There is the original supervised model, the separate reward model, and the policy being optimized. This multi-model structure makes the system harder to implement and slower to train. The paper highlights this complexity as a major practical burden of RLHF. In contrast to a single supervised objective, the RLHF loop combines preference modeling, reward estimation, and policy optimization, all of which have to work together.

# Practical Instability and Hyperparameter Sensitivity in RLHF

Beyond computation, the paper argues that conventional RLHF is often unstable. The reward model is only an estimate of human preferences, and the policy optimization step must rely on that estimate while also respecting a KL constraint. If the balance is off, the policy can drift too far, underperform, or become difficult to train. This is why the KL coefficient $\beta$ is so important: it determines how conservative the policy updates are.

The paper also notes that RLHF typically requires significant hyperparameter tuning. The best settings are not obvious in advance, and the optimization can behave differently depending on how reward is normalized, how strongly the reference policy is enforced, and how PPO is configured. These difficulties are one reason the authors view the conventional pipeline as powerful but cumbersome. It can produce impressive models, but it does so through a process that is expensive, sensitive, and operationally complex.

Taken together, the three-stage RLHF pipeline has a clear logic: start with a capable supervised model, learn a proxy for human preferences from pairwise comparisons, and then use reinforcement learning to move the policy toward higher-reward behavior without losing the structure of the original model. Its strength is that it can substantially improve alignment and usefulness. Its weakness, as the paper emphasizes, is that it requires multiple models, repeated sampling, and careful tuning to keep the optimization stable. This is exactly the complexity that Direct Preference Optimization is designed to remove.
