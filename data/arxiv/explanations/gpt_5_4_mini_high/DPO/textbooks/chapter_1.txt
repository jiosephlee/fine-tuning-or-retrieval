\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 1: The Alignment Problem in Language Models

# Why pretrained language models learn knowledge and skills without learning the exact behavior we want

The paper begins from a simple but important observation: large language models trained by unsupervised pretraining on very large text datasets acquire broad knowledge and impressive capabilities, but they do so without any direct supervision about how we would like them to behave when deployed. In other words, pretraining teaches the model to imitate the mixture of human text it sees, not to separate out the subset of responses that humans would consider safe, helpful, or otherwise desirable in a specific application.

This matters because the training data itself reflects many different human goals, priorities, and skill levels. Some of those goals are aligned with what we want from an assistant, while others are not. The result is that a pretrained model may know a great deal and still be poorly controlled. The paper emphasizes that the central alignment challenge is not teaching the model facts or general language ability; it is selecting the right behavior from within the model’s much broader space of learned behaviors.

# The distinction between model capabilities and model behavior

A major theme of the paper is that capabilities and behavior are not the same thing. A language model can possess the ability to represent or recognize many patterns without being properly biased toward the outputs we actually want to see. The model may understand a task, but that does not mean it will choose the task-appropriate response at generation time. This distinction is what makes alignment difficult: broad competence does not automatically translate into the desired policy.

The paper frames this as a problem of control. We want a model that can use its general knowledge and reasoning skills while still producing responses that match human preferences. That is why the authors emphasize “desired responses and behavior” as something that must be selected from a much wider set of “knowledge and abilities.” The alignment problem is therefore about shaping the model’s output distribution, not merely enlarging its internal store of learned information.

# Misalignment in the paper’s examples

To make the issue concrete, the paper gives examples that show why raw training data is not enough. Consider a coding assistant. We may want the model to understand common programming mistakes so that it can help correct them, but when it generates code, we do not want it to reproduce those mistakes. The model’s knowledge of errors is useful, yet the same knowledge can become harmful if it is not steered toward high-quality code generation.

The paper gives a second example involving misconceptions. A language model may need to be aware that a misconception is commonly believed by many people, but that does not mean it should answer queries as though the misconception were true. This example captures the alignment problem in a particularly clear way: the model may have learned the existence of a false belief from its data, but deployed behavior should reflect truthfulness and user intent, not merely statistical frequency in the training set.

# Why steerability, safety, and controllability matter

Because language models are increasingly used as interactive systems, the paper argues that selecting the right behavior is crucial for building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable. A model that can answer questions, write code, or summarize text is not automatically useful if its outputs are not reliably shaped toward the user’s goals. In deployment, the difference between “knows something” and “chooses to say the right thing” is decisive.

This is why the paper treats steerability as a central requirement rather than a nice extra feature. The goal is not just to create a model that can generate fluent text. The goal is to create a model whose generations can be biased toward the kinds of responses humans judge to be appropriate. The paper’s broader motivation is that if we can better align behavior with preference, then models become more useful and easier to control.

# Human demonstrations versus human preferences

The paper contrasts two broad kinds of supervision. The first is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations, where people provide high-quality target responses. This is the most straightforward way to teach a model what to say, and it is often used as an initial stage of training. The second is learning from human preferences, where people compare multiple model outputs and indicate which one they prefer.

The paper emphasizes that preference learning has become especially important because relative judgments are often easier to obtain than expert demonstrations. Rather than asking a labeler to write the perfect answer from scratch, one can ask which of two responses is better. That difference seems small, but it changes the practical data collection problem substantially. In the paper’s framing, this is one reason preference-based methods have become such a powerful route to alignment.

# Why relative quality judgments are easier to collect

The authors argue that relative preferences are easier to collect because they reduce the burden on annotators. A human labeler can compare two model completions and choose the better one without having to produce a complete reference answer. This makes it possible to gather large datasets of preference annotations even when gold-standard demonstrations would be expensive or difficult to produce.

The paper connects this idea to several tasks in which preference data has proved useful, including translation, summarization, story-telling, and instruction-following. In each case, the supervision signal is not an absolute score but a comparison: one response is better than another for a given prompt. The key point is that these relative judgments are not just easier to gather; they also fit naturally with the kind of training pipeline the paper aims to simplify.

# The practical burden of standard RLHF

Before introducing its new method, the paper reviews the standard reinforcement learning from human feedback pipeline. In its typical form, this pipeline has three phases: supervised fine-tuning, reward modeling, and reinforcement learning optimization. First, a pretrained language model is fine-tuned on high-quality downstream data to produce an SFT model. Then, the model generates pairs of answers to prompts, humans label which answer they prefer, and a reward model is trained to predict those preferences. Finally, a reinforcement learning algorithm is used to optimize the language model to maximize the learned reward while staying close to the reference policy through a KL-divergence penalty.

The paper stresses that this pipeline is powerful but cumbersome. It involves training multiple language models, sampling from the policy during training, and carefully managing the tradeoff between reward maximization and staying close to the original model. The need for reinforcement learning also makes the process more complex and often unstable. In practice, methods such as PPO are used, but the overall system is still much more difficult to implement and tune than standard supervised learning.

# The core claim of Direct Preference Optimization

The main claim of the paper is that this entire RLHF objective can be optimized directly from preferences, without separately fitting a reward model and then running reinforcement learning. The authors call their method Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO. The central idea is to reparameterize the reward model in a way that allows the optimal policy to be written in closed form. Once that reparameterization is in place, the learning problem becomes a simple classification objective over preference pairs.

In intuitive terms, DPO trains the policy to increase the relative probability of preferred responses and decrease the relative probability of dispreferred responses. The paper describes this as a binary cross-entropy loss over pairs of completions, but with an important dynamic weighting term that depends on how the current model’s implicit reward ordering compares with the observed preference. This means the policy is not updated in a naive way; instead, the update is strongest when the model is most wrong about which response should be preferred.

The significance of this design is that the policy network itself plays two roles at once. It is still a language model, but through the reparameterization it also defines an implicit reward. The paper’s core claim is therefore not merely that preferences can be used to fine-tune a model, but that the standard RLHF problem can be solved directly by optimizing the policy with a simple loss, without an explicit standalone reward model or a reinforcement learning loop.

# Main contributions and empirical claims of DPO

The paper’s practical contribution is a simpler and more stable preference optimization method that is computationally lightweight. According to the authors, DPO eliminates the need for sampling from the language model during fine-tuning and avoids the heavy hyperparameter tuning often required by RL-based approaches. It is presented as straightforward to implement, stable to train, and effective on offline preference data.

Empirically, the paper reports that DPO matches or improves on existing methods in several settings, using models up to $6$ billion parameters. In controlled sentiment generation, DPO produces the best reward-versus-KL frontier, outperforming PPO even when PPO is given access to the ground-truth reward. In summarization, DPO achieves a higher win rate than PPO and is more robust to sampling temperature. In single-turn dialogue, DPO also performs as well as or better than stronger baselines, including a computationally expensive best-of-$N$ method. The authors further report that DPO improves response quality while remaining much simpler to train, and that human evaluations broadly support the automated GPT-4 judgments used in the experiments.

Taken together, these results support the paper’s central message: the alignment problem for language models does not require a complicated reinforcement learning pipeline in order to learn from preferences. Instead, the model can be trained more directly, using a method that is both theoretically grounded and empirically competitive.
