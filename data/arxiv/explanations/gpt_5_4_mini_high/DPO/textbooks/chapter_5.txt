\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 5: Deriving Direct Preference Optimization

# Starting from the standard KL-regularized reward maximization objective

Direct Preference Optimization begins from the same constrained optimization problem used in standard RLHF. The language model is treated as a policy $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ that produces a completion $y$ given a prompt $x$, and the goal is to choose a policy that gets high reward while remaining close to a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)$. In the paper, this is written as

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr].
\]

The first term asks the policy to generate outputs with high learned reward, and the second term penalizes deviation from the reference model. The parameter $\beta$ controls the tradeoff. A smaller $\beta$ allows the policy to move further from the reference in order to chase reward, while a larger $\beta$ keeps the policy closer to the original model.

This objective is central because it captures the basic RLHF tension. On the one hand, we want the model to prefer responses that humans judge as better. On the other hand, we do not want the model to drift too far from the distribution where the reward model is reliable, or to collapse toward a narrow set of responses. The KL penalty is what enforces that restraint. DPO starts by accepting this objective, then asks whether the policy can be optimized directly from preference data without the usual reinforcement learning machinery.

# The closed-form optimal policy $\pi_r \propto \pi_\text{ref} \exp(r/\beta)$

A key step in the paper is that the KL-regularized reward maximization problem has an analytic solution. For a general reward function $r$, the optimal policy is

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right).
\]

This formula is the heart of the derivation. It says that the optimal policy is just the reference policy reweighted by an exponential of reward. If a completion has higher reward, its probability under the optimal policy increases relative to the reference. If it has lower reward, its probability is suppressed. The scaling by $\beta$ determines how sharply reward reshapes the reference distribution.

Intuitively, this is a soft version of “choose the best response.” The exponential ensures smooth reweighting rather than a hard argmax, so even lower-reward responses can retain some probability mass. That matters for language models, where diversity is important and where a narrow, deterministic answer distribution would be undesirable. The paper emphasizes that this closed-form expression is not just a theoretical curiosity: it is the bridge that lets one translate a reward-learning problem into a policy-learning problem.

# The role of the partition function $Z(x)$

The term

\[
Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\]

is the partition function. It normalizes the reweighted distribution so that the probabilities sum to one. Crucially, it depends on the prompt $x$ but not on the particular completion $y$. In the paper’s interpretation, it is the price of turning reward values into a valid probability distribution under the reference model.

This term is central to the derivation, but it is also what makes the closed-form policy difficult to use directly. Computing $Z(x)$ requires summing over all possible completions, which is intractable for language generation. Even if one has a maximum-likelihood estimate $r_\phi$ of the reward, the partition function remains expensive to estimate. That is why standard RLHF methods do not simply plug the reward into the closed-form policy expression and stop. DPO’s key insight is to rearrange the equations so that this intractable quantity disappears from the preference-learning objective.

# Rewriting reward as $\beta$ times the log policy ratio plus $\beta$ times $\log Z(x)$

Once the optimal policy is written in closed form, the reward can be solved for algebraically. Taking logs of the optimal-policy equation and rearranging gives

\[
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\]

This expression is the main change of variables used by DPO. It shows that reward can be represented by the log ratio between the learned policy and the reference policy, plus a term depending only on the prompt. The policy ratio captures how much the learned model departs from the reference on a given completion, while the $\log Z(x)$ term restores the proper normalization.

The important intuition is that the reward is only determined up to an additive function of $x$. That is enough for preference learning, because preferences compare completions for the same prompt. When two completions are compared under the same $x$, any term that depends only on $x$ will cancel. DPO exploits exactly this fact. Instead of trying to estimate reward absolutely, it learns the relative preference structure that matters for choosing among completions.

# Why the partition function is hard to use directly in practice

Although the closed-form mapping from reward to policy is elegant, it is not practical if taken literally. The partition function $Z(x)$ is a sum over all possible completions, which is enormous in language modeling. This means that directly evaluating the reward in terms of the policy can require an intractable normalization computation. The paper notes that this remains costly even if the reward itself is estimated by maximum likelihood.

This is one reason why standard RLHF pipelines become complicated. They typically avoid exact use of the closed-form policy and instead rely on actor-critic style optimization, often with learned value functions or sampled baselines to stabilize training. The partition function is also the source of variance problems: if one tries to estimate the relevant normalization terms from samples, the resulting gradients can become noisy and unstable. DPO’s goal is to avoid this obstacle by constructing a preference objective in which the troublesome normalization term never needs to be computed explicitly.

# Substituting the reward reparameterization into the Bradley-Terry preference model

The paper uses the Bradley-Terry model to connect latent rewards to observed human preferences. Under this model, if a human prefers $y_1$ to $y_2$ given prompt $x$, then the preference probability is

\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}
= \sigma\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\bigr).
\]

This form depends only on the difference between the two rewards. That is exactly what makes the DPO derivation work. If we substitute the reparameterized reward from the previous section into this model, then each reward becomes a log policy ratio plus the same prompt-dependent normalization term.

The useful result is that the human preference model can now be expressed in terms of policies rather than explicit rewards. Since the Bradley-Terry model is only sensitive to reward differences, the reparameterization turns the preference model into a direct statement about the learned policy’s relative probabilities for the two completions. In effect, the model says that a completion should be preferred when the policy assigns it a higher log probability relative to the reference policy.

# How the partition function cancels when taking reward differences

The cancellation of $Z(x)$ is the key algebraic simplification in DPO. If we write both rewards using

\[
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x),
\]

then subtracting the reward of $y_2$ from the reward of $y_1$ gives

\[
r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)
=
\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}
-
\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)}.
\]

The $\beta \log Z(x)$ term appears in both rewards, so it vanishes. This is why pairwise preference learning is so well matched to the reparameterization: the comparisons are made within a single prompt, and anything shared across the pair disappears.

This cancellation means the intractable normalization never enters the final preference probability. The paper stresses that this is not a trick specific to one reward function; it is a structural property of the preference model. As long as preferences are modeled through reward differences, the troublesome partition function can be eliminated from the learning objective.

# Expressing human preference probabilities directly in terms of the policy and the reference model

After substitution and cancellation, the paper obtains a preference probability written entirely in terms of the optimal policy and the reference model:

\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\]

This equation is the conceptual turning point of the paper. It shows that human preferences can be modeled directly as a function of the policy. The better the policy ranks the preferred response above the dispreferred one, relative to the reference model, the more likely the preference label becomes under the model.

The role of the reference policy is especially important here. The model is not learning absolute probabilities in isolation; it is learning how much to shift probability mass away from the reference in a way that matches preferences. This is why DPO is still grounded in the RLHF objective. The policy is not merely a language model trained on pairwise labels; it is the policy that would arise from the same KL-regularized reward maximization problem, expressed in a form that can be fit directly from data.

# The DPO objective as a binary cross-entropy loss over preferred and dispreferred completions

Once the preference probability is written in terms of the policy, maximum likelihood estimation gives a simple training objective. For a dataset of preference triples $\mathcal{D}=\{(x, y_w, y_l)\}$, the DPO loss is

\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\]

This is just binary cross-entropy applied to preference pairs. The model assigns a high probability to the human label when the preferred completion has a larger relative log probability than the dispreferred one. In this sense, DPO transforms the RLHF problem into a plain supervised learning problem over pairs of completions.

The significance of this objective is that it avoids the full RLHF pipeline. There is no explicit reward model to train and no reinforcement learning loop to optimize a policy against that reward. The same optimization target is still being pursued, but it is now encoded as a classification loss. That makes the method much simpler to implement and much lighter computationally, while still preserving the intended preference behavior.

# The connection between DPO and fitting an implicit reward model

DPO is not abandoning the idea of reward modeling; it is reparameterizing it. The paper defines the implicit reward associated with a policy as

\[
\hat{r}_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}.
\]

This quantity plays the role of a reward model even though there is no separate reward network. The policy itself carries the information that a reward model would normally store. This is why the paper says the language model is “secretly a reward model”: the relative probabilities assigned by the policy encode reward differences.

The gradient of the DPO objective makes this interpretation especially concrete. The update increases the likelihood of preferred completions and decreases the likelihood of dispreferred ones, but it does so with a weight that is larger when the current implicit reward model is ordering the pair incorrectly. In the paper’s expression, that weight is

\[
\sigma\bigl(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)\bigr).
\]

So if the model already strongly prefers the winning completion, the update is smaller; if it mistakenly prefers the losing completion, the update is larger. This dynamic weighting is important because a naive probability-ratio objective without it can lead to degeneration. In DPO, the policy and reward are not separate stages of training; they are two views of the same parameterization.

# How the same derivation extends beyond Bradley-Terry to Plackett-Luce ranking models

The paper develops the derivation using Bradley-Terry because it is the common pairwise preference model, but it also notes that the same logic extends to more general Plackett-Luce ranking models. The reason is structural: these models also express preference probabilities in terms of relative scores among completions, rather than absolute rewards. When several ranked responses are available, the same style of reparameterization can be applied.

This extension matters because it shows that DPO is not tied to a single narrow preference model. The paper argues that, under mild assumptions, the reward classes compatible with Plackett-Luce and Bradley-Terry can be represented by the same policy-based reparameterization. In other words, DPO does not reduce the expressive power of the reward model. It simply chooses a convenient representative from each equivalence class, one for which the optimal policy is analytically tractable and the preference loss can be optimized directly.

That is the broader lesson of the derivation. By starting from the standard KL-regularized RLHF objective, solving for the optimal policy, and substituting that solution into the preference model, the paper turns a difficult two-stage RLHF procedure into a single supervised learning problem. The resulting objective is simple, stable, and faithful to the original preference-learning goal.
