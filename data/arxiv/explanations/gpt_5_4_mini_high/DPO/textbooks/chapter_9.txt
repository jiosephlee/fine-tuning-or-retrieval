\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 9: A Controlled Testbed: Sentiment Generation Results

# Why controlled sentiment generation is an ideal diagnostic task

The sentiment-generation experiment is the paper’s cleanest testbed because it removes one of the hardest ambiguities in preference learning: uncertainty about the reward itself. In the other tasks studied in the paper, the “reward” is only indirectly inferred from human preferences, so improvements could come either from better policy optimization or from a better learned reward model. In controlled sentiment generation, that ambiguity disappears. The authors use a sentiment classifier as the true reward function, which means they can directly measure how well each method optimizes the intended objective.

This matters because the paper’s central claim is not merely that DPO produces good-looking outputs, but that it can optimize the same KL-constrained objective as RLHF methods in a simpler way. A controlled task makes that claim testable in the strongest possible sense. If DPO performs better here, then the gain cannot be attributed to hidden tricks in human evaluation or to differences in reward-model quality. Instead, it points directly to the optimization procedure itself.

The task is also a good diagnostic because it captures the basic structure of RLHF without the distraction of open-ended subjectivity. The prompt $x$ is a prefix of a movie review from IMDb, and the policy must generate a continuation $y$ with positive sentiment. This is simple enough to measure precisely, but still large enough to test the behavior of sequence models under preference optimization. In that sense, the experiment acts like a laboratory setting for comparing DPO and PPO under the exact objective they are supposed to solve.

# Training preferences from classifier-based sentiment comparisons

Rather than collecting human labels, the paper constructs preference pairs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier. The language model is prompted with review prefixes $x$, and it generates two candidate completions $y_1$ and $y_2$. The classifier then assigns a higher positive-sentiment probability to one completion than the other, and that ordering becomes the preference label: $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the preferred completion and $y_l$ is the dispreferred one.

This setup is important because it gives the authors a fully controlled preference dataset. Since the classifier is fixed, the reward signal is not noisy in the same way that human judgments can be. The paper uses this synthetic preference construction to create a dataset of comparisons that mirrors the structure of RLHF data, but with the advantage that the underlying reward can be evaluated exactly later. That makes it possible to separate the behavior of the optimization algorithm from the quality of the preference signal.

For supervised fine-tuning, the paper starts from GPT-2-large and fine-tunes it on reviews from the IMDb training split until convergence. This SFT model serves as the reference point for later preference optimization. In the terminology of the paper, the policy is then adjusted so that it produces outputs more aligned with the positive-sentiment preference data while staying close to this reference model. The classifier-generated comparisons are what let the paper test whether that adjustment is done efficiently.

# Computing average true reward and sequence-level KL on test prompts

To evaluate performance, the paper does not rely on a single scalar score. Instead, it measures two quantities that together define the RLHF objective: the average true reward and the KL divergence from the reference policy. In the sentiment setting, the “true reward” is the sentiment classifier score, so the authors can compute the actual reward achieved by each policy on test prompts.

The second quantity is the sequence-level KL divergence from the reference policy, defined in the paper as the sum of the per-timestep KL divergences. This matters because the KL term is what keeps the optimized policy from drifting too far away from the original model. A policy that maximizes reward aggressively but moves far from the reference model may be less desirable than a policy that achieves slightly less reward while remaining much closer to the base model. The paper’s evaluation therefore reflects the actual tradeoff in the RLHF objective rather than just output quality in isolation.

The authors evaluate policies on test prompts after every 100 training steps until convergence. This gives them a trajectory of performance, not just a final checkpoint. They also run multiple training configurations with different hyperparameters, so each method contributes several points to the final tradeoff curve. In total, this sweep includes 22 runs. The resulting data are used to build the reward-KL frontier for each algorithm.

# Reading reward-KL frontiers as efficiency curves rather than single numbers

A key idea in the paper’s controlled experiment is that the important object is not a single final score, but the frontier of achievable reward and KL tradeoffs. This is the right way to read the results because the RLHF objective itself balances reward maximization against staying near the reference policy. A method that gets high reward at the cost of very large KL is not necessarily better than one that gets slightly lower reward while remaining much closer to the reference model.

The reward-KL frontier is therefore an efficiency curve. It tells you how much reward a method can extract for a given amount of divergence from the base policy. In this view, a stronger algorithm is one whose frontier lies above and to the left of a weaker one: it achieves more reward for the same KL, or less KL for the same reward. That is exactly how the paper compares DPO and PPO in the sentiment task.

This perspective is especially important because it avoids hiding tradeoffs behind a single averaged number. The KL constraint is not a side issue; it is part of the objective. So the paper’s frontier-based evaluation directly answers the question of whether a method is optimizing the intended RLHF objective efficiently.

# How DPO compares with PPO when both target the same KL-constrained objective

The central result of the sentiment experiment is that DPO produces a much more efficient reward-KL frontier than PPO. The paper emphasizes that both methods are trying to solve the same underlying constrained optimization problem, but DPO does so through its direct preference objective rather than through an explicit RL loop. In the controlled sentiment setting, that difference shows up very clearly in the performance curves.

The comparison is especially meaningful because the authors do not hold PPO fixed to one brittle setting. They sweep PPO over several target KL values, specifically $\{3, 6, 9, 12\}$, so PPO gets a fair chance to explore different tradeoffs between reward and conservativeness. DPO is also evaluated over several values of its temperature-like parameter $\beta \in \{0.05, 0.1, 1, 5\}$. The point of this sweep is to see not just which method can produce one good checkpoint, but which method can trace out the better overall frontier.

Across this regime, DPO is described as strictly dominating PPO in reward/KL tradeoff. In practical terms, that means the DPO points are better positioned than the PPO points across the tested range: for comparable KL, DPO achieves more reward, and for comparable reward, it stays closer to the reference policy. This is a strong result because it shows that the direct preference formulation is not merely simpler; it is also more effective in this controlled environment.

# Why DPO’s frontier dominates PPO across the tested regime

The paper’s explanation for DPO’s advantage is not that it changes the objective, but that it optimizes the objective more cleanly. DPO uses a direct classification-style loss on preference pairs, and the update emphasizes the preferred completion relative to the dispreferred one while adapting the weight of each example according to how badly the implicit reward model currently ranks that pair. This weighting is part of what prevents the degenerate behavior that a naive probability-ratio approach can cause.

In the sentiment experiment, that translates into a better allocation of learning effort. The method is not wasting updates on examples that are already well ordered, and it is not pushing the model too aggressively in ways that cause unnecessary drift from the reference policy. The result is a frontier that is more efficient than PPO’s, even though PPO is also trying to balance reward and KL. The paper’s main empirical message here is that direct optimization of preferences can be a better way to realize the same theoretical objective.

The dominance is notable precisely because the task is controlled. Since the reward is known exactly, there is no ambiguity about whether the learned policy is optimizing the right signal. The fact that DPO still outperforms PPO in this setting suggests that the difference is not just about better supervision. It is about the optimization method itself.

# The surprising comparison to PPO-GT with access to ground-truth rewards

One of the most striking findings in the sentiment experiment is that DPO outperforms PPO even when PPO is given the ground-truth reward function. The paper calls this setting PPO-GT. This is a particularly strong baseline because it removes reward-model error from the comparison. If PPO were underperforming only because its learned reward model was imperfect, then PPO-GT should close the gap.

But that is not what happens. DPO still achieves a better reward-KL frontier than PPO-GT. The paper reports this as a surprising and important result, because it shows that having the correct reward signal is not enough to guarantee the best policy if the optimization method itself is inefficient. In other words, the bottleneck is not just reward estimation; it is also the way the policy is trained.

The paper even considers two PPO-GT implementations in the sentiment setting, including a modified version that normalizes rewards and further tunes hyperparameters to improve performance. Despite these adjustments, DPO remains stronger on the frontier. That makes the comparison especially compelling: DPO is not merely beating a weak PPO baseline, but an oracle-like version of PPO that already knows the true reward.

# What the results suggest about optimization efficiency versus reward-model accuracy

Because the sentiment experiment uses a true reward function, it helps disentangle two possible explanations for RLHF performance. One possibility is that RLHF methods fail mainly because reward models are inaccurate. Another is that the optimization step itself is unstable or inefficient. The results in this controlled setting strongly support the second explanation.

If reward-model accuracy were the main issue, then PPO-GT should have been the strongest method, or at least close to DPO. Instead, DPO remains better even when PPO is allowed to see the true reward. That means the direct preference formulation is not merely a workaround for reward-model errors; it is a more effective way to optimize a policy under the KL-constrained objective. The experiment therefore supports the paper’s broader view that direct preference optimization can replace the RL stage without losing performance.

This also helps explain why the paper emphasizes the closed-form connection between reward functions and optimal policies. DPO exploits that relationship directly, turning the preference learning problem into a simple binary classification objective on policy ratios. In the sentiment testbed, this simpler formulation is not just theoretically elegant. It produces a better empirical tradeoff between reward and conservativeness.

# The role of hyperparameter tuning and why DPO needs much less of it

Another practical lesson from the sentiment results is that DPO is easier to tune. The paper evaluates multiple hyperparameter settings for each method, but the overall message is that DPO reaches a strong frontier with very little tuning effort. This is important because in RLHF, tuning often matters a great deal: PPO performance can be sensitive to the target KL and other optimization details.

The paper’s comparison therefore includes a range of PPO settings and a range of DPO settings, but DPO still comes out ahead. That suggests its advantage is not due to a carefully hand-picked configuration. Instead, it seems to be robust across the tested parameter regime. In a practical workflow, that matters because it lowers the barrier to training preference-aligned language models.

This simplicity is part of the broader appeal of DPO. PPO-based RLHF typically requires training a reward model, then running an RL loop, and carefully managing how far the policy moves from the reference. DPO avoids that pipeline and still produces a superior reward-KL curve in the controlled sentiment task. The result is that one can get strong alignment behavior without the usual burden of reinforcement learning hyperparameter management.

# What these findings imply about RL-free preference optimization

The controlled sentiment experiment gives the clearest empirical support for the paper’s core claim: reinforcement learning is not necessary to optimize preference data effectively. In this task, DPO can match or exceed PPO-based RLHF while being much simpler to implement and train. The paper presents this as evidence that the policy itself can serve as an implicit reward model once the correct parameterization is chosen.

That conclusion is strongest in the sentiment setting because the reward is known. If DPO were only competitive on noisy human preference data, one might worry that the gains came from better modeling assumptions or evaluation quirks. But here the authors show that DPO can optimize the exact objective more efficiently than PPO, even when PPO is given the true reward. This makes the case that the RL component of RLHF is not only complex but also a source of inefficiency.

The sentiment results therefore serve as a proof of concept for RL-free preference optimization. They show that a direct classification loss over preference pairs can recover the desired behavior while preserving the KL regularization that keeps the model grounded in the reference distribution. In the paper’s broader story, this controlled experiment is the cleanest evidence that the DPO approach is both theoretically grounded and practically effective.
