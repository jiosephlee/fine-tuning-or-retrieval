\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 3: Mathematical Foundations for Preference Optimization

# Notation for Prompts, Completions, and Preference Pairs

The paper’s notation is built around three basic objects: a prompt $x$, a completion $y$, and a preference label that says which of two completions is better for that prompt. In the language-model setting, $x$ is the input text that conditions the model, and $y$ is the full generated response. A model with parameters $\theta$ is written as $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$, which means the probability that the policy assigns to an entire completion $y$ given the prompt $x$. When the paper discusses reward models, the corresponding scalar score is written as $r_\phi(x, y)$.

Preference data is expressed using pairs of completions. If a human prefers $y_1$ over $y_2$ for prompt $x$, the paper writes this as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the winning or preferred response and $y_l$ is the losing or dispreferred response. A dataset of such comparisons is denoted by $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$. This is the central training signal used throughout the paper: instead of asking for absolute ratings, the method relies on relative judgments between two candidate outputs for the same prompt.

This notation matters because it makes clear what is being learned. The model is not trying to predict a single label for a prompt in isolation. It is learning to rank or prefer one completion over another, conditioned on the same input. That comparison-based structure is what makes the Bradley-Terry preference model and the later optimization objectives possible.

# Sequence-Level Probabilities in Autoregressive Language Models

In the paper, $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ refers to the probability of a full completion, not just one token. This is important because the object being optimized in RLHF and DPO is a sequence-level policy over text outputs. A completion is treated as a whole trajectory of generated text, and the policy assigns it a probability conditioned on the prompt.

This sequence-level view is also why the optimization problem is not as simple as ordinary supervised learning. The paper emphasizes that language generation is discrete, so objectives that depend on sampled outputs are not directly differentiable in the usual way. That is one reason existing RLHF pipelines use reinforcement learning in the fine-tuning phase. In the experiments, the paper also measures sequence-level KL divergence, defined as the sum of per-timestep KL divergences, to quantify how far a trained policy moves from the reference policy.

The sequence-level notation is therefore doing a lot of work. It connects the probabilistic language model, the preference label, and the optimization objective. Once the policy is viewed as a distribution over full responses, the paper can compare reward maximization, preference classification, and KL regularization within a single mathematical framework.

# Pairwise Preference Data as a Learning Signal

The paper treats pairwise preference data as the main supervision signal for alignment. The idea is that, even if it is hard for humans to assign an absolute score to a response, it is often easier to say which of two responses is better. This is why the training data is made up of prompt-response pairs with an associated winner and loser.

The preference data is assumed to be sampled from an underlying human preference distribution $p^*$. In the RLHF pipeline described in the paper, prompts are used to sample two candidate responses, and human labelers choose the preferred one. The paper writes the preferred and dispreferred responses as $y_w$ and $y_l$, and these labels are then used either to fit a reward model or, in DPO, to train the policy directly.

This is a very different kind of supervision from standard supervised fine-tuning. In supervised fine-tuning, the model is trained on demonstrations of good outputs. In preference learning, the data does not say what the absolute best output is; it only says which of two outputs is better. The paper’s core claim is that this relative signal is enough to train useful and aligned language models, provided we interpret it with the right probabilistic model.

# The Bradley-Terry Model for Binary Preferences

The Bradley-Terry model is the paper’s main probabilistic model for pairwise preferences. It connects a latent reward function to the probability that one completion is preferred over another. Under this model, the probability that $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$ for prompt $x$ is

\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\]

The same expression can be written as a logistic function of the reward difference,

\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\!\left(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2)\right),
\]

where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. This form is especially intuitive: the larger the reward gap between the preferred and dispreferred responses, the closer the model’s predicted probability is to $1$. If the two rewards are equal, the model predicts a probability of $1/2$.

A key feature of the Bradley-Terry model is that only reward differences matter. Adding the same prompt-dependent constant to both rewards does not change the preference probability. This invariance is central to the theory in the paper, because it creates an identifiability issue: the reward is only determined up to a function of the prompt $x$. That fact reappears later in the discussion of reward normalization and the DPO reparameterization.

# The Plackett-Luce Family for Ranked Preferences

The paper notes that Bradley-Terry is a special case of a more general family of ranking models, the Plackett-Luce family. While Bradley-Terry handles pairwise comparisons, Plackett-Luce can represent preferences over several ranked answers. In other words, if a labeler provides a full ranking rather than just choosing a winner and loser, Plackett-Luce extends the same basic idea to that richer supervision signal.

The important point for the paper is that the same structural issues remain. Just as with Bradley-Terry, the reward is not uniquely identifiable from rankings alone, because only relative comparisons matter. The paper explicitly notes that the theory extends beyond pairwise comparisons and that the DPO derivation can also be formulated under Plackett-Luce. So although the paper focuses on pairwise data for simplicity, the underlying mathematical ideas are broader.

This broader family is useful to keep in mind because it shows that the preference-learning framework is not tied to one exact data format. What matters is that the model uses ordinal information, whether that is a single binary comparison or a larger ranking. The Bradley-Terry model is simply the cleanest place to start.

# Maximum-Likelihood Reward Learning from Comparisons

In the standard RLHF pipeline described in the paper, the first learning step is to fit a reward model from preference comparisons. The reward model is a parametric function $r_\phi(x, y)$ trained on the dataset $\mathcal{D}$. The training objective is maximum likelihood under the Bradley-Terry model.

Concretely, the paper writes the reward-model loss as

\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\left(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\right)\right].
\]

This is a negative log-likelihood objective for a binary classification problem. The model is asked to assign a higher reward to the preferred completion than to the dispreferred one. When the reward gap is large and correctly signed, the loss is small; when the model ranks the two responses incorrectly, the loss is large.

The paper also describes a common implementation detail: the reward network is often initialized from the supervised fine-tuned language model, with a linear layer added on top of the final transformer layer to output a single scalar reward value. That scalar plays the role of the latent utility in the preference model. In this way, reward learning turns preference data into a supervised prediction problem over reward differences.

# Binary Cross-Entropy and Logistic Loss in Preference Learning

The reward-model objective in the paper is not just similar to binary classification; it is exactly a logistic loss. The $\log \sigma(\cdot)$ term is the same structure used in binary cross-entropy, where the target is implicitly the event “the first completion is preferred over the second.” The model tries to make the reward difference $r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)$ positive and as large as the data supports.

This perspective makes the objective easy to interpret. If the model is uncertain and gives equal scores to both completions, the predicted probability of the preferred completion is $1/2$, and the loss remains relatively high. As the model becomes more confident in the correct ordering, the loss decreases. So the reward model is being trained to separate preferred from dispreferred responses with a probabilistic margin, rather than to predict a literal numeric score that has intrinsic meaning.

The same logistic structure later reappears in DPO, but with a different parameterization. Instead of fitting an explicit reward model and then using it in RL, DPO uses a direct policy-based expression inside the same logistic loss. That is one reason the paper presents the reward-model objective carefully: it is the template that DPO reuses in a different form.

# Reference Policies and KL-Regularized Policy Optimization

The paper’s RLHF objective does not simply maximize reward. It also constrains the learned policy to stay close to a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. The standard objective is

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_\theta(y \mid x)}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\!\left[\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\, \middle\|\, \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\right].
\]

Here $\beta$ controls the strength of the regularization. A larger $\beta$ means the policy is penalized more strongly for drifting away from the reference policy. In the paper, the reference policy is typically the initial supervised fine-tuned model $\pi^\text{SFT}$, and the training policy is also initialized from that same model.

The reason for this KL penalty is practical as well as theoretical. The paper explains that the reward model is only accurate in the region of behavior represented in the data, so if the policy moves too far away, the reward model can become unreliable. The KL penalty also helps preserve diversity and prevents the model from collapsing to a single high-reward response. In other words, the reference policy acts as an anchor that keeps optimization within a familiar and meaningful part of the response space.

This regularized objective is the mathematical heart of RLHF. It says that alignment is not just about maximizing a score; it is about improving quality while preserving closeness to a known-good baseline. DPO is built around the same principle, but it finds a way to optimize it without an explicit RL loop.

# PPO and Actor-Critic Fine-Tuning in RLHF

Because language generation is discrete, the KL-regularized objective cannot be optimized by ordinary gradient methods directly through the sampled text. The paper therefore describes reinforcement learning as the standard approach for the fine-tuning phase. In practice, prior work constructs a modified reward

\[
r(x, y) = r_\phi(x, y) - \beta\left(\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\right),
\]

and then maximizes it with PPO.

PPO is the main actor-critic method mentioned in the paper’s discussion of RLHF. The key point, from the paper’s perspective, is not the fine-grained mechanics of PPO but the fact that it is a reinforcement-learning algorithm used to optimize the policy under a reward signal. This introduces a much more complicated training pipeline than supervised learning: one must fit a reward model, sample from the policy during training, and then optimize the policy while maintaining stability under the KL constraint.

The paper emphasizes that this RL stage is often unstable and expensive. It requires training multiple models, depends on sampling from the language model during optimization, and can be sensitive to hyperparameters. That practical difficulty is one of the main motivations for DPO. The paper’s contribution is to keep the same basic regularized objective while removing the explicit RL step.

# Reward Normalization and Identifiability Issues

A central mathematical subtlety in the paper is that rewards are not uniquely identifiable from preference data. The paper defines two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ as equivalent if

\[
r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)
\]

for some function $f$. This means the two rewards differ only by a prompt-dependent offset. Under the Bradley-Terry model, and more generally under Plackett-Luce, equivalent reward functions induce the same preference distribution. The paper also states that reward functions from the same equivalence class induce the same optimal policy under the KL-constrained RL objective.

This non-identifiability is not a bug in the model; it is a structural feature of preference data. Since preferences depend on relative differences, any prompt-dependent baseline disappears from the comparisons. As a result, the reward model is only determined up to an additive function of $x$. The paper notes that this under-specification often forces practitioners to impose extra normalization constraints if they want guarantees for maximum-likelihood estimation.

Prior work often normalizes rewards to lower variance, and the paper mentions normalizing the rewards so that they have zero mean for each prompt. This kind of normalization chooses a particular representative from the larger equivalence class of reward functions without changing the preference probabilities. The DPO theory section then builds on this same fact, showing that one can reparameterize rewards in terms of a policy and a reference policy while fixing the prompt-dependent ambiguity. The broader lesson is that preference optimization is fundamentally about identifying a useful equivalence class of rewards, not a uniquely meaningful scalar score.
