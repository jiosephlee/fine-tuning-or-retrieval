\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How do you derive $\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)$ from the KL-constrained RLHF objective?
Question:
The paper says that for the objective
$$
\max_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(y\mid x)\|\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)),
$$
the optimal policy has the closed form
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$
I can see that this looks like a Gibbs distribution, but I am not following the derivation. Is this obtained with a Lagrange multiplier enforcing $\sum_y \pi(y\mid x)=1$? If so, can someone show the steps carefully?
Answer:
Yes. The optimization decomposes independently for each prompt $x$, so you can fix an $x$ and solve for the conditional distribution $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$ alone. Write $q_y=\pi(y\mid x)$, $p_y=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$, and $r_y=r(x,y)$. Then the objective for this fixed $x$ is
$$
\max_{\{q_y\}} \sum_y q_y r_y-\beta \sum_y q_y \log \frac{q_y}{p_y},
\qquad \text{subject to } \sum_y q_y=1.
$$
So yes, this is exactly a Lagrange-multiplier problem.

Introduce a multiplier $\lambda$ for the normalization constraint and form
$$
\mathcal{L}(q,\lambda)
=
\sum_y q_y r_y
-\beta \sum_y q_y \log \frac{q_y}{p_y}
+\lambda\left(\sum_y q_y-1\right).
$$
Now differentiate with respect to $q_y$:
$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial q_y}
=
r_y-\beta\left(\log\frac{q_y}{p_y}+1\right)+\lambda.
$$
At an optimum this must be zero, so
$$
r_y-\beta\left(\log\frac{q_y}{p_y}+1\right)+\lambda=0.
$$
Rearranging gives
$$
\log\frac{q_y}{p_y}
=
\frac{r_y}{\beta}+\frac{\lambda-\beta}{\beta},
$$
hence
$$
q_y
=
p_y \exp\!\left(\frac{r_y}{\beta}\right)\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda-\beta}{\beta}\right).
$$
The last exponential factor does not depend on $y$, so it is just a normalizing constant. Enforcing $\sum_y q_y=1$ gives
$$
1=\sum_y q_y
=
\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda-\beta}{\beta}\right)
\sum_y p_y \exp\!\left(\frac{r_y}{\beta}\right),
$$
so if we define
$$
Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right),
$$
then
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)=q_y
=
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right),
$$
which is exactly the paper’s equation.

An equivalent and often cleaner way to see it is to rewrite the objective as
$$
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi}[r(x,y)]-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
=
-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_r)+\beta \log Z(x),
$$
where $\pi_r$ is the distribution above. Since $\beta \log Z(x)$ does not depend on $\pi$, the objective is maximized when $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_r)=0$, i.e. when $\pi=\pi_r$. This is the same “exponentially tilted reference policy” that appears in the paper’s derivation.

So the short answer is: yes, it comes directly from a Lagrange multiplier enforcing normalization, and the result is the Gibbs/Boltzmann form relative to the reference policy.
