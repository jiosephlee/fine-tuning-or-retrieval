\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### If DPO and PPO optimize the same KL-regularized reward objective, why can DPO outperform PPO in the experiments?
Question:
One of the most surprising claims in the paper is that DPO and PPO target the same underlying RLHF objective, yet DPO often gets a better reward-KL frontier and even beats PPO with access to ground-truth rewards. If the optimization target is the same, should they not converge to the same solution given enough capacity? Is the difference purely due to PPO optimization instability and variance, or does DPO actually optimize a different practical objective once function approximation and finite data are taken into account?
Answer:
No contradiction is really hiding here: “DPO and PPO optimize the same objective” means they target the same *ideal RLHF solution* in the realizable, infinite-data limit, not that they follow the same optimization path in practice.

The key point in the paper is that the KL-regularized RLHF problem has a closed-form optimal policy
$$
\pi^*(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
$$
and under the Bradley-Terry preference model this can be reparameterized so that the preference probability depends only on the policy ratio $\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. That gives DPO’s loss,
$$
-\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right),
$$
which is a direct supervised objective on preference pairs. So DPO is not “a different goal”; it is a different parameterization of the same goal.

Why can it still outperform PPO? Because PPO is only an *approximate* optimizer for that goal. In the paper’s RLHF setup, PPO has to go through a learned reward model, an actor-critic loop, sampling from the current policy, and a value/baseline estimate. The paper explicitly points out that the normalization term in the equivalent reward can be viewed as a soft value function, and that estimating or approximating this term is a major source of variance and instability. Even when the reward is ground truth, PPO still has noisy policy-gradient updates, clipping effects, and sensitivity to hyperparameters. DPO avoids all of that by turning the problem into a stable binary classification loss over fixed preference data.

So the best way to think about the result is: in theory, the optima coincide; in practice, the finite-sample, finite-capacity, and imperfect-optimization problems are different. DPO is a much better-conditioned surrogate for reaching that optimum, and that is why it can trace a better reward-KL frontier and even beat PPO with access to ground-truth rewards in the paper’s experiments.

One subtle but important consequence is that DPO is not “just PPO with a different optimizer”; it changes the practical training problem. PPO is trying to solve the RL objective by noisy trajectory-level updates, whereas DPO directly fits the policy to the preference comparisons. Under approximation and finite data, those are not equivalent procedures, even if they share the same underlying target policy.
