\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Does DPO assume there are only two possible responses for each prompt?
Question:
I may be misunderstanding the setup of Direct Preference Optimization. The training data always seems to be a prompt $x$ together with a preferred answer $y_w$ and a dispreferred answer $y_l$. That makes it look like the model is only learning to choose between two fixed responses, but language modeling has an enormous output space. How does optimizing on pairwise comparisons over $(y_w, y_l)$ translate into a policy over all possible completions $y$ for the same prompt?
Answer:
No. The two responses $y_w$ and $y_l$ are just the two sampled candidates in each training example; they are not the only possible completions for the prompt.

What DPO is learning is still a full language model policy $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ over the entire space of completions $y$. The pairwise preference data is only the supervision signal. In the paper’s Bradley-Terry setup, a human comparison is modeled as depending on the difference of latent rewards, $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)=\sigma(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2))$. DPO then uses the reparameterization $r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$, and when you plug that into the pairwise preference model, the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels. That is the key point: pairwise data is enough because the preference model only cares about *relative* quality, not absolute scores.

So the loss in Eq. $\eqref{eq:optimum_model}$ does not mean “choose between only two fixed outputs.” It means “make the preferred sampled completion more likely than the dispreferred one, in a way consistent with a normalized policy over all sequences.” Since $\pi_\theta$ is a standard autoregressive LM, changing its parameters affects probabilities of many completions, not just the two examples in the batch. In this sense, DPO is much closer to learning a ranking or scoring function over a huge action space from pairwise comparisons than to training a binary classifier over two actions.

The paper also notes that the same framework extends beyond pairwise labels: with several ranked answers, one can use a Plackett-Luce style model. So the pairwise format is a convenience of the data collection procedure, not a restriction that the model only has two possible outputs per prompt.
