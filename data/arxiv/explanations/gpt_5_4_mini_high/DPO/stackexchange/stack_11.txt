\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What prevents DPO from collapsing to extreme probabilities on the training pairs?
Question:
Since the DPO loss rewards making the preferred completion more likely than the dispreferred one, I would expect the model to drive
$$
\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}
$$
to be as large as possible on every training example. Why does this not just push the model toward memorizing the dataset or making probabilities overly sharp? Is the only protection the reference model in the ratio, or is there some other regularizing effect in the DPO objective?
Answer:
What keeps DPO from simply pushing the training winners to probability $1$ is that DPO is **not** optimizing the raw margin
$$
\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)
$$
in isolation. In the paper, the loss is built from the *reference-normalized* quantity
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
$$
so the model is really learning a policy that is better than the reference on preferred completions, not just one that makes winners absolutely more likely. This is exactly the reparameterization of the KL-regularized RLHF objective; the reference model is therefore not just a baseline for convenience, it is the mechanism that plays the role of the KL constraint. In the paper’s own notation, the optimal policy has the form
$$
\pi^*(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
$$
so moving too far from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is inherently penalized. If the model tried to make every training pair extreme, that would correspond to an increasingly large deviation from the reference policy, which is exactly what the objective is designed to avoid.

There is also a very practical stabilizing effect in the gradient. The paper writes the DPO gradient as being weighted by
$$
\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr),
$$
which is large only when the model is getting the ordering wrong, and small once the preferred answer is already ranked above the dispreferred one. So DPO does not keep applying a strong force to make the margin larger and larger on examples it already fits; the update naturally saturates. That is the “dynamic, per-example importance weight” the authors emphasize, and they explicitly say that a naive version without this weighting can degenerate.

So the short answer is: the protection is mainly the **KL-to-reference structure**, with the **saturating logistic loss / dynamic weighting** providing an additional stabilizing effect. DPO can still overfit if $\beta$ is badly chosen or the reference model is poor, but it is not an unconstrained “push winner probability to $1$” objective. It is a preference version of KL-regularized policy optimization.
