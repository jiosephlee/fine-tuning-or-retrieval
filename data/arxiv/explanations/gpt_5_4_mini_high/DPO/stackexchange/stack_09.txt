\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How is DPO different from unlikelihood training if both increase $\log \pi(y_w\mid x)$ and decrease $\log \pi(y_l\mid x)$?
Question:
The paper compares DPO to unlikelihood-style training and says the important difference is a weight of the form
$$
\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)).
$$
But at a high level both methods seem to do the same thing: make the preferred answer more likely and the dispreferred answer less likely. Can someone explain, maybe with a simple example, what this weighting changes in practice and why removing it can lead to degeneration?
Answer:
At the level of the *sign* of the update, you are right: both methods try to make $y_w$ more likely and $y_l$ less likely. The key difference in DPO is that this is **not done with a fixed-strength push**. DPO is optimizing a pairwise logistic loss on the *relative* log-probability ratio to the reference policy, and its gradient contains the factor

$$
\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr),
\qquad
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$

This factor makes DPO behave like ordinary classification: pairs the model already gets right contribute very little gradient, while pairs the model gets wrong contribute a lot. In other words, DPO is not just saying “move $y_w$ up and $y_l$ down”; it is saying “move them up/down *in proportion to how badly the current policy violates the preference*.”

A simple way to see the difference is to imagine two preference pairs. For the first pair, the current model already strongly prefers the winner, say $\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)=5$. Then the DPO weight is $\sigma(-5)\approx 0.007$, so this example is basically ignored. For the second pair, suppose the model actually prefers the loser, so the margin is $-1$. Then the weight is $\sigma(1)\approx 0.73$, and the update is much larger. Unlikelihood-style training, by contrast, tends to treat both pairs much more uniformly: it keeps pushing down $y_l$ and up $y_w$ even when the pair is already well separated. That is exactly what the paper means by “naive” training without the weighting.

Why does that matter? Because language models are normalized distributions: if you keep endlessly suppressing dispreferred outputs without damping the easy cases, probability mass has to go somewhere. In practice, that can lead to overly sharp, low-diversity, or generic generations, which the paper calls degeneration. The sigmoid weight acts as a built-in brake: once the policy already ranks the pair correctly, the gradient dies away, so the model does not keep “over-correcting” the same examples. That is also why DPO is tied to the Bradley-Terry likelihood and the KL-regularized RL objective, while plain unlikelihood is just a heuristic push in the same general direction.

So the short version is: DPO and unlikelihood point in a similar direction, but DPO uses an **adaptive, confidence-dependent step size** and is **reference-relative**. That weighting is not cosmetic; it is what prevents the method from aggressively lowering probabilities everywhere and collapsing the model’s behavior.
