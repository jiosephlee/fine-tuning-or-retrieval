\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why is the implicit reward in DPO defined as $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$ instead of just using $\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)$?
Question:
The paper says 'your language model is secretly a reward model' and then defines an implicit reward
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}.
$$
I do not understand why the reward is a log-ratio against the reference policy rather than simply the model's own log-probability of the response. Is the point that reward should measure how much the trained policy departs from the reference? How should I interpret this quantity conceptually?
Answer:
The short answer is that DPO is not trying to define an absolute “goodness score” for a completion. It is choosing a reward parameterization that is exactly compatible with the KL-regularized RLHF objective the paper starts from.

In the paper, the underlying RLHF problem is
$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\right).
$$
If you solve this optimization exactly, the optimal policy has the form
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$
Rearranging gives
$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x),
$$
where $Z(x)$ is the normalizing constant. Since the Bradley-Terry preference model only depends on reward *differences* $r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)$, the term $\beta \log Z(x)$ cancels out. So, up to that irrelevant $x$-dependent constant, any reward in the correct equivalence class can be written as
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$

Conceptually, this means the reward is measuring not “how likely the model thinks $y$ is” in isolation, but “how much more the trained policy prefers $y$ than the reference policy does.” That is exactly the quantity relevant to KL-regularized alignment: you want to move probability mass toward preferred responses, but only relative to the base model. The reference model is the baseline that makes this a *preference update* rather than just a raw likelihood score.

If you used only $\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)$, you would lose that baseline. Then the score would mix together two things: the model’s current assignment of probability and the original model’s prior behavior. In the paper’s derivation, that would no longer match the reward class induced by the RLHF objective, and you would no longer get the clean cancellation that makes DPO work.

So the intuition is: $\hat r_\theta$ is a kind of advantage function in log-probability space. It is zero when $\pi_\theta=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, positive when the policy upweights a response relative to the reference, and negative when it downweights it. That is why the log-ratio, not the raw log-probability, is the natural object in DPO.
