\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What exactly does the hyperparameter $\beta$ control in DPO?
Question:
The paper says $\beta$ controls how far the policy can deviate from the reference model, but I am confused about the direction of the effect. In the optimal policy formula,
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta),
$$
smaller $\beta$ seems to magnify reward differences, which would make the policy less conservative. In the DPO loss, however, $\beta$ multiplies the log-ratio terms. Can someone give an intuitive explanation of how changing $\beta$ changes the learned policy, and why this is consistent with the KL-regularization view?
Answer:
In DPO, $\beta$ is the same knob as the KL penalty strength in the RLHF objective; the fact that it appears as $1/\beta$ in one equation and as a multiplier in another is just a matter of which quantity has been solved for.

In the original RL objective from the paper,
$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}),
$$
larger $\beta$ means “pay more for moving away from the reference model,” so the optimal policy stays closer to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Solving that constrained problem gives
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right).
$$
This is exactly a softmax with temperature $\beta$. Smaller $\beta$ makes the exponential sharper, so reward differences have a bigger effect and the policy becomes less conservative; larger $\beta$ flattens the distribution and keeps the policy closer to the reference.

The DPO loss looks different only because the reward has been reparameterized in terms of the policy:
$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
$$
When you plug this into the Bradley-Terry preference model, the partition function cancels, and you get the DPO margin
$$
\beta\left(\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right).
$$
So in DPO, $\beta$ is not “encouraging deviation” on its own; it is scaling the implicit reward units. For a fixed preference signal, a larger $\beta$ means the same reward difference can be represented with a smaller change in log-probability ratios, so the learned policy needs to move less away from the reference. A smaller $\beta$ means the policy must create larger log-ratio gaps to explain the same preferences, which corresponds to a less conservative, more shifted policy.

So the two views are consistent: in the RL view, $\beta$ is the KL regularization strength; in the DPO view, it is the inverse temperature that rescales the preference margin. Same hyperparameter, different algebra. In practice, the paper treats it as the policy-conservativeness knob.
