\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How should I interpret the theorem that any Bradley-Terry-consistent reward class can be written as $r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$?
Question:
The main theorem says that, under mild assumptions, all reward classes consistent with the Plackett-Luce or Bradley-Terry preference model can be represented through the reparameterization
$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}.
$$
This sounds very strong, and I am not sure what is being claimed. Is the theorem saying that DPO loses no expressive power compared with an explicit reward model, except for the usual ambiguity of adding $f(x)$? Also, what are the 'mild assumptions' doing here, and are there realistic cases where they fail?
Answer:
Yes — but the key is that the theorem is about *equivalence classes* of rewards, not about a unique reward function.

Under Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce, preferences only depend on reward *differences* for the same prompt $x$. So if you replace a reward $r(x,y)$ by $r(x,y)+f(x)$, nothing changes: the preference probabilities are identical, and the KL-regularized optimal policy is identical too. That is the usual non-identifiability.

What the theorem is saying is that, for any such reward class, you can choose a representative of the form
$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
This is not an extra assumption; it is just rewriting the closed-form KL-regularized optimum
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)
$$
and then solving for $r$. When you do that, you get
$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta \log Z(x),
$$
where $Z(x)$ is the normalizing constant. The extra term $\beta\log Z(x)$ depends only on $x$, so it is exactly the ambiguity the paper already factors out by defining rewards modulo $f(x)$. In other words, DPO is not claiming to recover a unique reward; it is choosing a canonical representative of each reward class, one whose partition function is absorbed into the policy normalization.

So your interpretation is basically right: within the Bradley-Terry/Plackett-Luce setup, DPO does not lose expressive power relative to an explicit reward model, except for the unavoidable $f(x)$ ambiguity. More precisely, it preserves the same set of preference distributions and the same optimal policy class. What it does *not* claim is that every imaginable preference pattern can be represented — only those consistent with the Bradley-Terry/Plackett-Luce assumptions.

The “mild assumptions” are the usual ones needed for the log-ratio reparameterization to make sense. The paper is relying on the existence of a valid normalized optimal policy, which means the partition function
$$
Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)
$$
must be finite, and the reference policy must have support where the optimal policy needs it. If $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)=0$ for some $y$, then the ratio is undefined there and that action/completion is effectively unreachable under this parameterization. Likewise, if the reward is so pathological that $Z(x)$ diverges, the closed-form normalization breaks. In the language-model setting of the paper, these are mostly theoretical edge cases; the practical assumption is that the reference LM assigns positive probability to the completions you care about and that the relevant sums are well behaved.

So the clean takeaway is: DPO is not a weaker model than explicit reward modeling under the paper’s preference assumptions. It is a reparameterization of the same underlying equivalence classes, chosen so that the reward-model step and RL step collapse into one classification objective.
