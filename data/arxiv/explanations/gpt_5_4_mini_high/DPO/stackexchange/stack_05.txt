\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### If the original RLHF objective is an expectation over $y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$, why does DPO not need on-policy sampling during training?
Question:
In standard RLHF, the optimization objective involves sampling responses from the current policy because we are maximizing expected reward under $\pi_\theta$. But DPO trains on a fixed offline dataset of preference pairs and uses a binary cross-entropy loss instead. If DPO is supposedly optimizing the same KL-regularized objective, why is it valid to remove the on-policy sampling step entirely? Is the equivalence exact, or only exact under the Bradley-Terry model and infinite data?
Answer:
The key point is that DPO is **not** trying to estimate the original policy-gradient objective by Monte Carlo under the current policy. Instead, it first uses the KL-regularized RLHF problem to derive a closed-form relationship between the optimal policy and the latent reward,

$$
\pi^*(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
$$

and then rewrites the reward as

$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta \log Z(x).
$$

Under the Bradley-Terry preference model, only reward differences matter, so the unknown normalization term $\beta \log Z(x)$ cancels out. That is why the preference probability can be written directly in terms of the policy:

$$
p^*(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right).
$$

Once you have this, training becomes ordinary maximum likelihood on a fixed dataset of preference pairs,

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal D}\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right),
$$

so there is no need to sample fresh responses from $\pi_\theta$ during training. The current policy only appears inside the log-probabilities of the already-collected responses in $\mathcal D$, which is why DPO is an offline, supervised-style optimization problem rather than an on-policy RL loop.

So the equivalence is **exact in the paper’s idealized setting**, but only under the modeling assumptions they state: the KL-regularized RL objective, a Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce preference model, and a sufficiently expressive policy class. In that sense, DPO is exactly optimizing the same target as RLHF, just after an analytic change of variables. But the usual caveats still apply: with finite data, imperfect optimization, or misspecified preferences, you only get an approximation. So the exactness is mathematical at the level of the assumed model, and asymptotic/statistical only in the sense of recovering the true optimum from enough correctly generated preference data.
