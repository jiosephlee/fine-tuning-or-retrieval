\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

# Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model

Large language models are usually trained in two very different phases. First, they learn to predict text from huge amounts of data. Then, if we want them to behave more helpfully or safely, we often add a second stage where humans compare model answers and say which one is better.

The standard RLHF pipeline turns those comparisons into a separate reward model, and then uses reinforcement learning to make the language model maximize that reward while staying close to its original behavior. The paper behind Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO, makes a striking claim: you do not actually need a separate reward model at all. With the right mathematical reparameterization, the language model itself can play that role implicitly.

## The usual RLHF pipeline

In the standard setup, you start with a supervised fine-tuned model, sample two candidate answers for a prompt, and ask humans which answer they prefer. Those pairwise preferences are used to train a reward model. Then a policy optimization method such as PPO updates the language model to produce outputs that score highly under that reward model, while a KL penalty keeps it from drifting too far from the reference model.

That works, but it is awkward in practice. You are training at least two models, and the second stage requires reinforcement learning, which is often expensive and unstable. The core question in this paper is whether the two stages are really necessary.

## The key observation: reward is only defined up to a constant

The paper starts from a standard KL-constrained RL objective:

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(y \mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta \, \mathrm{KL}\bigl[\pi_\theta(y \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\bigr].
\]

If you solve this optimization problem exactly, the optimal policy has a closed form:

\[
\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
\]

This equation is the heart of DPO. It says that the optimal policy is just the reference policy, tilted by an exponential of the reward.

If you rearrange it, you can express reward in terms of the policy:

\[
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\]

The important part is the log-probability ratio. The extra term \(\beta \log Z(x)\) depends only on the prompt \(x\), not on which answer you pick.

That matters because preference models such as Bradley-Terry only care about differences between two completions, not absolute reward values. If two answers are compared for the same prompt, the prompt-dependent constant cancels out. So the model only needs to learn the relative ranking.

In plain language: if an answer becomes more likely under the policy relative to the reference model, that increase can be interpreted as higher reward.

## What “secretly a reward model” means

This is the paper’s memorable point. The language model is not literally being turned into a separate reward network with a scalar output head. Instead, the policy itself defines an implicit reward:

\[
\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}.
\]

So the model’s own probability shift away from the reference model acts like a reward score. If the model assigns a preferred answer a higher relative probability than a rejected answer, that corresponds to a better reward under the RLHF objective.

That lets the authors replace the usual reward-model-plus-RL pipeline with a single classification loss:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}}) =
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}
\left[
\log \sigma \left(
\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)}
-
\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}
\right)
\right].
\]

Here \(y_w\) is the preferred answer and \(y_l\) is the rejected one. The model is trained to make the preferred completion more likely than the rejected one, relative to the reference policy.

## Why this is simpler than RLHF

This reframing removes the two most cumbersome parts of RLHF: fitting a separate reward model and then running reinforcement learning on top of it. DPO turns preference learning into a straightforward offline optimization problem. There is no need to sample from the model inside the training loop, and the method is much easier to implement.

It also changes the optimization dynamics in a useful way. The gradient of the DPO loss increases the likelihood of preferred answers and decreases the likelihood of rejected answers, but it weights each example by how wrong the model currently is. Examples where the implicit reward orders the answers poorly get more emphasis. The paper notes that a naive version without this weighting can lead to degeneration.

## Why the math is not just a trick

This is not a hack that narrows the space of possible reward functions. The paper shows that, under the Bradley-Terry and related Plackett-Luce preference models, reward functions that differ only by a prompt-dependent constant produce the same preferences and the same optimal policy. DPO simply chooses a convenient representative from each equivalence class, one that can be written as a log ratio to the reference model.

So the reparameterization does not reduce generality. It makes the optimal policy analytically tractable.

## What happens in practice

The empirical results in the paper show that this simplification does not come at the cost of performance. On controlled sentiment generation, DPO achieved a better reward-versus-KL frontier than PPO, even when PPO had access to ground-truth rewards. On summarization and single-turn dialogue, DPO matched or improved on PPO-based RLHF while being substantially simpler to train. The method was also more robust to sampling temperature than PPO in the reported experiments.

## The main takeaway

The deep idea in DPO is that preference optimization does not need to go through an explicit reward model. If you compare a preferred answer against a rejected one, the language model can learn the same signal directly through its probabilities relative to a reference model.

That is why the paper can say that your language model is secretly a reward model. The reward is not stored in a separate network. It is encoded in how the policy shifts probability mass toward answers humans prefer.
