\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

# The Math Idea That Makes DPO Work—Without the Headache

Reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, has become the standard way to turn a pretrained language model into something more useful and controllable. But the classic pipeline is awkward: first train a reward model from human preferences, then run reinforcement learning to make the policy chase that reward while staying close to the original model. It works, but it is expensive, fiddly, and often unstable.

The core contribution of Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO, is that you do not actually need that two-stage setup. The paper shows that if you write the RLHF objective in the right way, you can train the language model directly on preference pairs with a simple binary cross-entropy loss. The trick is a change of variables that lets the policy play the role of an implicit reward model.

## RLHF starts with a reward, but the optimal policy already contains one

The standard RLHF objective is a tradeoff between two things:

\[
\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi(y \mid x)}[r(x, y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi(y \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)].
\]

Here, $r(x,y)$ is the reward for response $y$ to prompt $x$, $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the reference model, and the KL term keeps the new policy from drifting too far away.

A useful fact is that the optimal policy for this objective has a closed form:

\[
\pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
\]

This equation is the heart of DPO. It says that the best policy under a KL constraint is just the reference policy, reweighted exponentially by reward. In other words, reward and policy are two views of the same object.

If you rearrange this equation, you can solve for the reward:

\[
r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\]

That second term, $\beta \log Z(x)$, depends only on the prompt $x$, not on the completion $y$. That turns out to matter a lot.

## Why the annoying constant disappears

Preference data are usually modeled with Bradley-Terry style comparisons. If a human prefers $y_w$ over $y_l$ for prompt $x$, the probability of that preference is written as

\[
p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x,y_w) - r^*(x,y_l)),
\]

where $\sigma$ is the logistic function.

Now substitute the reward expression above into the difference. Because both completions share the same prompt, the $Z(x)$ terms cancel:

\[
r^*(x,y_w) - r^*(x,y_l)
= \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w \mid x)}
- \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}.
\]

This is the key move. The preference probability can now be written directly in terms of the policy, with no explicit reward model at all. So instead of learning $r_\phi(x,y)$ and then using RL to find a policy that maximizes it, we can fit the policy so that it explains the observed preferences.

That gives the DPO objective:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) =
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}
\left[
\log \sigma\left(
\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w \mid x)}
- \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}
\right)
\right].
\]

If that looks like binary classification, that is because it is. For each prompt, the model is asked to make the preferred answer score higher than the rejected one.

## The intuition: make the preferred answer relatively more likely

A good way to read the DPO loss is as a pairwise ranking rule. The model should increase the relative log-probability of the winner and decrease the relative log-probability of the loser, compared with the reference model.

The reference model matters because it anchors the update. DPO is not trying to make every preferred answer infinitely likely. It is trying to move the policy in a controlled way so that, relative to the base model, preferred completions become more likely than dispreferred ones.

The scalar $\beta$ controls how conservative that move is. Larger $\beta$ means the model is pushed harder toward the preference signal; smaller $\beta$ keeps it closer to the reference policy.

There is also an important detail in the gradient: DPO does not treat every preference pair equally. Examples where the model is currently getting the ordering wrong receive more weight. This dynamic weighting is part of why the method avoids the collapse you can get from a naive “increase the winner, decrease the loser” objective.

## Why this does not throw away generality

One concern with this kind of reparameterization is whether it restricts the space of rewards you can represent. The paper argues it does not.

The reason is that reward functions are only identifiable up to a prompt-dependent constant. If you add a function $f(x)$ to every completion for the same prompt, preference probabilities do not change, and neither does the optimal policy under the KL-constrained RL objective. DPO effectively chooses one canonical representative from each such equivalence class: the one induced by the policy ratio to the reference model.

So DPO is not making the problem smaller in a bad way. It is just choosing a representation that makes the optimization tractable.

## Why this matters in practice

The practical payoff is simplicity. DPO does not need:

- a separately trained reward model,
- reinforcement learning in the loop,
- sampling from the policy during training,
- or the tuning headaches that often come with PPO-based RLHF.

Instead, it trains with a straightforward cross-entropy-style loss over preference pairs.

In the paper’s experiments, DPO matches or outperforms PPO-based RLHF on controlled sentiment generation, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, while being much easier to implement and train. It also does well without extensive hyperparameter tuning.

## The big picture

The clever part of DPO is not that it invents a new preference signal. It is that it notices the reward model and the policy are mathematically tied together under the standard RLHF objective. Once you exploit that relationship, the reward disappears as an explicit object, and preference learning becomes a direct supervised-style optimization problem.

That is the entire trick: take the reward that RLHF was trying to learn, express it as a log-ratio between the new policy and the reference model, let the prompt-wise normalization cancel inside preference comparisons, and train the policy directly with binary cross-entropy.

It is a neat piece of algebra, but more importantly, it turns a complicated RL pipeline into something much closer to ordinary learning from labeled pairs.
