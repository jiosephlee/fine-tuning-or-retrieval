\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

# How Do You Teach an AI What “Better” Means? The Power of Preference Data

## Why “the perfect answer” is hard to define

One of the hardest parts of aligning a language model is that “good” is contextual. A coding assistant should understand common mistakes without copying them. A chatbot may need to know that a common misconception exists, but it should not repeat that misconception as if it were true. In other words, we do not just want a model that knows things. We want a model that chooses the right response for the situation.

That is why preference data is so useful. Instead of asking a human annotator to write the ideal answer from scratch, we show them two model outputs and ask a much simpler question: which one is better?

This small change turns an open-ended generation problem into a comparison problem. And comparison is usually easier for people. It is faster, less ambiguous, and often more scalable than collecting perfect demonstrations for every possible prompt. For tasks like summarization or dialogue, that matters a lot: humans may disagree on the exact wording of a “best” answer, but they can still often agree on which of two answers is more helpful, safer, or more faithful.

## What preference data looks like

In the paper, a prompt is written as $x$, and two candidate responses are sampled for that prompt. A human labeler then chooses the preferred response, written as $y_w$, and the less preferred one, written as $y_l$.

That gives a training example of the form $(x, y_w, y_l)$. This is the basic unit of preference learning.

The key idea is that the model does not need an absolute score for every answer. It only needs relative information: given the same prompt, which response did humans prefer? Repeated many times, these pairwise judgments create a dataset that teaches the model what “better” means in practice.

A common way to interpret this kind of data is through the Bradley-Terry model. In simple terms, it says that each response has some hidden quality score, and the probability that one response is preferred over another depends on the difference between those scores. If a response has a higher hidden score, it should be more likely to win the comparison.

That is a powerful abstraction because it matches how people often judge language model outputs. We may not agree on a universal definition of a great summary or a perfect assistant reply, but we can still compare two candidates and usually identify the stronger one.

## The usual RLHF pipeline

Preference learning became especially important in reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. The standard pipeline has three stages.

First, a pretrained model is fine-tuned on high-quality supervised data to get a starting point, called the reference model. Then humans compare pairs of model outputs, and those comparisons are used to train a reward model. The reward model learns to predict which outputs humans like better. Finally, the language model is trained with reinforcement learning, often PPO, to produce outputs that score highly under the reward model while staying close to the reference model.

This works, but it is complicated. You are training multiple models, sampling from the language model during training, and trying to keep the optimization stable. The paper argues that this makes RLHF effective but cumbersome.

## DPO: use the comparisons directly

Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO, starts from the same preference data but removes the extra machinery.

Instead of first training a separate reward model and then using reinforcement learning to optimize the language model, DPO trains the policy directly from the pairwise comparisons. The model is encouraged to make the preferred response more likely than the dispreferred one, while still staying anchored to the reference model.

The resulting objective is a simple binary cross-entropy loss over preference pairs:

\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\]

You do not need to read that equation as a deep dive into the math. The intuition is simpler:

The model learns to increase the relative probability of the preferred answer and decrease the relative probability of the rejected answer. The reference model acts like an anchor, so the model does not drift too far from its original behavior.

The paper’s key insight is that this direct objective is not just a heuristic. Under the Bradley-Terry preference model, it matches the same underlying RLHF objective that would normally be solved with a reward model and reinforcement learning.

## Why this matters for preference learning

The big win here is that preference learning becomes much simpler.

With DPO, you do not need to:

- fit an explicit reward model,
- run a reinforcement learning loop,
- sample from the language model during fine-tuning, or
- spend a lot of effort tuning the RL setup.

Instead, you train with an ordinary classification-style loss on preference pairs. That makes the method much easier to implement and, according to the paper, more stable in practice.

There is also a useful detail in how DPO weights examples. The update gives more weight to comparisons where the model is currently getting the ordering wrong. That helps prevent the kind of degeneration you can get from a naive “push up the winner, push down the loser” objective.

In other words, DPO does not just learn from preferences. It learns from the model’s mistakes about preferences.

## What preference learning buys you

Preference data is especially good at capturing subjective qualities that are hard to specify in a label. You may not be able to write a rule for every case, but you can often tell which of two responses is better. That makes preference learning a natural fit for open-ended generation tasks.

In the paper’s experiments, DPO was tested on controlled sentiment generation, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. It matched or improved on the quality of methods based on PPO-style RLHF, while being much simpler to train. In the sentiment setting, it even produced a better reward-versus-KL tradeoff than PPO, including a version of PPO that had access to ground-truth rewards.

That is an important result for preference learning: you can get strong alignment behavior without turning the problem into a full reinforcement learning project.

## The practical lesson

If your goal is to teach a model what “better” means, asking humans to choose between two answers is often a better signal than asking them to produce the perfect answer from scratch. Pairwise judgments are easier to collect, easier to scale, and easier to use in training.

DPO shows that you can take those preferences and turn them directly into a training objective for the language model itself. The model then learns to prefer the kinds of responses humans prefer, while staying close to the behavior it already knows how to produce.

That makes preference learning not just a useful annotation strategy, but a practical training recipe for making language models more helpful, safer, and more controllable.
