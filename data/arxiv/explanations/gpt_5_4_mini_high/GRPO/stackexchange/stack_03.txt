\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### What exactly is removed during benchmark decontamination with the $10$-gram matching rule?
Question:
The paper says that to avoid contamination, any text segment containing a $10$-gram that exactly matches benchmark text is removed from the math training corpus, and shorter benchmark texts with at least $3$ grams are handled by exact matching. I am unsure what the unit of removal is here. Are they deleting only the matched segment, the whole web page, or the whole document chunk used for training? Also, since many math problems share standard phrases, how does this rule avoid both false positives and false negatives?
Answer:
The paper is not describing a surgical deletion of just the matching $10$-gram. The wording points to filtering at the level of the whole offending text segment, and in practice that means the entire web page or extracted segment containing the match is dropped from the math corpus. That is consistent with the sentence “filter out web pages containing questions or answers” and with the later phrasing “any text segment containing a $10$-gram string that matches exactly ... is removed.” So the match is the trigger; the unit removed is the larger text unit that contains it, not just the overlapping span.

The reason this works at all is that it is meant to catch near-verbatim contamination, not semantic similarity. A long exact overlap of $10$ consecutive tokens is pretty strong evidence that the training text has copied a benchmark item or answer, especially for math problems where the numbers, symbols, and wording make long exact matches rare by accident. For benchmark texts shorter than $10$ grams but at least $3$ grams, they switch to exact full-text matching because there is no meaningful $10$-gram to search for; the whole short snippet itself becomes the signature.

This is still only a heuristic, so it does not eliminate all false positives or false negatives. It can over-filter pages that legitimately quote a benchmark problem, and it can miss paraphrases, partial copies, or cases where only some of the benchmark content appears. The goal is not perfect contamination detection; it is to remove obvious memorization leakage with a rule that is simple, high-precision, and cheap to run over Common Crawl.
