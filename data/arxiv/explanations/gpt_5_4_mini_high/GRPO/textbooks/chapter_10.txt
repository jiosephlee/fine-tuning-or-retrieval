\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 10: What Reinforcement Learning Changes in DeepSeekMath

DeepSeekMath’s reinforcement learning stage is best understood as a controlled test of how much a strong supervised math model can still improve when it is trained on its own sampled solutions. The paper does not introduce RL as a way to replace pretraining or supervised fine-tuning. Instead, it starts from DeepSeekMath-Instruct $7$B and then uses GRPO to refine the model on only a narrow slice of English chain-of-thought data from GSM8K and MATH. The key question is whether this limited RL signal can improve the exact benchmarks it was trained on, and whether that improvement transfers to problems the model never saw during RL. The paper’s answer is yes, and the resulting behavior reveals a great deal about what RL is actually changing.

# Training DeepSeekMath-RL from DeepSeekMath-Instruct Using Only a Subset of English CoT Data

The RL stage in DeepSeekMath is intentionally narrow. The authors take DeepSeekMath-Instruct $7$B as the starting point and train it further using only a subset of the English instruction-tuning data, specifically chain-of-thought-format questions from GSM8K and MATH. In total, this RL training set contains around $144$K questions. Other supervised examples are excluded on purpose, because the authors want to measure whether reinforcement learning alone can improve benchmarks that do not appear directly in the RL phase.

That design choice matters because it makes the RL experiment easy to interpret. If performance improves on other tasks later, the improvement cannot be explained simply by showing the model more examples of those tasks. The policy is being updated from sampled answers to a limited set of English math questions, and the learning signal comes from comparing those sampled answers with a reward model. DeepSeekMath uses GRPO for this phase, which samples multiple outputs per question, scores them, and then updates the policy using group-relative advantages rather than a separate critic model. In other words, the model is not just being told what the answer is; it is being taught to prefer better candidate solutions over worse ones.

# In-Domain Gains on GSM8K and MATH

The clearest evidence that RL helps comes from the training benchmarks themselves. The paper reports that DeepSeekMath-RL improves DeepSeekMath-Instruct on GSM8K from $82.9\%$ to $88.2\%$ and on MATH from $46.8\%$ to $51.7\%$ when using chain-of-thought reasoning. These are substantial jumps, especially because the starting point was already strong. RL is therefore not fixing a weak model; it is pushing a strong model further.

The MATH result is especially important. MATH is a competition-level benchmark, and moving from the mid-$40$s to above $50\%$ is not a small incremental gain. It means the model is crossing a threshold that open-source systems had not previously reached in the paper’s setting. Because the RL phase uses only GSM8K and MATH CoT data, the improvement on these exact tasks shows that GRPO can sharpen performance even when the supervised model already knows the domain well. RL is acting less like a broad curriculum and more like a precision tool.

# Out-of-Domain Gains on CMATH and Other Benchmarks

One of the most interesting findings in the paper is that RL helps even on benchmarks outside the training distribution. The paper highlights CMATH as an example: DeepSeekMath-RL improves from $84.6\%$ to $88.8\%$ despite the fact that CMATH was not directly used in RL training. This is important because it shows that the RL step is not just memorizing the two training benchmarks.

The transfer is especially noteworthy because the RL data is English-only, while CMATH is a Chinese benchmark. The fact that performance still rises suggests that the policy update is shaping a more general mathematical reasoning behavior rather than overfitting to English phrasing or to the surface form of GSM8K and MATH. In the paper’s framing, RL is changing the model’s output distribution in a way that carries over to other math tasks, including multilingual ones. That is why the authors treat the out-of-domain improvements as evidence that RL is improving the reasoning policy itself.

# Why the RL Model Surpasses Open-Source Systems from $7$B to $70$B

The paper’s strongest comparison is not just between DeepSeekMath-Instruct and DeepSeekMath-RL, but between DeepSeekMath-RL and a wide range of open-source baselines. DeepSeekMath-RL $7$B surpasses open-source models across the evaluated benchmarks, including systems in the $7$B to $70$B range. This means it outperforms both general-purpose models and math-specialized ones, such as larger instruction-tuned systems and models that were explicitly trained for mathematical reasoning.

The paper’s explanation is not that size does not matter at all, but that size alone is not the decisive factor. DeepSeekMath is built on a strong foundation: it begins with a code-trained base model, continues pretraining on a large math corpus, is then instruction-tuned, and only after that is refined with RL. GRPO matters here because it lets the model compare several answers to the same question and reinforce the better ones relative to the others. That is a natural fit for mathematics, where candidate solutions to the same problem can be ranked against each other. The paper also finds that GRPO’s group-relative updates outperform simpler alternatives like online rejection sampling fine-tuning, especially because they can both reinforce good outputs and penalize weaker ones according to their reward scores.

# Tool-Free versus Tool-Assisted Performance after RL

DeepSeekMath is evaluated in two broad modes: one in which the model must produce self-contained text solutions, and another in which it is allowed to use Python-based tools. The paper reports results in both settings because they test different things. Tool use can help with computation, but it does not tell you whether the model itself has become a stronger reasoner. The no-tool setting is therefore the stricter test.

The headline RL result is tool-free: DeepSeekMath-RL reaches $51.7\%$ on MATH without external toolkits or voting. That is the result that shows the model’s internal reasoning has improved. The paper also evaluates models in tool-assisted settings and finds that DeepSeekMath remains competitive there as well, but the most important lesson is that RL is not merely teaching the model to rely on tools. It is improving the underlying policy that generates the reasoning trace. In that sense, tool-free performance is the cleaner indicator of what RL has changed.

# How Self-Consistency Interacts with a Stronger Reasoning Model

The paper’s self-consistency result makes the RL gains easier to interpret. DeepSeekMath achieves $60.9\%$ on MATH when evaluated with self-consistency over $64$ samples, compared with the single-sample $51.7\%$ result. That gap shows that the model often contains the right answer somewhere in its sampled output distribution, even when a single draw is not enough to reveal it.

This is exactly where a stronger reasoning model benefits from sampling. If the model has been trained so that correct solutions are more likely to appear among its higher-probability outputs, then taking many samples and aggregating them becomes much more effective. The paper’s own analysis of RL supports this: it finds that RL improves $Maj@K$ but not necessarily $Pass@K$. That means RL does not simply make every individual sample better in a uniform way. Instead, it makes the answer distribution more robust, so the correct response is more likely to rise into the top of the sampled set. Self-consistency then extracts that improvement.

# Why RL Can Improve Benchmark Accuracy Even after Strong Supervised Fine-Tuning

A natural question is why RL should still help after DeepSeekMath-Instruct is already strong. The paper’s answer is that supervised fine-tuning does not fully solve the alignment problem in reasoning. A model can look strong under SFT while still placing too little probability mass on the best answers. RL changes that allocation.

The authors argue that the improvement comes from making the output distribution more robust, not necessarily from changing some underlying “fundamental” reasoning ability in a broad sense. Their Pass@K versus Maj@K analysis is the key evidence: RL improves the chance that the correct response is among the model’s top sampled outputs, and that is enough to raise benchmark accuracy. The paper also notes that step-aware or process-supervised variants of GRPO can outperform outcome-only variants, which suggests that RL is especially useful when it can guide not just the final answer but the reasoning trajectory itself. So even after strong SFT, RL can still move the model in a useful direction by correcting how it distributes probability over candidate solutions.

# Why Reaching Over $50\%$ on MATH Matters

Crossing the $50\%$ mark on MATH is a milestone in the paper because it is the first time an open-source system in their setup exceeds that level on a competition-grade benchmark. The achievement is even more notable because it is obtained without external toolkits or voting techniques in the main reported score. That means the model itself, not a post hoc decoding trick, is responsible for the result.

The paper is careful not to claim that DeepSeekMath has solved the benchmark or surpassed the strongest closed models. GPT-4 and Gemini Ultra are still ahead in the broader comparison. But reaching $51.7\%$ places DeepSeekMath in a new range for open-source mathematical reasoning and shows that a carefully built pipeline of math pretraining, instruction tuning, and RL can narrow the gap substantially. In the paper’s terms, RL is the stage that turns a very good math model into one that finally crosses a symbolic threshold for the open-source community.
