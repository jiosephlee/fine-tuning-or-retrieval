\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 12: Lessons, Limitations, and Future Directions

# Evidence that code training improves mathematical reasoning with and without tool use

One of the clearest empirical lessons in the paper is that code training helps mathematical reasoning. The authors test this idea in both settings that matter for mathematical language models: solving problems by writing out a natural-language solution, and solving problems by generating and executing Python code. In the program-aided setting, code training already gives a noticeable boost before any math-specific training begins, and the subsequent math stage improves performance further. In the text-only setting, the same pattern appears: code training first improves the model’s reasoning ability, and math training then builds on that foundation. The paper treats this as partial evidence that code exposure can strengthen reasoning in a way that transfers to mathematics, not just to programming.

The important point is that the benefit is not restricted to tool use. If code only helped by teaching the model to generate executable programs, then one would expect improvements mainly when Python is allowed. Instead, the experiments show gains even when the model must produce self-contained mathematical solutions without tools. The authors therefore present code training as a useful precursor to math training, rather than as a narrow trick for program execution. Their conclusion is cautious, but clear: within their experimental setup, code training improves mathematical reasoning both with and without tool use.

# Two-stage versus one-stage code-and-math training

The paper compares two broad ways of combining code and math data. In the two-stage setup, the model first trains on code and then trains on math. In the one-stage setup, the model trains on a mixture of code and math from the start. These two choices have different tradeoffs, and the paper’s results show that they are not interchangeable.

Two-stage training is the cleaner route when the goal is to maximize mathematical reasoning without tools. The model uses the first stage to acquire code-related structure, then uses the second stage to specialize in math. According to the authors, this sequence produces the best performance for non-tool mathematical reasoning. The interpretation is intuitive: the model first learns a skill set that appears useful for reasoning, and then focuses that skill set on mathematics.

By contrast, one-stage mixed training is not uniformly better or worse. It has a clear advantage in preserving coding ability, and it also works well for program-aided mathematical reasoning. But for pure mathematical reasoning without tools, the authors find that mixing code and math in a single stage can compromise performance. Their explanation is that a model of this size, DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B in the ablation, may not have enough capacity to absorb both streams equally well at once. So the paper does not present a single universally superior strategy; instead, it shows that the best training recipe depends on whether the target behavior is text-only reasoning, code-based reasoning, or both.

# Catastrophic forgetting and why mixed training can preserve coding ability

The paper repeatedly returns to the issue of catastrophic forgetting, which is especially visible in the transition from code training to math training. If a model is trained on code and then trained only on math, it can lose some of the coding competence it previously acquired. This matters because mathematical reasoning and code generation are not independent skills in the experiments. The model is expected to remain strong in coding even as it becomes more mathematically capable.

The mixed one-stage setup helps address this. When code tokens and math tokens are trained together, the model keeps more of its coding performance than in the two-stage setup, where the later math stage can overwrite some of the earlier code knowledge. The paper also notes that this mixed training synergizes with program-aided mathematical reasoning, which makes sense because the model continues to see both the language of code and the language of math throughout training. At the same time, the authors emphasize that preserving coding ability by mixing data is not free: the same mixture can reduce the gains in pure, tool-free mathematical reasoning. So mixed training is a useful compromise, but not a perfect substitute for staged specialization.

# The surprising weakness of arXiv-only training for the evaluated math tasks

A striking negative result in the paper is that arXiv-only training does not seem to help the evaluated mathematical tasks very much. This is somewhat surprising, because arXiv papers are often assumed to be a rich source of mathematical knowledge and are commonly included in math-training corpora. The authors test arXiv corpora under different preprocessing pipelines, including MathPile and ArXiv-RedPajama, and they train models on these corpora alone. Across the benchmarks they consider, the models show little improvement, and in some cases even deterioration.

The key point is not just that arXiv is less helpful than expected, but that the paper’s tests fail to show strong benefits on the particular benchmarks they care about: quantitative reasoning, multiple-choice math reasoning, and formal mathematics. In that sense, the result is an empirical correction to a common assumption. A large collection of academic text is not automatically the best source for improving benchmark math performance. For the tasks evaluated here, the authors conclude that arXiv-only training is not especially effective.

# Caveats to the arXiv conclusion and where it might still help

The authors are careful not to overstate the arXiv result. They explicitly say that the conclusion should be taken with a grain of salt, because there are several things they did not study. They did not test every math-related task, and they point out that tasks such as informalization of theorems were not included. They also did not study what happens when arXiv is combined with other data sources, rather than used alone. And they acknowledge that the effect of arXiv might look different at larger model scales.

This is an important limitation because it shows that the negative result is contextual, not absolute. The paper is saying that within their experimental conditions, arXiv-only data did not help much on the benchmarks they evaluated. It does not claim that arXiv is useless in all settings. Instead, the authors leave open the possibility that arXiv could matter more for different tasks, different mixtures of data, or much larger models. That is why they frame the finding as a call for further study rather than a final verdict.

# Why RL improves $\mathrm{Maj@}K$ more than $\mathrm{Pass@}K$

In their reinforcement learning analysis, the authors compare two ways of measuring sampled performance: $\mathrm{Pass@}K$ and $\mathrm{Maj@}K$. Their main observation is that reinforcement learning improves $\mathrm{Maj@}K$ more clearly than $\mathrm{Pass@}K$. This difference is informative because the two metrics capture different things about the sample distribution. $\mathrm{Pass@}K$ asks whether at least one of the $K$ samples is correct, while $\mathrm{Maj@}K$ reflects whether the most common answer among those samples is correct.

The paper’s interpretation is that RL does not primarily increase the raw chance that a correct solution appears somewhere in the sampled set. Instead, it makes correct responses more likely to dominate the top of the output distribution. In other words, RL seems to improve how the model ranks and concentrates its answers, so that correct solutions become more robust among the model’s most likely outputs. This is why the authors describe the gain as a strengthening of the output distribution rather than a wholesale increase in problem-solving power.

# The interpretation that RL strengthens output distribution alignment more than fundamental capability

Building on the previous point, the paper argues that the benefit of RL is less about changing the model’s underlying competence and more about aligning its output distribution with the correct answers already within reach. The authors infer this from the fact that RL improves $\mathrm{Maj@}K$ without a corresponding improvement in $\mathrm{Pass@}K$. If RL were mainly creating new reasoning ability from scratch, one might expect both metrics to move together. Instead, the model seems to become better at placing the right response near the top of its distribution.

This interpretation is consistent with the broader story the paper tells about reinforcement learning. The instruction-tuned model already has substantial reasoning ability, but RL makes that ability more reliably visible in its outputs. The result is a more robust distribution, not necessarily a deeper transformation of the model’s internal reasoning machinery. The authors are careful to present this as an interpretation drawn from their experiments, but it is central to how they explain the effectiveness of RL in their setting.

# The misalignment problem in supervised reasoning models

The paper connects its RL findings to the idea of misalignment in supervised reasoning models. The basic concern is that a supervised fine-tuned model may know something useful, but not express it consistently in the right form when queried. In that case, the issue is not purely missing knowledge; it is a mismatch between what the model can do and what it tends to output. The paper points to prior work showing that reasoning performance can be improved by preference alignment strategies, which suggests that the model’s answer distribution can be steered toward better behavior.

This matters for understanding why RL helps after supervised fine-tuning. The authors’ view is that the instruction-tuned model already contains useful reasoning patterns, but they are not always aligned with the desired outputs. Reinforcement learning then acts as a corrective layer, encouraging the model to place correct answers higher in its response distribution. In that sense, RL is presented as a way to reduce misalignment rather than as a completely new source of reasoning skill.

# Current weaknesses in geometry, theorem proving, and few-shot learning relative to GPT-4

Although DeepSeekMath performs strongly on many quantitative benchmarks, the paper is explicit about several weaknesses. One is geometry. The authors say that the model’s geometry capability is relatively weaker than that of closed models, and in a dry run they report that it could not handle problems involving triangles and ellipses. They interpret this as possible evidence of data-selection bias in pre-training and fine-tuning. Another weakness is theorem proving: while the model shows strong few-shot autoformalization performance on miniF2F, the overall capability is still described as weaker than that of closed models in theorem-proof settings.

The paper also points out that DeepSeekMath is worse than GPT-4 on few-shot capability. GPT-4 can improve when given few-shot inputs, while DeepSeekMath shows similar performance in zero-shot and few-shot evaluation. The authors attribute this limitation in part to model scale. So although the model is highly competitive in open-source math reasoning, the paper does not present it as a fully closed-model-level system across all reasoning modes. Its strengths are real, but uneven.

# Potential improvements in data sources, out-of-distribution prompts, and advanced decoding strategies

The discussion section outlines several concrete directions for future work on the data side. The authors suggest that their current reinforcement learning pipeline only uses questions from the instruction-tuning stage and employs naive nucleus sampling to generate outputs. Because of that, they think one promising direction is to test RL on out-of-distribution prompts, not just on the same kinds of questions already seen during instruction tuning. They also mention advanced sampling or decoding strategies, such as tree-search-based methods, as possible ways to improve exploration.

In addition, the paper highlights efficient inference techniques as an important lever, because exploration efficiency affects how well the policy model can search for useful outputs. On the data side, the authors also want to continue improving the engineered data-selection pipeline that produced the DeepSeekMath corpus. Taken together, these suggestions show that the paper views progress as coming from both better training signals and better ways of generating and selecting training data.

# Robust RL under noisy rewards and weak-to-strong alignment

The paper is explicit that reinforcement learning methods usually rely on reward signals as if they were trustworthy, but in practice reward signals can be noisy. The authors note that even carefully annotated datasets such as PRM800K still contain a substantial fraction of incorrect annotations. That observation motivates a broader research direction: RL algorithms that are robust to imperfect reward signals. This is important because the model should not become overly sensitive to noise in the reward model, especially on difficult reasoning tasks.

The authors connect this challenge to weak-to-strong alignment. The idea, as they frame it, is that a weak supervisory signal can still be used to guide the learning of a stronger model, but only if the learning algorithm can tolerate the weakness or noise in that signal. In other words, the reward function need not be perfect, but the algorithm must be designed so that imperfect rewards do not derail learning. This is one of the paper’s main forward-looking points about how RL for language models should evolve.

# Open problems in reward-model generalization, uncertainty estimation, and efficient process reward modeling

The final future direction in the paper concerns the reward model itself. The authors identify three open problems. First, the reward model needs stronger generalization, especially to out-of-distribution questions and to outputs produced by more advanced decoding methods. If the reward model fails to generalize, then RL may end up stabilizing the model’s distribution without truly improving reasoning. Second, the paper highlights uncertainty estimation for reward models. The authors suggest that uncertainty could serve as a bridge between weak reward models and weak-to-strong learning algorithms, although they do not claim to solve this problem in the paper.

Third, they emphasize the need for efficient, high-quality process reward models. These are important because they can provide finer-grained feedback along the reasoning process, rather than only scoring the final answer. The paper treats this as especially relevant for mathematical reasoning, where intermediate steps carry a lot of information. Taken together, these open problems define the research agenda that follows from the paper’s own results: better data, better rewards, better algorithms, and better ways of aligning models so that their reasoning ability is not only present, but reliably expressed.
