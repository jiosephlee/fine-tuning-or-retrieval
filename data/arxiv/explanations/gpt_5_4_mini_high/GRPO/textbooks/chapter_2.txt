\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: Background Concepts and Evaluation Setup

# Continual pre-training versus supervised fine-tuning versus reinforcement learning

DeepSeekMath is easiest to understand as a three-stage training pipeline, and the paper’s evaluation setup is built around separating these stages conceptually. The first stage is continual pre-training: the model starts from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B and is trained further on a very large math-oriented corpus drawn from Common Crawl, together with some code and natural language data. This stage is not about teaching the model a narrow question-answer format. Instead, it is about exposing the model to a large amount of mathematical text so that it internalizes the patterns, vocabulary, and structures that appear in mathematical reasoning.

The second stage is supervised fine-tuning, or SFT. Here the goal changes. Rather than simply absorbing text, the model is shown explicit input-output examples of how mathematical questions should be answered. In this paper, those examples are written in chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated reasoning formats. SFT is therefore a form of guided imitation: it teaches the model how to present a solution, not just what mathematics looks like in raw text. The paper’s DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B model comes from this stage.

The third stage is reinforcement learning, or RL. In RL, the model is no longer trained primarily by matching a provided target answer. Instead, it samples candidate solutions, receives a reward signal, and adjusts its policy to prefer better outputs. In this paper, RL is used to further improve the instruction-tuned model, producing DeepSeekMath-RL 7B. The key point is that each stage serves a different purpose. Continual pre-training builds mathematical familiarity at scale, SFT teaches the model to express solutions in useful formats, and RL pushes the model toward outputs that are scored more highly by the reward system.

# Why code-trained models may be strong starting points for reasoning

A central empirical choice in the paper is to initialize DeepSeekMath from a code model rather than from a general language model. Specifically, the authors start from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B and report that this is a better choice than starting from a general LLM. The paper does not present this as a universal law, but as an observed advantage in their experiments.

The intuition suggested by the paper’s results is that code training already exposes the model to highly structured, executable sequences. Mathematical reasoning also has structure: symbols and steps must fit together in a precise order, and many problems can be checked through computation. Starting from a model that has already learned to handle structured logic and programming syntax appears to make later math training more effective. This is especially visible in the paper’s experiments showing that code training improves mathematical reasoning both with and without tool use.

The paper also reports that math training on top of code training improves general reasoning benchmarks such as MMLU and BBH. That matters because it suggests the benefit is not limited to “math-only” performance. In the authors’ view, code training appears to provide a useful base for broader reasoning behavior, and the math stage then amplifies that advantage.

# Chain-of-thought prompting for step-by-step solutions

Chain-of-thought, or CoT, prompting is the simplest reasoning format used throughout the paper. In CoT, the model is encouraged to produce a step-by-step solution rather than jumping immediately to an answer. The model’s output is therefore a visible reasoning trace, and the final answer is obtained after the intermediate steps. This is the format used in many of the paper’s benchmark evaluations, especially when the authors want to measure the model’s ability to solve problems using natural language alone.

The paper uses CoT in two closely related ways. First, it is a prompting method at evaluation time: the benchmark question is given to the model along with a small number of examples, and the model is asked to reason step by step. Second, it is a data format during SFT: many of the instruction-tuning examples are paired with CoT solutions. In both cases, the underlying idea is the same. Instead of treating the answer as a single token or a single line, the model is trained and evaluated on the structure of the reasoning process.

This matters for reading the paper because many of the reported gains are tied to the model’s ability to produce coherent intermediate steps. A model can be compared under a “direct answer” style, but the paper often prefers step-by-step reasoning because it better reflects the kind of outputs needed for difficult mathematical tasks. CoT therefore functions as both a training signal and an evaluation lens.

# Program-of-thought prompting and Python-based problem solving

Program-of-thought, or PoT, prompting is another important format in the paper. The key difference from CoT is that, instead of expressing all reasoning in natural language, the model writes a Python program. The program is then executed, and the execution result is treated as the answer. The paper explicitly notes that libraries such as `math` and `sympy` can be used for complicated calculations.

PoT is useful because some mathematical problems are easier to solve reliably when the model can delegate calculation to code. A natural language chain-of-thought can describe the logic of a solution, but it may still make arithmetic mistakes or lose track of a long computation. In PoT, the model can generate a program that carries out the computation directly. This is why the paper evaluates both “without tool use” and “with tool use” settings: the second setting asks whether the model can translate the problem into executable code and then interpret the result correctly.

In the paper’s SFT data, PoT is not treated as a niche trick. It is one of the main solution formats included in the instruction-tuning dataset, alongside CoT and tool-integrated reasoning. The evaluation section also makes clear that the authors care about program-aided mathematical reasoning on benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH. For a reader, the essential idea is that PoT turns reasoning into a combination of language and execution, which gives the model access to external computation inside the solution process.

# Tool-integrated reasoning and the role of external computation

Tool-integrated reasoning is the broader setting in which the model can mix natural language reasoning with external tools. In the paper, this includes Python-based problem solving, but the term is wider than PoT because it emphasizes integration rather than code writing alone. The model can reason in words, invoke a tool when needed, and use the result of that tool call inside the solution.

The paper uses tool-integrated reasoning in both training and evaluation. In the SFT data, some examples are annotated in this format. In evaluation, the authors explicitly distinguish between tasks solved with self-contained text and tasks solved using Python. This distinction is important because it lets them ask two different questions. One question is whether the model can reason on its own. Another is whether the model can coordinate reasoning with computation when tools are allowed.

This setup is especially useful for understanding the paper’s results on mathematical benchmarks. Some tasks benefit from a purely textual solution, while others become easier once the model can execute code. By reporting both kinds of performance, the paper avoids collapsing all mathematical reasoning into a single score. Instead, it measures how well the model can think, compute, and combine the two.

# Core reinforcement learning ideas: policy, reward, advantage, and KL regularization

The reinforcement learning part of the paper is built around a few essential ideas. The policy is the model that produces an output for a given question. The reward is a score assigned to that output by a reward model. The advantage measures how much better one output is than a baseline, and it determines whether the policy should be reinforced or penalized. KL regularization is used to keep the new policy close to a reference model so that training remains stable.

The paper begins from the standard PPO framework. PPO uses a clipped objective that compares the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$ with an older policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. The update is weighted by the advantage $A_t$, which in PPO is computed using a learned value function $V_{\psi}$ and generalized advantage estimation. PPO also includes a KL-style penalty involving a reference model, often the SFT model, so that the policy does not drift too far while maximizing reward. In the context of language models, this helps prevent the system from exploiting the reward model in uncontrolled ways.

GRPO, the paper’s main RL contribution, changes the way the baseline is computed. Instead of training a separate critic or value function, GRPO samples a group of $G$ outputs for the same question, scores them, and computes advantages from the relative rewards within that group. In the outcome-supervision variant, every token in an output gets the same group-normalized reward. In the process-supervision variant, each reasoning step gets its own reward, and the token-level advantage is built from the rewards of later steps. This group-relative strategy is one of the reasons GRPO uses less memory than PPO, because it avoids the extra value model.

The KL term is also handled differently. PPO typically adds a KL penalty inside the reward, while GRPO places the KL divergence directly in the loss. The paper emphasizes that the KL estimate is chosen so that it is positive and can be computed in an unbiased way. Conceptually, this regularization says that the model should improve with respect to the reward signal, but not at the cost of moving too far from the reference behavior. For a student, the main takeaway is that RL in this paper is not “free-form self-improvement”; it is a controlled update process built around policy gradients, reward comparisons, baseline subtraction, and regularization.

# Few-shot, zero-shot, Pass@K, Maj@K, and self-consistency

The paper uses several evaluation styles that are easy to confuse if one has not seen them before. Zero-shot evaluation means the model receives only the test question, with no worked examples in the prompt. Few-shot evaluation means the prompt includes a small number of examples showing the desired behavior. The paper frequently uses few-shot chain-of-thought prompting for benchmark comparisons, because that is a standard way to probe mathematical reasoning in language models.

Pass@K and Maj@K summarize performance when the model generates multiple candidate solutions. Pass@K asks whether at least one of the $K$ sampled outputs is correct. Maj@K asks whether the majority vote among those $K$ outputs is correct. These two metrics can behave differently. A model may occasionally produce a correct answer in one of many samples, which gives a good Pass@K, yet still fail to make the correct answer the majority choice. The paper uses this distinction in its RL analysis to argue that RL changes the shape of the output distribution, not just the probability of one lucky sample.

Self-consistency is closely related. In the paper, self-consistency over $64$ samples on MATH gives a stronger result than a single sample, because the model generates many reasoning paths and aggregates them. This is another way of using sampling to stabilize performance. The important idea is that multiple generations can reveal whether the model has a robust solution distribution, not merely whether it can occasionally stumble onto a correct answer.

# English mathematical benchmarks

The paper evaluates mathematical reasoning on several English benchmarks that cover different difficulty levels and formats. GSM8K is used for grade-school-level quantitative reasoning. MATH is the competition-level benchmark and is one of the central tests in the paper. SAT, OCW Courses, and MMLU-STEM are also included, giving the evaluation broader coverage across mathematical and STEM-style reasoning.

These benchmarks are valuable because they do not all test the same skill. Some are more about straightforward arithmetic or word problems, while others require multi-step symbolic reasoning or more advanced conceptual understanding. By evaluating across this set, the paper can show whether improvements are limited to one style of problem or extend across a wider range of mathematical tasks. The authors also distinguish between settings where tool use is disallowed and settings where the model may use Python, which helps separate internal reasoning from computation-aided reasoning.

The paper’s overall evaluation strategy on English benchmarks is therefore not just “what is the score on MATH.” It is a more detailed profiling of how the model behaves across task types, prompting styles, and tool-use settings. That is why the same model is often reported under multiple evaluation conditions.

# Chinese mathematical benchmarks

The paper also evaluates on Chinese benchmarks, reflecting the fact that the DeepSeekMath Corpus is multilingual rather than English-only. The Chinese benchmarks include MGSM-zh, CMATH, Gaokao-MathCloze, and Gaokao-MathQA. Together, these benchmarks test whether the gains from math pre-training transfer into Chinese mathematical reasoning tasks.

This part of the evaluation is important because the paper explicitly argues that its multilingual data selection pipeline improves performance on Chinese benchmarks. In the authors’ discussion, existing mathematical corpora are mostly English-centric, whereas their corpus includes high-quality non-English data. The result is that the model shows stronger behavior on Chinese reasoning tasks than one might expect from an English-centered math dataset.

For a first-time reader, the key lesson is that mathematical reasoning is not treated as language-independent in a naive sense. The paper measures whether a model trained on multilingual web data can carry its reasoning skills across languages. That is why the Chinese benchmark suite is not an afterthought: it is part of the evidence that the data pipeline matters.

# Formal mathematics with miniF2F and Isabelle

The paper does not restrict itself to informal math questions. It also evaluates formal mathematics through the informal-to-formal theorem proving task on miniF2F, using Isabelle as the proof assistant. In this setup, the model is asked to generate a formal proof based on an informal statement, a formal statement, and an informal proof. This is a very different kind of task from answering a word problem, because correctness must be expressed in a formal proof language.

To make this task manageable, the paper follows a proof-sketch approach and uses the automated prover Sledgehammer to fill in missing details. This lets the model contribute the overall structure of the proof while the prover handles some of the low-level proof search. The evaluation is therefore not just about generating text that sounds plausible; it is about producing a proof that can be checked in a formal system.

The authors use this benchmark to test whether their math training helps with formal theorem proving, which is one of the hardest forms of mathematical reasoning in the paper. The takeaway for a student is that formal mathematics sits at the extreme end of the reasoning spectrum: unlike typical benchmark questions, the output must satisfy a proof assistant, not just a human reader.

# General capability benchmarks: MMLU, BBH, HumanEval, and MBPP

Although the paper is centered on mathematics, it also evaluates general capability to make sure that math training does not narrowly improve only one skill while degrading others. MMLU is used to measure broad natural language understanding across $57$ tasks. BBH consists of $23$ difficult tasks that mostly require multi-step reasoning. HumanEval and MBPP are standard coding benchmarks used to assess code generation.

These benchmarks matter because they show how the model’s mathematical training affects broader behavior. The paper reports that math training improves MMLU and BBH, which suggests that the model’s reasoning ability is not limited to one domain. At the same time, by including HumanEval and MBPP, the paper checks whether continual training on math data and code preserves coding performance rather than destroying it. This is especially relevant because the model starts from a code-trained base and continues training on a mixture that still includes code.

For reading the paper, these benchmarks serve as a reality check. They show whether the model remains generally useful after specialized math training. That is why the evaluation section spans not only math problems but also language understanding, reasoning, and coding tasks.
