\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 3: Mining Mathematical Data from Common Crawl

# Why Common Crawl Is an Attractive Source for Large-Scale Domain Data

The central idea in this chapter is that a large public web crawl can contain much more domain-specific value than its noise level first suggests. In the paper, Common Crawl serves as the raw material for building the DeepSeekMath Corpus, and the authors’ results are meant to show that mathematical content is not scarce on the web; rather, it is scattered and mixed in with many pages that are not useful for training. The challenge is therefore not whether the web contains math, but how to isolate it reliably at scale.

This matters because the resulting corpus is not just a small curated set, but a large training resource with $120\text{B}$ math-related tokens. The paper’s broader message is that publicly accessible web data can support strong mathematical reasoning if the selection pipeline is careful enough. In other words, Common Crawl is attractive precisely because it is large, diverse, and open, and because a good filtering process can turn that scale into useful training data instead of letting the noise dominate.

# Using OpenWebMath as the Initial Seed Corpus

The mining process begins with a seed corpus, and the paper chooses OpenWebMath for that role. OpenWebMath is described as a collection of high-quality mathematical web texts, which makes it useful as a starting point because the system needs examples of what “good” mathematical web content looks like before it can search for more of it in Common Crawl. The seed corpus is not the final answer; it is the initial anchor that gives the mining pipeline a direction.

This is an important design choice because the first model does not need to recognize every possible kind of mathematics on the internet. Instead, it needs to learn a useful approximation of the text patterns that often appear in math-related pages. OpenWebMath provides those positive examples. The limitation, which the paper explicitly notes later, is that the initial seed does not have enough diversity, so many mathematical web pages remain undiscovered after the first round. That limitation is what motivates the iterative process.

# Training a fastText Classifier with Positive and Negative Examples

To turn the seed corpus into a search tool, the paper trains a fastText classifier. The setup is straightforward but effective: $500{,}000$ examples are sampled from OpenWebMath as positive training data, and another $500{,}000$ web pages from Common Crawl are used as negative examples. This creates a binary classifier whose job is to distinguish math-like pages from ordinary web pages.

The paper also gives the training configuration of the classifier, which helps show that this is intended as a practical, lightweight component rather than a large complex model. The vector dimension is set to $256$, the learning rate to $0.1$, the maximum word $n$-gram length to $3$, the minimum number of word occurrences to $3$, and the number of training epochs to $3$. The intuition is that the classifier learns a rough but useful notion of what math-related text looks like. Because the positive examples come from a high-quality seed, the classifier can then be used to “recall” more pages that resemble those examples in the larger crawl.

# Deduplication and Near-Deduplication of Raw Web Pages

Before the classifier is applied, the paper reduces the size of Common Crawl through URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication. This step matters because a raw web crawl contains repetition: the same page may appear multiple times, and closely related versions of a page may also show up repeatedly. If these were left in place, the mining process would waste effort scoring redundant content and would overrepresent duplicated material in the final corpus.

After deduplication and near-deduplication, the authors work with $40\text{B}$ HTML web pages. That number makes it clear how large the search space still is, even after cleaning. The point of this stage is not to make the data small, but to make it manageable and less redundant. Once the crawl has been consolidated in this way, the classifier can be applied to a much more meaningful set of candidates.

# Ranking Candidate Pages by Classifier Score and Selecting Top-Scoring Content

After training the fastText model, the paper applies it to the deduplicated crawl to recover pages that look like the seed corpus. The classifier assigns scores to candidate pages, and the pages are then ranked according to those scores. Only the top-scoring pages are kept. This is the main filtering mechanism that turns a noisy web crawl into a more focused mathematical corpus.

In the first iteration, the authors choose to keep the top $40\text{B}$ tokens. This selection is not arbitrary: the volume of retained data is evaluated through pre-training experiments, so the corpus size is treated as a tunable design choice rather than a fixed constant. The intuition is that higher classifier scores should correspond to pages with a greater likelihood of containing useful mathematical content, so ranking by score provides a simple way to prefer quality over quantity when the crawl is still too large to inspect manually.

# Choosing Data Volume Through Pre-Training Experiments on $40\text{B}$, $80\text{B}$, $120\text{B}$, and $160\text{B}$ Tokens

A key question in the paper is how much mined data should be kept. To answer that, the authors do not assume that more is automatically better. Instead, they evaluate several candidate corpus sizes by pre-training on the top $40\text{B}$, $80\text{B}$, $120\text{B}$, and $160\text{B}$ tokens. This makes the mining process evidence-driven: the quality of a selection is judged by how well a model trained on it performs downstream.

The experimental setup for this validation uses a $1.3\text{B}$-parameter model, DeepSeek-LLM $1.3\text{B}$, trained separately on each mathematical corpus for $150\text{B}$ tokens. The paper reports that the DeepSeekMath Corpus leads to better downstream performance than the other corpora compared in the study, which supports the idea that the mined data is not just large but also high quality. The first iteration keeps $40\text{B}$ tokens, but later rounds expand the corpus after the classifier improves. In this way, pre-training experiments are used as a practical feedback signal for deciding how aggressively to mine.

# Domain-Level Analysis to Discover New Math-Rich Websites

After the first round of mining, the authors notice a problem: many mathematical web pages remain uncollected. The reason is not that the crawl lacks such pages, but that the initial positive set is not diverse enough. To address this, the paper moves from page-level classification to domain-level analysis. Common Crawl is organized into disjoint domains, where a domain is defined as web pages sharing the same base URL.

For each domain, the authors compute the percentage of pages that were collected in the first iteration. Domains where more than $10\%$ of the pages have been collected are treated as math-related. The paper gives \texttt{mathoverflow.net} as an example. This step is clever because a domain that already contains many collected pages is likely to contain more pages of the same kind. Domain-level analysis therefore helps the system discover entire websites that are rich in mathematical content, rather than relying only on the initial seed’s surface patterns.

# Human Annotation of Math-Related URL Patterns Within Selected Domains

Once likely math-related domains are identified, the paper adds a human-in-the-loop step. The authors manually annotate the URLs within those domains that are associated with mathematical content. For example, they identify \texttt{mathoverflow.net/questions} as a math-related URL pattern. This does not mean every page on a domain is kept; rather, the annotation helps pinpoint which parts of the domain are worth expanding.

The pages linked to these URLs that were not already collected are then added to the seed corpus. This is an important refinement because it turns a broad domain-level signal into more precise training data. Human annotation helps the pipeline capture kinds of content that the classifier might miss on its own, especially when the relevant pages share a recognizable site structure. In effect, the manual step improves the seed set so that the next classifier will be better at finding additional mathematical pages.

# Iterative Classifier Improvement Across Four Collection Rounds

The mining procedure is iterative. After each round, the expanded seed corpus is used to train a better fastText classifier, and that improved classifier is used to recall more mathematical pages from Common Crawl. The paper reports that after four iterations, the process yields a large corpus of mathematical web pages. This repeated cycle is the heart of the method: mine, refine, retrain, and mine again.

The reason for iterating is that the first classifier is limited by the diversity of the initial seed. As more math-rich domains and URL patterns are discovered, the seed corpus becomes more representative of the broader space of mathematical web text. By the fourth iteration, the authors observe that nearly $98\%$ of the data had already been collected in the third iteration, so they stop data collection. That detail shows that the process begins to saturate: once the classifier and seed corpus have become strong enough, only a small amount of new data remains to be found.

# The Final Corpus: $35.5\text{M}$ Pages and $120\text{B}$ Math-Related Tokens

After four iterations, the paper’s mining pipeline produces $35.5\text{M}$ mathematical web pages totaling $120\text{B}$ tokens. The corpus is multilingual, with English and Chinese as the two most represented languages. This multilingual aspect is not incidental: the paper notes that the corpus improves Chinese mathematical benchmarks as well, which is consistent with the fact that the mining process did not restrict itself to English-only data.

The authors also take care to avoid benchmark contamination. They filter out web pages containing questions or answers from the evaluation benchmarks by removing any text segment that contains a $10$-gram string matching exactly with a benchmark substring. If a benchmark text is shorter than $10$ grams but has at least $3$ grams, exact matching is still used to filter contaminated pages. This decontamination step is essential because the corpus is intended for training, not for leaking evaluation content into the model. The final result is a large corpus that is not only bigger than earlier math corpora, but also supported by evidence from downstream pre-training performance.

# Why the Same Pipeline Could Generalize to Other Domains Such as Code

The paper emphasizes that this mining strategy is not exclusive to mathematics. In fact, the authors explicitly state that the approach is also applicable to other domains, such as coding. The reason is structural: the pipeline only assumes that there is a small high-quality seed corpus, a classifier that can be trained to recognize similar text, a large web crawl to search through, and an iterative mechanism for expanding the seed.

This makes the method broadly useful as a general recipe for domain data mining from web-scale sources. If a domain has enough characteristic textual patterns to support a classifier, then the same retrieve-filter-expand loop can be used to find more of that domain in Common Crawl. The DeepSeekMath Corpus is therefore not just a math dataset; it is also a demonstration of how to mine specialized knowledge from noisy public web data in a way that can be adapted to other fields.
