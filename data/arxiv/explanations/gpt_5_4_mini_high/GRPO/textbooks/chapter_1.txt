\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 1: Mathematical Reasoning and the DeepSeekMath Agenda

# Why Mathematical Reasoning Is Difficult for Language Models

The paper begins from a simple but important observation: mathematical reasoning is a hard problem for language models because it is not just a matter of producing fluent text. Mathematics is structured, stepwise, and unforgiving. A model must often keep track of several intermediate results, choose the right representation, and arrive at an answer that is not merely plausible but exactly correct. In the paper’s setting, this difficulty shows up across very different kinds of tasks, from grade-school arithmetic to competition-level problem solving, from geometry to formal theorem proving.

One reason this is challenging is that the evaluation target is often a complete solution rather than a short answer. The paper repeatedly tests models on self-contained text solutions, program-based solutions, and formal proofs. These are different levels of difficulty, but they all require the model to maintain consistency over many steps. A single mistake early in the reasoning chain can corrupt the final answer. The paper’s benchmarks make this explicit by spanning GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, miniF2F, and other tasks that ask for reasoning rather than pattern matching.

The paper also shows that mathematical reasoning is not one skill but a family of related skills. Some tasks reward step-by-step natural language reasoning. Others benefit from code, where the model writes a Python program and lets execution handle the arithmetic or symbolic manipulation. Still others, like formal theorem proving, require the model to produce a proof in a formal system such as Isabelle. Because these demands are so different, a model that looks strong on one math benchmark may still fail on another. DeepSeekMath is presented as a response to this broader and more demanding view of mathematical reasoning.

# The Gap Between Open-Source and Closed-Source Math-Capable Models

A central motivation of the paper is the gap between publicly available open-source models and the strongest closed-source systems. The authors note that models such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra are not publicly available, while open-source models lag behind them on mathematical reasoning benchmarks. This gap is especially visible on the competition-level MATH benchmark, which the paper treats as a demanding test of mathematical problem solving.

The paper’s argument is not simply that closed models are larger or more capable in some abstract sense. Instead, it emphasizes that open models have been missing two things at once: sufficiently strong math-oriented pre-training data and an effective post-training strategy for reasoning. The authors show that even a 7B model can reach strong results if it is trained with carefully selected math data and then refined with instruction tuning and reinforcement learning. This matters because it shifts the story away from raw parameter count and toward the quality of the training process.

The paper also shows that the gap is not purely about English-language math tasks. DeepSeekMath includes multilingual data and evaluates on Chinese benchmarks such as MGSM-zh, CMATH, Gaokao-MathCloze, and Gaokao-MathQA. The authors report that their corpus improves Chinese mathematical reasoning, whereas many previous mathematical corpora are English-centric. In this sense, the gap between open and closed models is also a data gap: what the model sees during training strongly shapes the kinds of reasoning it can do later.

# What DeepSeekMath Aims to Achieve

DeepSeekMath is designed to narrow this gap by building a domain-specific language model for mathematics that is strong enough to compete with much larger or proprietary systems. The paper’s overarching goal is to push open language models toward the performance level of the best closed models on academic mathematical benchmarks, while also understanding what actually produces the gains. The project is therefore both an engineering effort and a research study.

The paper’s strategy is to attack the problem from two sides. First, it tries to construct a large, high-quality corpus of math-related web data from Common Crawl. Second, it develops a reinforcement learning method, Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO, to improve the model after supervised fine-tuning. These two ideas are presented as the main reasons DeepSeekMath works: better pre-training data and better optimization during reinforcement learning.

Just as importantly, the paper is not only interested in final benchmark scores. It also asks what kinds of training data help mathematical reasoning, whether code training is beneficial, whether arXiv papers are useful, and how different alignment-style methods relate to one another. So DeepSeekMath is not merely a model; it is an agenda for understanding how reasoning ability emerges in language models and how it can be improved in a principled way.

# The Three-Stage System: DeepSeekMath-Base, DeepSeekMath-Instruct, and DeepSeekMath-RL

The system is built in three stages, and each stage plays a distinct role. DeepSeekMath-Base is the pre-trained foundation. It is initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B and then continually pre-trained for 500B tokens. The data mixture is deliberately designed: 56\% from the DeepSeekMath Corpus, 4\% from AlgebraicStack, 10\% from arXiv, 20\% from GitHub code, and the remaining 10\% from natural language data in English and Chinese drawn from Common Crawl. The paper argues that starting from a code-trained model is a better choice than starting from a general language model.

This base model is where the paper first demonstrates that math-focused data can materially improve reasoning. DeepSeekMath-Base achieves strong results on math benchmarks and also improves performance on MMLU and BBH, suggesting that math training does not only help with arithmetic or problem solving in a narrow sense. It also preserves coding performance because code tokens are included in the continual training mix.

DeepSeekMath-Instruct is the supervised fine-tuned version of the base model. The paper constructs a 776K-example mathematical instruction dataset spanning English and Chinese, with solutions written in chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated reasoning formats. This stage teaches the model how to present reasoning in a usable form, not just how to absorb math text. The resulting model is strong enough to beat all 7B counterparts and to be comparable with 70B open-source instruction-tuned models.

DeepSeekMath-RL is the final stage, where the instruction-tuned model is further improved by reinforcement learning. The training data for this phase is restricted to chain-of-thought-format questions related to GSM8K and MATH, totaling around 144K questions. Even though the RL stage uses only this limited subset, it still improves both in-domain and out-of-domain performance. The paper’s strongest MATH result, 51.7\%, comes from this stage, and self-consistency over 64 samples raises MATH performance to 60.9\%.

# Headline Results: MATH, Self-Consistency, and Cross-Benchmark Performance

The headline result most readers will notice first is the 51.7\% score on the competition-level MATH benchmark without external toolkits and voting techniques. In the paper’s narrative, this is important not only because it is a high number, but because it brings an open model close to the level of systems like Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4 on a hard benchmark. The paper presents this as a major step forward for open mathematical reasoning.

A second headline result is the self-consistency score of 60.9\% on MATH when using 64 samples. This shows that the model’s reasoning can be improved further by sampling multiple candidate solutions and aggregating them. The paper uses this to show that the model has latent capability beyond its single-sample answer rate, even before one invokes external tools.

The paper also reports strong results outside MATH. DeepSeekMath-Base reaches 64.2\% on GSM8K and 36.2\% on MATH, outperforming Minerva 540B. DeepSeekMath-Instruct performs strongly without tool use and remains competitive when tool use is allowed, approaching 60\% on MATH. DeepSeekMath-RL then improves GSM8K from 82.9\% to 88.2\% and MATH from 46.8\% to 51.7\%, while also improving an out-of-domain benchmark such as CMATH from 84.6\% to 88.8\%. The overall picture is that each stage contributes something real: pre-training builds capability, instruction tuning organizes it, and reinforcement learning sharpens it.

# The Two Central Ideas: Large-Scale Math Pre-Training and Group Relative Policy Optimization

The first central idea is that public web data contains much more mathematical content than is often assumed, but it has to be found carefully. The DeepSeekMath Corpus is built from Common Crawl using an iterative fastText-based selection pipeline. The initial seed comes from OpenWebMath. The authors train a classifier with 500,000 positive examples from the seed corpus and 500,000 negative examples from Common Crawl, then use it to mine additional math-like pages. They deduplicate Common Crawl to reduce it to 40B HTML pages, rank pages by classifier score, and keep the highest-scoring ones.

The process is iterative because the first pass does not capture all mathematical content. The authors identify math-related domains by looking at what fraction of pages from a domain were collected in the first iteration, manually annotate URLs within those domains, and add newly found pages back into the seed corpus. After four iterations, they end up with 35.5M mathematical web pages totaling 120B tokens. They also perform decontamination by removing web pages that overlap with benchmark questions and answers through exact n-gram matching. The point of all this machinery is that data quality and careful selection matter just as much as data volume.

The paper validates this corpus by training a 1.3B model on several candidate corpora, including MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof-Pile-2, and DeepSeekMath Corpus. The result is that DeepSeekMath Corpus is the strongest overall: it is higher-quality, multilingual, and larger-scale than the alternatives. The authors also find that code training before math training helps mathematical reasoning, while arXiv papers do not seem to provide notable gains on the benchmarks they study. These findings reinforce the main pre-training lesson of the paper: the source and composition of the data matter more than simply adding more text.

The second central idea is GRPO, the reinforcement learning method proposed in the paper. GRPO is a variant of PPO, but it removes the critic model and instead uses the average reward of multiple sampled outputs from the same question as the baseline. Intuitively, this means the model learns by comparing several answers to one another, rather than depending on a separate value function that predicts token-level expected reward. The paper presents this as a better fit for language-model training, where reward signals are often sparse and only the final answer may be clearly scored.

GRPO is also more memory-efficient than PPO because it avoids training the critic. The paper defines both outcome supervision and process supervision within the GRPO framework. Outcome supervision gives a single reward at the end of each output, while process supervision assigns rewards to reasoning steps and then propagates them through the output. The authors also explore iterative RL, where the reward model is continuously refreshed using new samples from the policy. In their experiments, GRPO improves performance beyond the already-strong DeepSeekMath-Instruct model, and it does so even when trained only on a subset of English instruction data.

# How the Work Fits Into Broader Research on Reasoning, Code, and Alignment

DeepSeekMath is not only about mathematics; it also helps organize a broader research picture around reasoning, code, and alignment. One of the paper’s most practical findings is that code training benefits mathematical reasoning. The authors test both two-stage and one-stage training setups and conclude that code training improves program-aided reasoning and also helps reasoning without tools. They also note that mixing code and math tokens in one-stage training can mitigate catastrophic forgetting for coding, although it may hurt non-tool mathematical reasoning in a small model.

The paper also links math training to general language understanding and reasoning. DeepSeekMath-Base improves on MMLU and BBH relative to its code-trained predecessor, suggesting that math-focused training may reinforce broader reasoning skills. At the same time, the model maintains strong coding ability on HumanEval and MBPP because code tokens remain part of the continual training mix. This gives the paper an important broader message: math training is not isolated from other capabilities, but interacts with them.

Finally, the paper places its reinforcement learning work in a unified framework that includes SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO. The common structure is that each method depends on a data source, a reward function, and an algorithm that turns those into gradient updates. Within that framework, the paper contrasts online and offline sampling, outcome and process supervision, and single-turn versus iterative RL. The authors argue that RL helps because it increases the probability mass on correct answers in the model’s output distribution, improving robustness rather than necessarily changing the model’s underlying capacity in a deep way. They also point to future directions such as better sampling strategies, more robust learning from noisy rewards, and stronger process reward models.

Taken together, these results show that DeepSeekMath is both a model and a research program. It argues that open language models can improve dramatically when they are trained on carefully mined mathematical web data and refined with an efficient RL method like GRPO. Just as importantly, it shows that understanding reasoning in language models requires attention to data, objectives, and training dynamics all at once.
