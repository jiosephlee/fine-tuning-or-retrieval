\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 9: Reward Design and Training Protocols for Mathematical RL

DeepSeekMath treats reinforcement learning as more than just a policy update rule. The paper shows that the way rewards are defined, normalized, and refreshed can strongly affect how well a language model learns mathematical reasoning. In particular, the authors build GRPO on top of DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B and study how different reward assignments change the behavior of the policy during training. The central theme is that mathematical RL is driven by a reward signal that can be coarse or fine-grained, static or iteratively refreshed, and that these choices matter as much as the optimizer itself.

# Building reward models for mathematical reasoning

In the paper, the reward function is the source of the training signal for reinforcement learning. Rather than relying on a hand-written rule at every update, DeepSeekMath uses reward models, denoted $r_\varphi$, to score sampled outputs. The reward-model training set is constructed following the procedure used in the paper’s earlier mathematical RL work, and the reward model is then used to evaluate candidate solutions produced by the current policy.

The paper also frames reward functions in a unified way: they can be based on a rule, where correctness is judged directly, or on a learned model, where a neural reward model estimates the quality of a response. This distinction matters because the reward signal is what ultimately tells the policy whether to increase or decrease the probability of generating a token. In the mathematical setting of DeepSeekMath, the reward model is not intended to solve the problem itself. Its role is narrower and more specific: it assigns a scalar score to a solution, or to parts of a solution, so that the policy can learn from those scores.

# Outcome supervision with final-answer rewards

The simplest reward setup explored in the paper is outcome supervision. For each question $q$, the current policy samples a group of outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$ from $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. A reward model then assigns a single scalar reward $r_i$ to each entire output $o_i$. In this setting, the reward is attached to the final outcome of the solution rather than to the intermediate reasoning steps.

This means that the model is judged on whether the whole answer is good, not on how it got there. The paper uses outcome supervision to define a final-answer style feedback signal, which is particularly natural when the goal is to decide whether a complete solution is correct. However, because the reward arrives only at the end, outcome supervision does not distinguish between good and bad intermediate tokens inside the same solution. The entire output is treated as one unit for reward purposes.

# Group normalization of rewards within each prompt

GRPO is “group relative” because it compares multiple sampled outputs for the same question. After the reward model produces rewards $\mathbf{r}=\{r_1, r_2, \cdots, r_G\}$ for the $G$ sampled outputs, the paper normalizes them within the group by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation. In outcome supervision, this gives

$$
\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})}.
$$

This normalization makes the learning signal relative to the other answers sampled for the same prompt. That is an important design choice because reward models are themselves trained on comparisons between outputs for the same question. The group normalization therefore matches the structure of the reward information: instead of asking whether a solution is good in an absolute sense, GRPO asks whether it is better or worse than its peers for that prompt. In the paper, this group-relative baseline also removes the need for a separate critic model, which is a major practical advantage over PPO.

# How outcome supervision assigns the same normalized advantage to all tokens in a solution

Under outcome supervision, the normalized reward $\widetilde{r}_i$ becomes the advantage for every token in the corresponding output. In the paper’s notation, the advantage is

$$
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i
$$

for all token positions $t$ in output $o_i$. So once the reward for the whole answer is known, every token in that answer receives the same learning signal.

The intuition is straightforward: if the final solution is judged positively, the policy should reinforce the entire sequence that produced it; if it is judged negatively, the whole sequence should be pushed down. This is simple and easy to apply, but it is also coarse. A long mathematical derivation may contain some tokens that are useful and others that are not, yet outcome supervision assigns them all the same credit or blame. The paper explicitly notes that such a signal may not be sufficient or efficient for complex mathematical reasoning, which motivates the move to process supervision.

# Process supervision with step-level rewards for intermediate reasoning

To obtain finer feedback, the paper also studies process supervision. Instead of scoring only the final answer, a process reward model scores each reasoning step. For a given sampled output $o_i$, the model produces step-level rewards at the ends of steps, written as $\{r_i^{index(1)}, r_i^{index(2)}, \cdots, r_i^{index(K_i)}\}$, where $K_i$ is the number of steps in the output.

This setup is important because it gives the policy feedback while the reasoning is still unfolding. A mathematical solution is often not just a final answer; it is a chain of intermediate deductions. Process supervision lets the training signal touch those intermediate deductions directly. The paper explores this because a reward at the very end may be too sparse for difficult problems. In their experiments, this finer-grained supervision is especially relevant for the more complex reasoning tasks where a complete answer depends on several linked steps.

# Step boundary detection and per-step reward aggregation

The paper identifies step boundaries using the end token index of each step. That is, the $j$-th step in output $o_i$ ends at token position $index(j)$. The process reward model assigns a reward to each such boundary, and the collection of all these rewards across the group is denoted by $\mathbf{R}$.

Just as in outcome supervision, these step rewards are normalized within the group by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation:

$$
\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)} = \frac{r_i^{index(j)} - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{R})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{R})}.
$$

This aggregation step matters because it turns raw step scores into a comparable scale across all sampled outputs for the same question. The paper’s design keeps the supervision tied to the structure of the reasoning trace: the reward model does not merely say “this answer is better,” but “this step, at this boundary, is better or worse than the others in the group.” That is what makes process supervision more localized than outcome supervision.

# Token-level advantage construction under process supervision

Once step rewards have been normalized, the paper assigns token-level advantages by summing the normalized rewards from the current token onward. Specifically,

$$
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}.
$$

This means that each token receives the reward contributions of the steps that follow it, including the step that ends at or after that token position.

The intuition is that a token should be credited for the future reasoning steps it helps make possible. In this way, process supervision spreads the supervision signal backward through the solution rather than flattening it across the entire output. Compared with outcome supervision, the gradient coefficient becomes more sensitive to where in the reasoning chain the model did something useful. The paper reports that GRPO with process supervision, denoted GRPO+PS, performs better than GRPO with outcome supervision, denoted GRPO+OS, which supports the idea that step-aware gradient coefficients are valuable in mathematical reasoning.

# Iterative GRPO with reward-model refresh and replay of historical data

The paper also studies an iterative variant of GRPO. The motivation is that as the policy improves, a reward model trained on earlier samples may become less suitable for supervising the current policy. To address this, the authors repeatedly generate new reward-model training data from the policy’s sampled outputs and continue training the reward model over time.

A replay mechanism is used in this refresh process: the updated reward model is trained on new data while also incorporating $10\%$ of historical data. This helps the reward model stay connected to earlier training examples while still adapting to the policy’s current behavior. The iterative loop alternates between sampling outputs from the policy, updating the reward model, and then using that refreshed reward model to train the policy again. The paper reports that this iterative RL procedure significantly improves performance, especially in the first iteration.

# Reference-model updates and exploration loops in iterative training

Algorithmically, iterative GRPO uses a moving reference model. At the start of each outer iteration, the current policy becomes the reference model, written as $\pi_{ref} \leftarrow \pi_\theta$. During the inner loop, the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$ samples outputs, the reward model scores them, advantages are computed, and the policy is updated. The paper’s GRPO objective also includes a KL regularization term between the current policy and the reference policy, which keeps the update from drifting too far.

This moving-reference design is one of the key protocol choices in the paper. It differs from the standard PPO setup, where a value function is trained alongside the policy and the reference model is typically the initial SFT model. GRPO removes the separate critic model and instead uses group-based baselines, while the KL term is applied directly in the loss rather than folded into the reward calculation. The result is a training loop that is both simpler in memory usage and tailored to the structure of sampled mathematical solutions.

# Practical RL settings: number of prompts, 64 sampled outputs per question, sequence length, batch size, learning rates, and KL coefficient

The paper’s RL experiments are run on DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B. The RL data consist of about $144\mathrm{K}$ chain-of-thought-format questions related to GSM8K and MATH, drawn from the supervised fine-tuning data. The authors deliberately exclude the other SFT questions so that they can study how RL affects benchmarks that were not directly seen during the RL phase. For reward-model training, they follow the construction procedure used in the paper and initialize the reward model from DeepSeekMath-Base 7B with a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$.

For GRPO, the policy learning rate is set to $10^{-6}$, the KL coefficient is $0.04$, and the model samples $64$ outputs per question. The maximum sequence length is $1024$, and the batch size is $1024$. The policy model receives a single update after each exploration stage. These are the concrete settings behind the reported DeepSeekMath-RL 7B results, including $88.2\%$ on GSM8K and $51.7\%$ on MATH.

# Defining in-domain and out-of-domain tasks during RL evaluation

The paper defines the RL in-domain tasks very specifically. For DeepSeekMath-RL 7B, GSM8K and MATH under chain-of-thought reasoning are treated as in-domain because they match the RL training data. All the other benchmarks in the evaluation suite are considered out-of-domain tasks.

This distinction is important because it lets the authors test whether RL improves only the exact tasks used for training or whether it also transfers to other mathematical settings. In the paper’s evaluation, out-of-domain tasks include other English and Chinese benchmarks, and the authors report that the RL stage improves performance there as well, including on CMATH. So the reward design is not only about boosting scores on the training-aligned tasks; it is also used to study whether the learned reasoning improvements generalize beyond them.
