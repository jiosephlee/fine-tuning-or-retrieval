\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 7: Supervised Fine-Tuning for Mathematical Assistance

# Goals of instruction tuning after continual pre-training

After continual pre-training, a language model may already know a great deal about mathematics, but that does not automatically mean it will answer math questions in the way a user expects. Supervised fine-tuning is the stage that turns broad mathematical competence into reliable instruction following. In the paper, this stage takes the base model DeepSeekMath-Base and trains it on curated problem-solution pairs so that it learns to respond in the formats that are useful for mathematical assistance.

The goal is not simply to make the model produce answers, but to make it produce answers in mathematically appropriate ways. That means it should be able to give a self-contained textual solution when no tools are allowed, or to express its reasoning in a form that can use a Python program when tool use is allowed. The resulting model, DeepSeekMath-Instruct $7$B, is therefore designed to bridge the gap between a mathematically capable base model and a model that can reliably follow mathematical instructions.

# The 776K-example mathematical SFT dataset

The supervised fine-tuning dataset contains $776$K training examples. This scale matters because mathematical instruction tuning is not just about seeing a small number of exemplars; it is about exposing the model to a wide range of problem types, difficulty levels, and solution styles. The paper constructs the dataset so that it covers both English and Chinese problems and spans multiple mathematical fields.

Just as importantly, the examples are not all written in the same way. They are paired with solutions in chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated reasoning formats. This means the model is trained not only on final answers, but on the kinds of reasoning traces that lead to those answers. In the paper’s design, the SFT corpus is intended to teach the model how to solve mathematical problems as well as how to present the solution in a form that matches the task.

# English sources: GSM8K, MATH, MathInstruct, and Lila-OOD

The English portion of the instruction-tuning data is built from several sources. The paper annotates GSM8K and MATH problems with tool-integrated solutions, which gives the model exposure to high-quality examples of how to solve quantitative reasoning problems step by step and, when helpful, with program-based tools. In addition, it adopts a subset of MathInstruct and the training set of Lila-OOD, where the problems are solved with chain-of-thought or program-of-thought reasoning.

This mixture is important because it gives the model a broad view of English mathematical problem solving. The paper says the English collection covers algebra, probability, number theory, calculus, and geometry. In other words, the model is not trained on one narrow kind of math question; it is trained across several common domains of mathematical reasoning, which helps it learn a more general instruction-following behavior.

# Chinese K-12 data collection across 76 subtopics

The paper also includes a substantial Chinese instruction-tuning component. It collects Chinese K-12 mathematical problems spanning $76$ subtopics, including topics such as linear equations. These examples are annotated in both chain-of-thought and tool-integrated reasoning format, so the model sees Chinese mathematical questions not just as isolated items, but as structured reasoning tasks with explicit solution paths.

This multilingual component is a major part of the design. The paper emphasizes that the DeepSeekMath instruction data is not English-only, and that this broader language coverage helps the model perform better on Chinese mathematical benchmarks. For a student reading the paper, the key idea is that instruction tuning is used here not merely to make the model “more chatty,” but to make it a mathematically useful assistant across languages and educational contexts.

# Solution formats: chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated reasoning

The paper uses three solution formats, and each serves a distinct purpose. Chain-of-thought is the familiar step-by-step textual explanation. It teaches the model to externalize intermediate reasoning rather than jumping directly to an answer. Program-of-thought goes a step further by asking the model to write a Python program, where libraries such as `math` and `sympy` can be used for computation, and the program’s execution result becomes the answer. Tool-integrated reasoning combines natural language reasoning with program-based tool use.

These formats matter because mathematical assistance is not only about correctness; it is also about how the model reaches the answer. CoT trains the model to explain. PoT trains it to compute. Tool-integrated reasoning trains it to mix explanation and computation when a problem benefits from both. In the paper, the instruction-tuning dataset deliberately includes all three, so the model can learn multiple ways of handling a problem rather than being locked into a single style.

# Training procedure: packed examples, 4K context, learning rate, and number of steps

The training procedure for DeepSeekMath-Instruct is straightforward but carefully specified. The model is initialized from DeepSeekMath-Base. Training examples are randomly concatenated until they reach a maximum context length of $4$K tokens. The model is then trained for $500$ steps with a batch size of $256$ and a constant learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-5}$.

Random concatenation is essentially a packing strategy: instead of presenting each example in isolation, the training procedure fills the available context window with multiple examples when possible. The paper does not frame this as a novelty; rather, it is part of a practical training recipe that lets the model learn efficiently from the instruction data. The overall message is that the instruction-tuning stage is compact compared with pre-training, but still sufficient to shape the model into a strong mathematical assistant.

# DeepSeekMath-Instruct performance on math benchmarks without tools

When tool use is disallowed, DeepSeekMath-Instruct $7$B is evaluated on quantitative reasoning benchmarks in English and Chinese as a pure language model that must produce self-contained solutions. In this setting, the paper reports strong step-by-step reasoning performance. On the competition-level MATH benchmark, the model surpasses all open-source models and most proprietary systems by at least $9\%$ absolute.

The comparison is especially notable because the model is only $7$B parameters. In the paper’s results, it rivals proprietary models such as GLM-4 and Baichuan-3 on MATH, while still underperforming GPT-4 and Gemini Ultra. The broader point is that instruction tuning on carefully curated mathematical data can produce a small model that is highly competitive, even if it does not fully close the gap to the strongest closed-source systems.

# DeepSeekMath-Instruct performance with tool use on quantitative reasoning tasks

The paper also evaluates the model in a setting where natural language reasoning and program-based tool use are allowed. This is important because many mathematical problems become easier when the model can compute intermediate quantities or verify steps with a program. Under this evaluation mode, DeepSeekMath-Instruct $7$B approaches an accuracy of $60\%$ on MATH and surpasses all existing open-source models.

The tool-use setting shows that the instruction-tuned model is not limited to pure text reasoning. It can also benefit from the kind of program-oriented reasoning introduced in the training data through program-of-thought and tool-integrated examples. On the other benchmarks, the model is competitive with DeepSeek-LLM-Chat $67$B, which the paper describes as a prior state-of-the-art model that is about $10$ times larger. That comparison is one of the clearest signs that the SFT data is doing more than just polishing the output style; it is helping the model perform at a much higher level than its size alone might suggest.

# Comparisons against large open-source chat models and proprietary systems

The paper benchmarks DeepSeekMath-Instruct against a wide range of systems. On the proprietary side, the comparison includes the GPT family, Gemini Ultra and Pro, Inflection-2, Grok-1, Baichuan-3, and GLM-4. On the open-source side, it includes general-purpose chat models such as DeepSeek-LLM-Chat $67$B, Qwen $72$B, SeaLLM-v2 $7$B, and ChatGLM3 $6$B, as well as math-oriented models including InternLM2-Math $20$B, Math-Shepherd-Mistral $7$B, WizardMath, MetaMath, ToRA, and MAmmoTH.

These comparisons show that the DeepSeekMath instruction-tuned model is not just strong relative to similarly sized open models; it is strong relative to much larger ones as well. The paper highlights that, without tools, it exceeds all open-source models and most proprietary ones on the hardest benchmark it reports, while with tools it surpasses the open-source field on MATH and remains competitive elsewhere. For a first-time reader, the significance is that the instruction-tuning stage is evaluated in a demanding setting against both large chat systems and specialized math systems, not against a weak baseline.

# Why a 7B instruct model can compete with much larger aligned models

The paper’s results suggest that parameter count is not the only thing that determines mathematical performance. DeepSeekMath-Instruct $7$B is relatively small, but it is built on a strong base model and trained on a carefully curated mathematical instruction set that covers multiple languages, multiple domains, and multiple reasoning styles. That combination gives it a very efficient route to becoming a capable math assistant.

A second reason is that the model is not trained on generic instruction data alone. It is trained on mathematical problem-solution pairs that directly match the tasks it is later evaluated on. The paper’s English and Chinese coverage, together with the use of chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated reasoning, gives the model examples of how to solve problems and how to present those solutions. In that sense, the model is small but focused: it learns the behaviors that matter most for mathematical assistance.

At the same time, the paper is careful not to overstate the result. DeepSeekMath-Instruct is very strong on quantitative reasoning, but it still trails the strongest closed-source systems such as GPT-4 and Gemini Ultra on some benchmarks. Even so, the paper shows that a well-designed supervised fine-tuning stage can bring a $7$B model into the range of much larger aligned models, which is exactly why the SFT stage is such an important part of the overall DeepSeekMath pipeline.
