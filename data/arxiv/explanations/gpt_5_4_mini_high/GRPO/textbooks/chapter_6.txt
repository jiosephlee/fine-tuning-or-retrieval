\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 6: DeepSeekMath-Base Across Mathematics, Proof, and General Reasoning

# Few-shot chain-of-thought evaluation on English and Chinese math benchmarks

DeepSeekMath-Base $7$B is the pre-alignment version of the model: it has been continually pre-trained, but it has not yet gone through supervised instruction tuning or reinforcement learning. That makes it especially useful for understanding what the pre-training stage itself contributes. In the paper, the model is evaluated with few-shot chain-of-thought prompting on a broad set of mathematical benchmarks in both English and Chinese, spanning problems from elementary quantitative reasoning to college-level competition math.

The idea behind few-shot chain-of-thought evaluation is straightforward. Instead of asking the model for a final answer immediately, the prompt includes a small number of solved examples that show intermediate reasoning steps. The benchmark then tests whether the model can continue that style of reasoning on a new problem. This is a more demanding test than checking only final answers, because the model has to maintain a coherent sequence of steps rather than relying on superficial pattern matching.

Across eight mathematical benchmarks, DeepSeekMath-Base is reported to lead the open-source base models tested in the paper. These benchmarks include GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, MMLU-STEM, and Gaokao-MathQA, together with the other English and Chinese math benchmarks described in the paper. The evaluation is important not only because it covers different difficulty levels, but also because it covers different formats. Some tasks are quantitative and require free-form derivations, while others are multiple-choice and still demand substantial reasoning.

One especially notable result is that the model performs strongly on Chinese benchmarks as well as English ones. The paper attributes this to the fact that the DeepSeekMath corpus is multilingual, rather than being restricted to English-only mathematics data. That matters because mathematical reasoning is often assumed to be language-neutral, but in practice the language in which a problem is written can affect performance. The model’s stronger Chinese results are presented as evidence that including high-quality non-English math data can broaden, rather than narrow, a model’s competence.

# Performance relative to open models such as Mistral $7$B and Llemma $34$B

The paper uses several open models as points of comparison, and the results show that DeepSeekMath-Base is not just a small improvement over them but a clear step ahead. In particular, it surpasses both Mistral $7$B and Llemma $34$B across the mathematical benchmarks discussed in the paper, regardless of whether those baselines had undergone math-specific training. This is important because the two baselines represent different starting points. Mistral $7$B is a general-purpose open model, while Llemma $34$B has already been adapted toward math using Proof-Pile-2.

That makes the comparison especially informative. If a math-specialized $7$B model can outperform a much larger $34$B model that was also trained for math, then the result is not simply about having more parameters or having any math training at all. It suggests that the content and construction of the training data matter a great deal. DeepSeekMath-Base benefits from the DeepSeekMath Corpus, which the paper argues is both larger and higher quality than the competing corpora used in prior work.

The paper also emphasizes that the model beats open-source base models by a substantial margin on the competition-level MATH benchmark. In the broader benchmark summary, it is described as outperforming the open-source base models by over $10\%$ absolute on MATH. This is a strong result because MATH is one of the hardest standard benchmarks in the paper, and it is intended to probe high-school and competition-level mathematical reasoning rather than simple arithmetic.

What this comparison tells the reader is that pre-training on carefully selected mathematical web data can be more effective than simply scaling a model or relying on a conventional mixture of general text and math papers. In other words, the benchmark results support the paper’s claim that high-quality math pre-training is a central ingredient in the model’s performance.

# Comparison to Minerva $540$B and what it says about data versus parameter count

One of the most striking claims in the paper is that DeepSeekMath-Base $7$B outperforms Minerva $540$B on the competition-level MATH benchmark. That comparison is intentionally dramatic: Minerva is a much larger closed model, and the paper describes it as $77$ times larger than DeepSeekMath-Base. Yet the smaller model still does better on this benchmark.

This result is the paper’s clearest argument that parameter count alone does not determine mathematical reasoning ability. If larger size were the main factor, the $540$B model should have had an overwhelming advantage. Instead, the paper shows that a much smaller model can compete effectively when it is trained on a large, high-quality math corpus and initialized from a code model rather than a generic language model.

The comparison to Minerva also helps clarify what the DeepSeekMath pipeline is trying to achieve. The model is not designed to prove that small models are always better than large ones. Rather, it demonstrates that data quality, data selection, and training composition can compensate for a large gap in size. The paper’s conclusion is not that scale does not matter, but that scale is only one part of the story.

This is why the authors frame the result as evidence that publicly available web data contains far more useful mathematical information than had previously been assumed. Their DeepSeekMath Corpus is much larger than the math web pages used by Minerva and larger than OpenWebMath as well. The implication is that better curation can unlock performance that would otherwise be hidden inside ordinary web text.

# Program-aided reasoning on GSM8K and MATH using Python

The paper also evaluates DeepSeekMath-Base in a setting where the model is allowed to write and execute Python programs. This is the program-of-thought style of evaluation, and it is used on GSM8K and MATH. In this setup, the model is prompted to solve a problem by writing a program, with libraries such as $\textit{math}$ and $\textit{sympy}$ available for calculation. The final output of the program is treated as the answer.

This evaluation matters because it separates two parts of problem solving that are often entangled in textual answers. The model must still understand the problem, decide how to represent it computationally, and produce a correct program. But it does not have to do all arithmetic or symbolic manipulation in its hidden chain of thought. Instead, it can offload exact computation to Python. That makes the test a useful measure of whether the model can turn mathematical reasoning into executable procedure.

The paper reports that DeepSeekMath-Base $7$B outperforms the prior state of the art, Llemma $34$B, in this tool-using setting. This is a particularly meaningful result because the tool-using mode is not a trivial extension of ordinary text answering. A model that is good at writing coherent explanatory prose is not automatically good at writing programs that compute the right answer. The fact that DeepSeekMath-Base does well here suggests that math pre-training improves not only abstract reasoning but also the ability to organize reasoning into precise algorithmic steps.

The broader significance is that math competence and code competence are not separate islands. In the paper’s framing, the same pre-training that helps with mathematical reasoning can also strengthen a model’s ability to translate reasoning into code. That is one reason the model’s pre-training mix includes code in addition to math and natural language.

# Formal theorem proving via autoformalization on miniF2F with Isabelle and Sledgehammer

The paper does not restrict its evaluation to ordinary word problems. It also tests the model on formal mathematics, specifically the informal-to-formal proving task from DSP-Proof on miniF2F. Here the goal is to generate a formal proof from three inputs: an informal statement, a formal version of the statement, and an informal proof. The proof assistant used in the evaluation is Isabelle.

This is a much stricter setting than standard benchmark math. In ordinary math benchmarks, a solution can be expressed in natural language and still be considered correct if it reaches the right answer. In formal theorem proving, by contrast, the output must satisfy the syntax and logic of a proof assistant. Even a mathematically sound argument is not enough if it is not rendered in the precise formal language required by the system.

To support the generation of formal proofs, the paper follows the DSP-Proof approach of using proof sketches and then invoking the automated prover Sledgehammer to fill in missing details. That means the model is not expected to solve every formalization step alone. Instead, it must produce a useful scaffold that can be completed by an external prover. This makes the task a test of how well the model can bridge informal mathematical intuition and formal symbolic expression.

DeepSeekMath-Base is reported to show strong few-shot autoformalization performance on this benchmark. That is important because formal theorem proving is a very different kind of reasoning from answering contest-style math questions. Success here suggests that the benefits of mathematical pre-training extend beyond numeric calculation and into structured logical representation. In other words, the model is learning something about the form of mathematics, not just the answers.

# Results on MMLU and BBH as measures of general understanding and reasoning

A key question in the paper is whether math pre-training helps only on math benchmarks or whether it transfers to broader reasoning tasks. To study that, the authors evaluate DeepSeekMath-Base on MMLU and BBH. MMLU contains $57$ multiple-choice tasks spanning diverse subjects, while BBH includes $23$ challenging tasks that mostly require multi-step reasoning.

These benchmarks are not math-specific in the same way that GSM8K or MATH are, but they still reward the ability to follow structured arguments, combine evidence across steps, and avoid shallow heuristics. That makes them useful for checking whether the model has gained more general reasoning ability, not just more mathematical fluency.

The paper reports that DeepSeekMath-Base shows significant improvements on both MMLU and BBH compared with its precursor, DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5. This is an important result because the model started from a code-oriented base, not from a general language model. The improvement implies that the mathematics-focused continuation of pre-training does more than specialize the model for equations. It also strengthens capabilities that show up in broader understanding and reasoning benchmarks.

The paper further notes that DeepSeekMath-Base outperforms Mistral $7$B on these reasoning-oriented benchmarks. This provides another piece of evidence for the wider transfer effect of math training. A model can become better at general reasoning by training on mathematics, because mathematics itself is a domain that requires structured inference, careful variable tracking, and disciplined step-by-step thinking. The benchmark results support that intuition directly.

# Results on HumanEval and MBPP as measures of retained coding ability

Because DeepSeekMath-Base is initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5, the authors also check whether math continuation harms coding performance. They evaluate on HumanEval and MBPP, two widely used code-generation benchmarks. HumanEval and MBPP are standard tests for whether a model can produce correct Python programs from natural-language descriptions.

This part of the evaluation matters because domain-specific training can sometimes cause forgetting. If a model is trained heavily on mathematics, it might become better at math but worse at code. The paper explicitly looks for this possibility and finds that DeepSeekMath-Base effectively preserves the coding ability of its code-trained predecessor.

The reason, according to the paper, is that the continual pre-training mix includes code tokens alongside math and natural language. In the reported training distribution, $20\%$ of the tokens are GitHub code, and the authors note that this helps maintain performance on code benchmarks. The model thus does not have to choose between math and coding competence; the pre-training recipe is designed to preserve both.

This result is especially important because it shows that math pre-training does not have to come at the cost of general utility. A model can gain mathematical reasoning power while remaining a capable code model. In practice, that means the same base model can support both symbolic reasoning and program synthesis, which is consistent with the paper’s broader view that code and math are closely related forms of structure-sensitive prediction.

# Why math pre-training can improve general reasoning without sacrificing code performance

Taken together, the benchmark results paint a coherent picture. Math pre-training improves the model’s performance on math benchmarks, but it also raises scores on MMLU and BBH, and it does so without erasing coding ability on HumanEval and MBPP. The paper’s interpretation is that mathematical data teaches the model something broadly useful about reasoning, while code data in the training mix protects the model’s programming skills.

The paper also provides an additional clue about why this works. In the discussion section, the authors report that code training before math training improves the ability to solve mathematical problems both with and without tool use. That means the relationship runs in both directions: code helps math, and math helps broader reasoning. The model seems to benefit from the shared structure of these domains, where exactness, multi-step planning, and compositional rules all matter.

At the same time, the paper is careful not to overclaim. It notes that arXiv papers, although commonly used in math pre-training, did not bring notable improvements in the benchmarks studied here. That contrast is revealing. It suggests that not every source of technical text is equally useful for improving reasoning. What matters is not merely domain relevance in the broad sense, but whether the data contains the right kind of mathematical structure and whether it is filtered and scaled effectively.

So the paper’s empirical lesson is not “more domain data is always better.” It is closer to “carefully selected domain data can unlock transfer to both narrow and broad capabilities.” The DeepSeekMath Corpus is the concrete example of that claim.

# The broader lesson: domain-specific training can amplify general capabilities

The largest lesson from the base-model evaluation is that domain-specific training does not have to be a narrowing force. In DeepSeekMath, mathematics pre-training improves a wide range of abilities that are useful beyond math: step-by-step reasoning, code generation, and even formal proof behavior. At the same time, the model remains competitive as a code model because the training mix preserves code tokens and starts from a code-oriented backbone.

This is the key intuition the paper asks the reader to take away. A model trained on carefully curated mathematical web data can become more than a specialist calculator. It can become a stronger reasoner in general, provided the data pipeline is good and the surrounding training recipe is balanced. The gains on MMLU and BBH show general transfer; the gains on HumanEval and MBPP show that the transfer does not require sacrificing other skills.

The paper also gives an important cautionary note. Even though DeepSeekMath-Base is very strong on quantitative reasoning, it is still weaker than closed models on geometry and theorem-proof tasks, and it has weaker few-shot capability than GPT-4 in the authors’ discussion. So the lesson is not that domain-specific training solves every problem. Rather, it shows that a thoughtfully engineered domain curriculum can push open models much further than one might expect, especially when the domain is mathematics.

In that sense, DeepSeekMath-Base serves as a case study in capability amplification. Mathematical pre-training does not simply add a new skill; it can sharpen the model’s overall reasoning machinery. That is why the base-model evaluation is so central to the paper: it isolates the effect of the pre-training data and shows that the gains are already visible before any alignment or reinforcement learning is applied.
