\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 4: Data Quality, Diversity, and Decontamination

# Benchmark contamination and why it threatens fair evaluation

A central lesson of DeepSeekMath is that a mathematical corpus is only useful if it can be trusted. In language model research, this means more than simply collecting a large amount of text. It also means making sure the training data does not accidentally include the benchmark questions the model will later be evaluated on. If that happens, the model may perform well because it has already seen the problem or a near copy of it, not because it has learned to reason correctly. In that case, the evaluation score becomes a blurred mixture of genuine generalization and memorization.

The paper treats this issue seriously because its goal is to compare corpora fairly. DeepSeekMath is evaluated on English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks such as GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, and AGIEval. If web pages containing those benchmark questions or answers were left in the pre-training corpus, the resulting numbers could overstate the model’s true ability. The authors therefore explicitly remove contaminated web pages before training. This is not just a bookkeeping detail: it is part of what makes the reported benchmark results meaningful.

# N-gram-based decontamination for English and Chinese benchmarks

To prevent benchmark contamination, the paper uses an exact-match filter based on n-grams. The basic idea is straightforward: if a training web page contains a text segment that exactly matches a sufficiently long substring from an evaluation benchmark, then that page is suspicious and should be removed. The authors apply this rule to benchmark questions and answers from both English mathematical benchmarks and Chinese ones.

The specific rule is that any text segment containing a $10$-gram string that exactly matches any substring from the evaluation benchmarks is filtered out of the math training corpus. For benchmark texts shorter than $10$ grams but at least $3$ grams long, the paper uses exact matching as well. This two-level rule reflects a practical compromise. Longer exact matches are strong evidence of contamination, while shorter benchmark fragments still provide enough structure to warrant removal. The outcome is a corpus that is benchmark-safe in the sense used by the paper: it is less likely to contain direct copies of evaluation content.

This decontamination procedure matters because mathematical benchmarks often involve precise wording, formulas, and solution steps. A model can gain an unfair advantage from even partial overlap, especially if the overlap includes the core statement of the problem. By removing pages that contain exact benchmark fragments, the authors reduce the risk that their reported scores are inflated by accidental leakage from the test set into the training set.

# Comparison corpora: MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2

To judge the quality of DeepSeekMath Corpus, the paper compares it against three existing mathematical corpora. The first is MathPile, a multi-source corpus of $8.9$B tokens built from textbooks, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, CommonCrawl, StackExchange, and arXiv. A key detail is that more than $85\%$ of MathPile comes from arXiv, so although it is labeled as a multi-source corpus, it is heavily dominated by scientific paper text.

The second comparison corpus is OpenWebMath, which contains $13.6$B tokens of CommonCrawl data filtered for mathematical content. This corpus is important because it also comes from the web, making it a natural point of comparison for DeepSeekMath Corpus. The third corpus is Proof-Pile-2, a larger mixture consisting of OpenWebMath, AlgebraicStack, and arXiv papers. When the authors experiment on Proof-Pile-2, they follow the paper’s prescribed arXiv:web:code ratio of $2{:}4{:}1$. Together, these corpora represent different philosophies of math data collection: one leans heavily on research papers, one on filtered web pages, and one on a broader mixture of web, code, and papers.

DeepSeekMath Corpus differs from all three in two important ways emphasized by the paper. First, it is much larger, reaching $120$B tokens. Second, it is built to preserve mathematical diversity rather than rely primarily on arXiv. This makes it possible to ask not only whether the corpus is big, but whether its content is the kind of mathematical text that actually helps models learn to reason.

# Controlled corpus-quality experiments using DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B

The paper does not compare these corpora in an uncontrolled way. Instead, it creates a clean experimental setup using the same base model for all corpus comparisons. Specifically, the authors take a general pre-trained language model with $1.3$B parameters, DeepSeek-LLM $1.3$B, and continue pre-training it on each mathematical corpus separately. Each corpus is used for $150$B tokens. This design isolates the effect of the corpus itself, because the architecture, the optimizer, and the overall training budget are held fixed.

This is an important methodological choice. If one model were larger, trained longer, or optimized differently, then any performance gap could be due to those confounding factors rather than corpus quality. By contrast, the paper’s setup asks a much cleaner question: if you feed the same model different math corpora under the same training regime, which corpus teaches it more useful mathematical behavior? That is the right experimental question for understanding data quality.

The evaluation uses few-shot chain-of-thought prompting on eight mathematical benchmarks. The paper reports the results as evidence about downstream reasoning, not just about next-token prediction. In other words, the corpus is judged by what it enables the model to do after pre-training, which is exactly the kind of judgment that matters for mathematical language models.

# Training settings for fair corpus comparison

The comparison experiments use the same training framework and nearly the same hyperparameters across corpora. All experiments are conducted with the HAI-LLM training framework. The optimizer is AdamW with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, and weight decay $0.1$. The learning rate follows a multi-step schedule: it reaches its peak after $2{,}000$ warmup steps, drops to $31.6\%$ of the peak after $80\%$ of training, and falls to $10.0\%$ of the peak after $90\%$ of training. The maximum learning rate is set to $5.3 \times 10^{-4}$, the batch size is $4$M tokens, and the context length is $4$K.

These details matter because corpus comparisons can otherwise be misleading. A corpus that appears better might simply have benefited from a more favorable optimization setup. By using one consistent training recipe, the paper ensures that differences in final performance are more plausibly attributed to the corpora themselves. That is the essence of a controlled experiment: hold everything constant except the factor you want to study.

The Proof-Pile-2 setting receives one additional corpus-specific detail, the arXiv:web:code ratio of $2{:}4{:}1$. The authors include that ratio because they are following the established practice for that dataset. Even with that adjustment, the overall experimental logic remains the same: the goal is not to make one corpus look artificially weak or strong, but to compare them on equal footing.

# Evidence that DeepSeekMath Corpus has higher average quality than competing corpora

The paper’s main conclusion from these corpus-comparison experiments is that DeepSeekMath Corpus is of higher average quality than the alternatives. When DeepSeek-LLM $1.3$B is trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus, it shows a clear performance lead over the models trained on MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2. This lead appears on all eight mathematical benchmarks used in the comparison.

One especially telling result is that the model trained on DeepSeekMath Corpus performs better than Proof-Pile-2 at $50$B tokens, which the paper describes as one full epoch of Proof-Pile-2. That comparison is informative because it controls for exposure to the training data. If a model trained on one corpus learns more quickly or reaches higher accuracy after the same amount of token exposure, then each token in that corpus is, on average, more useful for mathematical learning. The paper interprets this as evidence that DeepSeekMath Corpus has higher average quality.

This result also highlights a subtle but important point about corpus evaluation. A larger dataset is not automatically better if much of its content is noisy, redundant, or weakly related to the target skill. The DeepSeekMath experiments suggest that quality can dominate sheer source familiarity. A corpus mined and filtered for mathematical content can outperform one that is broader or paper-heavy, even when the model and training budget are held fixed.

# Multilingual coverage and why it helps Chinese mathematical reasoning

Another reason DeepSeekMath Corpus stands out is that it is multilingual. The paper says it includes data in multiple languages, with English and Chinese as the two most represented languages. This matters because mathematical reasoning is not language-neutral in practice. Even when the underlying logic is the same, the surface form of a problem, the vocabulary used, and the phrasing of the question can all affect model performance.

The paper reports that training on DeepSeekMath Corpus improves mathematical reasoning in both English and Chinese. That is notable because the competing mathematical corpora are primarily English-centric. In the paper’s experiments, those English-heavy corpora give limited improvements and may even hurt Chinese mathematical reasoning. DeepSeekMath Corpus does better because it does not treat non-English data as an afterthought. Instead, it incorporates high-quality non-English content into the math pre-training mixture.

For a student reading the paper, the important intuition is that multilingual coverage is not simply a matter of fairness or inclusiveness. It can directly improve the model’s ability to handle tasks in languages other than English. In this case, the paper connects that idea to Chinese mathematical benchmarks such as MGSM-zh, CMATH, Gaokao-MathCloze, and Gaokao-MathQA. The lesson is that a mathematically useful corpus should reflect the languages in which the model will actually be used.

# Scale effects and learning curves for large versus small math corpora

The paper argues that DeepSeekMath Corpus is not only cleaner and more diverse, but also much larger than existing mathematical corpora. It is several times larger than prior datasets, and the authors emphasize that size interacts with quality in an important way. When DeepSeek-LLM $1.3$B is trained on DeepSeekMath Corpus, the learning curve is steeper and the improvements last longer. That means performance keeps rising for more of the training process instead of flattening early.

This stands in contrast to the smaller corpora used as baselines. According to the paper, those corpora are much smaller and have already been repeated multiple times during training. Once a model has seen the same limited set of examples again and again, the training signal becomes less novel. The result is a plateau: performance rises quickly at first, then stops improving much because the model is no longer encountering enough fresh mathematical material.

The scale effect here is not just about having more data in the abstract. It is about whether the data contains enough distinct mathematical patterns to keep teaching the model. DeepSeekMath Corpus gives the model more room to learn because it contains more web pages, more topics, and more linguistic variety. That is why the paper treats large-scale corpus construction as a genuine research contribution rather than a purely engineering one.

# Why repeated training on small corpora quickly plateaus

The plateauing behavior of smaller corpora is an important part of the paper’s data-quality story. If a corpus is too small, repeated training rounds eventually recycle the same information. At that point, the model has already extracted much of what the data can offer, so additional epochs contribute less new knowledge. The paper observes this phenomenon in the baseline corpora, where performance quickly reaches a ceiling.

DeepSeekMath Corpus avoids this problem better because it is much larger and because its construction pipeline keeps adding new positive examples over multiple iterations. The paper ultimately collects $35.5$M mathematical web pages totaling $120$B tokens, and by the fourth iteration it observes that nearly $98\%$ of the data had already been collected in the third iteration. This kind of iterative mining is important because it expands the corpus beyond the initial seed and reduces the chance that the training set is dominated by a narrow slice of math content.

The practical lesson is that corpus repetition can be useful only up to a point. After that point, more passes over the same limited material do not buy much. A larger, more carefully curated corpus is more likely to keep yielding gains because it presents the model with genuinely new training signal. That is one of the main reasons the authors argue that data quality and scale must be studied together.

# What the validation experiments imply about data-centric model improvement

The validation experiments in the paper support a clear data-centric view of model improvement. They show that public web data, if mined and filtered carefully, can contain substantial mathematical value. They also show that a smaller model trained on high-quality data can rival or outperform much larger systems. This broader conclusion is reinforced later in the paper by DeepSeekMath-Base $7$B, which is initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 $7$B and trained on the full DeepSeekMath mixture. That model achieves $64.2\%$ on GSM8K and $36.2\%$ on MATH, outperforming Minerva $540$B.

Taken together, these results suggest that mathematical capability is not controlled by parameter count alone. Corpus construction, filtering, multilingual coverage, and benchmark safety all shape what the model learns. The paper’s experiments show that an engineered data pipeline can turn Common Crawl into a corpus that meaningfully supports reasoning, even when compared against corpora built from more obviously mathematical sources such as arXiv.

The final takeaway is that better data is not just “more data with less noise.” In DeepSeekMath, better data means a corpus that is large enough to sustain learning, diverse enough to cover multiple languages and mathematical styles, and clean enough to avoid benchmark contamination. The paper’s validation experiments make that argument concrete: if you want stronger mathematical language models, you must think carefully about what goes into the corpus before you ever think about model scale.
