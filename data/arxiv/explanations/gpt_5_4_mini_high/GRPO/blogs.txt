\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# How DeepSeekMath Brought Open-Source AI Closer to GPT-4 on Math

## Why mathematical reasoning is so hard for language models

Math is one of the toughest stress tests for a language model because success depends on more than knowing facts. A model has to follow a chain of steps, keep track of intermediate results, and avoid tiny errors that can break the final answer. On benchmarks like MATH, the problems are competition-level and often require structured reasoning rather than pattern matching.

That is why math has become such an important yardstick for AI progress. Closed models like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra have been strong here, but open-source systems have usually lagged behind.

## What makes DeepSeekMath notable

DeepSeekMath is interesting because it shows that a relatively small open model can get much closer to that closed-model tier than many people expected. The final RL-trained version, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B, reached 51.7% on MATH without external tools or voting. With self-consistency over 64 samples, it went up to 60.9%.

That result matters because it comes from a 7B model, not a giant frontier system. The paper’s broader message is that mathematical reasoning is not just a function of parameter count. Data quality, training mix, and post-training strategy matter a lot.

## The data pipeline is the real story

The authors did not rely on a small hand-curated math corpus. Instead, they mined Common Crawl and built a 120B-token math corpus using an iterative filtering pipeline.

They started with OpenWebMath as a seed set and trained a fastText classifier to distinguish math-like web pages from non-math pages. Then they used that classifier to search Common Crawl for more relevant pages, manually annotated math-heavy domains, and repeated the process over multiple iterations. By the end, they had 35.5 million math-related web pages totaling 120B tokens.

This is a big point: the web already contains a lot of useful mathematical content, but you need a good retrieval and filtering pipeline to extract it. The corpus is also multilingual, which likely helps explain why DeepSeekMath performs well on Chinese math benchmarks too.

## Starting from a code model helped

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B was initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, not from a general-purpose language model. The authors found that starting from a code-trained model was a better choice for math than starting from a generic LLM.

That fits the experimental results. Their discussion shows that code training improves mathematical reasoning, both with and without tool use. In other words, code is not just useful for coding tasks; it seems to transfer into step-by-step reasoning about math as well.

The base model was then trained on a mix of math, code, arXiv, and natural-language data. Even before instruction tuning and reinforcement learning, it was already strong. On their evaluation, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B was competitive with Minerva 540B on math benchmarks, which is a striking example of how far careful training can go.

## Instruction tuning and GRPO pushed it further

After pre-training, the authors fine-tuned the model on mathematical instruction data in chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated formats. That produced DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, which already outperformed other 7B models and was competitive with much larger open-source instruction-tuned models.

The final jump came from reinforcement learning with a method they call Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO. The key idea is simple: instead of training a separate critic model like PPO does, GRPO compares multiple sampled answers to the same question and uses their relative scores as the training signal. This cuts memory and compute cost while still improving the policy.

On the RL stage, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B improved from 82.9% to 88.2% on GSM8K and from 46.8% to 51.7% on MATH. Importantly, it was trained only on a subset of the instruction-tuning data, yet it improved both in-domain and out-of-domain math performance.

## Why this matters for open-source AI

DeepSeekMath shows that open-source math models do not need to be dominated by scale alone. A 7B model can get surprisingly far if it is built on high-quality data, started from the right base, and tuned with a good post-training recipe.

There are a few practical lessons here. First, web data is more valuable than many people assume, as long as you mine it carefully. Second, code training appears to be a useful stepping stone for math reasoning. Third, reinforcement learning can still help even after supervised fine-tuning has already produced a strong model. Finally, not all seemingly relevant sources are equally useful: the paper found that training on arXiv papers did not noticeably improve mathematical reasoning in their setup.

## The limitations still matter

DeepSeekMath is a major step forward, but it is not a solved problem. The paper notes that geometry and theorem-proving remain weaker areas, and that the model still trails GPT-4 on few-shot performance. So this is not a claim that open models have fully caught up.

What it does show is that the gap is narrower than it used to be. For the open-source ecosystem, that is a meaningful shift. It suggests that careful data engineering and lightweight but effective reinforcement learning can deliver real reasoning gains without depending on massive closed training runs.

DeepSeekMath is, in that sense, less a single model than a recipe: mine better data, start from a strong reasoning-friendly base, and use post-training methods that actually shape the model’s outputs. That recipe is likely to matter well beyond mathematics.



# The Secret Ingredient Was the Internet: Mining 120B Math Tokens from the Web

## Why this paper is really about data

DeepSeekMath is often presented as a story about reasoning, but the most interesting part is upstream: the data. The authors show that the web, specifically Common Crawl, contains a huge amount of mathematical text if you know how to find it. Their main contribution on the data side is the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a 120B-token math-focused pre-training set mined from web pages rather than assembled from a small number of hand-picked sources.

That matters because the paper’s best results do not come from a dramatically larger model. They come from a better training signal. A 7B model trained on carefully selected math data is competitive with, and sometimes better than, much larger systems.

## Starting with a small seed and bootstrapping from there

The paper does not try to search Common Crawl directly for every math page. Instead, it uses a bootstrapping loop.

They begin with OpenWebMath, a high-quality collection of math-related web text, and use it as a seed corpus. From that seed, they train a fastText classifier to recognize pages that look like math content. The initial training set is simple: 500,000 positive examples from the seed corpus and 500,000 negative examples from Common Crawl. The classifier is then applied to a deduplicated version of Common Crawl.

This is a practical strategy. Common Crawl is huge, noisy, and full of irrelevant pages. A lightweight classifier is a cheap first filter that can score pages at scale. The authors then rank pages by classifier score and keep the most promising ones.

In the first iteration, they choose the top 40B tokens. That is already a very large corpus, but the process does not stop there.

## Iteration is where the quality comes from

The key idea in the paper is that the initial seed is not diverse enough. A classifier trained only on OpenWebMath will miss many kinds of math pages, especially pages that use different styles, formats, or languages.

So the authors expand the seed corpus iteratively. They organize Common Crawl into domains, where a domain means web pages sharing the same base URL. For each domain, they measure how many pages were collected in the first pass. If more than 10% of a domain’s pages were already recovered, they treat that domain as math-related.

Then comes the human-in-the-loop part. They manually annotate URLs within those math-heavy domains, identify more math-relevant pages that were missed, and add those pages back into the seed corpus. The classifier is retrained on the expanded seed, and the process repeats.

After four iterations, the final corpus contains 35.5 million web pages and 120B tokens. By the fourth round, nearly 98% of the data had already been collected in the third round, so they stop.

That iterative expansion is the real trick. It is not just about finding more pages; it is about steadily broadening what counts as “math-like” so the classifier can recover more of the web’s long tail of mathematical content.

## Cleaning up the training set

Mining useful pages is only half the job. The authors also try to prevent benchmark contamination.

They filter out web pages containing exact overlaps with evaluation benchmarks, including GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, and AGIEval. The rule is straightforward: if a text segment contains a 10-gram that exactly matches a substring from the benchmark, it is removed. For benchmark texts shorter than 10-grams but at least 3-grams long, they also use exact matching to filter contaminated pages.

This is important because a large web crawl can accidentally contain benchmark questions or answers. Without decontamination, benchmark results are much less meaningful.

## Why this corpus mattered more than raw model size

To test whether the corpus was actually good, the authors ran controlled pre-training experiments on a 1.3B model, DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B, and trained it separately on different math corpora for 150B tokens.

They compare against MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2. The result is clear: the DeepSeekMath Corpus leads on the downstream math benchmarks they test. It even performs better than Proof-Pile-2 at 50B tokens, which is one full epoch of that corpus. That suggests the advantage is not just size, but average data quality.

The corpus also helps on Chinese math benchmarks. That is another meaningful result, because many earlier math corpora are heavily English-centric. By not restricting the crawl to English-only sources, DeepSeekMath gains coverage that transfers to Chinese reasoning tasks as well.

In other words, the paper’s data pipeline does three things at once: it scales, it preserves quality, and it keeps the data multilingual.

## The payoff: a smaller model beats much bigger ones

The most striking evidence that the corpus worked comes from DeepSeekMath-Base 7B. This model is initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B and trained for 500B tokens, with the data mix including 56% DeepSeekMath Corpus, 4% AlgebraicStack, 10% arXiv, 20% GitHub code, and 10% natural language data from Common Crawl in English and Chinese.

With that setup, the model reaches 64.2% on GSM8K and 36.2% on MATH, outperforming Minerva 540B, a model 77 times larger. It also surpasses all open-source base models the paper compares against.

That result is the core message of the paper: parameter count is not the only thing that matters. A smaller model trained on a better corpus can close a surprising amount of the gap.

## A useful negative result: arXiv was not the magic ingredient

The paper also reports a result that runs against a common assumption in math-focused LLM work: arXiv papers were not especially helpful in these experiments. Training on arXiv-only corpora did not produce notable gains on the benchmarks they tested, and sometimes even hurt performance.

That does not mean academic papers are useless in general. The authors are careful to note that they did not test every possible task or training mix. But within this study, the stronger signal came from the web-mined math corpus, not from more scientific prose.

## What to take away

The lesson from DeepSeekMath is not that the internet is naturally full of math-ready data. It is that the internet becomes useful when you build the right extraction pipeline.

The paper’s recipe is worth remembering:

- start from a high-quality seed,
- train a simple classifier,
- mine a huge web crawl,
- expand the seed iteratively,
- manually recover missed domains,
- and aggressively filter contamination.

That process turned Common Crawl into a 120B-token math corpus. And that corpus, more than model size alone, is what pushed a 7B model into the range of much larger systems.

If there is a secret ingredient here, it is not just scale. It is curation.



# Why Teaching AI to Code Can Make It Better at Math

## A surprisingly strong starting point

One of the most interesting results in *DeepSeekMath* is not just that the model got better at math, but *how* it got there. The authors did not start from a generic language model. They initialized DeepSeekMath-Base 7B from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, a model already trained heavily on code.

That choice turned out to matter a lot.

In their experiments, the paper shows that starting from a code-trained model is better than starting from a general-purpose LLM when the goal is mathematical reasoning. They also ran a direct comparison: train on code first, then math, versus train on general text first, then math. The code-first path produced better downstream mathematical performance. The effect showed up both when the model solved problems directly and when it used Python as a tool.

This is a useful result because it gives a partial answer to a long-running question in AI: does code training actually help reasoning? For math, at least, the answer from this paper is yes.

## Why code helps with math

The paper does not claim that code and math are the same thing. But they do share a lot of structure.

Both require the model to work with precise symbols, follow strict rules, and keep track of intermediate steps without drifting off course. In programming, a small mistake can break the whole program. In math, a small mistake can break the whole derivation. In both cases, success depends on more than just pattern matching on words.

That overlap shows up clearly in the way the paper evaluates mathematical reasoning. DeepSeekMath is tested in two modes: one where it has to produce a text solution on its own, and another where it can write Python code to help solve the problem. This second mode is important because it makes the connection between code and math explicit. If a model can translate a word problem into executable steps, it has to understand the structure of the problem well enough to manipulate it.

The paper’s results suggest that code-trained models are already better prepared for this kind of structured thinking. They are more comfortable with algorithmic decomposition, with maintaining state across multiple steps, and with generating outputs that have to be internally consistent. Those are exactly the habits that help on mathematical tasks.

## What DeepSeekMath found in practice

The authors tested several training setups to isolate the effect of code.

First, they compared a two-stage approach where the model is trained on code for 400B tokens and then on math for 150B tokens, against a control setup where the first stage uses general text instead of code. The code-first version did better on math benchmarks.

Second, they looked at a one-stage setup where code tokens and math tokens are mixed together. That mixture helped preserve coding ability and improved program-aided mathematical reasoning. But for smaller models, it could hurt pure text-based math reasoning. The paper suggests that this may be a capacity issue: a smaller model may not be able to absorb both streams equally well at the same time.

That nuance matters. The result is not “more code always helps everything.” It is more specific than that. Code seems especially valuable as a foundation for structured reasoning, and the training schedule matters. A model can benefit from code-first pretraining, but a naive mix of code and math is not automatically optimal.

The authors also report that their math training improved general reasoning benchmarks like MMLU and BBH, not just math-specific tasks. So the benefit is broader than arithmetic or contest problems. The model seems to gain a more general ability to handle structured problems.

## Why this matters for model training

The main lesson from DeepSeekMath is that if you want a model to reason well about math, you should not think only about math textbooks and problem sets. Code is also a valuable source of training signal.

That has a few implications.

First, code can act as a kind of reasoning scaffold. It teaches the model how to express multi-step procedures clearly and consistently. That makes it a good pretraining substrate for math, especially when the target task involves algorithms, calculations, or tool use.

Second, training strategy matters as much as data volume. DeepSeekMath is not just “trained on a lot of data.” It is trained with a deliberate sequence: code-heavy initialization, large-scale math web data, supervised fine-tuning, and then reinforcement learning. The code-first step is one part of a larger curriculum.

Third, not all technical text is equally useful. The paper reports that arXiv papers, which are often included in math training corpora, did not noticeably improve mathematical reasoning in their experiments. In contrast, code and carefully selected math-related web data did. That suggests future training pipelines should be more selective and more empirical about which sources actually help reasoning.

## The bigger picture

DeepSeekMath is a strong example of a broader trend: reasoning ability in language models may come from learning multiple forms of structure, not just natural language. Code is one of the cleanest and most demanding structured languages we have. It forces exactness, sequence, and explicit computation. Those same traits are useful in mathematics.

The paper’s takeaway is not that code makes a model “understand math” in a human sense. Rather, code seems to give the model a better procedural foundation for solving structured problems. Once that foundation is in place, math training can push it further.

For future AI systems, that suggests a practical recipe: if you want better mathematical reasoning, don’t treat code as a separate capability. Treat it as part of the path toward reasoning itself.



# GRPO Explained: The New Reinforcement Learning Trick Behind DeepSeekMath

DeepSeekMath is not just a bigger math model. A big part of its jump in performance comes from how the authors fine-tuned it after supervised training. The key ingredient is Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO, a reinforcement learning method designed to improve mathematical reasoning without the memory overhead of standard PPO.

## Why reinforcement learning still matters after supervised fine-tuning

Before reinforcement learning, DeepSeekMath already had a strong supervised fine-tuned model, DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B. It was trained on a large math instruction dataset with chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated examples. That already pushed it ahead of many open-source models.

But the paper shows that supervised fine-tuning is not the end of the story. Reinforcement learning on top of that model gave another noticeable jump. Using GRPO, the model improved from 82.9\% to 88.2\% on GSM8K and from 46.8\% to 51.7\% on MATH. The gains also carried over to out-of-domain math tasks, such as CMATH, where accuracy rose from 84.6\% to 88.8\%.

That is the central message: even after a strong instruction-tuned model is already in place, a well-designed RL stage can still sharpen its reasoning.

## The problem with PPO in LLM training

GRPO is best understood by starting with PPO, the reinforcement learning method it replaces.

In PPO-based language model training, there are usually two models involved:

- the policy model, which generates answers;
- the value function, or critic, which estimates how good an answer is expected to be.

The critic exists to provide a baseline for the policy update. In simple terms, it helps the algorithm decide whether a sampled answer was better or worse than expected. That baseline reduces variance in training, but it comes at a cost: the critic is another large model, so it adds memory and compute overhead.

That overhead matters a lot for language models. A separate value model can be expensive to train and maintain, especially when the policy itself is already large.

## What GRPO changes

GRPO removes the critic entirely.

Instead of training a separate value model, DeepSeekMath samples a group of answers for the same question and uses the group itself to build the baseline. For each prompt $q$, it generates a set of outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \dots, o_G\}$ from the current policy. A reward model scores each output, producing rewards $\{r_1, r_2, \dots, r_G\}$.

Then the rewards are normalized within the group. In the outcome-supervision setting, the advantage for an output is essentially:

\[
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})}
\]

for all tokens in that output.

The intuition is simple. If one answer is better than the others for the same problem, it gets reinforced. If it is worse than the group average, it gets penalized. So the model is not learning against an external critic; it is learning relative to its own sampled attempts.

This is why the method is called Group Relative Policy Optimization.

## Why the group baseline is a good fit for math

The paper argues that this setup matches the way reward models are usually trained. Reward models for reasoning tasks are often built from comparisons between multiple answers to the same question. GRPO uses that same comparative structure during optimization.

That is especially useful for math, where multiple attempts to solve the same problem can vary a lot in quality. Some may be nearly correct but contain a subtle mistake. Others may be correct but poorly reasoned. Comparing several samples from the same question gives the algorithm a much more informative signal than evaluating a single answer in isolation.

The paper also explores process supervision, where rewards are assigned at reasoning steps instead of only at the end. GRPO can use those step-level rewards as well, which makes the training signal finer-grained.

## Why GRPO uses less memory

The biggest practical advantage is resource usage.

PPO normally needs a value function model alongside the policy. That means extra parameters, extra forward passes, and extra training complexity. GRPO avoids that by estimating the baseline from the sampled group of outputs instead of from a learned critic.

So in effect, GRPO keeps the core benefit of policy optimization while dropping the part that is most expensive to maintain. The paper describes this as a significant reduction in training resources compared with PPO.

That makes GRPO a more attractive option when you want RL gains but do not want to pay the full cost of an actor-critic setup.

## What DeepSeekMath actually trained on

For the RL stage, DeepSeekMath does not use a massive new dataset. It uses a subset of the instruction-tuning data, specifically chain-of-thought questions related to GSM8K and MATH, totaling around 144K questions.

That is an important detail. The model is not being exposed to a radically new task distribution. It is being pushed harder on the same kind of reasoning problems it already knows, and RL is used to make its answers more robust.

The authors sample 64 outputs per question during RL, score them, and update the policy using GRPO. Even with that relatively focused setup, the resulting model improves across both in-domain and out-of-domain math benchmarks.

## What the results suggest

The paper’s RL analysis points to an interesting pattern. Reinforcement learning improved the model’s Maj@K behavior more than its Pass@K behavior. In other words, RL appears to make the model more likely to put the correct solution into its top candidates, rather than fundamentally changing its underlying capabilities.

That is a subtle but useful distinction. RL is not magically teaching the model brand-new math. It is improving how reliably the correct answer shows up when the model samples multiple responses. For practical reasoning systems, that can still be a major win.

## The big takeaway

GRPO is DeepSeekMath’s answer to a familiar problem in LLM reinforcement learning: how do you get the benefits of PPO without the cost of a separate critic model?

The paper’s solution is elegant. Sample multiple answers to the same question, score them with a reward model, and use their relative quality as the training signal. That keeps the algorithm lightweight, aligns well with comparison-based rewards, and still delivers real gains in mathematical reasoning.

In DeepSeekMath, that translated into a strong post-SFT boost: 88.2\% on GSM8K and 51.7\% on MATH, with improvements beyond the core benchmarks as well.

If you want a short summary, it is this: GRPO makes reinforcement learning for math models cheaper, simpler, and still effective.



# Bigger Isn’t Always Better: What DeepSeekMath Teaches Us About Training Smarter AI

DeepSeekMath is a good reminder that progress in language models is not just a matter of adding more parameters or scraping more text. In this paper, a 7B model trained on carefully selected math-heavy web data, then refined with reinforcement learning, reaches 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark without external tools or voting. That is strong enough to matter on its own, but the bigger story is the set of lessons behind it.

The paper’s core message is simple: if you want better mathematical reasoning, data quality and training design can matter more than raw scale.

## 1) High-quality domain data can beat brute-force scale

DeepSeekMath starts from a practical observation: Common Crawl contains much more useful math content than people usually assume, but you have to mine it carefully.

The authors build the DeepSeekMath Corpus through an iterative filtering pipeline. They start with OpenWebMath as a seed set, train a fastText classifier to recognize math-like pages, and use it to recall similar pages from deduplicated Common Crawl. They then refine the seed set with human annotation and repeat the process. After four iterations, they end up with 35.5 million mathematical web pages, totaling 120B tokens.

That corpus is not just large; it is also effective. A smaller 1.3B-parameter model trained on it outperforms models trained on other math corpora such as MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2. In the paper’s experiments, the DeepSeekMath corpus gives the best downstream results across eight math benchmarks, and the improvement shows up in both English and Chinese evaluation sets.

This is the first big takeaway: for domain reasoning, a well-built corpus can matter more than a bigger model.

For builders, the implication is practical. If your target domain is structured and specialized, spend real effort on data mining, deduplication, filtering, and contamination checks. DeepSeekMath explicitly removes benchmark overlap by matching 10-grams and shorter exact substrings against evaluation sets. That kind of engineering is not glamorous, but it is part of why the results are credible.

## 2) More academic text is not automatically better

A surprising result in the paper is that arXiv papers were not especially helpful for mathematical reasoning.

This is counter to a common intuition. If a model is being trained for math, surely more formal scientific writing should help. But when the authors test arXiv-heavy corpora such as MathPile and ArXiv-RedPajama, they do not see meaningful gains on the math benchmarks used in the paper. In some cases, performance even gets worse.

The authors are careful here: they do not claim arXiv is useless in all settings. They note that their experiments do not cover every possible math task, every data mixture, or larger model scales. Still, within the scope of this work, the result is clear enough to be worth paying attention to.

The practical lesson is not “never use arXiv.” It is “don’t assume that more formal-looking text is inherently more useful for reasoning.” Data source choice should be validated empirically, not by prestige.

## 3) Code is a surprisingly strong bridge to math

Another useful detail is where DeepSeekMath starts from. The base model is initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, not a general-purpose language model.

That matters. The paper finds that code training before math training improves mathematical reasoning, both when the model is allowed to use tools like Python and when it is not. This suggests that code and math share enough structure to transfer useful reasoning behavior.

The model also retains strong coding performance, and the math-focused continual training improves broader reasoning benchmarks like MMLU and BBH as well.

For practitioners, this is a strong signal. If you are training a model for math-heavy tasks, a code-pretrained backbone may be a better starting point than a generic one. And if you are mixing code and math data, the tradeoff is not always obvious: the paper finds that a one-stage mixture can help, but on a smaller model it can also hurt pure math performance without tools. In other words, transfer is real, but capacity still matters.

## 4) Reinforcement learning may sharpen answer selection more than raw capability

The second major part of the paper is Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO.

GRPO is a variant of PPO that removes the critic model. Instead of training a separate value function, it samples a group of answers for the same question, uses their relative rewards as a baseline, and updates the policy based on those group comparisons. This reduces memory use and fits the comparison-based nature of reward models more naturally.

The results are strong. Starting from DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, GRPO improves GSM8K from 82.9% to 88.2% and MATH from 46.8% to 51.7%, using only a subset of English instruction-tuning data related to GSM8K and MATH. It also improves out-of-domain mathematical benchmarks such as CMATH.

But the most interesting insight is not just that RL helps. It is how it helps.

The authors analyze Pass@K and Maj@K, and find that RL improves Maj@K much more than Pass@K. In other words, reinforcement learning seems to make the model more likely to put the correct answer near the top of its sampled outputs, rather than fundamentally expanding what it can solve from scratch.

That is an important distinction. RL is not magic reasoning fuel. In this setup, it looks more like a way to reshape the output distribution so that correct answers become more robustly selected.

The paper also finds that online sampling beats offline sampling, and that process supervision can outperform outcome supervision. Those results point in the same direction: the training signal matters, but so does how tightly it is tied to the model’s current behavior.

## 5) The practical takeaways

If you are building or researching math-capable models, DeepSeekMath suggests a few concrete habits:

- Put more effort into data curation than you think you need.
- Test whether each data source actually improves the task you care about.
- Do not assume that arXiv or other “formal” text is automatically useful.
- Consider code data as a useful precursor for mathematical reasoning.
- Treat reinforcement learning as a distribution-shaping tool, not a substitute for strong pretraining.

That last point may be the most important one. DeepSeekMath does not argue that scale is irrelevant. It argues that scale works much better when it is paired with the right data and the right optimization strategy.

## Closing thought

DeepSeekMath is impressive because it shows what a smaller model can do when the training recipe is well chosen. The model is not just “bigger in spirit.” It is better targeted. That is a useful direction for the field: not always more parameters, but more care in how we assemble data, pick starting points, and apply reinforcement learning.

In that sense, the paper’s lesson is broader than math. Smarter training often beats brute force.
