\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

Chapter 11: Experimental Evaluation and What the Numbers Mean

# Benchmark Hardware and the Role of the H100 SXM5

The paper evaluates \textsc{FlashAttention-3} on NVIDIA’s Hopper-generation H100 SXM5 GPU, specifically the $80$GB SXM5 variant. This choice is important because the paper’s main claims are about taking advantage of Hopper-specific features, especially asynchronous Tensor Core execution through WGMMA and asynchronous data movement through TMA, as well as FP8 support. In other words, the benchmark hardware is not just a neutral platform: it is the environment in which the algorithm is designed to shine.

When reading the results, it helps to remember that the paper is comparing \textsc{FlashAttention-3} against methods that do not fully exploit the same hardware features, or do so less effectively. That is why the hardware description is part of the argument. The numbers are not merely measuring an implementation in isolation; they are measuring how well different attention implementations use the available GPU execution model.

# Compared Methods and What Each Baseline Represents

The paper compares \textsc{FlashAttention-3} against four main alternatives: standard attention in PyTorch, \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, \textsc{FlashAttention-2} implemented in Triton, and NVIDIA’s cuDNN attention implementation. These baselines are useful because they represent different points in the design space.

Standard PyTorch attention is the most direct reference point. In the paper’s terminology, it materializes the intermediate score and probability matrices, $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$, to HBM. That means it uses more global memory traffic and therefore provides a clear contrast with the fused FlashAttention style. \textsc{FlashAttention-2} is the most immediate predecessor and shows what was already gained by reducing memory reads and writes. The Triton version of \textsc{FlashAttention-2} is especially relevant because it uses H100-specific instructions, so it helps separate algorithmic improvement from instruction-level optimization. Finally, cuDNN serves as a strong vendor baseline: it represents a carefully optimized library implementation for Hopper.

This set of comparisons lets the paper answer different questions at once. If \textsc{FlashAttention-3} beats standard attention, it confirms the value of fusion and tiling. If it beats \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, it shows that the new asynchronous and low-precision ideas matter beyond the earlier FlashAttention design. If it competes with or surpasses cuDNN, it indicates that the paper’s algorithmic choices are strong enough to stand alongside a highly optimized library.

# Benchmark Settings: Sequence Lengths, Token Budget, Hidden Size, Head Dimensions, and Causal Masking

The paper uses a carefully controlled family of benchmarks. Sequence length varies over $512$, $1$k, \dots, $16$k. At the same time, the batch size is chosen so that the total number of tokens is fixed at $16$k. This design means that the benchmark explores how performance changes as the sequence is made longer or shorter, while keeping the overall token budget constant.

The hidden dimension is set to $2048$, and the head dimension is varied among $64$, $128$, and $256$. These correspond to $32$, $16$, and $8$ heads, respectively. This matters because attention work depends not just on the sequence length but also on how the hidden dimension is partitioned across heads. By testing multiple head dimensions, the paper checks whether the speedups are robust across different attention shapes rather than tuned to one special case.

The paper also considers both non-causal and causal masking. With causal masking, only the lower-triangular part of the attention matrix is used, so the amount of work is effectively reduced. The paper reflects this directly in its FLOP accounting. This distinction is important because causal masking changes the computational pattern and can change how effectively a kernel overlaps work and hides latency.

# How the Paper Counts FLOPs

To interpret throughput numbers such as TFLOPs/s, one first needs the FLOP formulas used by the paper. For the forward pass, the paper uses
\[
4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head dimension} \cdot \text{number of heads}.
\]
This formula captures the computational cost of the two main matrix multiplications in attention, together with the factor used by the paper for its FLOP accounting. When causal masking is enabled, the paper divides this quantity by $2$ because approximately only half of the score matrix entries are computed.

For the backward pass, the paper multiplies the forward-pass FLOPs by $2.5$. The reason given is that the backward computation contains $5$ matmuls in total, compared with $2$ matmuls in the forward pass, due to recomputation. This accounting is useful because it lets the paper report backward throughput in a way that is comparable to the forward measurement, even though the underlying operations differ.

For a student learning to read these results, the key point is that TFLOPs/s is not just a raw runtime metric. It is runtime translated into a normalized measure of work per second. That lets the paper compare methods across different sequence lengths and masking settings on a common basis.

# FP16 Forward-Pass Results

The paper reports that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} achieves a $1.5$ to $2.0\times$ speedup over \textsc{FlashAttention-2} in the FP16 forward pass. In the strongest cases, it reaches up to $740$ TFLOPs/s. This is a substantial result because the forward pass is one of the central workloads in Transformer inference and training, and the paper’s improvements directly target the bottlenecks that limit it.

What makes this number meaningful is not just its absolute size, but its relation to prior work. \textsc{FlashAttention-2} already reduced memory traffic by fusing attention operations, so another factor-of-two-style improvement indicates that the new algorithm is making better use of Hopper’s asynchronous execution. In the paper’s framing, the improvement comes from producer-consumer asynchrony, the overlap of GEMM and softmax, and Hopper-specific low-level execution features. The speedup therefore suggests that the previous generation of FlashAttention had left a significant amount of Hopper’s capability unused.

The paper also notes that for a representative FP16 setting, the throughput rises from about $570$ TFLOPs/s to the low $600$ TFLOPs/s range when the new algorithmic ideas are enabled, and ultimately peaks near $740$ TFLOPs/s across the benchmark suite. That progression helps readers see that the final result is not a single lucky datapoint but part of a consistent performance trend.

# FP16 Backward-Pass Results

The backward pass shows similar, though slightly smaller, gains. The paper reports a $1.5$ to $1.75\times$ speedup over \textsc{FlashAttention-2}. This is still a strong improvement, especially because backward attention is generally more complex than the forward pass due to the need to propagate gradients through the softmax and the two matrix multiplications.

A useful way to read this result is to notice that the paper is not claiming a dramatic asymmetry between forward and backward. Instead, it shows that the same hardware-aware design principles carry over to both phases of training. That matters because training workloads depend on the backward pass just as much as the forward pass. A faster forward-only kernel would be incomplete if the backward path remained a bottleneck.

The paper’s comparison also reinforces an important lesson about performance evaluation: a method can be optimized in one direction without automatically solving the whole problem. Here, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} improves both phases, which suggests that its design changes are broadly beneficial rather than narrowly tailored to one pass.

# Peak Measured Throughput and What $75\%$ Utilization Means

At its peak, the paper reports that FP16 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} reaches up to $740$ TFLOPs/s, which the paper describes as about $75\%$ utilization of the H100’s theoretical maximum. This is a very high level of utilization for a real kernel, and it gives a sense of how close the implementation gets to the machine’s peak compute capability.

The significance of this number is easiest to understand by comparing it with the paper’s observation that \textsc{FlashAttention-2} on H100 reaches only about $35\%$ utilization. The point is not simply that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is faster; it is that it is much better at filling the GPU’s Tensor Core machinery with useful work. Since attention is often limited by a mix of memory movement, softmax overhead, and scheduling inefficiencies, reaching $75\%$ utilization suggests that the paper’s asynchrony and pipelining ideas are doing exactly what they are meant to do.

This is why the paper repeatedly emphasizes Hopper’s asynchronous Tensor Cores and TMA. The throughput figure is evidence that those hardware features are being turned into practical speed, not just mentioned in theory.

# FP8 Forward Performance

The FP8 results are even more striking in raw throughput. The paper reports that FP8 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} reaches close to $1.2$ PFLOPs/s. Since FP8 doubles the throughput of the relevant Tensor Core path relative to FP16 or BF16 on Hopper, the paper’s result reflects both the hardware advantage of FP8 and the algorithm’s ability to feed that hardware efficiently.

At the same time, the paper is careful to present FP8 not just as a performance story but as a trade-off between speed and precision. The FP8 kernel requires layout transformations, in-kernel transposition, and special handling of accumulator layouts so that the dependent WGMMAs can be issued correctly. The performance result therefore shows more than just a fast low-precision path; it shows that the implementation successfully navigates the hardware constraints associated with FP8 on Hopper.

For readers, the main lesson is that FP8 does not merely mean “smaller numbers and faster execution.” It means a carefully engineered pipeline that can exploit the hardware’s wider throughput while preserving enough numerical stability to remain useful.

# When FlashAttention-3 Surpasses cuDNN, and When It Is Only Competitive

The paper compares \textsc{FlashAttention-3} with a state-of-the-art attention implementation from cuDNN. For large sequence lengths, FP16 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} outperforms the cuDNN implementation, and FP8 is competitive with it. The paper also gives a more detailed breakdown for FP8.

More precisely, for head dimension $64$, FP8 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is ahead of cuDNN. For head dimensions $128$ and $256$, it is at par with cuDNN for the cases without causal masking, but behind with causal masking. This is a nuanced result, and it is important not to oversimplify it into “FlashAttention-3 always wins.”

That nuance teaches an important lesson about reading systems papers. The question is not whether one method is universally best, but under what configurations it wins. Here, the paper’s results show that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is strong across the board, but the relative balance between kernels depends on sequence length, head dimension, and whether masking is causal.

# Ablation Study: What Warp Specialization and GEMM-Softmax Pipelining Contribute

The ablation study isolates the effect of the paper’s algorithmic improvements by testing non-causal FP16 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} at fixed settings $\{\text{batch}, \text{seqlen}, \text{nheads}, \text{hdim}\} = \{4, 8448, 16, 128\}$. In this setting, the paper reports a throughput increase from about $570$ TFLOPs/s to $661$ TFLOPs/s when the new ideas are enabled.

This result matters because it shows that the gains are not merely the consequence of a more polished implementation. The paper specifically attributes the improvement to warp specialization and to overlapping between GEMM and softmax. In other words, the performance increase comes from better use of the GPU’s asynchronous execution model. The ablation is therefore a direct test of the paper’s central claim that algorithmic structure should be designed around hardware concurrency.

The change from $570$ to $661$ TFLOPs/s is also useful pedagogically because it is large enough to matter, but not so large that it looks implausible. It gives a concrete sense of how much a well-chosen scheduling strategy can improve a high-performance kernel once the major memory traffic optimizations are already in place.

# Numerical Error Methodology: FP64 Reference and Synthetic Outliers

To evaluate numerical accuracy, the paper compares \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, \textsc{FlashAttention-3}, and standard attention against an FP64 reference implementation. This is a standard and sensible way to measure numerical error: FP64 serves as the closest thing to a high-precision ground truth within the paper’s experimental setup.

The paper does not use arbitrary random inputs. Instead, it constructs synthetic data designed to mimic the outlier behavior seen in large language models. The entries of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ are sampled from
\[
\mathcal{N}(0, 1) + \mathcal{N}(0, 100)\cdot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001).
\]
This means most entries are drawn from a standard normal distribution, but a small fraction, $0.1\%$, receive an additional large-magnitude term with standard deviation $10$. The purpose is to stress the quantization and softmax computations in a way that resembles difficult real-model behavior.

The paper measures root mean squared error, or RMSE, relative to the FP64 output. RMSE is a natural choice here because it summarizes average deviation across all output entries in a single quantity, making it easy to compare methods under the same test distribution.

# Why the FP16 FlashAttention Variants Are More Accurate Than Standard Attention

The paper finds that both \textsc{FlashAttention-2} and \textsc{FlashAttention-3} in FP16 achieve $1.7\times$ lower RMSE than the standard attention implementation. The key reason given is that the FlashAttention variants keep intermediate results, especially the softmax rescaling, in FP32.

This result is important because it shows that a fused kernel can improve accuracy as well as speed. A common intuition is that faster GPU kernels must be making more aggressive approximations or losing precision. The paper’s result argues against that simplistic view. Here, the FlashAttention design reduces memory traffic while also preserving higher-precision intermediate computation where it matters.

For a student, the lesson is that numerical accuracy depends not only on the final datatype, but on where rounding happens throughout the computation. Keeping the intermediate softmax-related quantities in FP32 helps reduce accumulated error, which is exactly what the paper observes.

# Why FP8 FlashAttention-3 Is More Accurate Than Baseline FP8 Attention

The FP8 comparison is more delicate. The paper compares \textsc{FlashAttention-3} against a baseline FP8 attention implementation that uses per-tensor scaling, an FP32 matmul accumulator, and FP16 intermediate softmax results. Against that baseline, FP8 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is $2.6\times$ more accurate.

The paper attributes this improvement to two techniques: block quantization and incoherent processing. Block quantization uses one scale per block rather than one scale for the entire tensor, so the quantizer can adapt more locally to the distribution of values. This is especially relevant when different blocks of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ have different magnitude patterns. Incoherent processing, meanwhile, multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix before quantization, which spreads out outliers and makes the values easier to represent in FP8.

The key intuition is that the paper is not merely “using FP8 carefully.” It is redesigning the quantization strategy to match the structure of attention and to reduce the damage caused by outlier values. The $2.6\times$ accuracy gain is therefore a direct indication that the low-precision path is not only fast but also substantially better behaved than a straightforward FP8 baseline.

# What the Numbers Mean Overall

Taken together, the evaluation shows that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is doing two things at once. First, it pushes attention much closer to the H100’s compute limits by exploiting asynchrony, warp specialization, and overlapping of GEMM with softmax. Second, it makes FP8 practical for attention by addressing both hardware layout constraints and numerical sensitivity.

The most important lesson for reading the paper is that the throughput and accuracy numbers are not isolated facts. They are evidence that each design choice solves a specific problem. The speedups show that the algorithm better matches Hopper’s execution model. The ablation study shows that the new scheduling ideas matter. The numerical error experiments show that low precision need not come at the cost of unacceptable accuracy when the quantization strategy is designed carefully.

So the empirical story is not simply that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is “faster.” It is that the paper’s hardware-aware redesign changes what is possible for exact attention on modern GPUs, both in speed and in numerical reliability.
