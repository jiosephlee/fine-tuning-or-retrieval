\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

Chapter 1: Attention as the Bottleneck: Motivation for FlashAttention-3

# Why self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length

The core reason attention becomes expensive is simple: every query token compares itself against every key token. In the paper’s notation, given $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, the attention score matrix is $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$, followed by $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$ and then $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. Because $\mathbf{S}$ has $N^2$ entries, the cost of forming and processing these interactions grows quadratically with sequence length $N$. This is the central scaling issue: as the context gets longer, the pairwise interaction table becomes dramatically larger, and attention increasingly dominates runtime.

The paper emphasizes that this scaling matters because modern Transformers are often pushed into long-context settings. In that regime, the cost of attention is no longer a secondary concern hidden behind other layers. Instead, it becomes the part of the model that determines whether the system can practically handle long inputs at all. That is why the paper frames attention as the bottleneck for large language models and long-context applications.

# Long-context use cases that make this bottleneck important

The motivation for faster attention is not abstract. The paper gives several examples of what longer context can unlock. In language, it can support modeling and reasoning over multiple long documents and over files in large codebases. In other modalities, it can help with high-resolution images, audio, and video. The paper also points to applications such as user interaction with long histories and agent workflows that must operate over a long horizon.

These examples matter because they show that the sequence length $N$ is not just a theoretical variable. When the model is asked to process more text, more frames, or more structured interactions, the quadratic cost of attention grows quickly. So faster attention is presented as an enabling primitive: improving it can broaden what kinds of inputs and tasks a Transformer can realistically handle.

# Exact attention versus approximate attention methods

The paper places FlashAttention-3 in a broader landscape of attempts to make attention faster. That landscape includes approximation methods, software optimization, and even alternative architectures. The important distinction is that FlashAttention-3 belongs to the exact-attention line of work. It does not replace the attention computation with an approximation; instead, it aims to compute the same result more efficiently on the GPU.

This distinction is important for understanding the paper’s goals. Approximation methods may reduce cost by changing the operation, but the FlashAttention line tries to preserve the standard attention calculation while improving how it is executed. The paper builds directly on this exact-attention tradition, and the motivation for FlashAttention-3 is to keep the model’s mathematical behavior while making the implementation much better matched to modern hardware.

# Why software optimization remains important even with new architectures

A key message of the paper is that newer GPUs do not automatically solve the attention problem. Even on the Hopper H100 GPU, FlashAttention-2 reaches only about 35\% utilization, while optimized GEMM kernels achieve around 80\% to 90\%. That gap shows that raw hardware capability is not enough; the software has to use the hardware effectively.

The paper argues that part of the issue is implementation-level. For example, not using Hopper-specific instructions when targeting Tensor Cores can leave performance on the table. But the deeper issue is algorithmic: FlashAttention-2 still follows a largely synchronous execution model and does not explicitly exploit the hardware’s asynchronous execution and low-precision capabilities. The paper’s central point is that attention needs to be redesigned around the GPU’s actual execution structure, not just compiled onto it.

# From standard attention to FlashAttention and FlashAttention-2

The paper contrasts three stages of implementation. In standard attention, the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ are materialized to HBM, which means they are written out to and read back from slow global memory. This is expensive because those intermediates are large, and the extra memory traffic can dominate runtime.

FlashAttention changed this picture by fusing attention into a single GPU kernel and using a local softmax reduction so that the algorithm does not need to materialize those intermediates in HBM. The paper explains this as a tiling strategy that minimizes reads and writes to slow global memory. In other words, the computation is reorganized so that the GPU can keep the important intermediate information close to the compute units that need it.

FlashAttention-2 then restructured the algorithm further by parallelizing over the sequence length dimension and by making the inner loop operate over blocks of the key and value matrices. That improved occupancy and the distribution of work on the GPU. The paper views FlashAttention-2 as an important step forward, but also as incomplete on Hopper: it improved the algorithm, yet still did not fully exploit the asynchronous features and low-precision support of the newer architecture.

# The Hopper utilization gap: attention kernels versus GEMM kernels

The paper repeatedly uses Hopper as the concrete example for understanding the performance gap. Hopper has hardware features that matter here: Tensor Cores for matrix multiplication and the Tensor Memory Accelerator, or TMA, for asynchronous memory movement. These are separate from the CUDA cores that handle logic and floating-point operations. The hardware is therefore built for overlapping different kinds of work, but the software must be designed to take advantage of that overlap.

This is why the paper compares attention kernels with GEMM kernels. GEMM kernels are already very good at saturating the hardware, while FlashAttention-2 still trails far behind. The paper suggests that some of this gap comes from not using Hopper-specific instructions, but also from the fact that the algorithm does not yet exploit asynchrony in the right way. So the utilization gap is not just a benchmark number; it is the evidence that the algorithm and the hardware are not yet fully aligned.

# The three headline contributions of FlashAttention-3

FlashAttention-3 introduces three main ideas. The first is producer-consumer asynchrony. The paper uses warp-specialization, which splits the warps in a threadblock into producer and consumer roles. Producers handle data movement, while consumers handle computation. This separation helps hide latency because memory movement and computation can proceed in parallel, and Hopper’s support for register reallocation makes this division more practical.

The second idea is overlapping softmax with asynchronous block-wise GEMMs. The paper observes that softmax is much lower throughput than matrix multiplication, especially because exponential operations are far slower than Tensor Core matmuls. FlashAttention-3 therefore tries to execute the softmax work while the Tensor Cores are busy on GEMMs. The paper’s 2-stage pipeline makes this concrete: while one block’s softmax is being computed, the next block’s GEMM can already be underway in the asynchronous proxy.

The third idea is hardware-accelerated low precision, specifically FP8. The paper adapts the forward pass so that it can use Hopper’s FP8 Tensor Cores, which nearly doubles measured throughput. But this is not just a matter of changing the datatype. The algorithm has to respect layout constraints and control numerical error. That is why the paper introduces block quantization and incoherent processing as accuracy-preserving techniques.

# Main empirical claims

The paper’s main performance claim is that FlashAttention-3 gives a substantial speedup over FlashAttention-2 on the H100 SXM5 GPU. In FP16, it reaches about 1.5-2.0$\times$ speedup in the forward pass and about 1.5-1.75$\times$ in the backward pass. It also reaches up to 740 TFLOPs/s, which the paper describes as 75\% utilization of the H100’s theoretical maximum. In other words, the method does not just improve latency; it also drives the hardware much closer to its capacity.

The FP8 results are even more striking in raw throughput. The paper reports nearly 1.2 PFLOPs/s in FP8. At the same time, the paper finds that for large sequence lengths, FP16 FlashAttention-3 outperforms and FP8 is competitive with a state-of-the-art cuDNN implementation of attention, though the exact comparison depends on head dimension and causal masking. So the empirical story is not simply “FP8 is faster”; it is that the new design makes FP8 a serious performance option on modern hardware.

The accuracy story is equally important. For FP16, FlashAttention-3 matches the numerical error of FlashAttention-2 and is better than the standard attention implementation because intermediate results such as softmax rescaling are kept in FP32. For FP8, the paper reports that block quantization and incoherent processing reduce numerical error by 2.6$\times$ relative to a baseline FP8 attention method that uses per-tensor quantization. That result matters because it shows the low-precision speedup is not bought at the expense of uncontrolled error.

# How to read the paper as an algorithm and hardware-software co-design study

The best way to read the paper is to treat it as both an algorithm paper and a hardware-software co-design paper. On the algorithm side, it rewrites attention around tiling, pipelining, and local state such as blockwise softmax statistics. On the hardware side, it carefully maps those ideas onto Hopper’s actual execution model: TMA for data movement, WGMMA for asynchronous matrix multiplication, register reallocation with $\verb|setmaxnreg|$, and low-precision Tensor Core constraints.

This matters because many of the paper’s key ideas only make sense when both sides are understood together. For example, the overlap between softmax and GEMM is not a generic software trick; it is designed around the fact that different hardware units have very different throughput and can be scheduled concurrently. Likewise, the FP8 section is not just about reducing bit width; it is about making the data layout and accumulator layout compatible with Hopper’s instruction requirements. The paper’s contribution is therefore not only a faster attention kernel, but a demonstration that careful coordination between algorithm design and GPU architecture can produce major gains in both speed and accuracy.
