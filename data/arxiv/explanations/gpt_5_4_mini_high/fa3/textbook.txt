\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

Attention as the Bottleneck: Motivation for FlashAttention-3

# Why self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length

The core reason attention becomes expensive is simple: every query token compares itself against every key token. In the paper’s notation, given $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, the attention score matrix is $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$, followed by $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$ and then $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. Because $\mathbf{S}$ has $N^2$ entries, the cost of forming and processing these interactions grows quadratically with sequence length $N$. This is the central scaling issue: as the context gets longer, the pairwise interaction table becomes dramatically larger, and attention increasingly dominates runtime.

The paper emphasizes that this scaling matters because modern Transformers are often pushed into long-context settings. In that regime, the cost of attention is no longer a secondary concern hidden behind other layers. Instead, it becomes the part of the model that determines whether the system can practically handle long inputs at all. That is why the paper frames attention as the bottleneck for large language models and long-context applications.

# Long-context use cases that make this bottleneck important

The motivation for faster attention is not abstract. The paper gives several examples of what longer context can unlock. In language, it can support modeling and reasoning over multiple long documents and over files in large codebases. In other modalities, it can help with high-resolution images, audio, and video. The paper also points to applications such as user interaction with long histories and agent workflows that must operate over a long horizon.

These examples matter because they show that the sequence length $N$ is not just a theoretical variable. When the model is asked to process more text, more frames, or more structured interactions, the quadratic cost of attention grows quickly. So faster attention is presented as an enabling primitive: improving it can broaden what kinds of inputs and tasks a Transformer can realistically handle.

# Exact attention versus approximate attention methods

The paper places FlashAttention-3 in a broader landscape of attempts to make attention faster. That landscape includes approximation methods, software optimization, and even alternative architectures. The important distinction is that FlashAttention-3 belongs to the exact-attention line of work. It does not replace the attention computation with an approximation; instead, it aims to compute the same result more efficiently on the GPU.

This distinction is important for understanding the paper’s goals. Approximation methods may reduce cost by changing the operation, but the FlashAttention line tries to preserve the standard attention calculation while improving how it is executed. The paper builds directly on this exact-attention tradition, and the motivation for FlashAttention-3 is to keep the model’s mathematical behavior while making the implementation much better matched to modern hardware.

# Why software optimization remains important even with new architectures

A key message of the paper is that newer GPUs do not automatically solve the attention problem. Even on the Hopper H100 GPU, FlashAttention-2 reaches only about 35\% utilization, while optimized GEMM kernels achieve around 80\% to 90\%. That gap shows that raw hardware capability is not enough; the software has to use the hardware effectively.

The paper argues that part of the issue is implementation-level. For example, not using Hopper-specific instructions when targeting Tensor Cores can leave performance on the table. But the deeper issue is algorithmic: FlashAttention-2 still follows a largely synchronous execution model and does not explicitly exploit the hardware’s asynchronous execution and low-precision capabilities. The paper’s central point is that attention needs to be redesigned around the GPU’s actual execution structure, not just compiled onto it.

# From standard attention to FlashAttention and FlashAttention-2

The paper contrasts three stages of implementation. In standard attention, the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ are materialized to HBM, which means they are written out to and read back from slow global memory. This is expensive because those intermediates are large, and the extra memory traffic can dominate runtime.

FlashAttention changed this picture by fusing attention into a single GPU kernel and using a local softmax reduction so that the algorithm does not need to materialize those intermediates in HBM. The paper explains this as a tiling strategy that minimizes reads and writes to slow global memory. In other words, the computation is reorganized so that the GPU can keep the important intermediate information close to the compute units that need it.

FlashAttention-2 then restructured the algorithm further by parallelizing over the sequence length dimension and by making the inner loop operate over blocks of the key and value matrices. That improved occupancy and the distribution of work on the GPU. The paper views FlashAttention-2 as an important step forward, but also as incomplete on Hopper: it improved the algorithm, yet still did not fully exploit the asynchronous features and low-precision support of the newer architecture.

# The Hopper utilization gap: attention kernels versus GEMM kernels

The paper repeatedly uses Hopper as the concrete example for understanding the performance gap. Hopper has hardware features that matter here: Tensor Cores for matrix multiplication and the Tensor Memory Accelerator, or TMA, for asynchronous memory movement. These are separate from the CUDA cores that handle logic and floating-point operations. The hardware is therefore built for overlapping different kinds of work, but the software must be designed to take advantage of that overlap.

This is why the paper compares attention kernels with GEMM kernels. GEMM kernels are already very good at saturating the hardware, while FlashAttention-2 still trails far behind. The paper suggests that some of this gap comes from not using Hopper-specific instructions, but also from the fact that the algorithm does not yet exploit asynchrony in the right way. So the utilization gap is not just a benchmark number; it is the evidence that the algorithm and the hardware are not yet fully aligned.

# The three headline contributions of FlashAttention-3

FlashAttention-3 introduces three main ideas. The first is producer-consumer asynchrony. The paper uses warp-specialization, which splits the warps in a threadblock into producer and consumer roles. Producers handle data movement, while consumers handle computation. This separation helps hide latency because memory movement and computation can proceed in parallel, and Hopper’s support for register reallocation makes this division more practical.

The second idea is overlapping softmax with asynchronous block-wise GEMMs. The paper observes that softmax is much lower throughput than matrix multiplication, especially because exponential operations are far slower than Tensor Core matmuls. FlashAttention-3 therefore tries to execute the softmax work while the Tensor Cores are busy on GEMMs. The paper’s 2-stage pipeline makes this concrete: while one block’s softmax is being computed, the next block’s GEMM can already be underway in the asynchronous proxy.

The third idea is hardware-accelerated low precision, specifically FP8. The paper adapts the forward pass so that it can use Hopper’s FP8 Tensor Cores, which nearly doubles measured throughput. But this is not just a matter of changing the datatype. The algorithm has to respect layout constraints and control numerical error. That is why the paper introduces block quantization and incoherent processing as accuracy-preserving techniques.

# Main empirical claims

The paper’s main performance claim is that FlashAttention-3 gives a substantial speedup over FlashAttention-2 on the H100 SXM5 GPU. In FP16, it reaches about 1.5-2.0$\times$ speedup in the forward pass and about 1.5-1.75$\times$ in the backward pass. It also reaches up to 740 TFLOPs/s, which the paper describes as 75\% utilization of the H100’s theoretical maximum. In other words, the method does not just improve latency; it also drives the hardware much closer to its capacity.

The FP8 results are even more striking in raw throughput. The paper reports nearly 1.2 PFLOPs/s in FP8. At the same time, the paper finds that for large sequence lengths, FP16 FlashAttention-3 outperforms and FP8 is competitive with a state-of-the-art cuDNN implementation of attention, though the exact comparison depends on head dimension and causal masking. So the empirical story is not simply “FP8 is faster”; it is that the new design makes FP8 a serious performance option on modern hardware.

The accuracy story is equally important. For FP16, FlashAttention-3 matches the numerical error of FlashAttention-2 and is better than the standard attention implementation because intermediate results such as softmax rescaling are kept in FP32. For FP8, the paper reports that block quantization and incoherent processing reduce numerical error by 2.6$\times$ relative to a baseline FP8 attention method that uses per-tensor quantization. That result matters because it shows the low-precision speedup is not bought at the expense of uncontrolled error.

# How to read the paper as an algorithm and hardware-software co-design study

The best way to read the paper is to treat it as both an algorithm paper and a hardware-software co-design paper. On the algorithm side, it rewrites attention around tiling, pipelining, and local state such as blockwise softmax statistics. On the hardware side, it carefully maps those ideas onto Hopper’s actual execution model: TMA for data movement, WGMMA for asynchronous matrix multiplication, register reallocation with $\verb|setmaxnreg|$, and low-precision Tensor Core constraints.

This matters because many of the paper’s key ideas only make sense when both sides are understood together. For example, the overlap between softmax and GEMM is not a generic software trick; it is designed around the fact that different hardware units have very different throughput and can be scheduled concurrently. Likewise, the FP8 section is not just about reducing bit width; it is about making the data layout and accumulator layout compatible with Hopper’s instruction requirements. The paper’s contribution is therefore not only a faster attention kernel, but a demonstration that careful coordination between algorithm design and GPU architecture can produce major gains in both speed and accuracy.

Mathematical Foundations of Multi-Head Attention

# Notation for Queries, Keys, Values, Sequence Length, and Head Dimension

The paper starts from the single-head setting, which is the cleanest way to write attention mathematically. Let $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ denote the query, key, and value matrices for one head. Here $N$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the head dimension. Each row corresponds to one position in the sequence, so the $i$th row of $\mathbf{Q}$ is the query vector for token $i$, the $j$th row of $\mathbf{K}$ is the key vector for token $j$, and the $j$th row of $\mathbf{V}$ is the corresponding value vector.

This notation is important because it already reveals the central shape mismatch in attention. The inputs $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ all have size $N \times d$, but the score matrix that compares every query to every key has size $N \times N$. That is the key mathematical reason attention becomes expensive when $N$ grows. The paper uses this same notation throughout, so understanding it well is the first step to reading the later algorithmic sections.

# Forward-Pass Equations: Scores, Probabilities, and Output

For a single head, the paper defines attention through three equations:
\[
\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}, \qquad
\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}, \qquad
\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}.
\]
The matrix $\mathbf{S}$ stores the raw compatibility scores between every query and every key. The matrix $\mathbf{P}$ converts those scores into row-wise normalized weights, and the output $\mathbf{O}$ uses those weights to form a weighted combination of the value vectors.

The intuition is simple but powerful. Each row of $\mathbf{S}$ corresponds to one query position and contains its scores against all keys. Applying softmax row-wise means that each query produces its own distribution over sequence positions. Then $\mathbf{O}$ takes those weights and mixes the rows of $\mathbf{V}$ accordingly. In other words, attention is a data-dependent averaging operation: the query chooses how much to attend to each value through its similarity to the keys.

# Scaling and Numerical Stabilization

The paper writes the score matrix with a scalar factor $\alpha$, and notes that one typically uses $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$. This is part of the standard attention definition used in the paper. In practice, the dot product $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ aggregates $d$ products for each score, so its magnitude depends on the head dimension. Scaling by $1/\sqrt{d}$ keeps the scores in a numerically reasonable range before softmax is applied.

The paper also emphasizes a second stabilization step: subtracting the row-wise maximum from $\mathbf{S}$ before exponentiation. This is done to prevent numerical instability with the exponential function. Since softmax is applied row-wise, subtracting the same constant from every entry in a row does not change the normalized result, but it does reduce the chance that the exponentials become too large. A row can therefore be written conceptually as
\[
\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{s}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{s} - m)}{\sum_k \exp(s_k - m)},
\qquad m = \max_k s_k,
\]
which is exactly the kind of row-local normalization the paper builds on later.

# Extending the Same Math to Multi-Head Attention

The single-head equations are the mathematical core of multi-head attention. In the paper’s description, each head has its own set of query, key, and value projections, but the computation within each head is the same as the single-head case above. The full multi-head layer is therefore just many copies of the same attention operation, each running on its own projected inputs.

This matters because it tells us what changes and what does not change when moving from one head to many. The algebra inside a head does not become more complicated; instead, the computation is replicated across heads and batches. The paper explicitly notes that both the forward and backward passes parallelize across the number of heads and batches. So the single-head formulation is not a simplification in the sense of changing the math; it is the basic unit from which the full multi-head layer is built.

# Backward Pass Through Attention

To differentiate attention, the paper introduces a scalar loss $\phi$ and the notation $\mathbf{d}(-) = \partial \phi / \partial (-)$. Given the output gradient $\mathbf{dO} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, the backward pass is obtained by the chain rule:
\[
\mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d},
\]
\[
\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO}\mathbf{V}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N},
\]
\[
\mathbf{dS} = \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N},
\]
\[
\mathbf{dQ} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d},
\qquad
\mathbf{dK} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}^\top \mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}.
\]

These formulas are just the reverse of the forward computation. The gradient $\mathbf{dV}$ comes from the fact that $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, so gradients flow back through the transpose of $\mathbf{P}$. The gradient $\mathbf{dP}$ comes from differentiating the same matrix product with respect to the weights. Then $\mathbf{dS}$ is obtained by applying the derivative of softmax row by row, and finally the gradients with respect to $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ come from differentiating the score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$. As with the forward pass, the backward pass parallelizes across heads and batches.

# The Row-Wise Structure of Softmax Derivatives

The paper writes the derivative of softmax for a vector $s$ as
\[
\mathbf{d}s = (\mathrm{diag}(p) - p p^\top)\mathbf{d}p,
\qquad
p = \mathrm{softmax}(s),
\]
and states that this is applied row-wise when $\mathbf{S}$ is a matrix. This row-wise structure is essential. It means that each row of $\mathbf{S}$ is differentiated independently of the others, because softmax is itself defined independently on each row.

This is the key reason the derivative looks like a matrix built from a single row vector. The term $\mathrm{diag}(p)$ captures how each score affects its own softmax output, while the term $p p^\top$ captures the fact that the entries within a row compete with one another for probability mass. Raising one score in a row affects not only its own output but also the normalized weights assigned to the other entries in that same row. The paper uses this structure directly in its local and block-wise attention algorithms.

# Why Attention Creates Large Intermediate Matrices

The equations themselves show why attention is costly. The inputs and outputs have shape $N \times d$, but the score matrix $\mathbf{S}$ and the normalized weight matrix $\mathbf{P}$ both have shape $N \times N$. When the sequence length grows, these intermediate matrices become much larger than the data vectors they connect. That quadratic scaling in $N$ is the mathematical reason attention becomes a bottleneck in long-context settings.

The paper defines standard attention as an implementation that materializes $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM. This means the large intermediate matrices are not just conceptual objects in the math; they are actual data that can have to be written and read back from slow global memory. The paper’s motivation for FlashAttention is to avoid exactly this pattern. Once you see that $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ are large, row-wise intermediates, it becomes clear why memory traffic, not just arithmetic, matters so much.

# How These Equations Expose Opportunities for Fusion and Recomputation

The forward equations have a very regular structure: matrix multiplication, then softmax, then matrix multiplication again. That structure makes it possible to fuse operations instead of treating them as separate steps with large intermediates written out in between. The paper’s description of FlashAttention builds directly on this observation by using a local version of the softmax reduction and rescalings of blocks of $\mathbf{O}$ so that the final output can be produced without materializing all of $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$.

The same idea also explains why recomputation is useful in the backward pass. If storing intermediate results is expensive, it can be better to rebuild certain quantities when needed rather than save everything. The paper’s benchmarking even reflects this trade-off: backward FLOPs are counted as $2.5$ times forward FLOPs, because the backward pass uses recomputation. So the mathematical form of attention does more than define the layer; it also reveals where an implementation can save memory, where it can fuse work, and where it may need to trade extra computation for less data movement.

GPU Systems Primer for Attention Kernels

# The GPU Memory Hierarchy

The paper presents Hopper as a machine with several levels of memory, each serving a different role in an attention kernel. At the outermost level is global memory, also called HBM, which is off-chip DRAM accessible to all streaming multiprocessors. Above that sits the L2 cache, which transparently caches data coming from global memory. Inside each SM is shared memory, a small on-chip, programmer-managed, highly banked cache. At the closest level is the register file, which is private to each thread.

For attention, this hierarchy matters because the kernel repeatedly reuses tiles of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$. The key systems idea in the paper is that the more often a kernel can keep intermediate values in shared memory or registers instead of writing them back to HBM, the better it can exploit the GPU. FlashAttention-3 is built around exactly that principle.

# Bandwidth Versus Capacity Across Memory Levels

The paper emphasizes a familiar but crucial tradeoff: memory levels with larger capacity tend to offer lower bandwidth, while the smallest on-chip levels are the most bandwidth-rich. Global memory can hold a great deal of data, but it is the least attractive place to repeatedly store intermediate attention results. Shared memory and registers are much smaller, but they are the places where the kernel can move data quickly and repeatedly without paying the cost of HBM traffic.

This is why the paper treats memory locality as a first-class algorithmic concern. Standard attention materializes the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM, which is expensive. FlashAttention instead uses a local softmax reduction so that the attention computation can be fused into a single kernel and intermediate values can remain on-chip. The hardware hierarchy is not just a background detail; it is the reason the algorithm is structured around tiles and staged reuse.

# The Thread Hierarchy on Hopper

Hopper organizes execution units through a hierarchy of threads, warps, warpgroups, threadblocks, threadblock clusters, and grids. A warp contains 32 threads, and a warpgroup contains 4 contiguous warps. At larger scales, a threadblock is also called a CTA, and Hopper adds threadblock clusters above CTAs. These groupings determine how work is distributed and how closely different pieces of work can cooperate.

The paper also points out that this hierarchy is tied to placement on hardware. Threads in the same CTA are co-scheduled on the same SM, and CTAs in the same cluster are co-scheduled on the same GPC. Shared memory is directly addressable by all threads within a CTA, while registers are private to each thread, with at most 256 registers per thread. For attention kernels, this means the CTA is the natural unit of cooperation: it is large enough to do meaningful work, but small enough that threads can share on-chip state.

# Why CTAs and Shared Memory Matter for Fused Attention

The paper’s attention kernels are organized around CTA-level tiles. In the forward pass, a CTA works on a tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and produces the corresponding output tile $\mathbf{O}_i$. This structure is important because attention is not just a single matrix multiplication. It is a sequence of operations, including score computation, softmax, and the value projection, and those operations need somewhere local to share data while they are being fused.

Shared memory is that local workspace. FlashAttention-3 uses it to stage blocks of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ so that the kernel can avoid repeatedly reading and writing intermediate matrices to HBM. The paper describes FlashAttention’s local softmax as the mechanism that makes this possible: rather than materializing the full score and probability matrices, the kernel updates running quantities on-chip and only writes the final output. This is why fused attention kernels are naturally CTA-centric rather than built around isolated threads.

# Tensor Cores, CUDA Cores, and Specialized Hardware Units

A central message of the paper is that Hopper is not a uniform compute engine. Different hardware units specialize in different kinds of work. Tensor Cores are designed for matrix multiplication. CUDA cores handle logic, integer computation, and the remaining floating-point work. In addition, Hopper provides dedicated support for memory movement through the Tensor Memory Accelerator, or TMA.

This specialization is what makes asynchrony useful. The GPU can move data and perform matrix multiplies using hardware units that are separate from the cores running the rest of the program. FlashAttention-3 takes advantage of that separation rather than treating the GPU as if all work had to proceed in one synchronous stream. The result is a kernel that can keep more of the machine busy at the same time.

# Tensor Memory Accelerator for Asynchronous Data Movement

The Tensor Memory Accelerator is Hopper’s dedicated hardware unit for asynchronous memory copy between global memory and shared memory. In the paper’s execution model, TMA is the mechanism that makes data movement a separate activity instead of something that blocks the rest of the kernel. That is especially valuable for attention, where a CTA repeatedly has to stage new tiles of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ into shared memory.

The intuition is simple: while one part of the kernel is waiting for data to arrive, another part of the kernel should be doing useful work. TMA makes that possible by letting producer warps issue loads without tying up the computation side. In FlashAttention-3, this is one of the ingredients that enables the kernel to overlap memory movement with the matrix-multiplication and softmax work performed by the consumers.

# WGMMA: Hopper’s Asynchronous Matrix-Multiply Instruction

On Hopper, the Tensor Core interface used in the paper is the warpgroup-wide WGMMA instruction. Unlike the synchronous execution model assumed by earlier algorithms, WGMMA is asynchronous, and it can source its inputs directly from shared memory. This means that a warpgroup can launch a matrix multiply and then continue organizing later work while the hardware completes the operation.

The paper uses this property to rework the attention mainloop. In the forward pass, WGMMA performs the GEMMs that compute scores and apply values, and its asynchronous behavior creates room to hide other work behind it. This is particularly valuable because attention contains a mix of very high-throughput matrix multiplication and comparatively low-throughput operations such as the softmax’s exponentials and reductions. Hopper’s asynchronous WGMMA gives the kernel a way to keep the Tensor Cores busy while other work is being prepared or finished.

# Warp Specialization and Producer-Consumer Kernels

Warp specialization is the paper’s answer to the fact that data movement and computation are different jobs. Instead of having all warps in a CTA do everything, the kernel divides them into producer and consumer roles. Producer warps handle data movement, while consumer warps handle matrix multiplication and the related on-chip computation. In the paper’s wording, the warps of a CTA are split into producers or consumers that only ever issue either data movement or computation.

This separation helps in two ways. First, it gives the compiler a cleaner scheduling problem, because each group of warps has a more focused instruction pattern. Second, it lets the GPU hide latency more effectively, because the producer side can be staging upcoming tiles while the consumer side is working on the current ones. For attention, this maps naturally onto the flow from loading $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ to computing $\mathbf{S}$, softmax, and $\mathbf{O}$.

# Dynamic Register Redistribution with \texttt{setmaxnreg}

Hopper adds another feature that makes warp specialization practical: dynamic reallocation of registers between warpgroups using \texttt{setmaxnreg}. The paper highlights that not all warps need the same register budget. The warps issuing TMA are relatively lightweight, while the warps performing matrix multiplications need more registers to hold their operands and intermediate accumulators.

This matters because attention kernels are register-sensitive. If the consumer side can obtain a larger share of the register file, it is less likely to spill intermediate values and more likely to sustain high throughput. The producer side can give up some of its register allocation because it has a simpler job. The paper treats this as one of the key Hopper-specific tools that makes producer-consumer scheduling more efficient.

# FP16, BF16, and FP8 on Modern GPUs

The paper frames low precision as another form of specialization. Modern GPUs include hardware support for FP16, BF16, and FP8, and Hopper’s FP8 WGMMA can deliver 2x the throughput per SM compared with FP16 or BF16. This is one reason low-precision attention is attractive: if the hardware can do more work per cycle at the same power and chip area, then the throughput of the whole model can improve substantially.

At the same time, the paper is careful to note that lower precision is not free. FP8 introduces more numerical error than FP16 or BF16, so the kernel design has to be adapted to preserve accuracy. That is why FlashAttention-3 does not simply switch the datatype and stop there. The hardware opportunity is real, but so is the need to respect the numerical behavior of the format.

# Operand Layout Requirements for FP16 versus FP8 WGMMA

The paper defines the layout requirement in terms of a GEMM call that multiplies $A \times B^\top$, where $A$ is an $M \times K$ matrix and $B$ is an $N \times K$ matrix. An operand is called mn-major if it is contiguous in the outer $M$ or $N$ dimension, and k-major if it is contiguous in the inner $K$ dimension. This distinction is important because WGMMA does not accept all layouts equally.

For FP16 WGMMA, both mn-major and k-major input operands are accepted when the data lives in shared memory. For FP8 WGMMA, however, only the k-major format is supported. That stricter requirement creates a practical obstacle in attention, where one often wants to run back-to-back GEMMs in a single kernel. The paper notes that the output accumulator layout of one FP8 WGMMA does not naturally match the operand layout expected by the next, so dependent FP8 WGMMAs require explicit layout handling. This is one of the main reasons FlashAttention-3 needs additional transformations when it targets FP8.

From Standard Attention to FlashAttention and FlashAttention-2

# Standard attention on GPUs and the cost of materializing $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ in HBM

For a single attention head, the paper defines the query, key, and value matrices as $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, where $N$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the head dimension. Standard attention computes the score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, applies a row-wise softmax to obtain $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, and then produces the output $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. In practice, $\alpha$ is usually set to $1/\sqrt{d}$, and the row maximum of $\mathbf{S}$ is subtracted before exponentiation to improve numerical stability. This is the familiar mathematical definition of attention, but on a GPU the way these steps are scheduled matters just as much as the arithmetic itself.

The paper emphasizes that a standard GPU implementation wastes bandwidth by materializing the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ in high-bandwidth memory, or HBM. Both of these matrices are $N \times N$, so they grow quadratically with sequence length. That means the expensive part is not only the matrix multiplication, but also the repeated writing and reading of these large intermediates through the slowest level of the GPU memory hierarchy that is shared across all streaming multiprocessors. The paper’s background section contrasts this with the much smaller and faster on-chip storage available in shared memory and registers. The core bottleneck, then, is that standard attention keeps returning to HBM between stages that conceptually belong together.

This is why standard attention becomes especially costly in long-context settings. As the sequence length grows, the score matrix and probability matrix become large enough that the memory traffic associated with them dominates the runtime. The paper presents FlashAttention as a response to this exact problem: not by changing the math of attention, but by changing where and when the intermediate results live.

# The key insight of FlashAttention: IO-aware exact attention

The central idea behind FlashAttention is that attention can be computed exactly while avoiding the expensive read-write pattern of standard attention. The paper is careful to call FlashAttention an exact-attention algorithm. It does not approximate $\mathbf{S}$ or $\mathbf{P}$, and it does not change the definition of the output. Instead, it reorganizes the computation so that the GPU spends far less time moving data between HBM and on-chip storage.

This is what the paper means by an IO-aware design. Rather than treating attention as a sequence of separate stages that each write their full outputs to global memory, FlashAttention fuses the stages into one GPU kernel and keeps intermediate quantities on chip as long as possible. The score computation, the softmax normalization, and the multiplication by $\mathbf{V}$ are all coordinated so that the algorithm can stream through blocks of the keys and values while maintaining only the state it needs for the current query block.

The intuition is simple but important. If the GPU can compute a piece of the attention scores, immediately use it to update the softmax state, and then immediately use that result to accumulate the output, there is no reason to materialize a full $N \times N$ matrix in HBM. The algorithm is still doing the same mathematical operation, but it is doing so in a way that respects the cost structure of the hardware. That is the key insight that made FlashAttention influential.

# Tiling attention into query and key-value blocks

FlashAttention works by dividing attention into tiles. In the paper’s notation, a query block $\mathbf{Q}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{B_r \times d}$ is paired with blocks of keys and values, $\mathbf{K}_j, \mathbf{V}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{B_c \times d}$. The full query sequence is split into $T_r = \lceil N/B_r \rceil$ query blocks, and the key and value sequences are split into $T_c = \lceil N/B_c \rceil$ blocks. A thread block, or CTA, can then work on one query tile at a time while streaming over the key and value tiles.

This tiling is not just a convenience for implementation. It is the mechanism that makes the algorithm memory efficient. A query tile can stay on chip while the kernel iterates over all key-value tiles. At each step, it computes the score submatrix for the current tile pair, updates the running softmax state, and folds the corresponding value contribution into the output tile. Because only blocks are held at once, the algorithm avoids ever needing to keep the entire $\mathbf{S}$ or $\mathbf{P}$ matrix in HBM.

The paper also uses this tiling structure to explain how the algorithm fits the GPU execution model. The forward pass is embarrassingly parallel across batch elements, attention heads, and query blocks. That means many CTAs can run independently, each producing a different output tile. Within each CTA, however, the work is organized as a loop over key and value blocks. That inner loop is where the core FlashAttention idea lives: stream, update, accumulate, and move on.

# Online or local softmax with running maxima and normalization factors

The most subtle part of FlashAttention is the softmax computation. If attention were computed on the full score matrix at once, a row-wise softmax would be straightforward. But when the row is split across blocks, the algorithm must preserve the exact global normalization while only seeing one block at a time. FlashAttention solves this with a local, or online, softmax that tracks a running row maximum and a running normalization factor.

The paper’s algorithm maintains two row-wise quantities for each query tile: $m_i$, the running maximum, and $\ell_i$, the running sum of exponentials. When a new score block $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ arrives, the algorithm first updates the row maximum by taking
$m_i = \max(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}, \mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}))$.
It then forms the shifted exponentials
$\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \exp(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i)$
and updates the normalization factor with
$\ell_i = \exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i)\,\ell_i + \mathrm{rowsum}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)})$.

The intuition is that the softmax denominator changes whenever a new block reveals a larger score than any block seen before. Rather than recomputing everything from scratch, the algorithm rescales the old contribution by $\exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i)$ so that it stays consistent with the new maximum. This is what allows the blockwise computation to remain exact. The output is not an approximation formed from partial softmaxes; it is the same softmax you would get from the full matrix, assembled incrementally.

This running-max formulation also explains why the algorithm is numerically stable. Subtracting the current maximum before exponentiation keeps the exponentials from blowing up, just as in the usual softmax implementation. The difference is that the maximum is now maintained across blocks, not over a fully materialized row. That is the key to making softmax compatible with tiling.

# Why keeping intermediate softmax state on-chip improves both speed and accuracy

FlashAttention’s softmax state is small compared with $\mathbf{S}$ or $\mathbf{P}$, so it is a good candidate to keep on chip. The paper’s algorithm stores the running output $\mathbf{O}_i$, the running maximum $m_i$, and the normalization factor $\ell_i$ on chip while it streams through the key and value blocks. This avoids the expensive global-memory round trips that would otherwise be needed if each intermediate step were written to HBM.

The performance benefit is direct: HBM traffic is reduced because only the final output tile and a few small auxiliary values need to be written back. The paper frames this as the central reason FlashAttention is faster than standard attention. It is not that the GPU does less arithmetic. It is that the arithmetic is organized so the GPU does not waste time moving temporary matrices through memory.

There is also an accuracy benefit. The paper reports that FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3 in FP16 have lower numerical error than the standard implementation because intermediate results, such as the softmax rescaling, are kept in FP32. In other words, the on-chip recurrence is not just a performance trick; it is also a numerically careful way to perform the same computation. By maintaining the running normalization and rescaling partial outputs in a stable form, FlashAttention avoids some of the error that can accumulate when intermediate attention matrices are materialized and processed in a less controlled way.

# How FlashAttention fuses score computation, softmax, and value aggregation

The fusion in FlashAttention is easiest to understand by following one block of the algorithm from start to finish. For a fixed query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$, the kernel loads a key tile $\mathbf{K}_j$, computes the score block $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, updates the softmax state using the running maximum and normalization factor, and then uses the resulting block of exponentials to update the output tile through multiplication with $\mathbf{V}_j$. The paper’s pseudocode expresses this as an update of the form
$\mathbf{O}_i = \mathrm{diag}(\exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i))^{-1} \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$,
followed by a final division by $\ell_i$ at the end of the loop.

What matters is that the score block never needs to become a standalone global-memory object. The softmax is computed immediately after the scores are formed, and the result is immediately consumed by the value aggregation. This is the essence of fusion. The attention kernel is no longer three separate stages connected by large temporary matrices; it is one streaming computation whose intermediate state stays local.

This is also why FlashAttention can be described as a local version of the softmax reduction. The local softmax and the rescaling of the output blocks are what allow the blockwise computation to reconstruct the exact final output. The paper points readers to the earlier FlashAttention-2 derivation for the formal justification, but the intuition is already visible in the recurrence: every new tile is folded into the same running output, with the normalization adjusted so that nothing is lost.

# What FlashAttention-2 changes: better work partitioning and sequence-length parallelism

FlashAttention-2 keeps the same basic exact-attention goal, but it improves how work is distributed on the GPU. According to the paper, the key restructuring is to parallelize over the sequence-length dimension and to organize the forward pass so that the inner loop operates over blocks of the key and value matrices. This improves occupancy and balances the workload more effectively across the GPU.

The practical motivation is that the original FlashAttention design, while already memory efficient, still left room for better use of the hardware. FlashAttention-2 addresses this by making the algorithm more parallel where possible and by ensuring that the blocks of work are arranged so the GPU can keep more execution resources busy. In the paper’s discussion, this is one of the reasons FlashAttention-2 is faster than the first FlashAttention on newer devices.

At a conceptual level, FlashAttention-2 preserves the same blockwise recurrence that makes FlashAttention exact, but it organizes the computation so that more of the sequence can be covered concurrently. The paper describes this as an improvement in occupancy and distribution of work. In other words, FlashAttention-2 does not merely reduce memory traffic; it also tries to use the GPU’s execution capacity more evenly.

# CTA-level execution in FlashAttention-2

The natural unit of work in FlashAttention-2 is a CTA operating on a query tile. Since the forward pass is parallel across batches, heads, and query blocks, one CTA can be understood as responsible for producing one output tile $\mathbf{O}_i$ from one query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$. Within that CTA, the key and value blocks are streamed one after another, and each block updates the same running softmax state.

This CTA-level view is important because it shows how the algorithm maps onto the GPU’s thread hierarchy. The CTA is the level at which the tile is owned, the on-chip state is maintained, and the final output is assembled. The paper uses exactly this sort of tile-centric perspective when it later presents its more advanced algorithms, but the same mental model applies to FlashAttention-2 as well. For a student reading attention kernels in the literature, this is the right way to think about them: a CTA owns a query tile, loops over the key-value tiles, and accumulates a tile of the output.

The appeal of this organization is that it matches the structure of attention itself. Each output row depends on all keys and values, but it does not depend on the full score matrix being present all at once. That makes the blockwise CTA loop a natural fit. FlashAttention-2 uses that fit to get better occupancy than the original FlashAttention design, while preserving exactness.

# Why FlashAttention-2 still underutilizes Hopper hardware

Even with these algorithmic improvements, the paper reports that FlashAttention-2 still falls short of what Hopper hardware can do. On the H100 GPU, FlashAttention-2 achieves only about $35\%$ utilization, while optimized GEMM kernels can reach roughly $80\%$ to $90\%$. That gap is the central motivation for the new work in the paper.

The reason is not simply that FlashAttention-2 is a bad algorithm. The paper is explicit that the limitation is partly implementation-level and partly more fundamental. On the implementation side, the paper notes that some kernels do not use Hopper-specific instructions where they could, instead carrying over choices from earlier architectures. But more fundamentally, FlashAttention-2 still follows a simplified synchronous execution model. It does not explicitly take advantage of the asynchrony available in newer Hopper hardware, where data movement and matrix multiplication can be handled by specialized units with different execution characteristics.

The paper also points out that Hopper introduces new hardware capabilities that are not fully exploited by FlashAttention-2, including asynchronous data movement and low-precision computation support. The important lesson is that an algorithm can be excellent in a platform-independent sense and still fail to saturate a specific GPU. FlashAttention-2 is a good example of this gap.

# The difference between algorithmic efficiency and hardware-specific efficiency

FlashAttention and FlashAttention-2 are highly algorithmically efficient because they reduce the amount of expensive memory traffic while preserving the exact attention computation. That is an enormous improvement over standard attention. But algorithmic efficiency is not the same thing as hardware-specific efficiency. An algorithm can do less unnecessary work and still leave performance on the table if it does not match the strengths of the target device.

The paper’s Hopper discussion makes this distinction very clear. Hopper GPUs have specialized units for matrix multiplication and memory movement, and they are designed to hide latency through concurrency and asynchrony. An algorithm that is written as if all operations happen synchronously, one after another, will not necessarily keep those specialized units busy in the best possible way. That is why the paper can simultaneously say that FlashAttention-2 is much better than standard attention and still say that it underutilizes Hopper.

This distinction is one of the most important lessons in modern systems-for-ML research. Algorithmic improvements matter, but they are only part of the story. To get the best results on a specific GPU, the algorithm must also be aligned with the hardware’s execution model. FlashAttention-2 moved attention far in that direction by making it IO-aware and by improving work partitioning. The paper argues that Hopper-specific asynchrony and low-precision support were the pieces still left on the table.

Diagnosing the Hopper Bottleneck and Setting the Design Goals

# Observed utilization on H100

The starting point of the paper is a performance gap. On the Hopper H100 GPU, \textsc{FlashAttention-2} reaches only about 35\% utilization, while optimized GEMM kernels can reach roughly 80--90\%. That gap matters because attention is one of the central kernels in Transformer models, and its cost grows quickly with sequence length. If a kernel built to reduce memory traffic and improve locality still falls far short of what Hopper can deliver for matrix multiplication, then there is clearly more structure in the hardware that the algorithm is not using.

This observation is important because it reframes the problem. The issue is not that attention is inherently a poor fit for GPUs, or that the earlier FlashAttention idea failed to eliminate the main memory bottlenecks. Rather, the issue is that Hopper offers new execution capabilities that \textsc{FlashAttention-2} does not fully exploit. In other words, the kernel is already much better than standard attention, but the hardware has moved on, and the design has not yet caught up.

# Why Hopper-specific instructions alone are not enough

The paper acknowledges one obvious source of the gap: implementation details. Some of the performance loss comes from not using Hopper-specific instructions where older Ampere-oriented ones were used instead. The authors note that Hopper-specific instruction sets and tile-based abstractions, as seen in other work and in cuDNN 9, can simplify the implementation and improve performance. So, at the most surface level, the remedy is to use the right instructions for the right architecture.

But the paper is careful to say that this is not the whole story. Even if one updates the instruction selection, the underlying algorithm of \textsc{FlashAttention-2} still follows a design that is basically synchronous. It treats the major phases of attention as if they must proceed in a strict sequence, instead of taking advantage of the fact that Hopper can overlap some kinds of work. So Hopper-specific instructions help, but they do not by themselves change the structure of the computation enough to close the utilization gap.

# The simplified synchronous model inherited from previous attention kernels

To understand the limitation, it helps to recall the attention computation itself. For a single head, attention forms the score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, applies row-wise softmax to get $\mathbf{P}$, and then multiplies by $\mathbf{V}$ to obtain $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. FlashAttention made this computation more efficient by fusing operations and avoiding unnecessary reads and writes to high-bandwidth memory, but \textsc{FlashAttention-2} still structures the work in a way that assumes a largely synchronous schedule.

The key limitation of that model is that it does not explicitly reason about asynchrony. It is natural to think of the kernel as a sequence of steps: load data, compute the first GEMM, compute softmax, compute the second GEMM, and then proceed to the next block. That model is easy to reason about, but it leaves little room to hide latency. On Hopper, that is a missed opportunity, because different parts of the chip can do useful work at the same time. The paper argues that the algorithm itself should be redesigned around that fact rather than merely implemented more efficiently in the old style.

# How asynchronous Tensor Cores and TMA change the optimization landscape

Hopper changes the optimization landscape because it separates important functions into hardware units that can operate asynchronously. The Tensor Memory Accelerator, or TMA, is a dedicated unit for moving data between global memory and shared memory. The Tensor Core path is also asynchronous through the warpgroup-wide WGMMA instruction, which can source its inputs directly from shared memory. This means that memory movement and matrix multiplication are no longer simply blocking steps that must be completed before the next instruction can begin.

This shift matters because it opens the door to pipeline-style scheduling. Instead of having all warps in a threadblock do the same thing at the same time, the kernel can split the work into producer and consumer roles. Some warps can specialize in moving data, while others specialize in computation. Hopper even supports dynamic register reallocation between warpgroups, so the warps doing matrix multiplication can receive more registers than the warps that are mostly issuing TMA operations. The paper’s point is not merely that Hopper is faster, but that its hardware organization invites a different software organization.

# The throughput mismatch between GEMM and non-GEMM operations

The next diagnosis is that not all operations in attention benefit equally from Hopper’s Tensor Cores. Matrix multiplication is extremely fast on modern GPUs, but softmax involves operations such as floating point multiply-add and exponential that do not enjoy the same throughput. The paper gives a concrete comparison on H100 SXM5: FP16 matmul reaches 989 TFLOPs/s, while special functions such as exponential reach only about 3.9 TFLOPs/s. That is an enormous asymmetry.

The authors also quantify why this matters inside attention. For forward attention with head dimension 128, the total matmul FLOP count is about 512 times larger than the FLOP count associated with exponential operations, but the exponential path has roughly 256 times lower throughput. The result is that the softmax-related work can consume a surprisingly large fraction of the cycle budget, even though it looks minor if one only counts floating-point operations. This is the central performance imbalance that the paper diagnoses.

# Why softmax can become a major hidden bottleneck even in matmul-heavy attention

This mismatch makes softmax a hidden bottleneck. Attention feels like a GEMM-heavy workload because the dominant algebraic expressions are matrix multiplications, but wall-clock time is determined by the slowest part of the pipeline, not by FLOP counts alone. If the Tensor Cores are moving much faster than the units that handle exponential and related softmax work, then softmax becomes a point where the rest of the kernel can stall.

The situation is even worse in FP8. The paper notes that FP8 increases matmul throughput again, but the throughput of exponential does not improve in the same way. So the gap between matrix multiplication and softmax widens. This is why the paper does not treat softmax as a small afterthought. Instead, it treats softmax as a major scheduling problem that must be managed explicitly if the GPU is to be kept busy.

# The need to overlap data movement, GEMMs, and softmax-related work

Once the throughput imbalance is recognized, the design direction becomes clear: the kernel must overlap as much work as possible. Data movement from global memory to shared memory should be hidden behind computation, and softmax-related work should be interleaved with matrix multiplication rather than executed as a separate blocking phase. The paper’s three major techniques all follow from this goal.

The first overlap is between producers and consumers of data. By using warp-specialization, some warps can fetch tiles while others compute on already available tiles. The second overlap is between the two GEMMs and the softmax in the inner loop. Because the second GEMM depends on the softmax output, a naive implementation would serialize them. The paper’s key idea is to restructure the computation across iterations so that one block’s softmax can run while another block’s matrix multiplication is already in flight. That is how asynchronous execution becomes a performance tool rather than just a hardware feature.

# Why low precision is attractive but numerically risky

The paper also uses Hopper’s low-precision support as part of the diagnosis. Low precision is attractive because it can roughly double or quadruple throughput for the same power and chip area. On Hopper, FP8 Tensor Cores can deliver about $2\times$ the throughput per SM compared to FP16 or BF16. So from a performance standpoint, FP8 is a natural target.

At the same time, the paper stresses that low precision is numerically risky. FP8 has fewer mantissa and exponent bits than FP16 or BF16, so it is inherently more sensitive to quantization error. The problem is made harder by the presence of outlier features in large language models, where some values are much larger in magnitude than most others. Those outliers make it difficult to choose a single scaling factor that works well for an entire tensor. The lesson is that low precision is not free performance; it has to be engineered carefully if the model is to remain accurate.

# The resulting design goals

Putting the diagnosis together, the paper arrives at a clear set of design goals. First, the kernel should hide latency wherever possible, especially the latency of memory movement and softmax-related operations. Second, it should raise Tensor Core utilization by keeping matrix multiplication units busy while other work proceeds in parallel. Third, it should preserve the exact attention computation rather than replacing it with an approximation, since the paper builds on exact-attention algorithms. Fourth, when moving to FP8, it should control numerical error rather than simply accepting the accuracy loss that comes with naive low-precision quantization.

These goals explain the three ideas that define \textsc{FlashAttention-3}: producer-consumer asynchrony through warp-specialization, overlapping softmax with asynchronous block-wise GEMMs, and FP8 support with block quantization and incoherent processing. In that sense, the paper is not just introducing a faster kernel. It is showing how to redesign attention around the realities of Hopper’s execution model so that the hardware’s asynchronous and low-precision capabilities become part of the algorithm itself.

Producer-Consumer Asynchrony with Warp Specialization

# CTA-level decomposition over query blocks

The forward pass in \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is organized around a CTA-level decomposition. At this level, one threadblock, or CTA, is assigned a single query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{B_r \times d}$ and is responsible for computing the matching output tile $\mathbf{O}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{B_r \times d}$. The rest of the attention computation is then carried out by streaming through the key and value matrices in smaller blocks. This is a natural way to parallelize attention on a GPU because the forward pass is already embarrassingly parallel across the batch size, the number of heads, and the query sequence length.

This decomposition matters because it turns attention into a repeated interaction between one fixed query block and many key/value blocks. Instead of thinking of attention as a single large matrix operation over the full sequence, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} treats it as a sequence of blockwise updates. Each CTA can therefore keep the state for its own query block on-chip while it repeatedly consumes key and value tiles. That is the basic structure that makes producer-consumer asynchrony possible.

# Choosing query and key block sizes $B_r$ and $B_c$

The algorithm parameterizes the tiling of the sequence dimension with two block sizes. The query matrix is divided into blocks of size $B_r$, giving $T_r = \lceil N / B_r \rceil$ query tiles $\mathbf{Q}_1, \dots, \mathbf{Q}_{T_r}$. The key and value matrices are divided into blocks of size $B_c$, giving $T_c = \lceil N / B_c \rceil$ key/value tiles. In the CTA-level view, one CTA works on one query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and iterates over all $T_c$ key/value tiles.

These block sizes are not just bookkeeping. They determine the shape of the work assigned to the CTA and the amount of data that must be staged on-chip at once. A larger tile can increase the amount of computation done per load, but it also increases pressure on shared memory and registers. The paper emphasizes that this trade-off is practical and should be guided by profiling, especially because the pipelined design introduces extra register demands. The important point is that $B_r$ and $B_c$ are the knobs that let the kernel fit the attention computation to the GPU’s on-chip resources.

# The circular shared-memory buffer with multiple stages

To move data efficiently between global memory and the compute units, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} uses a circular shared-memory buffer with $s$ stages. Each stage is a slot in shared memory that can hold a tile of key and value data. The producer side fills these slots, and the consumer side reads from them. Because the buffer is circular, the stage index advances modulo $s$, so later tiles can reuse earlier slots once those slots have been released.

The circular structure is what makes the pipeline practical. It allows the kernel to keep a bounded amount of data in shared memory while still streaming through the full sequence. During the first $s$ iterations, the buffer is simply being filled, so the producer does not yet need to wait for old stages to be freed. After that, the producer must wait until a stage has been consumed before reusing it. This arrangement preserves correctness while keeping the data flow moving steadily through the kernel.

# Producer warpgroups: loading $Q$, $K$, and $V$ tiles with TMA

In the warp-specialized design, some warpgroups are assigned the producer role. Their job is data movement, not arithmetic. They use Hopper’s Tensor Memory Accelerator, or TMA, to move tiles from HBM into shared memory. The producer first loads the query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and then loads each key and value tile $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ into the appropriate stage of the circular buffer.

This role separation is important because TMA is asynchronous. The producer does not need to stop and wait for each transfer to complete before issuing the next one, as long as the pipeline protocol is respected. In the algorithm, the producer issues a load, commits it to notify the consumers, and then continues to the next transfer. Since its purpose is only to keep the buffer supplied, it can be given a smaller register budget than the compute side. That makes the producer a good candidate for a lightweight warpgroup.

# Consumer warpgroups: computing score tiles, local softmax, and output updates

The consumer warpgroups perform the actual attention computation. Once the required tiles are present in shared memory, the consumer computes the score block
$\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$.
This first matrix multiplication is performed with WGMMA and is referred to in the paper as an SS-GEMM, meaning that both operands are sourced from shared memory. The consumer then uses the score block to update the local softmax statistics for the query tile.

The core of this update is the running maximum and normalization factor. The consumer stores a previous maximum $m_i^{\mathrm{old}}$, updates it to
$m_i = \max\!\bigl(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}, \mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)})\bigr)$,
and forms
$\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \exp(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i)$.
It then updates the normalization term as
$\ell_i = \exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i)\ell_i + \mathrm{rowsum}\!\bigl(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}\bigr)$.
After that, the consumer updates the output accumulator with the value tile using another WGMMA, namely
$\mathbf{O}_i = \mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(\exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i)\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$.
This is the RS-GEMM in the paper, because the first operand is taken from registers and the second from shared memory.

# Barrier synchronization between producers and consumers

The producer and consumer roles are connected through explicit synchronization barriers. The producer commits each load when it is ready, and the consumer waits for the corresponding tile before using it. For example, the consumer waits for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ before beginning the inner loop, and then waits for each $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ tile before using it in the corresponding GEMM. This ensures that computation never reads data that has not yet been loaded.

The reverse direction is just as important. After a consumer finishes with a buffer stage, it releases that stage so the producer can reuse it. In the pseudocode, the producer also waits when a stage is still occupied. These barriers are local to the pipeline rather than global to the whole GPU, so they coordinate just the data that needs to be shared between the producer and consumer warpgroups. That is what makes the design fine-grained enough to overlap work without losing correctness.

# Why asynchronous loads allow the producer to stay ahead of consumers

The reason this design helps is that memory movement and computation are carried out by different hardware units. Hopper provides TMA for memory copies and WGMMA for matrix multiplication, and both are asynchronous. This means the producer can keep issuing loads while the consumer is still computing on previously loaded tiles. The two roles do not have to march in lockstep.

The paper points out that the producer does not issue waits for the first $s$ iterations because the circular buffer is still filling. In effect, the producer gets ahead of the consumer and stays ahead as long as the pipeline has free stages. This is exactly the behavior one wants in a throughput-oriented GPU kernel: whenever one part of the machine is busy with math, another part should be busy preparing the next data. The producer-consumer split gives the kernel a way to express that overlap directly.

# SS-GEMM and RS-GEMM in the forward pass

The forward pass uses two different GEMM forms inside the consumer loop, and the distinction is tied to where each operand lives. The score computation
$\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$
is an SS-GEMM, because both operands are held in shared memory when WGMMA executes it. This fits naturally with the producer side, which has already loaded the query and key tiles into the shared-memory buffer.

The output update
$\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$
is an RS-GEMM. Here the softmaxed score block $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ is available on-chip in registers, while the value tile $\mathbf{V}_j$ is in shared memory. The paper uses the SS and RS prefixes to indicate this operand placement. This is not just a naming detail; it reflects how the kernel is structured around the data already available to the consumer at each point in the loop.

# Keeping $\mathbf{O}$, $m$, and $\ell$ on-chip during the main loop

A key feature of the algorithm is that the output accumulator and softmax state remain on-chip while the loop over key/value tiles runs. The consumer initializes $\mathbf{O}_i$ to zero and initializes $m_i$ and $\ell_i$ to $-\infty$- and zero-like starting values, then updates them block by block. Nothing needs to be written back to HBM until the end of the query tile’s computation.

This on-chip state is what makes the local softmax exact. The running maximum $m_i$ and the running normalization $\ell_i$ allow the kernel to combine contributions from many blocks without materializing the full score matrix $\mathbf{S}$ or probability matrix $\mathbf{P}$ in global memory. In other words, the kernel preserves the mathematical effect of row-wise softmax while using a streaming, blockwise implementation. At the end of the loop, the consumer performs the final rescaling
$\mathbf{O}_i = \mathrm{diag}(\ell_i)^{-1}\mathbf{O}_i$
and writes $\mathbf{O}_i$ and the corresponding $L_i = m_i + \log(\ell_i)$ back to HBM.

# Releasing shared-memory stages back to the producer

The shared-memory buffer only works because the consumer tells the producer when a stage is no longer needed. Once the consumer has used a key tile and the matching value tile, it releases the stage that held them. This frees that slot for the producer to overwrite with a future tile.

In the pseudocode, the consumer releases the key stage and the value stage separately, each according to the circular indexing scheme. That detail matches the fact that the key and value tiles are consumed at different points in the loop. This release mechanism is what makes the buffer safe to reuse. Without it, the producer could overwrite data that the consumer still needs; with it, the kernel gets reuse without corruption.

# Using \texttt{setmaxnreg} to shift registers away from producer warps and toward compute warps

Hopper supports dynamic register reallocation between warpgroups through \texttt{setmaxnreg}. \textsc{FlashAttention-3} uses this to tailor register usage to the roles of the warpgroups. The producer side does not need many registers, because its job is mostly to issue TMA loads. The consumer side, in contrast, needs more registers to hold intermediate values and to keep the computation flowing smoothly through WGMMA and the softmax updates.

The paper’s design explicitly deallocates a predetermined number of registers from the producer and reallocates them to the consumer warps as a function of the number of consumer warps. This is a practical consequence of warp specialization: once the work is split into separate producer and consumer roles, the hardware resources can also be split more intelligently. Rather than giving every warp the same register budget, the kernel gives more to the warps that actually need them.

# How this design extends FlashAttention-2 without changing the exact attention result

The main mathematical computation is still exact attention. What changes in \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is not the result, but the way the result is scheduled on the GPU. The kernel still processes each query tile against all key/value tiles, and it still uses the same local softmax logic as FlashAttention. The running maximum, normalization, and rescaling steps ensure that the blockwise computation is equivalent to the standard row-wise softmax formulation.

What \textsc{FlashAttention-3} adds is a more hardware-aware execution model. FlashAttention-2 already reduced HBM traffic by keeping intermediates on-chip, but it used a more synchronous view of the inner loop. \textsc{FlashAttention-3} keeps the same exact attention semantics while restructuring the kernel around asynchronous producers and consumers, TMA loads, WGMMA compute, and a circular shared-memory buffer. In that sense, the algorithm is an extension of FlashAttention-2 that preserves the exact output while exposing more opportunities for overlap on Hopper GPUs.

Overlapping Softmax and GEMM: Pingpong Scheduling and Intra-Warpgroup Pipelines

# Why Sequential Dependencies Normally Serialize $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, Softmax, and $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$

The second FlashAttention-3 idea starts from a simple observation: even though attention is built from matrix multiplications, not all of its work is equally fast on modern GPUs. The two GEMMs, $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, are exactly the kind of operations Tensor Cores are built to accelerate. By contrast, softmax involves reductions, subtraction of a running maximum, exponentials, and normalization, all of which run on lower-throughput hardware. On Hopper, the paper emphasizes that FP16 matmul throughput is vastly higher than the throughput of special functions such as exponential, so softmax can become a visible bottleneck even when the matmuls are highly optimized.

What makes this a scheduling problem is that attention is not just a bag of independent operations. For a block of keys and values, the kernel must first compute a score block such as $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, then apply the local softmax update, and only then can it form the contribution $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$. The output probabilities do not exist until the scores are available, and the value multiplication does not exist until the probabilities have been normalized. In a straightforward implementation, these dependencies force a strict sequence: GEMM, then softmax, then GEMM again. Even though the GPU is highly parallel, this logical dependence chain serializes the work inside each block.

FlashAttention’s key insight is that softmax can be computed block by block without materializing the full $N \times N$ matrix, but that does not remove the dependencies. It only changes them into running state. The running maximum $m_i$ and running sum $\ell_i$ must be updated as each new score block arrives, and earlier output contributions must be rescaled when the maximum changes. That is what gives the algorithm its numerical stability and memory efficiency, but it also means that the next step in the computation depends on what the previous step produced. FlashAttention-3 takes this same dependency structure and tries to hide it behind asynchronous hardware execution.

# The Intuition Behind Hiding Softmax Under Asynchronous WGMMA Execution

The paper’s intuition is that if one part of the machine is much slower than another, the slow work should happen while the fast work is already in flight. On Hopper, the Tensor Core GEMM instruction exposed as WGMMA is asynchronous, and TMA can move data from global memory to shared memory asynchronously as well. That means a kernel does not need to stop and wait after launching a matmul. It can issue the operation, move on to other useful work, and only synchronize later when the result is actually needed.

This is exactly the right setting for softmax overlap. Softmax is comparatively low-throughput, but it is not dependent on the next matmul launch in the same way that the matmul output depends on its operands. So the desired schedule is to issue a WGMMA, let it run on the Tensor Cores, and use the intervening time to perform the softmax bookkeeping on the scores from a neighboring block. In other words, the kernel tries to keep the Tensor Cores busy with GEMMs while the multi-function unit handles the exponentials and related scalar work.

The paper also notes a useful asymmetry: the matmul pipeline gets faster on Hopper, but the exponential throughput does not improve in the same way. That makes the imbalance larger, not smaller. So the more the kernel can treat softmax as background work, the better its overall utilization becomes. FlashAttention-3 does this in two ways: across warpgroups, by pingpong scheduling, and within a warpgroup, by a two-stage pipeline that overlaps the second GEMM with the next softmax.

# Pingpong Scheduling Across Two Consumer Warpgroups

Pingpong scheduling is the cross-warpgroup version of this idea. The kernel is warp-specialized, so some warps act as producers that bring data into shared memory, while consumer warpgroups carry out the attention computation. The paper’s pingpong scheme arranges two consumer warpgroups so that one is doing GEMMs while the other is doing softmax. Then they swap roles, like two players passing the ball back and forth, which is where the “pingpong” name comes from.

The point is not simply to run two warpgroups at once. The point is to bias the execution so that their expensive operations complement one another. The softmax work of one warpgroup can be issued while the other warpgroup is occupying the Tensor Cores with its GEMMs. Because the lower-throughput softmax uses different execution resources from matmul, the two can coexist more effectively than if each warpgroup tried to complete its entire block in isolation. The result is a more balanced use of the hardware pipeline.

The paper reports that this is not merely a theoretical idea. In one example, the authors say that pingpong scheduling improves performance from about 570 TFLOPS to roughly 620--640 TFLOPS for FP16 forward attention with head dimension 128 and sequence length 8192. They also caution that the execution is not as clean in practice as the idealized picture, but it still helps. The important lesson is that scheduling across warpgroups can be used deliberately to align the slow softmax phase of one group with the fast GEMM phase of another.

# Using \texttt{bar.sync} to Influence Execution Order Across Warpgroups

To make pingpong scheduling happen, the kernel needs some way to shape the order in which the GPU issues work. The paper uses synchronization barriers, specifically \texttt{bar.sync} instructions, to influence this order. The goal is to force the GEMMs of one warpgroup to be scheduled before the GEMMs of the other warpgroup. Once that ordering is established, the first warpgroup can move into its softmax phase while the second warpgroup is still performing GEMMs.

This use of barriers is subtle. The barrier is not there because the math requires global synchronization after every block. Instead, it is there because scheduling on a GPU is a hardware-managed process, and the programmer can sometimes guide that process by creating precise points where one group must wait for another. In the paper’s description, this makes warpgroup 1 finish its GEMM phases first, then spend time on softmax while warpgroup 2 occupies the Tensor Cores. After that, the roles reverse. The barrier creates the alternation that pingpong scheduling depends on.

The important intuition is that the barrier is being used as a performance tool, not just a correctness tool. It helps construct a useful temporal arrangement of work, where one warpgroup’s non-matmul instructions are displaced into the time window created by another warpgroup’s matmuls. That is how the paper turns a synchronization primitive into an opportunity for overlap.

# Scheduling One Warpgroup’s Softmax While Another Warpgroup’s GEMMs Run

Once the warpgroups are arranged in this alternating pattern, the overlap becomes easy to describe. Suppose warpgroup 1 has just launched a GEMM and is waiting for the corresponding score block to become available. At that moment, warpgroup 2 can be using the Tensor Cores for its own GEMMs. The softmax computation for warpgroup 1, which is slower but does not need the Tensor Cores, can run while warpgroup 2 is keeping those Tensor Cores occupied.

This is why the paper emphasizes the difference between the hardware units. The Tensor Cores are excellent at matmul, but the softmax-related exponential and reduction work is handled elsewhere. If both warpgroups tried to do the same type of work at the same time, some hardware would sit idle. By contrast, if one warpgroup is doing softmax while the other is doing GEMM, the two phases can coexist more productively. The overlap is not perfect, but it is enough to raise utilization.

The word “while” matters here. The kernel is not changing the math of attention; it is changing when the various pieces of the math happen. That is the core software-pipelining idea in FlashAttention-3. Softmax is not eliminated, and the GEMMs are not fused away. They are simply scheduled so that the low-throughput phase occupies time that would otherwise be lost to waiting.

# The Two-Stage Pipeline with $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$

The intra-warpgroup pipeline refines the same idea inside a single consumer warpgroup. The paper presents it as a two-stage algorithm built around two score buffers, $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$. At the start, the kernel computes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_0^\top$ and then uses that block to initialize the running softmax state. After that, each loop iteration computes the next score block and keeps it available for the following iteration.

The reason for having both $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ is that the current block still needs to be consumed by softmax and value multiplication while the next block is already being prepared. The kernel computes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ using WGMMA, but it does not immediately force that result to be used. Instead, it stores the result in registers and only later waits for the relevant WGMMA when the pipeline reaches the softmax stage. This is the essence of the two-stage design: one block is being consumed while the next is being produced.

The paper notes that the number of overlap stages is bounded by, but does not have to equal, the number of stages in the circular shared-memory buffer. That means the shared-memory ring buffer and the register-level software pipeline are related but distinct. The shared-memory buffer feeds data to the consumer warps, while the register buffer lets the kernel hold onto an intermediate score block long enough to overlap it with later work.

# Overlapping the $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$ GEMM from One Iteration with Softmax from the Next Iteration

The key overlap inside the two-stage pipeline is between the second GEMM of one iteration and the softmax work for the next score block. In the paper’s pseudocode, the kernel launches the WGMMA that computes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, then launches the WGMMA that computes $\mathbf{O}_i \leftarrow \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$, and only then waits for the score GEMM to finish so that it can run the softmax update. This means that while one WGMMA is still in flight, the kernel can be doing the reduction and exponential work on the previously completed block.

This is the heart of the overlap. The second GEMM, which uses the current probabilities and the value block, is asynchronous, so the kernel does not have to sit idle while it executes. During that time, the softmax for the next block can be computed, as long as the next score block has already been launched and is ready when needed. In the paper’s description, the second WGMMA of iteration $j$ is overlapped with the softmax operations from iteration $j+1$. That is a classic software pipeline: the machine works on two adjacent iterations at once, with one stage computing while another stage prepares the next input.

The practical benefit is that the low-throughput softmax work is no longer isolated as a pure stall between GEMMs. Instead, it is hidden in the shadow of a second GEMM. Because the Tensor Cores are asynchronous, the kernel can keep them busy without waiting for the softmax to finish, and because the softmax does not need the Tensor Cores, it can run alongside the ongoing matmul. The result is a better balanced inner loop.

# Loop-Carried Dependencies and How Register Buffering Breaks Them

At first glance, the pipeline seems impossible because the operations are still logically dependent. The softmax for a block needs the score matrix from the previous GEMM, and the second GEMM needs the probabilities from softmax. So how can one iteration overlap with the next? The answer is that the algorithm does not remove the dependencies; it moves them across iteration boundaries and stores the intermediate result in registers.

This is why the buffer names matter. When the kernel computes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$, it does not immediately discard it. Instead, it keeps that block in registers while it finishes the softmax and value update for the current block. Then, at the end of the iteration, it copies $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ into $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ so the next iteration can begin from a score block that is already available. The loop-carried dependency is therefore turned into a pipeline register: a saved intermediate that lets one stage start before the previous stage has completely finished everything else.

This is exactly the kind of dependence breaking that software pipelining is designed for. The original chain was “compute scores, wait, compute softmax, wait, compute output.” The pipelined chain becomes “launch the next scores, work on the current softmax and output, then later consume the next scores.” The algorithm is still correct because the mathematical order is preserved, but the hardware sees more overlap and less waiting.

# The Epilogue: Final Rescaling and Writing $\mathbf{O}$ and Log-Sum-Exp State

After all the key and value blocks have been processed, the kernel still has one final job: it must normalize the accumulated output and store the summary statistics that describe the row-wise softmax. The paper’s epilogue computes
\[
\mathbf{O}_i = \mathrm{diag}(\ell_i)^{-1} \mathbf{O}_i
\]
and
\[
L_i = m_i + \log(\ell_i),
\]
and then writes $\mathbf{O}_i$ and $L_i$ back to high-bandwidth memory as the $i$-th block of the output and log-sum-exp state.

This epilogue is the final step that turns the running, blockwise accumulation into the actual attention output. During the loop, the kernel has been maintaining output contributions in a form that can be rescaled whenever the running maximum changes. That is why the final normalization must happen only after all blocks have been seen. Once the loop is done, the accumulated value in $\mathbf{O}_i$ can be divided by the final row sums in $\ell_i$, and the blockwise log-sum-exp quantity $L_i$ can be formed from the final maximum and sum.

For a student reading the paper, the important point is that the epilogue is not a leftover detail. It is the mathematical closure of the entire blockwise softmax procedure. The overlap tricks speed up the inner loop, but the algorithm still has to end by producing the correctly normalized output and the corresponding summary state.

# Compiler Reordering and the Need to Verify Actual SASS Output

The paper is careful to point out that pseudocode is not the same as machine code. Even if the algorithm is written in a particular order, the compiler may reorder instructions for its own optimization goals. That is especially important here, because the whole performance trick depends on a carefully arranged sequence of asynchronous WGMMA launches, waits, and softmax instructions. If the compiler moves instructions around too aggressively, the intended overlap can be weakened or lost.

For that reason, the authors do not rely on the high-level pseudocode alone. They inspect the generated SASS, the actual machine-level instruction stream, to check that the overlap appears as intended. This is an important lesson for anyone studying GPU kernels: when performance depends on precise interleaving, the source code is only a proposal. The real question is what the compiler emitted and whether the hardware is actually seeing the schedule the programmer expected.

The chapter’s broader message is that modern GPU optimization is partly a scheduling problem and partly a compilation problem. The algorithm must be designed to admit overlap, but the implementation must also preserve that overlap after compilation. FlashAttention-3 explicitly acknowledges that requirement.

# Register Pressure, Tile-Size Trade-Offs, and Spill Avoidance

The two-stage pipeline buys overlap, but it is not free. The paper explains that the additional score buffer $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ must live in registers, which increases register usage by roughly $B_r \times B_c \times \mathrm{sizeof}(\mathrm{float})$ per threadblock. That is a substantial cost, especially when the kernel is already trying to use large tiles to improve arithmetic intensity and occupancy. The same registers that would help with larger tiles are now also needed to hold the pipelined intermediate.

This creates a real trade-off. Larger tiles can improve GEMM efficiency, but they consume more registers. Deeper pipelining can hide more softmax work, but it also consumes more registers. If the register demand becomes too large, the compiler may spill values, which means writing them to slower memory and losing the performance that the pipeline was meant to gain. So the practical question is not whether more overlap is always better, but how much overlap can be supported before register pressure becomes harmful.

The paper’s advice is to profile and balance these factors rather than assume a single universally best configuration. The point of the pipelining design is to expose an opportunity for higher utilization; the final kernel configuration still has to fit within the hardware’s register budget. That is why spill avoidance is central to the implementation discussion.

# The Optional Three-Stage Pipeline and Why It Is Harder to Balance

The paper also mentions a possible three-stage variant. Conceptually, this would extend the two-stage scheme by adding another layer of overlap, allowing even more of the softmax work to be hidden behind asynchronous WGMMA execution. The appeal is straightforward: if two stages can overlap a GEMM with softmax, perhaps a third stage can smooth the pipeline further and improve Tensor Core utilization again.

But the cost grows quickly. An extra stage means more state must be kept alive at once, which means even more registers. That makes the trade-off between tile size and pipeline depth harder to balance. The kernel must now decide not only how large its blocks should be, but also how many partially completed iterations it can hold in flight without exceeding the register budget or causing spills. The paper therefore treats the three-stage version as an interesting extension rather than the main design point.

The lesson is a familiar one in GPU programming: deeper pipelines can improve throughput, but only if the hardware can afford to carry the additional state. FlashAttention-3’s two-stage pipeline is the practical middle ground emphasized in the paper, while the three-stage version illustrates how far the same idea might be pushed if the register budget allowed it.

Engineering FlashAttention-3 for FP8 Throughput

FlashAttention-3’s FP8 path is best understood as an exercise in matching an algorithm to Hopper’s execution model. The paper’s central observation is that attention is dominated by two matrix multiplications, $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}$, so if those multiplications can run on Hopper’s FP8 Tensor Cores, the overall kernel can become substantially faster. But the paper also shows that Hopper’s FP8 instructions are not just “FP16, but smaller.” They are more restrictive about how data must be arranged in shared memory and in registers. FlashAttention-3 therefore does not merely switch precision; it reshapes the dataflow so the FP8 hardware can be used legally and efficiently.

The efficiency story is separate from the paper’s accuracy story. The authors also use block quantization and incoherent processing to reduce FP8 error, but the focus of this chapter is the throughput side: why FP8 is attractive on Hopper, why it is harder to use than FP16, and how FlashAttention-3 solves the resulting layout problems inside the kernel.

# Why FP8 Can Nearly Double Matmul Throughput Relative to FP16

The reason FP8 is attractive is simple: Hopper’s WGMMA instruction can target FP8 Tensor Cores, and on Hopper these Tensor Cores deliver roughly $2\times$ the throughput per SM compared with FP16 or BF16. Since the attention forward pass is dominated by GEMMs, this hardware advantage can translate directly into higher attention throughput. In the paper’s experiments, this is part of why the FP8 version of FlashAttention-3 reaches close to $1.2$ PFLOPs/s.

This is not just a theoretical hardware claim. The paper frames low precision as a proven way to get more throughput from the same chip resources. That matters because attention kernels are often limited not by arithmetic availability in the abstract, but by how many useful low-precision multiply-accumulate operations the hardware can issue per unit time. If the dominant operations can be executed in FP8, the kernel has a chance to spend much less time waiting for the Tensor Cores to finish.

# The e4m3 FP8 Format and Its Hardware Support on Hopper

The paper uses the FP8 e4m3 format, which stores only $3$ bits of mantissa and $4$ bits of exponent. That smaller representation is the source of the speedup, but also of the numerical challenge. With fewer bits, each number is represented more coarsely, so FP8 has higher numerical error than FP16 or BF16. The paper is explicit that this precision reduction is a trade-off: the hardware gets faster, but the values become harder to represent accurately.

Hopper’s hardware support makes this trade-off usable. The key point is not merely that FP8 exists as a datatype, but that Hopper’s Tensor Cores can compute with it directly through WGMMA. FlashAttention-3 is designed around that fact. It assumes that the matrix multiplications are the expensive part of attention and tries to make those multiplications happen in the format Hopper accelerates most aggressively, while keeping the rest of the kernel compatible with that choice.

# Why FP8 WGMMA Is More Restrictive than FP16 WGMMA

To understand the FP8 implementation, it helps to define the layout language the paper uses. For a GEMM call that multiplies $A \times B^\top$, where $A$ is an $M \times K$ matrix and $B$ is an $N \times K$ matrix, the paper calls an operand mn-major if it is contiguous in the outer $M$ or $N$ dimension, and k-major if it is contiguous in the inner $K$ dimension. This distinction matters because the hardware does not accept every logical matrix shape in every physical layout.

The crucial asymmetry is that FP16 WGMMA is flexible: in shared memory, both mn-major and k-major input operands are accepted. FP8 WGMMA is stricter: in shared memory, only k-major format is supported. That means an operand that is perfectly fine for FP16 may be illegal or unusable for FP8. In a kernel like attention, where one wants to chain two GEMMs back-to-back, this reduces the freedom to reuse data layouts from one stage to the next.

# The k-major Requirement for FP8 Operands in Shared Memory

The k-major requirement is easiest to think of as a demand about how tiles should be laid out before the Tensor Core sees them. If a tile is k-major, the data are contiguous along the inner dimension that the GEMM uses for accumulation. For FP8 WGMMA on Hopper, that is the only shared-memory layout the instruction can consume. So before a tile reaches the Tensor Core, the kernel must make sure it is physically arranged in the right way.

In attention, this requirement is especially important for the second GEMM. The second multiplication uses the softmaxed probability tile and the value tile, and the value tile has to be presented in a layout that WGMMA can accept. Because the Tensor Memory Accelerator load itself cannot change the contiguous dimension, simply moving data from global memory to shared memory does not solve the problem. The tile must already be in the right logical orientation, or it must be rearranged on chip.

# Why V’s Default Layout Is Inconvenient for the Second GEMM in Attention

The paper notes that $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ are typically stored contiguously in the head dimension. That default layout is natural for many Transformer implementations, but it is not the same as the layout Hopper’s FP8 WGMMA wants for the second GEMM. For the second multiplication in attention, the tiles of $\mathbf{V}$ loaded into shared memory need to be contiguous in the sequence-length dimension so they satisfy the k-major constraint.

This is the first major mismatch in the FP8 path. The value matrix is laid out in a way that is convenient for the model and for common tensor storage conventions, but inconvenient for the exact sequence of operations that FlashAttention-3 wants to run on Hopper. Since the kernel is meant to be fused and fast, it cannot afford to treat this as a separate preprocessing step in the usual way. The layout problem has to be solved inside the attention kernel itself.

# Design Options: Preprocessing Transpose in Global Memory Versus In-Kernel Transpose

The paper considers two broad ways to fix the $\mathbf{V}$ layout. One option is to transpose $\mathbf{V}$ in global memory before attention begins. That transposition could be fused into the epilogue of a prior step, such as rotary embedding, or it could be done by a standalone preprocessing transpose kernel. Either way, the goal would be to exchange the sequence-length and head-dimension strides so the data arrive in a form suitable for FP8 WGMMA.

The alternative is to transpose tiles after they are loaded into shared memory. The paper chooses this option. The reason is practical: fusing a transpose into a standard library pipeline is difficult, and a standalone preprocessing transpose is too expensive in a memory-bound setting such as inference. FlashAttention is built around minimizing reads and writes to slow global memory, so adding an extra full pass over memory would work against the central design principle of the algorithm.

# Why the Paper Chooses In-Kernel Transpose for Practical Deployments

The in-kernel transpose keeps the rearrangement on chip, where it is much cheaper than touching global memory again. That is important because the paper’s goal is not simply to make FP8 mathematically possible, but to make it practical in a production-style setting. If the transpose were done as a separate kernel or as a preprocessing pass, much of the benefit of using FP8 Tensor Cores could be lost to extra memory traffic.

The paper also emphasizes that FlashAttention-3 already works on blocks. That block structure makes it natural to rearrange only the tile currently needed by the kernel, rather than the entire tensor at once. In other words, the algorithm’s tiling strategy and Hopper’s asynchronous execution model fit together: the kernel can move and transform a tile just in time, use it immediately, and then discard it. This is much more consistent with a fused attention kernel than a global preprocessing stage would be.

# Using ldmatrix and stmatrix to Transpose FP8 Tiles Through Registers

To implement the on-chip transpose, the paper uses the LDSM and STSM instructions, which move data between shared memory and the register file at a granularity of $128$ bytes. Conceptually, a warp of threads loads a tile from shared memory into registers and then stores it back, but with the data rearranged. This makes the pair useful not just for copying, but for transposing a tile while it is already on chip.

There is one important detail specific to FP8. The PTX documentation describes these instructions as copying $8 \times 8$ matrices with $16$-bit entries, but FP8 values can be packed two at a time so the same mechanism can be used for FP8 tiles. The transpose versions cannot split packed $8$-bit entries, so some register movement is needed between the load and the store to complete a tile-wise transpose. The paper does not spell out every low-level step, but the main idea is that the transpose is done efficiently using register traffic, not expensive global-memory movement.

# Overlapping the Transpose of the Next V Tile with Current GEMMs

Once the pipeline is running, the transpose does not have to happen in isolation. After the first iteration, the next $\mathbf{V}$ tile can be transposed while the current iteration is still busy executing the two WGMMAs that use the previous $\mathbf{V}$ tile and the current $\mathbf{K}$ tile. This is exactly the kind of overlap Hopper’s asynchronous execution model is designed to enable.

The intuition is that the transpose work is relatively lightweight compared with the matrix multiplications, so the kernel should try to hide it “in the shadow” of the Tensor Core work. If the producer warpgroup is rearranging the next value tile while the consumer warpgroups are multiplying the current tiles, then the expensive Tensor Cores stay busy and the layout fix becomes almost invisible in the overall schedule. That is the same style of hiding latency that appears throughout the paper’s asynchronous design.

# The Mismatch Between FP8 Accumulator Layout and Operand-A Register Layout

The second FP8-specific layout issue is more subtle. After the first GEMM computes the score tile, its result sits in an FP32 accumulator. But the paper points out that the memory layout of this FP32 accumulator is not the same as the layout expected when that data are used as operand $A$ for the second GEMM in registers. So even if the values are numerically correct, they are still physically arranged in the wrong order for the next WGMMA.

This distinction between values and layout is crucial. The problem is not the mathematics of attention; it is the physical organization of the fragments inside the hardware. The first GEMM produces a block of scores, but to feed the second GEMM efficiently, those scores have to be presented in the particular register order Hopper expects. Without that rearrangement, the kernel cannot chain the dependent FP8 instructions cleanly.

# Byte-Permute Transformations to Convert the First GEMM’s Output into the Second GEMM’s Expected Layout

The paper solves this register-layout mismatch with byte permutes. Concretely, it changes the order within each fragment to $\{d_0, d_1, d_4, d_5, d_2, d_3, d_6, d_7\}$, and then repeats that pattern over every $8$ bytes. This is a local, register-level transformation: it does not recompute the matrix product, and it does not touch global memory. It simply rearranges the bytes so the fragment matches what the next WGMMA expects.

In logical terms, this permutation changes the column order of the probability tile. The paper’s example is that columns such as $0$, $1$, $8$, and $9$ are moved to the front. The important thing for a student to notice is that the permutation is not arbitrary. It is chosen specifically so the output fragment of the first GEMM becomes a valid input fragment for the second GEMM, and so the layout of the $\mathbf{V}$ tile can be arranged to match it.

# Coordinating P-Tile Column Permutations with Matching V-Tile Row Permutations

The final step is to make the two halves of the second GEMM agree with each other. If the probability tile $\mathbf{P}$ has its columns permuted in registers, then the value tile must be written out with a matching row permutation. That way, the product still represents the same mathematical attention computation even though both operands have been rearranged internally.

This coordination is what makes the FP8 path work as a fused kernel. The in-kernel transpose can write the $\mathbf{V}$ tile with exactly the row order needed to match the permuted probability fragment, so the second GEMM receives compatible pieces on both sides. The paper notes that this extra freedom also avoids the need for shuffle instructions that would otherwise move register ownership across threads. The result is a low-precision attention kernel whose data layout is engineered end to end for Hopper’s FP8 Tensor Cores.

Taken together, these transformations show that FP8 performance is not just a matter of choosing a smaller number format. FlashAttention-3 gets its FP8 throughput by making Hopper’s stricter instruction requirements work with the structure of attention itself. The kernel transposes $\mathbf{V}$ on chip, permutes score fragments in registers, and aligns the two GEMMs so they can run back to back without violating the hardware’s layout rules. That is what turns Hopper’s FP8 capability into practical attention throughput.

Making FP8 Accurate: Block Quantization and Incoherent Processing

# Sources of numerical error in FP8 attention

The paper’s FP8 setting uses the Hopper GPU’s low-precision Tensor Cores, which can nearly double throughput relative to FP16 or BF16. That speedup is attractive, but it comes with a cost: fewer bits mean less room to represent values accurately. In the paper’s discussion of FP8, the relevant format is e4m3, which uses only 3 bits for the mantissa and 4 bits for the exponent. Compared with FP16 or BF16, this gives the hardware much less precision, so small differences in scale can show up as noticeably larger numerical error.

In attention, this issue matters because the computation is not just a single matrix multiplication. The forward pass combines two GEMMs with a softmax in between, and the softmax is particularly sensitive to the magnitudes of its inputs. Even if the matrix multiplications themselves are accelerated in FP8, the values passed between stages must still be represented well enough that the final attention output remains reliable. The paper’s central point is that simply turning on FP8 is not enough; the low-precision format must be paired with a strategy for controlling quantization error.

The paper evaluates this directly by comparing against a reference implementation in FP64. To make the test realistic for large language models, it uses inputs with occasional outlier entries, generated from a distribution of the form $\mathcal{N}(0,1)+\mathcal{N}(0,100)\cdot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001)$. This creates mostly ordinary values, but with a small fraction of much larger ones. That setup is important because the paper argues that these rare outliers are exactly the kind of values that make FP8 quantization difficult.

# Why per-tensor scaling can fail when tensors contain outliers

The baseline FP8 attention scheme discussed in the paper uses per-tensor scaling, meaning that a single scale factor is chosen for an entire tensor such as $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, or $\mathbf{V}$. This is simple and works reasonably well when all values in the tensor have comparable magnitudes. But once a tensor contains a few unusually large entries, that single scale must be chosen to accommodate them. As a result, the scale becomes too coarse for the many smaller values that make up the bulk of the tensor.

This is the core failure mode that the paper highlights. The presence of outliers in large models means that a tensor can have a few entries much larger than most others. If one scale factor has to cover everything, then the quantizer spends its representational capacity on the rare extremes, leaving less effective precision for the common case. In other words, the outliers dominate the scaling decision even though they are not representative of the tensor as a whole.

The paper connects this directly to large language models, where outlier features and activations are known to appear. In that setting, a quantization scheme that treats the entire tensor uniformly is likely to be too blunt. The key lesson is that for FP8 attention, accuracy depends not just on the bit-width itself, but on whether the scaling strategy respects the structure of the data. Per-tensor scaling does not, and the paper presents block quantization and incoherent processing as remedies.

# Block quantization at tile granularity

Block quantization replaces the single scale per tensor with one scale per block. In the paper, each of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ is split into blocks of size $B_r \times d$ or $B_c \times d$, and each block is quantized separately. This is a much finer-grained approach than per-tensor scaling, because the scale is allowed to adapt to local variations in magnitude rather than being forced to serve the entire tensor at once.

The intuition is straightforward: if one part of a tensor contains outliers and another part does not, it is wasteful to use the same scale for both. By quantizing block by block, the scale can reflect the actual range of values in each portion of the tensor. This reduces the chance that a few large values will distort the representation of many smaller ones. The paper presents this as one of the main ways to make FP8 attention substantially more accurate.

What makes block quantization especially natural in FlashAttention-3 is that the algorithm itself already works on tiles. The attention computation is organized around blocks of the query, key, and value matrices, and the softmax is performed locally on these blocks rather than on a fully materialized score matrix. Because the algorithm is already tiled, the extra scale factors fit into the same block structure instead of forcing a separate global treatment. The paper notes that the score matrix blocks can be scaled to account for block quantization at no computation cost, which means the accuracy improvement comes from fitting the quantization scheme to the existing kernel structure rather than from adding a new expensive stage.

The paper also emphasizes that block quantization can be fused with an operation that already happens before attention, such as rotary embedding. Since rotary embedding is memory-bandwidth bound, incorporating the quantization step there does not introduce an additional slowdown. This is important because it shows that the higher accuracy of block quantization does not have to come with a runtime penalty. In the paper’s design, the blockwise representation is not just more accurate; it is also compatible with the performance goals of the kernel.

# Incoherent processing with random orthogonal transforms

Block quantization addresses where the scale is applied, but it does not change the underlying distribution of values. Incoherent processing adds a second idea: before quantization, the paper multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by the same random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$. Because $\mathbf{M}$ is orthogonal, it satisfies $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = I$, and therefore
$$
(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top.
$$
This means that, in exact arithmetic, the attention scores are unchanged.

The point of doing this is not to alter attention, but to alter how the values look to the quantizer. The paper describes this as “spreading out” outliers. Each entry of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}$ or $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}$ becomes a random sum of entries of $\mathbf{Q}$ or $\mathbf{K}$, so values that were concentrated in a few large coordinates are redistributed across the transformed representation. That redistribution makes the magnitudes more even, which in turn reduces quantization error.

This is why the method is called incoherent processing: it makes the data less aligned with the coordinate axes that the quantizer sees. If a tensor has a few extremely large features, those features can be problematic when quantization is done coordinate by coordinate. After the orthogonal mixing, the same information is still present, but it is no longer concentrated in the same way. The paper’s claim is not that this removes outliers, but that it makes their effect less damaging during FP8 quantization.

# Efficient constructions and fusion with earlier steps

A practical transformation would be too expensive if it required a dense matrix multiply. The paper therefore uses a structured construction: the random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ is taken as a product of random diagonal matrices with entries $\pm 1$ and a Hadamard matrix. This allows the multiplication to be carried out in $O(d \log d)$ time rather than $O(d^2)$ time. The paper presents this as the key to making incoherent processing efficient enough to be useful inside the attention kernel.

The structured transform also interacts nicely with the rest of the FlashAttention-3 pipeline. The paper notes that the random orthogonal transform can be fused with rotary embedding at no extra computation cost. That means the mixing step does not need to become an isolated preprocessing pass. Instead, it can be folded into work that is already being done, which preserves the kernel’s efficiency while improving the quality of the FP8 representation.

In the kernel design, these choices matter because attention is already sensitive to memory traffic and instruction scheduling. Any accuracy technique that adds separate passes over memory would undermine the point of FlashAttention in the first place. By choosing a fast structured transform and by fusing it with nearby operations, the paper keeps incoherent processing compatible with the same performance-minded design that motivates FlashAttention-3 overall.

# How the two methods improve FP8 accuracy in the paper’s experiments

The paper evaluates these ideas by comparing FP8 FlashAttention-3 to a baseline FP8 attention implementation that uses per-tensor scaling. In the baseline, the matmul accumulator is kept in FP32 and the intermediate softmax results are kept in FP16, but the scaling is still done per tensor. Under the synthetic outlier distribution used in the experiments, that baseline suffers noticeably more numerical error than the proposed method.

With block quantization and incoherent processing, FP8 FlashAttention-3 is reported to be $2.6\times$ more accurate than the baseline FP8 attention with per-tensor quantization. The paper measures accuracy using root mean squared error against an FP64 reference, so the comparison is directly about how close the low-precision output is to a high-precision target. This result is the main empirical justification for the techniques in this chapter.

The broader message is that FP8 can be practical only if the kernel is designed to respect the structure of the data and the hardware at the same time. Block quantization uses the blockwise structure of FlashAttention to localize scaling decisions, and incoherent processing changes the coordinate system so that outliers matter less during quantization. Together, they let FlashAttention-3 preserve much more of the accuracy that naive FP8 would lose, while still benefiting from the speed of Hopper’s low-precision Tensor Cores.

Backward Pass, Attention Variants, and Implementation Considerations

# Why backward attention is more expensive than forward attention

The paper makes clear that the backward pass is not just the forward pass run in reverse. In the forward direction, attention computes $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, then $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, and finally $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. In the backward direction, the chain rule expands this into several separate matrix multiplications: $\mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO}$, $\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO}\mathbf{V}^\top$, $\mathbf{dS} = \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP})$, $\mathbf{dQ} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{dK} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}^\top \mathbf{Q}$. The paper’s background section presents these formulas explicitly to show that gradient computation is structurally more involved than the forward computation.

This extra structure is reflected in the paper’s benchmark accounting. The authors note that the forward pass has two matmuls, while the backward pass has five matmuls because of recomputation, so they approximate backward FLOPs as $2.5$ times the forward FLOPs. That is the key reason backward attention is more expensive: even before considering memory movement, the derivative formulas themselves create more GEMM work. For a system paper like this one, that distinction matters because it explains why training kernels must be optimized separately rather than assumed to inherit forward-pass speed automatically.

# Recomputation versus storing intermediates in memory

A central idea behind FlashAttention, and therefore FlashAttention-3, is that it is often better to recompute some quantities than to write large intermediates to HBM and read them back later. The paper contrasts its approach with standard attention, which materializes the intermediate score matrix $\mathbf{S}$ and probability matrix $\mathbf{P}$ in HBM. Those matrices are large, and moving them through memory is expensive relative to keeping the computation on chip. FlashAttention avoids that cost by fusing the attention operations into a single kernel and using local softmax accumulation.

The same tradeoff appears in the backward pass. The gradient formulas depend on softmax quantities, but FlashAttention-3 does not treat that as a reason to store the whole $N \times N$ attention matrix. Instead, it follows the broader FlashAttention philosophy: spend more arithmetic if that helps avoid HBM traffic. The paper’s backward FLOP accounting, where the backward pass is counted as $2.5$ times the forward pass, is a compact way of expressing this design choice. In other words, recomputation is not a flaw of the method; it is an explicit part of the performance strategy.

# How the online-softmax state supports the backward pass

The online softmax used in the forward pass is more than a numerical trick. In the paper’s forward algorithm, each query row maintains the running maximum $m_i$ and running normalizer $\ell_i$ as the key blocks are streamed through the kernel. These values are updated block by block so that the kernel can compute the exact softmax without ever forming the full score matrix in HBM. At the end of the forward pass, the kernel computes $L_i = m_i + \log(\ell_i)$ and writes both $\mathbf{O}_i$ and $L_i$ to HBM.

That compact state is exactly the sort of information a backward kernel wants. The backward formulas require softmax-related information, but not necessarily a stored copy of every score. The online-softmax summary captures the normalization behavior of each row in a small amount of data, which is much cheaper to keep around than $\mathbf{S}$ or $\mathbf{P}$. The paper’s forward kernel therefore sets up the backward computation by preserving the row-wise log-sum-exp state, rather than forcing the implementation to cache enormous intermediates.

# The paper’s reported backward-pass speedups

The paper reports that FlashAttention-3 also improves training-time performance, not just inference-time performance. On the H100 SXM5 GPU, FP16 FlashAttention-3 is reported to be about $1.5$--$1.75\times$ faster than FlashAttention-2 in the backward pass. This is a meaningful gain because backward kernels are inherently heavier than forward kernels, so any improvement there directly affects end-to-end training throughput.

The authors present these backward results alongside their forward benchmarks to show that the hardware-aware redesign is not limited to the easier case. The forward pass reaches up to $740$ TFLOPs/s, while the backward pass still benefits substantially even though it must execute more matrix multiplications and manage more dependence structure. The fact that the backward speedup is somewhat smaller than the forward speedup is consistent with the paper’s own FLOP accounting: the backward path simply has more work to optimize.

# Extending warp specialization and overlap ideas to backward kernels

The excerpt gives explicit pseudocode for the forward kernel, but the underlying execution principles are broader than forward attention alone. The paper’s main hardware ideas are warp specialization, asynchronous data movement, and overlapping Tensor Core work with lower-throughput softmax-style work. Those are not forward-specific concepts. They are general scheduling tools for kernels that have a mix of GEMMs and scalar or elementwise operations, which is exactly the kind of mix the backward pass also contains.

The backward formulas for attention again involve matmuls and softmax derivatives, so the same Hopper execution model is relevant. The paper’s measured backward speedup indicates that these ideas do carry through to training. Even though the excerpt does not spell out the backward pseudocode in the same level of detail as the forward pass, the paper’s overall message is that the same asynchronous, warp-specialized philosophy is applied to make both directions of attention faster.

# Multi-query attention and grouped-query attention indexing strategies

The paper states that for multi-query attention and grouped-query attention, it follows the approach of FlashAttention-2 and adjusts tensor indexing accordingly. The purpose is to adapt the kernel to these attention variants without changing the underlying attention math. In the paper’s terms, the implementation modifies how tensors are indexed so that the kernel can handle the different sharing structure of queries, keys, and values.

The important point is that this is an implementation strategy, not a new attention formula. Multi-query and grouped-query attention reuse key and value information across query heads or groups, so the kernel has to map the right query blocks to the right key/value blocks. The paper’s solution is to change indexing rather than duplicate data, which keeps the attention computation compatible with the memory-efficient design of FlashAttention.

# Avoiding duplication of K and V in memory for MQA and GQA

Avoiding duplication of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ is important because FlashAttention is built around reducing memory traffic. If the kernel were to replicate those tensors in HBM for every head or group, it would waste both bandwidth and storage, undermining one of the main advantages of the method. The paper explicitly says that its indexing strategy for multi-query attention and grouped-query attention is designed to avoid that duplication.

This is another example of the paper’s broader design pattern: prefer a smarter view of the same data over copying it. By keeping a single representation of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ and letting the kernel address it appropriately, the implementation preserves the memory efficiency of the original FlashAttention approach. That matters especially in long-context settings, where memory pressure is already high and attention is already the computational bottleneck.

# Persistent kernels and load-balancing strategies

The paper notes an implementation difference between its FP16 and FP8 kernels that affects practical performance. For the benchmarks, FP16 FlashAttention-3 uses a persistent kernel and load-balancing strategy, while FP8 FlashAttention-3 does not. The paper treats this as one reason FP8 does not always match the strongest FP8 cuDNN kernels on small sequence lengths and with causal masking.

This distinction matters because good arithmetic alone is not enough; the kernel also has to keep the GPU busy in a balanced way. A persistent kernel and its load-balancing logic are part of how the FP16 implementation reaches strong utilization on H100. The paper positions the lack of the same treatment in FP8 as a limitation and a future improvement opportunity, rather than as a limitation of FP8 precision itself.

# Why the FP16 kernel includes persistence while the FP8 kernel currently does not

The paper is explicit that this is a current asymmetry in the implementation. FP16 FlashAttention-3 already includes the persistent-kernel design and the associated load-balancing strategy in its benchmarked implementation, whereas FP8 FlashAttention-3 does not yet incorporate that machinery. The result is that FP16 is more complete from a systems standpoint, even though FP8 is extremely fast in raw compute throughput.

The paper also links this to its limitations section. It says that future work includes optimizing for LLM inference and integrating a persistent kernel design into the FP8 kernel. That framing is important: the authors are not claiming FP8 is inherently less capable, only that the current FP8 implementation does not yet exploit all of the scheduling refinements available to the FP16 path. This helps explain why FP8 is less competitive in the smaller-sequence and causal-masking cases reported in the paper.

# Practical implementation with CUTLASS primitives

The paper implements FlashAttention-3 using CUTLASS primitives, specifically the Hopper-oriented WGMMA and TMA abstractions. This choice matches the algorithmic design closely. The core of FlashAttention-3 is built around asynchronous data movement from GMEM to SMEM and asynchronous block GEMMs on the Tensor Cores, so an implementation framework that already exposes those hardware capabilities is a natural fit.

The paper also emphasizes that implementation is not just about writing the idealized algorithm. The pseudocode represents the intended execution order, but the compiler can reorder instructions in ways that affect the overlap between GEMM and softmax. The authors therefore inspect the generated SASS to verify that the expected pipelining occurs. They also discuss register pressure, since deeper pipelines and larger tiles both consume registers and can force tradeoffs between pipeline depth and tile size. Those are exactly the kinds of practical concerns that matter when translating a hardware-aware algorithm into a fast kernel.

# Interfacing optimized kernels with PyTorch and Hugging Face libraries

The paper closes by saying that FlashAttention-3 is open-sourced under a permissive license and that the authors plan to integrate it with PyTorch and Hugging Face. This is an important implementation choice in its own right. A fast kernel only has broad impact if it can be used inside the software stacks that researchers and developers already rely on.

That integration goal fits the paper’s broader motivation. Attention is a core Transformer primitive, and the benefits of better speed and better numerical behavior only reach most users if the implementation is available through familiar libraries. By planning integration with PyTorch and Hugging Face, the authors are signaling that FlashAttention-3 is intended not just as a research prototype, but as a practical primitive for real training and inference workflows.

Experimental Evaluation and What the Numbers Mean

# Benchmark Hardware and the Role of the H100 SXM5

The paper evaluates \textsc{FlashAttention-3} on NVIDIA’s Hopper-generation H100 SXM5 GPU, specifically the $80$GB SXM5 variant. This choice is important because the paper’s main claims are about taking advantage of Hopper-specific features, especially asynchronous Tensor Core execution through WGMMA and asynchronous data movement through TMA, as well as FP8 support. In other words, the benchmark hardware is not just a neutral platform: it is the environment in which the algorithm is designed to shine.

When reading the results, it helps to remember that the paper is comparing \textsc{FlashAttention-3} against methods that do not fully exploit the same hardware features, or do so less effectively. That is why the hardware description is part of the argument. The numbers are not merely measuring an implementation in isolation; they are measuring how well different attention implementations use the available GPU execution model.

# Compared Methods and What Each Baseline Represents

The paper compares \textsc{FlashAttention-3} against four main alternatives: standard attention in PyTorch, \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, \textsc{FlashAttention-2} implemented in Triton, and NVIDIA’s cuDNN attention implementation. These baselines are useful because they represent different points in the design space.

Standard PyTorch attention is the most direct reference point. In the paper’s terminology, it materializes the intermediate score and probability matrices, $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$, to HBM. That means it uses more global memory traffic and therefore provides a clear contrast with the fused FlashAttention style. \textsc{FlashAttention-2} is the most immediate predecessor and shows what was already gained by reducing memory reads and writes. The Triton version of \textsc{FlashAttention-2} is especially relevant because it uses H100-specific instructions, so it helps separate algorithmic improvement from instruction-level optimization. Finally, cuDNN serves as a strong vendor baseline: it represents a carefully optimized library implementation for Hopper.

This set of comparisons lets the paper answer different questions at once. If \textsc{FlashAttention-3} beats standard attention, it confirms the value of fusion and tiling. If it beats \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, it shows that the new asynchronous and low-precision ideas matter beyond the earlier FlashAttention design. If it competes with or surpasses cuDNN, it indicates that the paper’s algorithmic choices are strong enough to stand alongside a highly optimized library.

# Benchmark Settings: Sequence Lengths, Token Budget, Hidden Size, Head Dimensions, and Causal Masking

The paper uses a carefully controlled family of benchmarks. Sequence length varies over $512$, $1$k, \dots, $16$k. At the same time, the batch size is chosen so that the total number of tokens is fixed at $16$k. This design means that the benchmark explores how performance changes as the sequence is made longer or shorter, while keeping the overall token budget constant.

The hidden dimension is set to $2048$, and the head dimension is varied among $64$, $128$, and $256$. These correspond to $32$, $16$, and $8$ heads, respectively. This matters because attention work depends not just on the sequence length but also on how the hidden dimension is partitioned across heads. By testing multiple head dimensions, the paper checks whether the speedups are robust across different attention shapes rather than tuned to one special case.

The paper also considers both non-causal and causal masking. With causal masking, only the lower-triangular part of the attention matrix is used, so the amount of work is effectively reduced. The paper reflects this directly in its FLOP accounting. This distinction is important because causal masking changes the computational pattern and can change how effectively a kernel overlaps work and hides latency.

# How the Paper Counts FLOPs

To interpret throughput numbers such as TFLOPs/s, one first needs the FLOP formulas used by the paper. For the forward pass, the paper uses
\[
4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head dimension} \cdot \text{number of heads}.
\]
This formula captures the computational cost of the two main matrix multiplications in attention, together with the factor used by the paper for its FLOP accounting. When causal masking is enabled, the paper divides this quantity by $2$ because approximately only half of the score matrix entries are computed.

For the backward pass, the paper multiplies the forward-pass FLOPs by $2.5$. The reason given is that the backward computation contains $5$ matmuls in total, compared with $2$ matmuls in the forward pass, due to recomputation. This accounting is useful because it lets the paper report backward throughput in a way that is comparable to the forward measurement, even though the underlying operations differ.

For a student learning to read these results, the key point is that TFLOPs/s is not just a raw runtime metric. It is runtime translated into a normalized measure of work per second. That lets the paper compare methods across different sequence lengths and masking settings on a common basis.

# FP16 Forward-Pass Results

The paper reports that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} achieves a $1.5$ to $2.0\times$ speedup over \textsc{FlashAttention-2} in the FP16 forward pass. In the strongest cases, it reaches up to $740$ TFLOPs/s. This is a substantial result because the forward pass is one of the central workloads in Transformer inference and training, and the paper’s improvements directly target the bottlenecks that limit it.

What makes this number meaningful is not just its absolute size, but its relation to prior work. \textsc{FlashAttention-2} already reduced memory traffic by fusing attention operations, so another factor-of-two-style improvement indicates that the new algorithm is making better use of Hopper’s asynchronous execution. In the paper’s framing, the improvement comes from producer-consumer asynchrony, the overlap of GEMM and softmax, and Hopper-specific low-level execution features. The speedup therefore suggests that the previous generation of FlashAttention had left a significant amount of Hopper’s capability unused.

The paper also notes that for a representative FP16 setting, the throughput rises from about $570$ TFLOPs/s to the low $600$ TFLOPs/s range when the new algorithmic ideas are enabled, and ultimately peaks near $740$ TFLOPs/s across the benchmark suite. That progression helps readers see that the final result is not a single lucky datapoint but part of a consistent performance trend.

# FP16 Backward-Pass Results

The backward pass shows similar, though slightly smaller, gains. The paper reports a $1.5$ to $1.75\times$ speedup over \textsc{FlashAttention-2}. This is still a strong improvement, especially because backward attention is generally more complex than the forward pass due to the need to propagate gradients through the softmax and the two matrix multiplications.

A useful way to read this result is to notice that the paper is not claiming a dramatic asymmetry between forward and backward. Instead, it shows that the same hardware-aware design principles carry over to both phases of training. That matters because training workloads depend on the backward pass just as much as the forward pass. A faster forward-only kernel would be incomplete if the backward path remained a bottleneck.

The paper’s comparison also reinforces an important lesson about performance evaluation: a method can be optimized in one direction without automatically solving the whole problem. Here, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} improves both phases, which suggests that its design changes are broadly beneficial rather than narrowly tailored to one pass.

# Peak Measured Throughput and What $75\%$ Utilization Means

At its peak, the paper reports that FP16 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} reaches up to $740$ TFLOPs/s, which the paper describes as about $75\%$ utilization of the H100’s theoretical maximum. This is a very high level of utilization for a real kernel, and it gives a sense of how close the implementation gets to the machine’s peak compute capability.

The significance of this number is easiest to understand by comparing it with the paper’s observation that \textsc{FlashAttention-2} on H100 reaches only about $35\%$ utilization. The point is not simply that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is faster; it is that it is much better at filling the GPU’s Tensor Core machinery with useful work. Since attention is often limited by a mix of memory movement, softmax overhead, and scheduling inefficiencies, reaching $75\%$ utilization suggests that the paper’s asynchrony and pipelining ideas are doing exactly what they are meant to do.

This is why the paper repeatedly emphasizes Hopper’s asynchronous Tensor Cores and TMA. The throughput figure is evidence that those hardware features are being turned into practical speed, not just mentioned in theory.

# FP8 Forward Performance

The FP8 results are even more striking in raw throughput. The paper reports that FP8 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} reaches close to $1.2$ PFLOPs/s. Since FP8 doubles the throughput of the relevant Tensor Core path relative to FP16 or BF16 on Hopper, the paper’s result reflects both the hardware advantage of FP8 and the algorithm’s ability to feed that hardware efficiently.

At the same time, the paper is careful to present FP8 not just as a performance story but as a trade-off between speed and precision. The FP8 kernel requires layout transformations, in-kernel transposition, and special handling of accumulator layouts so that the dependent WGMMAs can be issued correctly. The performance result therefore shows more than just a fast low-precision path; it shows that the implementation successfully navigates the hardware constraints associated with FP8 on Hopper.

For readers, the main lesson is that FP8 does not merely mean “smaller numbers and faster execution.” It means a carefully engineered pipeline that can exploit the hardware’s wider throughput while preserving enough numerical stability to remain useful.

# When FlashAttention-3 Surpasses cuDNN, and When It Is Only Competitive

The paper compares \textsc{FlashAttention-3} with a state-of-the-art attention implementation from cuDNN. For large sequence lengths, FP16 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} outperforms the cuDNN implementation, and FP8 is competitive with it. The paper also gives a more detailed breakdown for FP8.

More precisely, for head dimension $64$, FP8 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is ahead of cuDNN. For head dimensions $128$ and $256$, it is at par with cuDNN for the cases without causal masking, but behind with causal masking. This is a nuanced result, and it is important not to oversimplify it into “FlashAttention-3 always wins.”

That nuance teaches an important lesson about reading systems papers. The question is not whether one method is universally best, but under what configurations it wins. Here, the paper’s results show that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is strong across the board, but the relative balance between kernels depends on sequence length, head dimension, and whether masking is causal.

# Ablation Study: What Warp Specialization and GEMM-Softmax Pipelining Contribute

The ablation study isolates the effect of the paper’s algorithmic improvements by testing non-causal FP16 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} at fixed settings $\{\text{batch}, \text{seqlen}, \text{nheads}, \text{hdim}\} = \{4, 8448, 16, 128\}$. In this setting, the paper reports a throughput increase from about $570$ TFLOPs/s to $661$ TFLOPs/s when the new ideas are enabled.

This result matters because it shows that the gains are not merely the consequence of a more polished implementation. The paper specifically attributes the improvement to warp specialization and to overlapping between GEMM and softmax. In other words, the performance increase comes from better use of the GPU’s asynchronous execution model. The ablation is therefore a direct test of the paper’s central claim that algorithmic structure should be designed around hardware concurrency.

The change from $570$ to $661$ TFLOPs/s is also useful pedagogically because it is large enough to matter, but not so large that it looks implausible. It gives a concrete sense of how much a well-chosen scheduling strategy can improve a high-performance kernel once the major memory traffic optimizations are already in place.

# Numerical Error Methodology: FP64 Reference and Synthetic Outliers

To evaluate numerical accuracy, the paper compares \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, \textsc{FlashAttention-3}, and standard attention against an FP64 reference implementation. This is a standard and sensible way to measure numerical error: FP64 serves as the closest thing to a high-precision ground truth within the paper’s experimental setup.

The paper does not use arbitrary random inputs. Instead, it constructs synthetic data designed to mimic the outlier behavior seen in large language models. The entries of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ are sampled from
\[
\mathcal{N}(0, 1) + \mathcal{N}(0, 100)\cdot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001).
\]
This means most entries are drawn from a standard normal distribution, but a small fraction, $0.1\%$, receive an additional large-magnitude term with standard deviation $10$. The purpose is to stress the quantization and softmax computations in a way that resembles difficult real-model behavior.

The paper measures root mean squared error, or RMSE, relative to the FP64 output. RMSE is a natural choice here because it summarizes average deviation across all output entries in a single quantity, making it easy to compare methods under the same test distribution.

# Why the FP16 FlashAttention Variants Are More Accurate Than Standard Attention

The paper finds that both \textsc{FlashAttention-2} and \textsc{FlashAttention-3} in FP16 achieve $1.7\times$ lower RMSE than the standard attention implementation. The key reason given is that the FlashAttention variants keep intermediate results, especially the softmax rescaling, in FP32.

This result is important because it shows that a fused kernel can improve accuracy as well as speed. A common intuition is that faster GPU kernels must be making more aggressive approximations or losing precision. The paper’s result argues against that simplistic view. Here, the FlashAttention design reduces memory traffic while also preserving higher-precision intermediate computation where it matters.

For a student, the lesson is that numerical accuracy depends not only on the final datatype, but on where rounding happens throughout the computation. Keeping the intermediate softmax-related quantities in FP32 helps reduce accumulated error, which is exactly what the paper observes.

# Why FP8 FlashAttention-3 Is More Accurate Than Baseline FP8 Attention

The FP8 comparison is more delicate. The paper compares \textsc{FlashAttention-3} against a baseline FP8 attention implementation that uses per-tensor scaling, an FP32 matmul accumulator, and FP16 intermediate softmax results. Against that baseline, FP8 \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is $2.6\times$ more accurate.

The paper attributes this improvement to two techniques: block quantization and incoherent processing. Block quantization uses one scale per block rather than one scale for the entire tensor, so the quantizer can adapt more locally to the distribution of values. This is especially relevant when different blocks of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ have different magnitude patterns. Incoherent processing, meanwhile, multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix before quantization, which spreads out outliers and makes the values easier to represent in FP8.

The key intuition is that the paper is not merely “using FP8 carefully.” It is redesigning the quantization strategy to match the structure of attention and to reduce the damage caused by outlier values. The $2.6\times$ accuracy gain is therefore a direct indication that the low-precision path is not only fast but also substantially better behaved than a straightforward FP8 baseline.

# What the Numbers Mean Overall

Taken together, the evaluation shows that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is doing two things at once. First, it pushes attention much closer to the H100’s compute limits by exploiting asynchrony, warp specialization, and overlapping of GEMM with softmax. Second, it makes FP8 practical for attention by addressing both hardware layout constraints and numerical sensitivity.

The most important lesson for reading the paper is that the throughput and accuracy numbers are not isolated facts. They are evidence that each design choice solves a specific problem. The speedups show that the algorithm better matches Hopper’s execution model. The ablation study shows that the new scheduling ideas matter. The numerical error experiments show that low precision need not come at the cost of unacceptable accuracy when the quantization strategy is designed carefully.

So the empirical story is not simply that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is “faster.” It is that the paper’s hardware-aware redesign changes what is possible for exact attention on modern GPUs, both in speed and in numerical reliability.

Limitations, Broader Lessons, and Future Directions

# What FlashAttention-3 Demonstrates about Hardware-Aware Algorithm Design

The central lesson of FlashAttention-3 is that high performance on modern GPUs comes from designing the algorithm around the hardware, not from treating the hardware as a generic black box. The paper shows that Hopper’s asynchronous Tensor Cores, Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA), and FP8 Tensor Core support are not just implementation conveniences; they fundamentally change what an efficient attention kernel should look like. By organizing work so that some warps act as producers and others as consumers, and by overlapping data movement with computation, FlashAttention-3 reaches much higher utilization than FlashAttention-2 on the H100 GPU.

This is why the paper emphasizes three techniques together rather than any single trick. Warp-specialization hides memory and issue latency, interleaving softmax with asynchronous block GEMMs hides the lower-throughput non-matmul work, and FP8 computation nearly doubles throughput when the layout and numerical issues are handled correctly. The result is not just a faster kernel in isolation. It is evidence that attention kernels can be rethought as carefully scheduled pipelines that match the structure of the underlying machine, achieving up to $740$ TFLOPs/s in FP16 and close to $1.2$ PFLOPs/s in FP8.

# The Importance of Treating Attention as Both a Mathematical and Systems Problem

FlashAttention-3 also reinforces that attention must be understood in two ways at once. Mathematically, attention is a precise computation involving $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, a row-wise softmax, and multiplication by $\mathbf{V}$. Its forward and backward passes have exact formulas, and its numerics depend on details like subtracting the row maximum before exponentiation. These mathematical steps are not optional, because they are what make the computation stable and correct.

At the same time, the same attention computation is deeply shaped by systems concerns. On a GPU, the decisive questions are where intermediate values live, how data is moved between HBM, shared memory, and registers, and how the kernel is scheduled across warps and warpgroups. FlashAttention already showed that one can avoid materializing the large intermediate score and probability matrices in HBM. FlashAttention-3 extends that idea by overlapping stages of the exact computation without changing the result. The paper’s numerical results reflect this dual perspective: the implementation remains exact attention, but it is made practical only by matching the mathematics to the GPU execution model.

# Current Limitations for LLM Inference Workloads

The paper is explicit that some important work remains for LLM inference. One limitation is that the current FP8 implementation is not yet tuned as fully as the FP16 path for all inference settings. The authors note that they plan to optimize for LLM inference in future work, which signals that the present kernel is a strong step forward but not the final answer for deployment scenarios.

This matters because inference workloads are often sensitive to memory traffic, launch overheads, and differences in sequence length. The paper already makes an inference-driven design choice in its FP8 implementation by preferring an in-kernel transpose over a standalone preprocessing transpose, because a separate transpose kernel would be too wasteful in a memory-bound setting. That choice shows how closely the kernel design is tied to inference realities. At the same time, the benchmark results also show that there is still room to improve how the method behaves across the full range of inference settings, especially when sequence lengths are small or masking patterns reduce the amount of useful work.

# The Missing Persistent-Kernel Design in the FP8 Implementation

A particularly important limitation called out by the paper is that the FP8 benchmark path does not yet include the persistent-kernel design used in the FP16 benchmark. The authors explicitly note that FP16 FlashAttention-3 has a persistent kernel and load-balancing strategy for their benchmarks, while FP8 FlashAttention-3 does not. This is not a minor implementation detail; the paper says it partly explains why the FP8 path does not perform as well on small sequence lengths and with causal masking compared to the FP8 kernels in cuDNN.

The broader point is that low-precision computation alone does not guarantee the best end-to-end performance. A kernel still needs a scheduling strategy that fits the workload shape. The persistent-kernel omission highlights that FlashAttention-3’s FP8 pathway is still evolving, and that some of the best performance gains will likely come from combining low-precision arithmetic with equally careful load balancing and kernel persistence. In other words, the numerical format is only one part of the performance story; the work distribution across the GPU matters just as much.

# Why Causal Masking and Small Sequence Lengths Can Reduce FP8 Advantages

The paper’s benchmarks show that FP8 FlashAttention-3 is strongest in the large-sequence regime, while smaller sequences and causal masking reduce its relative advantage. This is consistent with the structure of the algorithm. When the sequence length is large, there is enough GEMM work to amortize the overhead of the FP8-specific layout transformations, in-kernel transpose, and register permutations. When the sequence is shorter, or when causal masking removes roughly half of the pairwise interactions, less work remains to cover those fixed costs.

The paper gives a concrete version of this effect in its comparison with cuDNN: for head dimension $64$, FP8 FlashAttention-3 is ahead, while for head dimensions $128$ and $256$ it is at par without causal masking and behind with causal masking. The authors connect this behavior to the lack of a persistent-kernel design in FP8 and to load balancing differences. The important lesson is that low precision helps most when the kernel can keep the hardware busy long enough for its benefits to dominate its coordination overheads. When the workload is smaller or more constrained, those overheads become more visible.

# Open Questions for Low-Precision Attention During Large-Scale Training

The paper is encouraging about FP8 accuracy, but it does not claim to settle the question of low-precision attention in large-scale training. To study numerical behavior, the authors compare FlashAttention-2, FlashAttention-3, and standard attention against an FP64 reference, using synthetic outlier-like inputs drawn from a distribution that includes occasional large-magnitude values. In that setting, FP16 FlashAttention-3 matches the numerical error of FlashAttention-2 and improves over standard attention because intermediate results are kept in FP32. For FP8, FlashAttention-3 with block quantization and incoherent processing is $2.6\times$ more accurate than standard attention with per-tensor quantization.

Even with those results, the paper explicitly says that understanding the effects of low-precision attention in large-scale training remains an open problem. That is a significant future direction because training and inference are not identical settings, and the paper only establishes one part of the story: that the proposed FP8 techniques reduce error on the tested cases. The results are therefore best read as evidence that low-precision attention can be made much safer, not as the final word on how such methods will behave across full training runs at scale.

# Portability Beyond Hopper

Although the paper is framed around NVIDIA Hopper, the authors emphasize that the ideas are not inherently limited to that architecture. Their abstract states that the method is operative for any GPU architecture with sufficiently robust asynchronous execution and low-precision capabilities. The conclusion also says that they expect the techniques to apply to other hardware accelerators. This matters because the paper’s core ideas are architectural rather than Hopper-specific in spirit.

The portability comes from the level at which the algorithm is redesigned. Producer-consumer asynchrony, overlapping softmax with GEMMs, and low-precision block quantization are all general strategies, even if the concrete mechanisms on Hopper are TMA, WGMMA, and FP8 Tensor Cores. As other accelerators develop similar support for asynchronous data movement and low-precision matrix multiplication, the same kinds of tile-based, pipeline-based attention kernels should remain relevant. The paper therefore points to a broader design pattern for future hardware, not just a one-off optimization for one generation of GPUs.

# How Asynchronous Execution and Low Precision May Shape Future Attention Kernels

One of the clearest forward-looking messages of the paper is that future attention kernels will likely be built around asynchronous execution. FlashAttention-3 shows that even within a single warpgroup, the softmax can be overlapped with GEMM work by moving intermediate values through registers and carefully scheduling WGMMA instructions. At a larger scale, pingpong scheduling lets one warpgroup perform softmax while another performs GEMMs. These are not small implementation details; they suggest a future in which attention kernels are explicitly structured as overlapping pipelines.

The paper also hints that this direction can be pushed further. It describes a 3-stage pipelining variant as a possible extension of the 2-stage design, although it comes with higher register pressure and harder trade-offs between tile size and pipeline depth. That trade-off is instructive: deeper pipelines may improve Tensor Core utilization, but they also consume more on-chip resources. Low precision will likely follow a similar path. The paper’s block quantization, incoherent processing, and layout transformations show that future attention kernels will not simply “use FP8,” but will instead integrate low precision only when the data layout and error control are carefully managed.

# Implications for Long-Context Modeling and Downstream Applications

The motivation for all of this work is larger than a faster kernel. The paper begins by noting that attention is a bottleneck because it scales quadratically with sequence length, and that improving long-context attention would unlock new capabilities. Those include reasoning over multiple long documents, working over large codebases, and supporting new modalities such as high-resolution images, audio, and video. The paper also points to user-interaction systems with long histories and agent workflows with long horizons.

FlashAttention-3 matters in that broader context because it pushes exact attention closer to the performance needed for these applications. By reducing the cost of long-context attention without changing the computation itself, it helps make longer sequences more practical for both research and deployment. The paper’s benchmarks show that the method can outperform FlashAttention-2 by $1.5$-$2.0\times$ in FP16 and approach $1.2$ PFLOPs/s in FP8, which is exactly the kind of efficiency gain that can make long-context modeling less constrained by hardware bottlenecks.

# Why Open Sourcing Matters

Finally, the paper’s decision to open-source FlashAttention-3 with a permissive license is itself an important part of the story. The authors say they plan to integrate it with PyTorch and Hugging Face so that researchers and developers can benefit from it. That matters because the contribution is not merely a theoretical algorithm; it is a set of concrete implementation techniques involving scheduling, layout transformations, and numerical handling that are most useful when they can be adopted directly in real systems.

Open sourcing also helps make the broader lessons usable. The paper’s ideas only have their full value if others can inspect, benchmark, and build on them in their own settings. Since the method is tightly connected to GPU execution details, releasing the implementation lowers the barrier for experimentation on future hardware and for adaptation to new attention variants. In that sense, the open-source release is not an afterthought. It is part of how the paper’s hardware-aware ideas can move from one high-end GPU implementation into the wider research and deployment ecosystem.
