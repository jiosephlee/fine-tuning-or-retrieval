\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Why is FeatLLM expected to work on synthetic datasets if Step-1 relies on prior knowledge from pretraining?
Question:
The paper emphasizes that the LLM first uses prior knowledge to infer relationships between features and the task, and this is supposed to be beneficial. But then in the experiments, two synthetic datasets are included specifically to show that the model is not just memorizing benchmark data.

I'm confused about how the method should succeed on synthetic tasks like classifying sequence types or mixture concentration if the LLM cannot rely on domain knowledge from pretraining in the same way it could for medical or financial datasets. Is the idea that the LLM's general reasoning ability is enough, even when task-specific prior knowledge is unavailable?

I would like to understand what part of FeatLLM is supposed to transfer to those synthetic settings.
Answer:
Yes — but the paper is using “prior knowledge” in a broader sense than “already knows the domain facts from pretraining.”

What FeatLLM needs in Step-1 is not necessarily medical or financial expertise; it needs the LLM to infer a plausible relationship between the feature descriptions, the example values, and the label. The paper explicitly says that Step-1 is done “without referring to example demonstrations, relying instead on general knowledge or common sense,” and that Step-2 then uses the few-shot examples plus that initial hypothesis to derive class rules. So the transferable part is the LLM’s ability to do weak rule induction, not memorized domain knowledge.

That is why the synthetic datasets still make sense. In those tasks, the useful structure is simple and explicit enough that the LLM can often reason about it from the task description and the few demonstrations even if it has never seen that exact benchmark before. For example, in the sequence-type task, the model does not need to “know” the dataset from pretraining; it only needs to recognize patterns like arithmetic growth, geometric growth, Fibonacci-like recurrence, or Collatz behavior from the serialized examples and class names. In the solution-mix task, it only needs to infer a threshold on a mixture proportion. Those are not domain-specific facts in the same way as “older patients are at higher risk” or “certain lab values indicate disease”; they are generic structural patterns that an LLM can sometimes reason about.

So the point of the synthetic datasets is not that FeatLLM depends on pretraining knowledge there, but almost the opposite: they check whether the method can still work when memorization of the benchmark is impossible. If FeatLLM performs well there, that suggests it is extracting and converting structure into rules, rather than recalling answers from training text.

The downstream mechanism is also important here. Even if Step-1 is only partially helpful on a synthetic task, Step-2 turns candidate rules into binary features, and the linear model learns which of those rules matter. So the method is not all-or-nothing: the LLM proposes rules, and the trained linear layer filters and weights them.

In short, what transfers to the synthetic setting is the reasoning-to-rule-extraction pipeline, not domain knowledge itself. The paper’s claim is that LLMs can act as general feature engineers; domain knowledge helps on real-world datasets, but the same rule-induction machinery can still be useful on synthetic ones where only generic reasoning is available.
