\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

# Can AI-Generated Rules Be Trusted? What FeatLLM Reveals About Interpretability and Robustness

Large language models are often used as black-box predictors: you give them a table, and they return a label. The FeatLLM paper takes a different path. Instead of asking the LLM to make the final prediction for every sample, it asks the model to generate rules that explain how a class should be identified. Those rules are then turned into features for a simple downstream classifier.

That shift matters. It makes the system cheaper at inference time, easier to inspect, and more robust in low-shot tabular learning. But it also raises an important question: if the rules come from an LLM, can we really trust them?

## From predictions to rules

FeatLLM treats the LLM as a feature engineer. Given a task description, feature descriptions, and a few labeled examples, the model is prompted in two stages. First, it is encouraged to infer the relationship between the features and the target using its prior knowledge. Second, it uses that understanding plus the examples to produce class-specific rules.

These rules are meant to be human-readable. In practice, they describe conditions on the input table, such as whether a feature crosses a threshold or falls into a certain category. The paper constrains the response format so the rules can be parsed automatically, then converts each rule into a binary feature: does the sample satisfy the rule or not?

That is the core interpretability move in FeatLLM. The model is no longer hiding its reasoning inside a huge neural network. Instead, it externalizes part of that reasoning as explicit criteria that can be read, checked, and reused.

## Why the rules are more than just decoration

Once the rules are generated, FeatLLM does not rely on them blindly. It fits a simple linear model on top of the binary rule features. The model learns non-negative weights, so rules that are useful for a class can increase that class’s score, while less useful ones can be downweighted.

This is an important design choice. The generated rules are not treated as perfect ground truth. They are treated as candidate signals, and the downstream model decides which ones matter more in the few-shot data. The paper’s analysis shows that many generated rules are informative even before tuning, and that tuning helps filter out weaker rules.

In other words, interpretability here is not just about being able to read the rules. It is also about being able to test them against data.

## Why ensembling helps

A single LLM prompt can produce useful rules, but it can also miss important conditions or include noisy ones. FeatLLM improves robustness by repeating the whole process multiple times and combining the results.

The paper uses two ideas here: ensembling and bagging. It randomizes the order of few-shot examples, sets a higher temperature for rule generation, and samples subsets of features or instances across trials. This creates diversity across rule sets. Then the predictions from multiple trials are averaged.

This matters for two reasons. First, if one trial generates a weak or flawed rule, other trials can compensate. The paper connects this to self-consistency: rules that appear repeatedly across trials are more likely to be reliable. Second, bagging helps with a practical limitation of LLMs, namely prompt length. When a dataset has many features, the full serialized table can become too long. Sampling subsets keeps the prompt manageable.

So robustness here is not just statistical; it is also operational.

## What the spurious-correlation experiment says

One of the most interesting parts of the paper is its test for spurious correlations. The authors take the Heart dataset and attach irrelevant columns from the Adult dataset, then gradually add more unrelated features. The goal is to see how well each method resists noise.

FeatLLM degrades the least under this setting. The paper attributes this to the way the LLM is guided to think about the relationship between the task and the features before extracting rules. That prior-knowledge step helps prevent rules from being built on irrelevant columns. In fact, the paper reports that rules based on noisy columns accounted for only 1-2% of the total.

That is a strong sign of robustness, especially in low-shot settings where models are prone to overfitting whatever signal they can find.

But the result is not a blanket guarantee. The paper also notes that FeatLLM can still learn spurious correlations and misunderstand causal relationships when the added columns come from closely related data. So the system is better at filtering obvious noise than at solving causality in the general sense.

## The trust problem: LLM prior knowledge cuts both ways

This is the key caveat. FeatLLM relies on the LLM’s prior knowledge to propose rules. That is exactly what makes it powerful in few-shot settings, but it is also where bias can enter.

If the LLM has an incorrect assumption about a domain, that assumption can shape the generated rules. Because those rules are then converted into features, the bias is not just in a textual explanation. It can directly affect the downstream classifier.

The paper explicitly flags this issue in its broader impact discussion. The prior knowledge embedded in the LLM may include societal biases or misinformation, and that knowledge can influence predictions, sometimes even more than the few labeled samples provided by the user.

So the answer to “can AI-generated rules be trusted?” is: only partially, and only with validation. FeatLLM makes the rules visible, which helps. But visibility is not the same as correctness.

## What FeatLLM changes in practice

FeatLLM is best understood as a compromise between LLM flexibility and traditional model reliability. It uses the LLM where it is strong: understanding language, using prior knowledge, and proposing candidate decision criteria. Then it hands off to a simpler model for prediction and weighting.

That design gives it several advantages. It avoids per-sample LLM inference at test time. It requires only API-level access, not fine-tuning. It also works better with long-feature tables than end-to-end LLM prediction approaches, because the feature engineering happens through repeated sampled prompts rather than one giant prompt for every test case.

Across 13 tabular datasets, the paper reports that FeatLLM consistently ranks at or near the top, outperforming alternatives such as TabLLM and STUNT by about 10% on average. Just as importantly for this discussion, it does so while making the learned signal more inspectable.

## The bottom line

FeatLLM shows that LLMs do not have to be used as opaque predictors. They can also act as rule generators, helping turn tabular problems into simpler, more interpretable feature-based models.

But the paper is careful not to oversell the trustworthiness of those rules. Ensembling and bagging make the system more robust, and the spurious-correlation experiments show real resilience to irrelevant features. Still, the generated rules inherit whatever prior knowledge the LLM brings to the task, including its blind spots.

That is the real lesson here: AI-generated rules can be useful, readable, and surprisingly robust, but they should be treated as learned hypotheses, not as ground truth.
