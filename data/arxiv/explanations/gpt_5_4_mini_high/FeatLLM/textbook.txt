\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

The Few-Shot Tabular Learning Challenge

# What Tabular Learning Is and Why It Matters

Tabular learning is the problem of making predictions from data organized in rows and columns, where each row is one example and each column is a feature. In the paper, the authors treat this as a classification problem: each sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ is described by $d$ input features, and the goal is to predict one of a predefined set of classes. What makes tabular learning especially practical is that so many real-world decisions are naturally recorded in this format. A patient can be represented by clinical measurements, a customer by banking history, or an applicant by financial and demographic attributes. These are not abstract examples; the paper explicitly points to tasks such as predicting whether someone earns over \$50{,}000, whether a customer subscribes to a term deposit, whether a donor will return, whether a person has diabetes, and whether a patient has chronic heart failure.

The paper emphasizes that tabular learning is central in domains like healthcare and finance because these domains often rely on structured records rather than unstructured text. In healthcare, the same paper notes that knowledge such as increased disease risk in older individuals can be genuinely helpful. In finance, information about credit risk or customer behavior can similarly matter. Unlike tasks where the signal is hidden in raw images or long passages of text, tabular prediction often depends on understanding how a relatively small number of named features relate to the target. That is why the paper places so much importance on feature names and feature definitions: they are part of the information needed to interpret the table correctly.

At the same time, the paper makes clear that tabular learning becomes difficult when the number of features is large. Some datasets used in the experiments contain more than 100 attributes, which already creates practical challenges for methods that need to serialize the table into text and send it to a language model. So tabular learning is both important and hard: the data are structured, but the structure alone does not make the prediction problem simple.

# The Few-Shot Setting: Limited Labels, High Variance, and Risk of Overfitting

The specific challenge addressed in the paper is few-shot tabular learning, where only a very small number of labeled examples are available. The paper describes this as sampling only $k$ labeled examples from a dataset of size $N$, with $k < N$, and then training the model using just those few examples. In the experiments, the paper studies low-shot settings such as 4-shot and 16-shot learning. The basic difficulty is intuitive: with only a handful of labeled rows, the model has very little evidence about which feature patterns really matter.

This scarcity of labels creates a fragile learning process. When the training set is tiny, the model can easily become overly sensitive to whichever examples happened to be sampled. That means the learned pattern may depend more on chance than on a stable relationship between the features and the target. The paper frames this as a risk of learning spurious correlations. In other words, the model may latch onto relationships that appear in a few training cases but do not actually reflect the true structure of the problem.

The paper also points out that few-shot learning is especially dangerous in tabular settings because the data may contain many columns, not all of which are relevant. If a model is forced to make sense of a large table from only a few labeled rows, it can start treating irrelevant columns as if they were meaningful. This is one reason the paper repeatedly emphasizes low-shot regimes: the difficulty is not just that there are few examples, but that the examples are too few to reliably separate useful patterns from accidental ones.

# Why Standard Supervised Tabular Models Struggle with Very Small Training Sets

The paper compares its method with conventional tabular learners such as logistic regression, XGBoost, and RandomForest. These are strong standard baselines, but the paper’s motivation is that they are not naturally tailored to extremely small labeled datasets. When training data are scarce, these models have limited evidence for estimating which features matter and how they should be combined. As a result, they can overfit or fail to generalize well.

This problem is especially severe when the dataset is heterogeneous. The paper notes that tabular data vary widely in type and structure, which makes it hard to design one universally effective augmentation or pretraining strategy. In a low-shot regime, a conventional supervised learner does not have the luxury of seeing enough examples to learn robust rules on its own. The paper explicitly connects this to the tendency of few-shot methods to learn spurious correlations, which is exactly what one wants to avoid in high-stakes applications.

The paper also makes an important point about tabular learning methods that rely on unlabeled data or pretraining. Some approaches attempt to improve performance by using additional unlabeled datasets, reconstruction objectives, or transfer learning across many tabular datasets. These can help, but they also assume the availability of extra data sources or specialized pretraining pipelines. The paper’s broader argument is that, in genuinely small-data situations, the central problem is not only model capacity. It is the lack of reliable prior information. Without enough labels, a standard supervised model has little basis for deciding which columns are informative and which are noise.

# The Value of Prior Knowledge When Labeled Data Are Scarce

The paper argues that when labels are scarce, prior knowledge becomes especially valuable. Prior knowledge can act as an inductive bias, helping a model focus on patterns that make sense rather than patterns that merely happen to appear in a few samples. In human terms, this is the difference between guessing blindly from a tiny set of observations and reasoning with some background understanding of the task.

This is exactly why the paper repeatedly uses healthcare and finance as motivating domains. In those settings, there are often known relationships that can guide prediction. The paper gives the example that older individuals may have increased risk for certain diseases. In finance, one can imagine that certain combinations of attributes are more informative than others for predicting credit risk or customer behavior. The key point is not that the model should memorize domain rules by hand, but that it should be able to exploit the kinds of background knowledge that experts would normally bring to the task.

The paper sees large language models as a possible source of this prior knowledge. Because they are trained on extensive text corpora, they may have absorbed broad information about the world, including domain-relevant patterns. The authors argue that this is particularly useful in a low-shot regime because the model can supplement the tiny labeled dataset with knowledge that was learned before the task even began. In this sense, the paper treats prior knowledge not as a luxury, but as a practical necessity for reliable tabular prediction when supervision is limited.

# How Large Language Models Encode Broad World Knowledge

The paper’s motivation for using large language models is that they have already been trained on large and diverse text corpora, which gives them a rich store of prior knowledge. This prior knowledge can help them handle tasks they have not been explicitly fine-tuned for. The paper cites the general observation that LLMs can generalize to unseen tasks by taking a task description and a few examples in the prompt, then using that context to reason about the problem. This is the basic idea behind in-context learning.

For tabular tasks, the paper describes a common strategy: convert the table into text, include feature descriptions and task descriptions, and then feed this serialized prompt into the LLM. The model can then use both the textual prompt and its background knowledge to infer relationships between features and the target label. In the paper’s view, this is promising because the LLM is no longer just processing arbitrary rows and columns; it is being asked to interpret them in light of natural-language descriptions.

But the paper also makes clear that this approach has limitations. Existing LLM-based tabular methods often require one inference through the LLM per sample at test time, which makes them expensive. Some approaches also require parameter-efficient fine-tuning, which is not always possible when one only has API-level access to a proprietary model. Another issue is prompt length: when a table has many features, serializing the data can create very long prompts, and performance may drop or the method may become infeasible. So although LLMs bring valuable world knowledge, the standard way of using them still leaves important practical problems unsolved.

# The Paper’s Core Question: Can LLMs Act as Feature Engineers Instead of End-to-End Predictors?

This is the central question motivating the paper. Instead of treating the LLM as a black-box classifier that must produce a final prediction for every sample, the authors ask whether the LLM can be used more like a feature engineer. In other words, can the model identify the criteria that matter for a class and turn those criteria into reusable features, rather than directly outputting the label each time?

The intuition is important. If an LLM can infer a rule such as “this class is likely when certain conditions hold,” then that rule can be applied to data as a feature. Once the useful rules have been extracted, the final prediction does not need to go through the LLM again. A simpler downstream model can use the generated features to estimate class likelihood. This shifts the LLM’s job from prediction to knowledge extraction.

The paper presents this shift as a way to solve several of the problems discussed above. First, it reduces inference cost because the LLM does not need to be queried for every test example. Second, it requires only API-level access, rather than training or tuning access to the LLM itself. Third, it helps address prompt size limitations because the final classifier no longer needs to send the full table through the LLM at inference time. Most importantly, it reframes the LLM’s value: the model is not just a predictor, but a source of task-relevant rules distilled from its prior knowledge and a few labeled examples.

That is the core idea behind the paper’s opening motivation. The challenge is not simply to make language models read tables. It is to find a way for them to contribute their broad prior knowledge in a form that is practical for few-shot tabular learning. The paper’s answer is to use them as feature engineers.

2. Background: Existing Approaches to Few-Shot Tabular Learning

# Conventional tabular baselines: logistic regression, random forests, and gradient-boosted trees

A natural starting point for few-shot tabular learning is the family of standard supervised models that have long been used on structured data. In the paper, these include logistic regression, random forests, and XGBoost, which represents gradient-boosted trees. These methods are familiar because they are simple to train, easy to deploy, and often strong on tabular problems when enough labeled data are available. In the few-shot setting, however, the learner only sees a small labeled subset of the data, so the central question is whether these conventional models can still generalize well when there are only $k$ labeled examples rather than a large training set.

The paper treats these models as important baselines because they do not rely on any external language model or special pretraining strategy. They learn directly from the labeled tabular samples, which makes them conceptually straightforward. At the same time, this simplicity also reveals their weakness in low-data regimes: with only a handful of examples, they may not recover the deeper patterns that connect the input features to the target label. The paper emphasizes that few-shot tabular tasks are especially vulnerable to spurious correlations, so a baseline that only fits the observed labels can easily miss the underlying structure. This is why conventional tabular methods remain relevant as reference points, but are often not enough on their own when labels are scarce.

# Semi-supervised and self-supervised methods for tabular data

Because labeled data can be expensive, one major line of work tries to compensate by exploiting unlabeled data. The paper describes semi-supervised approaches that use large quantities of unlabeled samples together with carefully designed augmentation procedures, as well as self-supervised approaches that pretrain models by corrupting or masking part of the data and then asking the model to reconstruct it. The intuition is that even without labels, the model can still learn useful representations of the data distribution, and those representations may transfer to the downstream prediction task.

Several methods in this family are mentioned in the paper as examples of prior work on tabular learning. Some methods use unlabeled datasets to improve training, while others rely on reconstruction objectives or contrastive learning objectives to pretrain a representation before the few-shot task begins. The paper also notes that such strategies can provide useful inductive biases, especially when the labeled set is very small. In principle, this reduces the chance that the model will simply memorize a few training examples or lock onto accidental correlations.

However, the paper also points out that these methods do not fully solve the few-shot problem. If the available unlabeled data are limited, mismatched, or hard to augment in a useful way, the benefit can be modest. Likewise, if the representation learned during pretraining does not align well with the target task, the model may not transfer effectively. This is one reason that self-supervised and semi-supervised tabular methods, while promising, have not become universally effective solutions for low-shot tabular prediction.

# Why augmentation and pretraining are harder for heterogeneous tabular features than for images or text

A key difficulty for tabular learning is that tables are heterogeneous. Unlike images, where pixels share a common structure, or text, where tokens belong to a sequential language format, tabular features can differ sharply from one another in type and meaning. Some columns are numeric, some are categorical, and others may have specialized definitions that only make sense in a particular domain. The paper stresses that this heterogeneity makes it difficult to design augmentations that work well across datasets.

This matters because many self-supervised methods depend on augmentations or corruption schemes. The challenge is that there is no universally good way to perturb table entries without potentially damaging the semantics of the data. A transformation that is harmless in one column may be meaningless or even misleading in another. The paper therefore argues that methods that rely on general-purpose augmentations, which are often effective in other domains, are much less reliable for tabular data. In few-shot settings, where every labeled example is already precious, poor augmentations can be especially harmful because they may obscure the true structure rather than reveal it.

The paper also suggests that the diversity of tabular domains makes pretraining difficult to standardize. Tabular datasets can come from healthcare, finance, biology, or synthetic tasks, and each domain may encode different kinds of feature relationships. That means a pretraining objective that works well on one collection of tables may not translate well to another. In short, tabular learning is not just a smaller-data version of image or text learning; its feature structure is fundamentally less uniform, and that makes generic augmentation and pretraining strategies harder to apply.

# Transfer learning for tables: TransTab, TabPFN, and related ideas

Another response to data scarcity is transfer learning. Instead of learning only from the target task, these methods train on many tabular datasets first and then transfer what they learned to a new task. The paper describes this family as using a broad range of tables beyond the target dataset, with the goal of learning priors that can support zero-shot or few-shot inference on new problems. This idea is appealing because the model can accumulate experience across tasks, much like a human who has seen many tables before encountering a new one.

The paper highlights TransTab as one example. TransTab uses transformer-based models and learns semantic relationships between columns through column names, not just through the raw table values. This is important because column names can carry meaningful hints about what each feature represents. By leveraging both the names and the values, the model can build a richer understanding of the table structure. The paper notes that knowledge learned from many different tables and columns can then help on unseen tasks, especially in zero-shot or few-shot regimes.

TabPFN is another representative approach. According to the paper, it mixes different types of distributions to create synthetic data for pretraining a Bayesian neural network. After this pretraining, inference on a target task is performed by providing both the training and test samples, without any additional task-specific training. The paper presents this as a strong example of how broad prior knowledge can improve few-shot performance. In contrast to methods that learn only from the current target dataset, TabPFN tries to bake in knowledge about many possible data distributions ahead of time.

# Language-interfaced tabular learning through serialization of tables into text

The paper next turns to a different strategy: using large language models as a bridge between language and tables. The basic idea is to serialize tabular data into text and then place that text into an LLM prompt. In this setup, the model receives not only the table values but also feature descriptions and a task description written in natural language. This allows the LLM to use its prior knowledge about the world and about the task context when making predictions. The paper emphasizes that this approach is attractive because LLMs are trained on extensive and diverse text corpora, so they may already know useful facts that are not present in the few labeled samples.

This interface between tables and language is central to recent LLM-based tabular methods. A table row can be rewritten as a natural-language description such as “feature $f_1$ is value $x_1$” and so on, followed by the answer label. The paper notes that earlier work adopts precisely this kind of serialization so that the LLM can process structured data as text. Once the table has been converted into language, the model can reason over feature names, feature definitions, and the task prompt in a way that resembles ordinary question answering.

At the same time, this approach changes the role of the LLM. Rather than being used only as a generic text generator, the model is asked to interpret tabular structure and infer how features relate to the target label. The paper frames this as an effort to bring prior knowledge into tabular learning, especially when the labeled data are scarce. But the cost of using language as the interface is that prediction now depends heavily on the quality of the prompt and on the LLM’s ability to process long serialized tables.

# In-context learning for tabular tasks

A particularly important variant of language-interfaced tabular learning is in-context learning. In this approach, the model is not fine-tuned at all. Instead, a few labeled examples are placed directly into the prompt, and the LLM is expected to infer the pattern from those demonstrations. The paper describes this as embedding few-shot training samples into the input prompt without tuning. The idea is that the examples provide enough context for the LLM to infer the task structure and then answer a new query.

The appeal of in-context learning is that it avoids the need for task-specific parameter updates. If the LLM already has strong general reasoning ability, then perhaps it can use the prompt examples as a temporary training set. In tabular problems, this means the model can inspect a small number of rows, note the feature names and values, and attempt to infer which patterns distinguish the classes. The paper positions this as one of the first ways to bring LLM reasoning into low-shot tabular prediction without explicit retraining.

But in-context learning also inherits the practical limitations of prompt-based inference. The model must process all of the relevant information every time it makes a prediction, and the prompt must remain within the model’s context window. As the number of features grows, the serialized table becomes longer, and the task becomes harder to fit into the prompt. This is one reason that, despite its elegance, in-context learning is not always a practical solution for large tabular datasets.

# Parameter-efficient tuning approaches such as LoRA and IA3

Another major direction is to fine-tune the LLM, but in a way that changes only a small part of the model. The paper mentions parameter-efficient tuning methods such as LoRA and IA3. These methods aim to make adaptation cheaper and more feasible than full fine-tuning by limiting the number of trainable parameters. In the tabular setting, the hope is that a pretrained LLM can be adapted to a specific task using only a few labeled examples and a lightweight tuning mechanism.

This strategy is attractive because it allows the model to retain much of its general knowledge while still specializing to the target dataset. The paper treats this as one of the main pathways by which LLMs have been applied to tabular learning. Instead of training a large model from scratch, the system can be adjusted in a controlled way to better reflect the target task. That makes parameter-efficient tuning a natural compromise between using a frozen model and fully retraining a large one.

Still, the paper notes that tuning is not always possible in practice. Some large models are available only through inference APIs, which means users cannot access their training code or modify their parameters. In those cases, even parameter-efficient methods may be infeasible. This practical constraint is a major reason the paper seeks a method that can work with API-only access and no model training.

# Representative LLM-tabular systems: TabLLM, TABLET, and related methods

The paper discusses several representative systems that combine LLMs with tabular learning. TabLLM is one such example. It uses parameter-efficient tuning, specifically a method like IA3, to train the LLM on few-shot tabular data. The paper notes that this approach depends on publicly available checkpoints, which makes it less flexible than methods that can work with any API-accessible LLM. TabLLM is therefore a useful example of the power of LLM-based tabular learning, but also of its dependence on model access.

TABLET is another representative system. According to the paper, TABLET adds additional hints to the prompt by using an external classifier together with rule sets and prototypes. This means the LLM is not asked to work entirely from raw serialized data; instead, the method supplies extra structure intended to improve inference. The general idea is that the prompt can be strengthened by including information that helps the LLM focus on the most relevant aspects of the task.

These methods show that the field has moved beyond simple prompt formatting. Researchers have tried tuning, prompt augmentation, and external hints to make LLMs better at tabular prediction. The paper places FeatLLM in this landscape as a different idea: rather than using the LLM to produce the final prediction for every sample, it asks the LLM to extract useful rules that can later be used by a simpler downstream model. That is a shift from end-to-end prediction toward feature engineering.

# Key limitations of prior LLM-based methods: per-sample inference cost, fine-tuning requirements, and prompt length constraints

The paper argues that the main weakness of existing LLM-based tabular systems is not just accuracy, but practicality. First, many methods require at least one LLM inference for every test sample. That means the model must repeatedly process the serialized table and make a fresh prediction each time. This is expensive, especially when the number of samples is large. Even if the LLM is accurate, the inference cost can become a serious obstacle in real applications.

Second, many methods require fine-tuning. This is a problem when the LLM is not openly trainable or when the training code is not accessible. The paper stresses that recent high-performance LLMs often allow only inference via API, so methods that assume direct parameter updates cannot always be used. This creates a gap between what is technically possible in research settings and what is actually available in deployment settings.

Third, prompt length is a fundamental constraint. Tabular datasets can contain many attributes, and when each feature must be serialized into natural language, the prompt can become very long. The paper notes that this becomes especially problematic for datasets with over $100$ attributes. Once the prompt exceeds the available context window, the method may become infeasible or suffer a drop in performance. This limitation is one of the clearest reasons the paper seeks a different strategy: instead of asking the LLM to reason over the entire table for every prediction, FeatLLM uses the LLM to extract rules once, then hands the resulting features to a simple downstream model.

Together, these limitations explain the motivation for FeatLLM. Earlier methods showed that LLMs can help on tabular tasks, but they often did so at the cost of heavy inference, tuning requirements, or prompt-size fragility. The background of the field, then, is a progression from conventional tabular baselines, to unlabeled-data methods, to transfer learning, and finally to LLM-based prompting and tuning. FeatLLM builds on that history by keeping the language model’s prior knowledge while moving the final prediction step into a lighter and more practical tabular pipeline.

3. The Core Idea of FeatLLM

# From black-box prediction to explicit feature engineering

The central shift in FeatLLM is that the large language model is no longer asked to solve the entire tabular classification problem directly, sample by sample. In earlier language-interfaced tabular methods, the LLM receives a serialized table row and is expected to output the class. FeatLLM changes that role: instead of being the final predictor, the LLM becomes a feature engineer that searches for reusable criteria underlying the task. Those criteria are then turned into features that a simple downstream model can use.

This matters because the outputs of a tabular task often depend on patterns that can be phrased as rules. The paper describes the LLM as inferring the “criteria” behind a class prediction, such as which combinations of feature conditions indicate a positive outcome. Once such criteria are found, they can be applied across many samples, so the knowledge extracted from the LLM is not consumed once and discarded. It becomes a stable representation that can support prediction for the whole dataset.

# Using LLMs to infer criteria or rules associated with each class

FeatLLM does not ask the LLM to invent rules arbitrarily. Instead, it structures the prompt so the model first understands the task, then uses that understanding to derive class-specific rules. The prompt has three parts: basic information description, reasoning instruction, and response instruction. The basic information includes the task description, feature descriptions, and a few serialized training examples. The task is phrased as a natural-language question, and the feature descriptions can include value types, definitions, and examples of categorical values.

The reasoning instruction is designed to mimic how a human expert might approach the problem. In the first step, the LLM is encouraged to infer the relationship, tendency, or causal connection between the task and the features without looking at the example demonstrations. This is meant to activate the model’s prior knowledge and common sense. In the second step, the LLM uses that prior understanding together with the few examples to generate decisive rules for each class. The paper fixes the number of rules per class to 10, which helps prevent the prompt from becoming too small to be useful or too large to fit comfortably. The response instruction then forces the output into a format that is easier to parse later, and the model is guided to pay attention to feature types so that the rules do not mismatch the data type of a column.

# Why rule discovery can be cheaper than end-to-end LLM inference at test time

The main cost savings come from moving the expensive part of reasoning away from test-time prediction. In an end-to-end LLM approach, every new sample must be sent through the LLM, which means one inference call per sample. FeatLLM instead spends LLM calls upfront to discover rules, and then reuses those rules for all later predictions. Once the rules are extracted, the LLM is no longer needed to classify each test row.

This design makes the cost more like feature construction than repeated inference. After the rules are obtained, the actual prediction step is done by a simple model operating on binary rule features. Because this model is lightweight, the test-time computation is much smaller than repeatedly querying a language model. The paper emphasizes that this is especially important in practical settings where many samples must be classified, because the reusable rules amortize the cost of the LLM over the entire dataset.

# Separation of responsibilities: LLM for knowledge extraction, simple model for prediction

FeatLLM deliberately separates what each component is responsible for. The LLM is used for extracting candidate rules from the few-shot prompt, drawing on its prior knowledge and the task description. The downstream model is then responsible for deciding which of those rules matter and how much they should contribute to each class prediction. This division avoids forcing the LLM to perform every part of the decision process on its own.

To make this work, each rule becomes a binary feature indicating whether a sample satisfies that rule. If a sample matches a rule for a given class, the corresponding feature is set to $1$; otherwise it is $0$. For sample $\mathbf{x}^i$, the generated binary features for class $k$ are denoted $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$, where $R$ is the number of rules per class. The model then uses a linear form to compute class logits:
\[
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,
\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1^i, \ldots, \text{logit}_c^i]).
\]
The weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ are trained from the few labeled examples using cross-entropy loss. By projecting the weights into the non-negative region, the method ensures that a generated feature cannot reduce the likelihood of a class during learning. In effect, the LLM proposes possible decision rules, and the simple model learns which of those rules should actually influence the final probability.

# How this design enables API-only usage without model fine-tuning

A key practical advantage of FeatLLM is that it does not require fine-tuning the LLM itself. The method relies on in-context learning: the prompt contains the task description and example demonstrations, and the LLM uses that context to generate rules. There is no need to update the LLM’s parameters, and no training code for the language model is required.

This is important because the paper explicitly notes that many strong LLMs are only available through inference APIs, not through accessible training or tuning interfaces. FeatLLM fits that constraint naturally. It only needs API-level access to the LLM, which makes it usable even when the model cannot be fine-tuned with methods such as LoRA or IA3. The only trainable part is the simple downstream classifier, so the method remains compatible with restricted-access language models.

# How the approach reduces inference latency and avoids repeated LLM queries for every sample

The reduction in inference latency follows directly from the separation between rule discovery and prediction. Traditional language-model-based tabular methods have to send each sample through the LLM, which is slow and expensive. In FeatLLM, once the rule set has been generated and converted into features, inference is performed by the lightweight downstream model alone. That means the costly LLM step is not repeated for every test row.

The paper reports that this leads to inference times comparable to conventional tabular methods rather than to other LLM-based approaches. Because the final predictor is a simple linear model over binary features, it can operate quickly once the features are computed. The architecture therefore turns the LLM into a one-time knowledge extractor rather than a per-sample decision engine, which is the main reason the method can be much faster at test time.

# How bagging helps address long prompts and large feature sets

FeatLLM uses bagging to handle two related challenges: prompt-size limits and robustness. Tabular data can contain many columns, and serializing all of them into text can make the prompt very long. That creates problems for LLM context windows. Bagging helps by allowing the method to work on subsets of features or instances in each trial, reducing the amount of text that must fit into one prompt.

The ensemble process also increases diversity across trials. The paper introduces randomness by using a sufficiently high temperature during rule generation and by randomizing the order of the few-shot demonstrations, since prompt order can influence the LLM’s output. Multiple trials are then combined into an ensemble. If some trials produce incorrect rules, other trials may produce correct ones that compensate. This is closely related to the paper’s discussion of self-consistency: rules that recur across multiple trials are more likely to be reliable. Bagging therefore serves both a computational purpose and a statistical one. It lets FeatLLM stay within prompt limits while making the final predictions more stable.

# Overview of the full FeatLLM pipeline from prompts to final ensemble predictions

The full pipeline begins with a labeled tabular dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^N$, together with feature names and their natural-language descriptions. For a few-shot setting, only $k$ labeled examples are sampled for training. These examples are serialized into text in the format used by earlier tabular LLM work, and they are placed in a prompt that also includes task information and the reasoning and response instructions. The LLM first reflects on the relationship between the task and the features, then produces a fixed number of rules for each class.

Those natural-language rules are not used directly. Instead, the paper feeds the rules back into the LLM with an instruction to generate simple code that can parse the rules into executable form. The resulting code is run with Python’s `exec()` function, producing binary features that indicate whether each sample satisfies each rule. A low-complexity classifier, such as the linear model described earlier, is trained on those features using the few-shot labels. This whole process is repeated multiple times with bagging, feature or instance subsampling, randomized example order, and prompt-level randomness. Finally, the predicted class probabilities from all trials are averaged to produce the ensemble prediction. In this way, FeatLLM transforms an LLM’s prior knowledge into reusable feature rules and then lets a simple model carry out the final classification.

4. Problem Formulation and Learning Setting

# Formal Definition of the Labeled Tabular Dataset

FeatLLM is built for supervised tabular classification. The starting point is a labeled tabular dataset written as $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, where each pair contains one sample and its label. The notation makes two things explicit. First, there are $N$ total labeled examples available in the full dataset. Second, each sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ is a vector of $d$ input features, so the tabular data contain $d$ columns or attributes for every row. The label $\mathbf{y}^i$ is the target the model must predict.

This formulation matters because FeatLLM does not treat a table merely as a collection of numbers or categories. It treats the table as a supervised learning problem in which each sample has a known target class, and the method aims to learn from only a small portion of the available labeled data. The paper is explicit that the task is classification, and the label belongs to a predefined set of classes. In other words, the method is not designed for continuous prediction in this paper, but for deciding which class a sample should be assigned to.

The dataset abstraction is also important because it separates the raw sample values from the information needed to interpret them. In FeatLLM, the LLM is not asked to operate on bare values alone. Instead, the table is paired with natural language descriptions of the features, which gives the model a way to reason about what each column means.

# Input Features, Feature Names, and Feature Descriptions in Natural Language

Every feature in the tabular dataset has a natural language name, denoted by $F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^{d}$. Each $f_j$ is the name or identifier of the $j$th feature. The paper also assumes that feature definitions are provided separately. This means the model is not only given the raw value of a feature, but can also be given a textual explanation of what that feature represents.

The paper further clarifies that the feature description can include the value type and, optionally, the definition of the feature. For categorical variables, examples of possible categories can also be provided. These details are not decorative; they are part of the input that makes language-based reasoning possible. A feature name alone may be too ambiguous to guide a model, especially if the same word could refer to different domains or if the values are not self-explanatory. By contrast, a name together with a type and a definition gives the LLM the semantic context needed to interpret the feature correctly.

This is one of the key reasons FeatLLM can function as a feature engineer. The method expects the LLM to infer relationships between the task and the input features using a prompt that includes the task description, the feature descriptions, and a few labeled examples. Because the features are described in natural language, the LLM can use its prior knowledge about what the features mean and how they may relate to the target label. That is a very different setup from treating the table as uninterpreted values.

# Classification as the Target Setting, Including Binary and Multiclass Tasks

The learning setting in FeatLLM is classification. The paper states that the label $\mathbf{y}^i$ is one of a predefined set of classes, which means the method is designed to decide among discrete outcomes rather than predict a continuous value. This includes both binary tasks, where there are two possible classes, and multiclass tasks, where there are more than two.

This distinction matters because the downstream reasoning in FeatLLM is organized around class-specific rules. The method does not simply ask whether a sample belongs to some abstract target. Instead, it aims to derive rules that help separate one class from another. In the methods section, the generated rules are associated with each class, and the final prediction is based on estimating the likelihood of each class. That design is naturally aligned with classification, where the central question is which label best matches the sample.

The experimental evaluation reflects this setting. The paper reports results across binary and multiclass tabular datasets from multiple domains. So while the formalism is general, it is always anchored in a discrete-label problem. This is important for understanding the rest of the framework, because the rules extracted by the LLM are not meant to describe the table in a generic way; they are meant to support class discrimination.

# The $k$-Shot Protocol and How Training Examples Are Sampled

FeatLLM is designed for the low-shot regime. The paper defines the $k$-shot setting as follows: from the full dataset of $N$ labeled samples, only $k$ labeled samples, where $k < N$, are randomly sampled to train the model. In practice, this means the learning algorithm must operate with very limited labeled data.

The low-shot setting is central to the motivation of the paper. In many tabular problems, collecting labels is expensive or difficult, so the method must make strong use of the small number of examples it is given. FeatLLM responds to this by using those few examples in two ways at once. They serve as training data for the downstream model, and they also serve as demonstrations inside the prompt that is shown to the LLM. This dual use of the same few labeled samples is a defining part of the framework.

Because only a small number of examples is available, sampling matters. The paper specifies that the labeled samples are randomly chosen from the dataset to form the $k$-shot training set. This random choice reflects the practical reality of low-shot learning: the method must be able to function from whatever limited labeled subset is available, while still trying to generalize to the remaining samples. The authors also note that some experiments use very small shot counts, such as 4-shot or 16-shot settings, to test the method in especially data-scarce conditions.

The low-shot setup also explains why the paper emphasizes prior knowledge. When labels are scarce, the model cannot rely only on statistical pattern learning from the data. It must also exploit the semantic information in the feature names and descriptions, as well as the reasoning ability of the LLM. The problem formulation therefore makes the few-shot regime not just an experimental choice, but a core assumption of the method.

# The Importance of Column Names, Types, and Definitions for Language-Based Reasoning

The paper places substantial emphasis on the role of feature metadata in enabling language-based reasoning. Column names, value types, and definitions are not treated as optional extras. They are necessary for turning a table into a task that an LLM can reason about meaningfully.

The reason is straightforward: the LLM is asked to infer relationships between features and labels, and to generate class-specific rules. Without names and definitions, the model would only see raw serialized values, which would make it much harder to infer what the variables mean. The feature type is especially important because it helps prevent type mismatches when the LLM formulates rules. For example, a rule for a numerical feature should be expressed differently from a rule for a categorical feature, and the prompt is designed to remind the LLM of these distinctions.

The paper also explains that the first stage of the prompting procedure asks the LLM to infer the causal relationship or tendency between the features and the task description without referring to the examples. This is meant to activate the model's prior knowledge and common sense about the domain. Then, in the second stage, the LLM uses the demonstrations together with the earlier reasoning to derive rules. That two-step structure only works because the feature metadata are available to anchor the language reasoning in the actual structure of the table.

The feature descriptions therefore serve a dual role. They help the LLM interpret the table, and they help it avoid getting distracted by irrelevant columns. The paper argues that this structured reasoning helps the model focus on more significant features and reduce the risk of spurious correlations. In a low-shot setting, that kind of guidance is especially important because the training data alone may not be sufficient to identify the right patterns.

# The Distinction Between Raw Tabular Inputs and Generated Rule-Based Features

A central idea in FeatLLM is that the raw tabular inputs and the final features used for prediction are not the same thing. The original data are the input samples $\mathbf{x}^i$ with their $d$ features. These raw values are used by the LLM to generate rules. The rules are then parsed and converted into new binary features.

In the paper, each generated binary feature indicates whether a sample satisfies a rule. For a given class, the sample is mapped to a binary vector of rule-based features. The notation used in the methods section is $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$, where $R$ is the number of rules per class. Each entry in this vector tells us whether the sample satisfies one of the extracted rules. This means the model is no longer working directly with the original table in the downstream classifier. Instead, it works with a transformed representation built from rules discovered by the LLM.

This distinction is essential to understanding the learning setting. The raw tabular input is the source of information, but the generated rule-based features are the representation that the final predictive model uses. FeatLLM therefore shifts part of the learning problem into feature construction. The LLM acts as a rule generator, and the downstream model acts on the resulting binary indicators.

The paper's formulation shows why this is beneficial in the few-shot setting. Rather than requiring the LLM to make a prediction for every sample at inference time, FeatLLM extracts rules once and then uses those rules as features for a simple classifier. So the raw table is transformed into a new feature space that is better suited for learning from few examples. This separation between original inputs and rule-based features is the core of the method's formulation and the key to its efficiency.

5. Prompting an LLM to Extract Predictive Rules

# Why prompt design matters for reliable rule extraction

In \textsf{FeatLLM}, the language model is not being asked to make the final prediction for every sample directly. Instead, it is being asked to act like a feature engineer: it must discover rules that capture how feature conditions relate to each class. Those rules are then turned into binary features and passed to a simple downstream classifier. That means the prompt is doing much more than “asking a question.” It is shaping the entire representation that the rest of the system will use.

This is why prompt design is central to the method. If the prompt does not give the model enough context, the model may miss important relationships between features and labels. If it is too unconstrained, the model may produce rules that are hard to parse, too long, or based on irrelevant columns. The paper’s prompting strategy is designed to support two goals at once: first, to encourage the model to reason about the task in a meaningful way; and second, to make the resulting rules usable as programmatic features. In that sense, the prompt is not just a communication channel. It is the mechanism that turns the LLM’s prior knowledge into something the downstream model can actually use.

# The three parts of the prompt

The prompt in \textsf{FeatLLM} is organized into three main parts: basic information description, reasoning instruction, and response instruction. This structure is intended to mimic how an expert human might approach a tabular classification problem. A person solving such a task would first inspect the task and the meaning of the columns, then think through how the features might relate to the target, and finally state the decision rule clearly. The prompt mirrors that process.

The basic information description gives the model the raw context it needs to understand the dataset. The reasoning instruction tells it how to think through the task in two steps. The response instruction tells it how to express the result so that the extracted rules can later be parsed automatically. Together, these three parts make the LLM’s output both more reliable and more useful.

# Basic information: task descriptions, feature descriptions, and few-shot demonstrations

The first part of the prompt supplies the information needed to understand the problem itself. This includes a task description, feature descriptions, and example demonstrations.

The task description is written as a natural-language question. The paper gives an example such as, “Does this patient’s myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?” Framing the task this way helps the model connect the columns to the prediction goal in a form it can easily interpret. Instead of seeing an abstract label, the model sees a concrete question about the class being predicted.

Feature descriptions are also included. These descriptions indicate the value type of each feature and may additionally provide a definition. For categorical variables, the prompt can include examples of the possible categories. This is important because the model needs to know not only what a feature is called, but also what kind of values it can take. The paper explicitly notes that these descriptions help avoid type mismatches when the model later writes rules. For example, if a column is numeric, the resulting rule should be expressed differently than for a categorical feature. Supplying the feature type gives the model a better chance of producing rules that match the actual data structure.

The prompt also includes few-shot demonstrations. These are training samples serialized into text together with their ground-truth labels. The idea is to show the model examples of the task in the same format in which the data are presented. This gives the LLM concrete evidence about how feature values relate to labels, while still leaving room for the model’s prior knowledge to generalize beyond the examples.

# Serializing tabular rows into text

A central part of the prompt design is the serialization of each tabular row into a sentence-like text format. The paper uses a template adopted from prior work, written as

\[
\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i,F) = ``\ f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ ...\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i\ "
\]

Here, each feature name $f_j$ is paired with the corresponding value $\mathbf{x}^i_j$, and the label $\mathbf{y}^i$ appears at the end as the answer. This format preserves the identity of each feature while translating the row into natural language.

The intuition behind this serialization is straightforward. LLMs are trained on text, so presenting tabular data as text makes it easier for them to process. At the same time, the clause-by-clause structure keeps the important tabular relationships intact. Each demonstration becomes a small example of how the input features are described and how they map to a class label. In \textsf{FeatLLM}, these demonstrations are not just for prediction; they are also used as evidence when the model later derives rules.

# Two-step reasoning for class-specific rule generation

The reasoning instruction is the core of the prompt’s deliberate structure. The paper does not simply ask the model to generate rules immediately. Instead, it divides the process into two steps and introduces this with a sentence similar in spirit to chain-of-thought prompting. The goal is to encourage the model to reason through the task rather than jump directly to an answer.

In the first step, the model is asked to infer the causal relationship or tendency between features and the task description. Importantly, this step is done without referring to the example demonstrations. The paper explains that this allows the model to rely on general knowledge or common sense and fully extract its prior knowledge about the problem. In other words, the model first thinks about what kinds of feature patterns would matter in principle.

In the second step, the model uses the example demonstrations together with the understanding developed in the first step to derive rules for each class. This is where the prompt moves from general understanding to class-specific decision criteria. The paper’s point is that this two-stage process helps the model focus on meaningful features rather than simply matching superficial patterns in the examples. By separating prior knowledge from example-based rule derivation, the prompt encourages a more disciplined reasoning path.

# Reducing spurious correlations and setting a fixed number of rules

One of the main reasons for the two-step design is to reduce the chance that the model will latch onto spurious correlations. In low-shot tabular learning, a small number of examples can make irrelevant columns look important by accident. If the model relies too heavily on those accidental patterns, the rules it produces may not generalize well. \textsf{FeatLLM} tries to prevent this by first asking the model to think about the task more broadly, before it uses the demonstrations to specialize the rules.

The paper also places a fixed limit on the number of rules extracted per class: $10$. This is not arbitrary. The authors explain that they want to avoid extracting too few rules, which could lead to underfitting, and too many rules, which could make the prompt excessively large. Fixing the number of rules gives the method a stable output size and helps keep the prompt within a manageable length. It also makes the downstream feature set more regular, since each class is represented by the same number of candidate rules.

The prompt additionally lets the model refer to the feature types given in the basic information section. This matters because rules must be compatible with the underlying data. A rule written without attention to type could compare a categorical value as if it were numeric, or otherwise produce an invalid condition. So the two-step reasoning instruction is not only about improving semantic quality; it also supports syntactic and structural correctness.

# Response instruction and formatting for downstream parsing

The final part of the prompt is the response instruction. Its purpose is practical: \textsf{FeatLLM} needs to turn the model’s natural-language rules into executable logic, so the output must be easy to parse. To support this, the prompt specifies the required format for the response for each answer class, and the structure that each rule should follow.

This matters because an unconstrained LLM may mix multiple rules together, or express them using logical operators such as `AND` or `OR` in ways that are difficult to convert into code. The response instruction reduces that ambiguity by telling the model how each rule should be written and how the final answer should be organized. The paper emphasizes that the instructions are designed so the model can choose the appropriate format based on the feature type. That makes the rules more directly usable for later parsing and feature generation.

Although this may seem like a small formatting detail, it is crucial to the overall method. Since \textsf{FeatLLM} uses the generated rules to create binary features automatically, the rules cannot remain as loose natural language. They must be structured enough to be transformed into program code. The response instruction therefore serves as the bridge between the LLM’s reasoning and the downstream classifier.

# Chain-of-thought-style guidance as a reasoning scaffold

The paper’s reasoning instruction begins with an introductory sentence similar to chain-of-thought prompting. The role of this guidance is not to ask the model for a final answer immediately, but to encourage a more explicit intermediate reasoning process. In the context of \textsf{FeatLLM}, that intermediate reasoning is especially valuable because the model is not just predicting a class. It is discovering the class conditions themselves.

This chain-of-thought-style structure is what allows the prompt to move from general task understanding to class-specific rule formation. The first step activates broad knowledge about the domain and the likely relevance of different feature types. The second step then grounds that reasoning in the few-shot examples. The result is a prompt that tries to combine prior knowledge, example-based learning, and output formatting in a single, carefully sequenced procedure. That sequence is the heart of how \textsf{FeatLLM} gets the LLM to extract predictive rules that are both meaningful and usable.

6. Turning Natural-Language Rules into Machine-Usable Features

# Why Natural-Language Rules Cannot Be Used Directly by Standard Classifiers

In FeatLLM, the LLM does not stop after producing a class prediction in words. Instead, it first outputs rules: textual conditions that describe what kind of sample should belong to a class. These rules are useful as human-readable reasoning, but they are not yet something a downstream model can consume. A standard classifier in FeatLLM expects numeric features, not a paragraph of natural language. So the central task after rule generation is to convert each rule into something that can be evaluated automatically on the tabular data.

That conversion matters because the downstream model in FeatLLM is intentionally simple. The paper uses the rules to create new binary features, and then a low-complexity model learns how much each feature should matter for each class. In other words, the rules are not the final prediction mechanism. They are intermediate knowledge that must be translated into machine-evaluable form before the classifier can estimate class likelihood.

# The Challenge of Noisy Syntax in LLM Outputs

A major difficulty is that LLM outputs are not perfectly stable at the surface-form level, even when the meaning is the same. The paper notes that, despite formatting constraints, the output can still include noisy syntactic changes. For example, an LLM might write a numeric condition with the symbol `$>$` in one response and the phrase “greater than” in another, or use `$<$` versus “less than.” These are semantically equivalent, but they are not identical strings.

This variability creates a problem for any parser that depends on exact wording. FeatLLM needs to know whether a rule says that a feature exceeds some threshold, not whether the model happened to write the condition using a symbol or a phrase. The same issue appears when rules are combined with logical operators. Without careful guidance, the model may merge conditions with AND or OR in ways that are harder to parse consistently. So the challenge is not just understanding what the rule means; it is also handling the many ways the same meaning may be expressed.

# Why Hand-Built Parsers Are Difficult to Maintain

One possible reaction to noisy syntax is to build a handcrafted parser that recognizes many possible phrasings. The paper argues against that direction. A hand-built parser would have to anticipate the many variations that an LLM can produce, including different symbols, words, and logical constructions. As the range of possible outputs grows, the parser becomes increasingly brittle.

That brittleness is especially problematic in FeatLLM because the framework is meant to work across many tabular datasets and feature types. The rule language is not fixed to one narrow domain. Instead, the LLM is asked to generate rules from different tasks, labels, and feature descriptions, which means the parser would need to handle a broad and changing set of expressions. Rather than constructing a complex custom system for every likely variation, the paper takes a more flexible route and uses the LLM itself to interpret and rewrite the rules.

# Using the LLM as a Semantic Parser and Code Generator

FeatLLM’s distinctive choice is to treat the LLM as a semantic parser and code generator. The reason this works is the same reason the LLM was useful earlier in the pipeline: it can use semantic understanding even when the surface syntax changes. The paper explicitly notes that the LLM is good at understanding meaning despite syntactic variation, and it is also capable of producing simple code because it has been trained on code corpora.

This makes the LLM well suited to bridge the gap between natural language and execution. Instead of asking a human engineer to write parsing rules for every phrasing, FeatLLM asks the LLM to generate code that implements the rule. The generated code is not a separate prediction model. It is a translation mechanism: it takes the textual rule and turns it into a program that can decide whether a sample satisfies that rule.

# Prompting the LLM with Function Signatures, Input-Output Descriptions, and Rules

To guide the code generation step, the paper constructs a prompt that includes a function name, input and output descriptions, and the inferred rules themselves. This is important because it gives the LLM a concrete programming task rather than an open-ended translation request. The function signature tells the model what kind of computation to write. The input description tells it what data it will receive. The output description tells it what the result should be. The rules provide the logic that the function needs to implement.

This prompt structure narrows the generation space and makes the output more usable. The paper’s broader prompt design also includes response instructions that specify how the rule and response should be formatted, and the LLM is encouraged to choose a format appropriate to the feature type. That earlier guidance reduces ambiguity, but it does not eliminate all variation. The parsing prompt is therefore a second layer of structure: it takes the rule text and asks the LLM to produce code that can be executed directly.

# Generating Python Code That Checks Whether a Sample Satisfies Each Rule

The code that FeatLLM asks the LLM to generate is meant to check whether a sample satisfies each rule. In the paper’s framework, each rule becomes a condition on the tabular features, and the generated code implements that condition in Python. This means the rule is no longer just a sentence; it becomes logic that can be applied to the data.

The paper emphasizes that the generated code is simple and targeted. Its job is not to solve the whole classification problem, but only to determine rule satisfaction. Once written, the code can inspect a sample and return whether the rule holds. Because the rules were already constrained to be easier to parse, the code generation step can focus on faithfully reproducing those conditions in executable form. The result is a direct bridge from rule language to feature computation.

# Executing Generated Code with Python `exec()` to Transform Data

After code generation, FeatLLM does not stop at text output. The generated code is executed with Python’s \texttt{exec()} function to perform data conversion. This is the moment when the rule becomes a computation on the actual tabular samples. The paper uses \texttt{exec()} as the mechanism that turns the LLM-produced program into a working transformer.

This execution step is crucial because it completes the move from language to features. The code is not kept as a suggestion or a description; it is run so that the sample data can be transformed automatically. In the paper’s pipeline, this is how the outputs of the LLM become usable inputs for the downstream model. Once the generated code has been executed, the system has a numerical representation of the rules rather than a textual one.

# Constructing Binary Features That Mark Whether Each Rule Is Satisfied

The transformed representation is binary. For each rule, FeatLLM creates a feature that indicates whether the sample satisfies that rule, with values in $\{0,1\}$. If a sample meets the condition, the feature is $1$; otherwise it is $0$. The paper describes this as creating new binary features for each class, so that the set of features records how the sample relates to the class-specific rules.

This binary encoding is the key interface between the LLM and the downstream learner. A sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ is no longer represented only by its original tabular fields. It is also represented by a rule-satisfaction vector, such as $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$, where each component corresponds to one of the $R$ rules for class $k$. These binary indicators summarize whether the sample fits the LLM’s extracted criteria, making the information immediately usable by the linear model that follows.

# Benefits and Risks of Code Generation in a Learning Pipeline

The main benefit of this design is robustness to language variation. Because the LLM is allowed to generate code from the meaning of the rule, FeatLLM does not need a rigid parser for every possible wording. This is especially helpful when the model rewrites symbols as words or uses slightly different syntactic forms. The approach also fits the paper’s broader goal of reducing inference cost: once the rules have been extracted and translated into executable form, inference can proceed with the downstream model alone, without querying the LLM for every test sample.

The risks are tied to the same dependency on generation quality. If the generated code does not faithfully implement the intended rule, the resulting binary feature will be wrong, and the downstream model will inherit that error. The paper addresses this general issue in several ways. It constrains the prompt format, uses the LLM’s semantic ability to reduce sensitivity to syntactic noise, and later relies on tuning and ensemble methods to compensate for imperfect rules. In this sense, code generation is powerful, but it is not treated as flawless; it is one component in a larger pipeline designed to absorb occasional errors while still producing useful features.

In FeatLLM, this transformation step is what turns LLM reasoning into tabular learning features. The rules may begin as natural language, but after parsing, code generation, and execution, they become binary variables that a simple model can use effectively.

7. Predicting Class Probabilities from Rule-Based Features

# Why counting satisfied rules is a natural starting point

After \textsf{FeatLLM} has extracted rules from the few-shot examples, the next question is how to turn those rules into a prediction. The most immediate idea is also the most intuitive one: if a sample satisfies many rules associated with a class, then that class should become more likely. In the paper, this is described as counting how many rules of each class a sample satisfies, which is equivalent to summing the binary indicators for that class. This is a natural starting point because the rules are meant to act as criteria for class membership, so a larger number of satisfied criteria should provide stronger evidence for that class.

This idea fits the overall philosophy of the method. \textsf{FeatLLM} does not ask the language model to output the final answer directly for every test sample. Instead, the LLM is used to generate human-readable rules, and those rules become features. Once the features exist, a simple counting-based rule can already give a rough measure of class support. In other words, the rules are not merely textual explanations; they are operational signals that can be evaluated on data.

# Why simple counting is insufficient when rules vary in usefulness

Although counting satisfied rules is a sensible baseline, the paper emphasizes that not all rules are equally valuable. Some rules may be highly informative, while others may be weak, redundant, or only loosely connected to the target. If we simply count every satisfied rule equally, then a weak rule would contribute as much as a strong one. That would ignore differences in rule quality and could reduce predictive performance.

This is why \textsf{FeatLLM} does not stop at rule generation. After the rules are converted into binary features, the method learns how important each rule is from the few labeled training samples. The downstream model therefore acts as a filter as well as a predictor: it can give more weight to rules that consistently align with the labels and less weight to rules that do not. The paper’s ablation study supports this design, showing that omitting weight tuning hurts performance. This means that even when the LLM has produced useful rules, learning their relative importance still matters.

# Class-specific binary feature vectors for rule satisfaction

The rules are organized separately for each class, and the paper represents the rule satisfaction for a sample using a class-specific binary feature vector. For a sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ and class $k$, this vector is denoted by $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$, where $R$ is the number of rules per class. Each entry in $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ indicates whether the sample satisfies one specific rule for class $k$. A value of $1$ means the rule is satisfied, and a value of $0$ means it is not.

This class-specific representation is important because the rules are not generic across all classes. Rather, each class has its own set of criteria extracted by the LLM. By separating the features this way, the model can evaluate evidence for each class independently. The binary encoding also keeps the representation simple and easy to use in a downstream model. The resulting features are not complicated embeddings or dense vectors; they are just rule indicators that say whether particular conditions hold for the sample.

# The linear model used to score each class

Once the binary rule features are available, \textsf{FeatLLM} uses a conventional machine learning model to score the classes. The paper gives the example of a linear model without bias. For each class $k$, the model computes a class score from the binary rule vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i$. This score is then used to estimate class likelihood.

This choice is central to the paper’s design. The goal is not to build a sophisticated neural classifier on top of the rules. Instead, the rules already encode much of the task-specific knowledge, so the remaining learner only needs to combine them intelligently. A linear model is sufficient for this purpose because it can assign importance to each rule and produce a class score by a weighted sum. This keeps inference lightweight and avoids the need for passing each test sample through the LLM.

# The non-negative weight constraint and its interpretation

A distinctive feature of the downstream model is that the rule weights are constrained to be non-negative. In the paper, this is written as $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$, meaning that any negative value is projected to $0$. This constraint is not just a technical detail; it has a clear interpretation.

Because the features represent whether a sample satisfies rules that were generated as evidence for a class, a satisfied rule should not reduce the likelihood of that class during learning. The non-negative projection ensures that the model can only reward satisfied rules, not penalize them. In the paper’s terms, this prevents the generated features from reducing class likelihood. That makes the learned weights easier to interpret as rule importance values: a larger weight means the rule contributes more positively to the class score, while a weight near zero means the rule is not very useful.

# Computing logits through weighted sums of satisfied rules

The class score in \textsf{FeatLLM} is computed as a dot product between the non-negative weights and the binary rule vector. For class $k$ and sample $i$, the paper defines the logit as
\[
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i.
\]
Since $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ is binary, this weighted sum only includes the rules that the sample actually satisfies. If a rule is not satisfied, its contribution is $0$. Thus the logit is essentially the total amount of positive evidence for class $k$, with each piece of evidence scaled by how important the model has learned that rule to be.

This formulation is simple but powerful. It mirrors the counting intuition from the previous section, but improves on it by allowing some satisfied rules to matter more than others. If a sample satisfies many low-value rules, the score will not be inflated as much as if it satisfies a smaller number of highly informative rules. The dot product therefore gives the model a way to refine the naive counting strategy while preserving its interpretability.

# Applying softmax to obtain class probabilities

After computing a logit for each class, \textsf{FeatLLM} converts those logits into class probabilities using the softmax function. If there are $c$ classes, the logits are collected into the vector $[\text{logit}_1^i, \ldots, \text{logit}_c^i]$, and the probability vector $\mathbf{p}^i$ is computed as
\[
\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1^i, \ldots, \text{logit}_c^i]).
\]
This step makes the output suitable for classification, since the resulting probabilities provide a normalized comparison among the classes. A class with a larger logit receives a larger probability, but the probabilities still sum to $1$ across classes.

In this framework, the logits summarize evidence from the rule features, and softmax turns those evidence scores into class likelihoods. This is the final prediction mechanism used by the downstream model. The method remains very simple computationally, which is one of the paper’s main goals: once the rules are available, prediction should not require a complex inference pipeline.

# Optimizing cross-entropy loss with few-shot labeled data

The weights in the linear model are learned from the few available labeled training samples. The paper states that cross-entropy loss is used to optimize the weights $\mathbf{w}_k$. This means the model is trained to assign high probability to the true class of each labeled example while reducing probability mass on incorrect classes.

The few-shot setting makes this learning problem especially delicate, because the model only has a small number of labeled examples to estimate the importance of each rule. That is why the paper treats the downstream model as intentionally low-complexity. A simple linear model is less likely to overfit than a larger model when training data are scarce. At the same time, the rule features supplied by the LLM provide a strong prior structure, so the linear model does not need to discover the entire task from scratch. It only needs to calibrate how much each rule should matter for the observed training samples.

# Learning which rules are most important for each class

One of the main purposes of the downstream training stage is to learn rule importance. The paper explicitly says that the model learns the importance of each feature and how it affects class likelihood. In the linear scoring formulation, importance is encoded directly in the magnitude of the weights. A large weight means the associated rule has strong influence on the class logit, while a small weight means the rule contributes little.

This is an important refinement over simply accepting all rules from the LLM at face value. The LLM may generate many plausible rules, but the data may show that some are more reliable than others. The downstream model therefore serves as a data-driven correction mechanism. It takes the rule candidates from the LLM and learns which ones actually help classification for the target task. The paper’s analysis of feature quality reinforces this interpretation, showing that after tuning, rules with little relevance can be automatically filtered out by having low learned weights.

# Why the downstream model remains simple despite LLM-generated features

A key design choice in \textsf{FeatLLM} is that the downstream predictor stays simple even though the features are produced by a sophisticated LLM. The reason is that the complexity has already been moved into the feature engineering stage. The LLM is used to generate criteria that capture task knowledge, and once those criteria are available, the prediction problem becomes much easier.

This separation of responsibilities is one of the paper’s central ideas. The LLM does the difficult reasoning work of proposing rules, while the downstream learner only aggregates rule satisfaction into a class score. Because of this, inference does not require querying the LLM for each sample. The paper argues that this reduces latency and makes the method more practical than end-to-end LLM-based tabular prediction approaches. The downstream model also remains compatible with API-only access to the LLM, since no fine-tuning or training of the language model itself is required.

# Interpretability of learned weights over rules

The learned weights are not only useful for prediction; they also make the final model easier to interpret. Because each feature corresponds to a specific rule, and each weight measures that rule’s contribution to the class logit, the model reveals which rules are most influential. This gives a direct view of how the classifier is making decisions.

In this sense, the linear model preserves the interpretability of the rule-based representation. If a rule has a high learned weight, that rule is important evidence for the class. If a rule ends up with a very small weight, it is effectively ignored. The paper even notes that low-weight rules can be filtered out after tuning. So the weights serve a dual role: they improve classification performance and provide a transparent summary of rule usefulness.

This interpretability is consistent with the broader design of \textsf{FeatLLM}. The framework uses the LLM to generate rules that are understandable in natural language, then converts those rules into binary features, and finally learns a simple weighted combination of them. The resulting model is still compact and explainable, but it benefits from the prior knowledge and reasoning ability of the LLM.

8. Ensembling, Bagging, and Managing Prompt Length

FeatLLM is not a one-shot prompting method that trusts a single LLM response. Instead, it treats rule generation as a repeated process: the same tabular problem is presented multiple times under slightly different prompt conditions, and the resulting rule sets are combined into an ensemble. This chapter explains why that repetition matters, how FeatLLM creates diversity across trials, and why bagging is doing two jobs at once: it improves robustness and it makes long prompts manageable.

The central idea is that a single rule set produced by an LLM is only one possible interpretation of the tabular task. Because the model works from a few demonstrations, a natural-language description of the features, and the task instruction, the rules it produces can be incomplete, noisy, or biased toward a narrow slice of the data. FeatLLM therefore does not ask whether one generated rule set is perfect. It asks instead whether many slightly different rule sets, when combined, can produce a more reliable feature representation than any single prompt could.

# Why a single LLM-generated rule set may be noisy or incomplete

A single rule set is fragile because it is only one sample from the space of plausible explanations the LLM might produce. In FeatLLM, the LLM is not directly outputting final class labels. It is being asked to infer the criteria that distinguish classes and then to express those criteria as explicit rules. That is a harder and more indirect task than ordinary prediction, and it is especially sensitive in the low-shot regime, where only a small number of labeled examples are available. If the LLM emphasizes the wrong features, misses an important condition, or produces a rule that only partially reflects the class boundary, the downstream classifier inherits those mistakes.

The paper also makes clear that the model is meant to filter out uninformative columns, but that this filtering is not guaranteed from one prompt alone. A single rule set can fail in two different ways. It can be incomplete, meaning it does not cover enough of the relevant relationships in the data. Or it can be noisy, meaning it includes rules that are only loosely related to the target. Because the generated rules are later converted into binary features, any weakness in the rule set is not merely a textual issue; it becomes part of the learned representation used for prediction.

# Repeated rule extraction across multiple trials

FeatLLM addresses this fragility by repeating the entire rule-generation process multiple times. Each trial begins with a prompt that contains the task description, feature descriptions, and few-shot demonstrations. The LLM then proposes a set of rules for each class. Those rules are parsed into executable form, transformed into binary features, and used to train a low-complexity classifier. The result of one trial is therefore a complete but partial view of the task.

This repeated process is important because it lets FeatLLM collect several candidate explanations instead of committing to the first one. The paper describes the method as generating multiple inference models and then combining them via ensembling. In effect, each trial is a weak learner: it may be useful, but it is not expected to be sufficient on its own. Diversity across trials is therefore a feature, not a flaw. Different trials may uncover different aspects of the same underlying task, and the ensemble can use that variety to make a more stable decision.

# Using non-determinism in LLM sampling through temperature

To make repeated trials genuinely different, FeatLLM introduces non-determinism into LLM inference. The paper states that a sufficiently high temperature is used during rule generation, and in the implementation it sets the temperature to $0.5$. The purpose is not randomness for its own sake. It is to encourage the LLM to explore multiple plausible rule sets rather than repeatedly producing the same response.

This matters because if every trial produced nearly identical rules, then repeated prompting would give little benefit. Temperature-based sampling helps expose alternative ways the model might interpret the task. Some sampled rule sets will be better than others, but the ensemble is designed to absorb that variation. In other words, FeatLLM uses sampling to create a set of candidate explanations, and then relies on downstream aggregation to decide which ones deserve the most influence.

# Randomizing the order of few-shot demonstrations

The paper also randomizes the order of the few-shot demonstrations. This is a small implementation detail, but it plays an important role because the order of examples can affect the outcome of LLM queries. In-context learning is not invariant to permutation, so the same examples shown in a different sequence may lead the model to slightly different rule sets. FeatLLM uses that sensitivity deliberately to create diversity across trials.

Randomizing the order is especially useful because it does not require additional data. The same few labeled examples can be reused in multiple arrangements, allowing the model to see them through different contexts. In a low-shot setting, that is valuable: every labeled sample is precious, so the method should extract as much variation as possible from the small amount of supervision it already has. By shuffling the demonstrations, FeatLLM increases the chance that stable patterns will recur even when their context changes.

# Feature bagging and instance bagging in the prompt construction process

Bagging is the other main source of diversity, and it also serves a practical purpose. FeatLLM can select a subset of features or a subset of instances for each trial. Feature bagging means that not every column needs to be serialized into the prompt for every run. Instance bagging means that not every labeled example needs to be included as a demonstration in every run. Both forms reduce prompt length.

This is crucial because FeatLLM's prompt contains several components: task description, feature descriptions, and serialized examples. When a table has many columns or many labeled samples, including everything in one prompt may exceed the context window or make the prompt unwieldy. Bagging keeps each prompt manageable. At the same time, because different trials select different subsets, the full dataset can still be explored over the course of the ensemble. The method therefore balances compact prompts with broad coverage across repeated runs.

# Training multiple weak learners and averaging their predicted probabilities

After rule generation, each trial produces binary features that indicate whether a sample satisfies each rule. FeatLLM then trains a simple model on those features. The paper uses a linear model without bias, with class-specific weights $\mathbf{w}[t]$ for trial $t$. For a sample $\mathbf{x}^i$, the generated binary feature vector for class $k$ is $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$, where $R$ is the number of rules per class. The model computes logits and then converts them into class probabilities with a softmax.

The final prediction is not taken from a single trial. Instead, FeatLLM averages the predicted probabilities across all $T$ trials. If $\mathbf{p}^i[t]$ denotes the probability vector from trial $t$, then the ensemble prediction is the average of those vectors. This means that each trial contributes a complete probabilistic opinion, and the final answer reflects their combined judgment. Averaging probabilities is a natural fit here because it softens the effect of any one imperfect rule set or any one imperfect set of learned weights.

# Connections to ensemble learning and random forests

The paper explicitly frames its bagging strategy as borrowing from ensemble methods such as random forests. The connection is not that FeatLLM builds trees, but that it uses the same broad idea of combining multiple randomized learners rather than depending on one model alone. In both cases, different subsets or different random views of the data are used to produce multiple predictors, and then those predictors are aggregated.

What changes in FeatLLM is where the randomness is applied. Traditional bagging methods randomize over data subsets to build several models. FeatLLM randomizes both the prompt content and the rule extraction process itself. That makes the ensemble especially well suited to a setting where the primary model is an LLM used for feature engineering. The ensemble is not just a statistical device; it is also a way to compensate for the fact that rule generation is itself a probabilistic process.

# Self-consistency intuition: rules repeated across trials are more likely to be reliable

The paper's self-consistency intuition is simple: if a rule appears repeatedly across different trials, it is more likely to be correct. This is because the rule has survived changes in prompt ordering, sampling randomness, and bagged subsets of features or instances. In that sense, repeated appearance acts like a form of agreement among independent attempts to solve the same problem.

This intuition is especially useful in low-shot learning, where individual prompts may be unstable. A rule that only appears once may reflect a chance interaction between one demonstration order, one subset of features, and one sampled response. A rule that reappears across many trials is more likely to reflect a genuine relation between the task and the data. The ensemble does not explicitly vote on rules, but the repeated extraction process creates a natural filter: stable rules matter more because they are seen more often and they support more consistent downstream predictions.

# How bagging helps when the table has too many features or examples for one prompt

Bagging is also a direct solution to prompt-size limitations. The paper emphasizes that when tabular data have many features, the serialized text can become too long for a single prompt. This problem becomes more severe as the number of features or labeled examples grows. In such cases, a single prompt may simply be infeasible. Even if it fits, it may become so long that the LLM's performance degrades.

By selecting only a subset of features or instances for each trial, FeatLLM can work with tables that would otherwise be too large to handle comfortably in one prompt. The key point is that no individual trial needs to see everything. Across the ensemble, the method can still cover the broader space of features and examples. This is why the paper describes the ensemble of weak learners as able to nullify the impact of incomplete feature or instance coverage in any single trial.

# Trade-offs between diversity, coverage, and computational budget

The ensemble strategy works because it balances three competing goals. First, it needs diversity: the trials should not all return the same rule set. Temperature sampling and shuffled demonstrations help create that diversity. Second, it needs coverage: the trials must collectively see enough of the table to capture meaningful relationships. Bagging helps by distributing the available features and instances across runs. Third, it must respect computational budget, because every trial requires another LLM query during feature engineering.

The paper's hyperparameter analysis reflects this balance. Performance improves as the number of ensembles increases, but the gains begin to converge around 20 ensembles. Similarly, the paper uses 10 rules per class as a practical compromise between prompt length and performance. More trials and more rules can add information, but they also increase prompt size and the number of API calls. FeatLLM therefore uses ensembling not as an unlimited scaling strategy, but as a controlled way to gain robustness while staying within the limits of prompt length and cost.

One final advantage of the design is that the LLM is not queried for every test sample. The cost of repeated prompting is paid during the feature-engineering stage, while inference uses only the learned downstream model. That makes the ensemble much more practical than adapting the same idea to methods that require per-sample LLM inference. In those methods, adding more prompt variants would multiply the runtime cost at prediction time. In FeatLLM, by contrast, the repeated prompts are part of building the feature space once, after which prediction is handled by the simple model on the generated binary features.

9. Experimental Design: Datasets, Baselines, and Implementation

# Overview of the 13 Evaluation Datasets

The experimental study evaluates \textsf{FeatLLM} on 13 tabular datasets in low-shot regimes. This choice is important because the paper is not trying to show performance on a single convenient benchmark; it is trying to test whether the same framework can work across a broad spread of tabular prediction tasks, data domains, and levels of difficulty. The datasets also vary in size and complexity, and the paper notes that some of them contain more than 100 attributes, which creates a realistic challenge for LLM-based methods because long serialized tables can strain the prompt budget.

A key property of the datasets used in the paper is that each attribute comes with a clear name and description. That matters because \textsf{FeatLLM} relies on natural-language feature information when it asks the LLM to infer rules. The evaluation setup therefore matches the method’s intended use: the data are not just numerical arrays, but tabular problems with enough semantic context for the LLM to reason about feature meaning.

# Classic Tabular Benchmarks

The paper includes nine datasets that serve as classic tabular benchmarks. Adult is used to predict whether an individual earns over \$50,000 annually. Bank predicts whether a customer will subscribe to a term deposit. Blood predicts whether donors will return for subsequent donations. Car predicts the quality of a car. Communities predicts the crime rate in a specified region. Credit-g predicts whether an individual poses a good or bad credit risk. Diabetes predicts the presence of diabetes in an individual. Heart predicts the occurrence of coronary artery disease in an individual. Myocardial predicts whether an individual suffers from chronic heart failure.

These benchmarks cover several familiar application areas, including finance, health, and social or behavioral prediction. That diversity matters because the paper wants to show that \textsf{FeatLLM} is not tuned to one domain-specific pattern. Instead, the evaluation spans tasks where the feature meanings are naturally different, which is exactly the kind of setting where a language model’s prior knowledge could help in feature engineering.

# Recently Added or Synthetic Datasets

To make the evaluation more demanding, the paper also includes four datasets that are either recently added or synthetic. Cultivars is used to predict how high the grain yield of a soybean cultivar will be. NHANES predicts a person’s age group from the record. Sequence-type classifies a sequence as one of four types: arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, or Collatz. Solution-mix predicts whether the percentage concentration of a mixture from four solutions exceeds $0.5$.

These datasets expand the task range beyond the older benchmark family. Cultivars and NHANES were recently donated to the UCI Machine Learning Repository after September 2023, and Sequence-type and Solution-mix are synthetic. The paper’s motivation for including them is straightforward: they are meant to test the method on problems that are less likely to have been absorbed into the LLM’s prior training exposure.

# Why Recent and Synthetic Datasets Matter

The paper is careful to address the possibility that strong LLM-based tabular results might come from memorization rather than genuine reasoning. The recently donated UCI datasets help with this concern because they were added after September 2023, which means they were not part of the pre-training data for the LLM API used by the model, namely GPT-3.5-turbo-0613. The synthetic datasets go one step further: since they were constructed artificially, they cannot be memorized in the same way as a naturally occurring dataset from public sources.

This design choice strengthens the evaluation. If a method performs well on these newer and synthetic tasks, then its success is less plausibly explained by the LLM simply recognizing familiar patterns from its pre-training corpus. In other words, the protocol is trying to distinguish useful prior knowledge and reasoning from accidental exposure.

# Binary Versus Multiclass Evaluation Settings

The paper evaluates both binary and multiclass classification tasks. This is important because few-shot tabular learning should not only work when there are two labels; it should also remain usable when the target has several possible classes. The inclusion of Sequence-type, which has four classes, makes this especially explicit.

The mixed label structure also makes the benchmark suite more realistic. Some tabular problems are naturally yes-or-no decisions, while others require distinguishing among multiple categories. By keeping both settings in the evaluation, the paper tests whether \textsf{FeatLLM} can support a wider range of tabular prediction scenarios without changing its core mechanism.

# Conventional Machine-Learning Baselines

The first baseline group consists of standard tabular learning methods: Logistic regression, XGBoost, and RandomForest. These baselines serve as the classical reference points for the experiments. They are the methods a reader would expect to compare against when asking whether a new low-shot technique is actually competitive on tabular data.

The role of these baselines is especially important because \textsf{FeatLLM} does not just compete with other LLM-based methods; it also has to show that it can stand up to established supervised learning techniques. This comparison helps the paper evaluate whether the rule-based features generated by the LLM are genuinely useful, or whether a conventional learner trained directly on the raw tabular inputs would already be sufficient.

# Baselines Using Unlabeled Data: SCARF and STUNT

The second baseline group assumes access to unlabeled datasets. The paper includes SCARF and STUNT in this category. These methods are relevant because they reflect a common strategy in few-shot learning: when labels are scarce, one can try to gain information from additional unlabeled examples.

At the same time, the paper points out a practical limitation of this assumption. Obtaining large quantities of unlabeled data may be infeasible in real applications. That makes these baselines useful for comparison, but also somewhat idealized from the perspective of deployment. Including them therefore helps the paper show where \textsf{FeatLLM} stands relative to methods that rely on extra data that may not actually be available.

# Transfer-Pretrained Baseline: TabPFN

A third baseline is TabPFN, which uses transfer pretraining. According to the paper, TabPFN mixes different types of distributions to create synthetic data for pretraining a Bayesian neural network. After pretraining, inference is performed by inputting both the training and test samples of the target task, and no further training is needed.

This baseline is important because it represents another way to inject prior knowledge into tabular learning without relying on task-specific fine-tuning on the target dataset. The paper includes TabPFN to show how \textsf{FeatLLM} compares to a model that has already been exposed to a broad variety of synthetic tabular distributions before the target task is even considered.

# LLM-Based Baselines: In-Context Learning, TABLET, and TabLLM

The final baseline group uses LLMs directly. The paper compares against in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM. In-context learning simply embeds few-shot training samples in the prompt without any tuning. TABLET goes further by adding hints through an external classifier built from rule-sets and prototypes. TabLLM uses parameter-efficient tuning techniques such as IA3 to train the LLM with few-shot samples.

These baselines are particularly relevant because they represent the main family of prior LLM-based tabular methods that \textsf{FeatLLM} is trying to improve upon. The paper emphasizes a major difference: those methods keep inference inside the LLM pipeline for every sample, while \textsf{FeatLLM} aims to use the LLM only to generate rules once and then move prediction to a simpler downstream model.

# Choice of LLM Backbone

For \textsf{FeatLLM}, the paper uses GPT-3.5 as the LLM backbone. At the same time, the method is designed to be agnostic to the specific LLM choice, and the paper notes that additional backbone results are reported in the appendix. This matters because the main contribution is the framework, not a special dependence on one proprietary model.

For the baselines, GPT-3.5 is also used for in-context learning. TabLLM is different: in the original setup, it uses T0 because TabLLM restricts the LLM to publicly available checkpoints, so GPT-3.5 is not accessible there. This detail is important for reproducing the comparison fairly, since it explains why different LLM backbones appear in different parts of the evaluation.

# Key Implementation Settings

The paper reports a concrete set of implementation choices for \textsf{FeatLLM}. The LLM inference temperature is set to $0.5$, and the top-p value is left at the API default of $1$. The number of ensembles is set to $20$, and the number of rules extracted per class is set to $10$. These settings control the balance between diversity, prompt length, and downstream model complexity.

The paper’s hyperparameter analysis supports these choices. Performance improves as the number of ensembles increases, but the gains begin to converge around $20$. The choice of $10$ rules per class is presented as a practical compromise: more rules can add information, but they also enlarge the prompt and the input dimensionality, which can become a problem in the low-shot regime. The temperature setting is used to introduce randomness in rule generation, which helps create diversity across ensemble members.

The implementation also specifies the runtime environment. One A100 GPU is used as the default, except for TabLLM, which uses four A100 GPUs for model parallelism. This kind of detail is useful for readers trying to understand the practical cost of the comparisons, especially because the paper argues that \textsf{FeatLLM} keeps inference relatively lightweight once the rules have been generated.

# Optimization Details for the Linear Model

After the LLM generates rules and those rules are converted into binary features, the paper trains a simple linear model on top of them. The optimizer is Adam with a learning rate of $0.01$, and training lasts for $200$ epochs. This downstream model is intentionally simple because the point of \textsf{FeatLLM} is to move the burden of representation discovery to the LLM and then use a lightweight learner for prediction.

The paper also uses $k$-fold cross-validation for optimal epoch selection in this training stage. This helps avoid relying on a single arbitrary stopping point. Since the model is trained in few-shot conditions, even modest choices in optimization can matter, so the use of cross-validation is part of making the evaluation more stable.

# Cross-Validation and Hyperparameter Selection

The paper uses different selection procedures for different kinds of methods. For conventional machine-learning baselines such as Logistic regression, XGBoost, and RandomForest, the parameters are selected by Grid-search combined with $k$-fold cross-validation. The paper sets $k$ to either $2$ or $4$, while making sure that the training set contains at least one example of each class.

That detail is important in a low-shot setting, because very small training sets can easily omit a class if the split is careless. By requiring at least one example of each class, the paper avoids degenerate training folds and makes the comparison more meaningful. The same general cross-validation idea is also used to choose the epoch for the linear model in \textsf{FeatLLM}, although the paper distinguishes this from the grid-search procedure used for the conventional baselines.

# Evaluation Metric and Repeated Runs

The paper reports results using AUC values. To reduce dependence on a single random split, each experiment is repeated three times with different randomness in the choice of training and test sets. The final results are reported as the average and standard deviation of the AUC across these runs.

This protocol is appropriate for few-shot tabular learning because the outcome can vary noticeably when only a tiny labeled set is available. A single split might overstate or understate a method’s quality simply by chance, so averaging over repeated runs gives a more reliable picture of performance. In the paper’s comparisons, this evaluation procedure is used consistently across the benchmark suite, making the reported results easier to interpret as genuine method-level differences rather than accidental effects of one random sample selection.

10. Main Empirical Results

# Performance Across All Datasets

The paper evaluates \textsf{FeatLLM} on $13$ tabular classification datasets drawn from a wide range of domains, including finance, health, biology, and synthetic reasoning tasks. These datasets vary substantially in size and complexity, and some of them, such as Communities and Myocardial, contain more than $100$ attributes. The authors report performance in low-shot regimes and use the area under the ROC curve, or AUC, as the main metric. Each experiment is repeated three times with different random training/test splits, and the paper reports the average and standard deviation of AUC.

The main empirical message is simple: \textsf{FeatLLM} is consistently among the strongest methods across the benchmark suite. The paper states that it either ranks first or second when compared with the other baselines. That kind of result matters because the datasets are not all similar; they differ in feature types, label structure, and the amount of context that must be serialized into the prompt. A method that performs well across such diverse tables is not just fitting one particular dataset family. It is learning a general strategy for turning a few labeled examples into useful tabular features.

This broad success is especially important because the evaluation includes recent and synthetic datasets that are less likely to have been memorized by the LLM backbone. In other words, the gains cannot be explained away as simple recall from pretraining. The results therefore support the paper’s central claim that LLMs can help with tabular learning in a way that generalizes beyond ordinary benchmarks.

# Average Improvement Over Alternatives such as TabLLM and STUNT

The abstract reports that \textsf{FeatLLM} outperforms alternatives such as TabLLM and STUNT by about $10\%$ on average. That number is important because it summarizes performance across many datasets rather than highlighting only a few favorable cases. When a paper reports an average improvement of this kind, the right interpretation is that the advantage is broad and stable, not limited to a single domain or a single split.

The comparison to TabLLM and STUNT also clarifies what kind of progress the paper is claiming. Both are strong few-shot tabular methods, but they still depend more directly on using the LLM as a predictor or on other training-heavy mechanisms. \textsf{FeatLLM}, by contrast, uses the LLM to generate rules and then relies on a simple downstream model. The performance gain therefore suggests that rule extraction can be a better use of the LLM than asking it to make the final prediction for every sample.

It is also useful to interpret the average improvement together with the paper’s note that some competing methods perform well on specific datasets but do not improve substantially overall. STUNT and TabPFN, for example, show notable gains in certain cases, but their average advantage over conventional machine learning is not as strong as \textsf{FeatLLM}'s. So the reported $10\%$ average improvement is not just a small edge; it indicates a meaningful shift in how well the method transfers across tables.

# Where FeatLLM Ranks First or Second

The paper emphasizes ranking as well as raw score. According to the reported tables, \textsf{FeatLLM} consistently takes either the top spot or second place among all baselines. In empirical work, this kind of rank consistency is often more informative than a single best-case number, because it reveals how often a method remains competitive when the task changes.

A method that is repeatedly near the top is robust in the sense that it rarely collapses on unfamiliar data. That matters in tabular learning because the datasets differ in ways that make simple heuristics unreliable. Some have a small number of features; others are high-dimensional. Some are more semantically obvious to an LLM, while others require more careful reasoning over column definitions and few examples. \textsf{FeatLLM}'s repeated top-$2$ placement suggests that its rule-generation approach remains useful across these settings.

This rank pattern also helps explain why the authors present \textsf{FeatLLM} as a practical alternative rather than just a conceptual idea. If a method is only occasionally best but frequently far below the leaders, it is hard to justify as a general-purpose solution. Being first or second most of the time indicates that \textsf{FeatLLM} is not narrowly tuned to a small subset of tasks; it is repeatedly competitive where few-shot tabular prediction is hard.

# Few-Shot Efficiency: $4$-Shot FeatLLM Compared with $32$-Shot Conventional Methods

One of the strongest empirical claims in the paper is data efficiency. The authors show that \textsf{FeatLLM} with just $4$ labeled examples can achieve performance comparable to conventional tabular baselines that require $32$ shots. That is a striking result because it means the model is extracting much more value from each labeled sample than standard methods do.

The right way to read this comparison is not that $4$ examples are magically enough for all tabular problems. Instead, the result shows that the LLM helps convert a tiny amount of supervision into a much richer feature representation. The few labeled examples are not used to train a large predictive model end to end. Rather, they help the LLM infer rules, which are then turned into binary features for the downstream classifier. This makes each label count more.

For a student interpreting few-shot learning results, this is the key intuition: data efficiency is not just about reducing model size. It is also about changing the representation. \textsf{FeatLLM} succeeds because it uses the LLM’s prior knowledge to propose meaningful criteria, so the downstream model is operating on features that are already aligned with the task. That is why a $4$-shot model can compete with a much more label-hungry conventional pipeline.

# Performance on Datasets with Many Features

The paper pays special attention to datasets with many columns, such as Communities and Myocardial, because they expose one of the central limitations of LLM-based tabular methods: prompt length. When a table has many attributes, serializing all feature values and descriptions into text can produce an input that is too long for comfortable use or too noisy for effective reasoning. In the reported experiments, such datasets are precisely where some LLM baselines struggle most.

\textsf{FeatLLM} is designed to cope with this problem more gracefully. Its rule-generation process uses feature bagging and ensembling, which means that each trial can focus on only a subset of the full table. This matters empirically because it lets the method remain usable even when the full table is large. The paper explicitly notes that the framework works effectively on datasets with numerous features, where direct LLM baselines fail or become infeasible.

The empirical takeaway is that feature count is not just a nuisance detail; it can determine whether an LLM-based tabular method is practical at all. Methods that require the entire table to fit into a single prompt are fragile when the number of columns grows. \textsf{FeatLLM}'s ability to maintain performance in these cases is one of the clearest signs that its feature-engineering setup is better suited to real-world tabular data.

# Why Some LLM Baselines Fail on High-Dimensional Tables

The paper argues that the failure mode of prior LLM baselines is structural, not incidental. Most existing LLM-based tabular methods make end-to-end predictions by passing each sample through the LLM at inference time. That means the entire serialized table, along with the task description and examples, must fit in the prompt for every prediction. As the number of features increases, the prompt becomes longer, and the model either cannot process it comfortably or starts to degrade in quality.

This explains why methods such as in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM do not scale well to tables with many features. They are bound to the prompt format, so their limitations are inherited from the LLM’s context window and from the difficulty of reasoning over long serialized inputs. The paper emphasizes that these methods failed to infer successfully on datasets with large feature counts. In practice, that means a method may look strong on small tabular tasks but become fragile as soon as the table is wide.

The important contrast is that \textsf{FeatLLM} moves the LLM’s role earlier in the pipeline. Instead of asking the model to produce the final label for every test example, it asks the model to generate reusable rules. Once the rules are extracted and converted into features, prediction is delegated to a simple classifier. This architectural shift is what lets \textsf{FeatLLM} avoid the scaling problem that hurts other LLM baselines.

# Advantages of Not Requiring Per-Sample LLM Inference at Test Time

A major practical advantage of \textsf{FeatLLM} is that inference does not require running the LLM on each test sample. This is a very different operating mode from end-to-end LLM tabular systems, where each prediction must be sent through the language model. The paper reports that on the Adult dataset with $16$ shots, \textsf{FeatLLM} has relatively low inference time, comparable to conventional methods, whereas the other LLM-based approaches have much higher inference time.

This distinction is crucial in real deployments. If every new sample must trigger a separate LLM query, then latency and cost both grow with the number of predictions. By contrast, \textsf{FeatLLM} pays the LLM cost mainly during feature engineering, then uses a lightweight linear model at inference time. That means the expensive reasoning step is amortized over many future predictions, which is exactly what makes the approach attractive for practical tabular systems.

The paper also notes that the training time of \textsf{FeatLLM} is comparable to other LLM-based methods and involves only $30$ API queries, which can be parallelized. This is a strong practical point because it means the method does not require training access to the LLM, only API-level access. In addition, the authors argue that adapting the same bagging and ensemble idea to prior LLM-based methods would double the number of per-sample LLM inferences, making those approaches much less practical. So the empirical runtime results support the broader design choice of using the LLM once for feature creation rather than repeatedly for prediction.

# Interpreting Performance Variability Across Domains and Datasets

Although \textsf{FeatLLM} is strong overall, the paper does not claim uniform perfection. Different methods behave differently across datasets. STUNT and TabPFN, for example, can show substantial improvements on some tasks, even if their overall gains are not as strong as \textsf{FeatLLM}'s. This kind of variability is normal in tabular learning because tabular datasets differ dramatically in semantics, feature types, and how much prior knowledge is actually useful.

The paper’s broader experimental story is that \textsf{FeatLLM} is robust because it combines two sources of information: prior knowledge from the LLM and the limited labeled data in the target task. When a dataset aligns well with the LLM’s background knowledge, the generated rules can be especially informative. When the table is larger or noisier, bagging and ensembling help stabilize the results. This is why the method performs well across such a diverse benchmark suite rather than only in a narrow family of tasks.

The authors further support this interpretation with analyses showing that the quality of the generated rules remains high and that irrelevant rules are filtered out by the downstream linear model. So the variability across datasets should not be read as instability in the method; instead, it reflects the natural difficulty differences among tasks. \textsf{FeatLLM} is strong precisely because it can adapt its rule-based features to those differences.

# What the Results Suggest About LLM Prior Knowledge as a Source of Useful Tabular Features

Taken together, the empirical results support the central thesis of the paper: an LLM’s prior knowledge is valuable not only for making direct predictions, but also for discovering features. The strongest evidence is that \textsf{FeatLLM} performs well in very low-shot settings, where ordinary supervised learners have little data to work with. That is exactly the situation in which external prior knowledge should matter most.

The paper’s feature-quality analysis reinforces this interpretation. It reports that most generated rules are relevant to the target attribute before tuning, and that the linear model can filter out rules with little usefulness during training. It also finds that only a small fraction of rules based on noisy columns, about $1$--$2\%$, are produced in the spurious-correlation test. These findings suggest that the LLM is not merely producing arbitrary text; it is actually proposing structured, informative conditions that can become usable tabular features.

There is also an important caution embedded in the results. The paper notes that if unrelated columns are drawn from closely related data, \textsf{FeatLLM} can still learn spurious correlations and misunderstand causal relationships. So the method is not infallible. Even so, the overall empirical picture is clear: when used as a feature engineer rather than as a black-box predictor, the LLM can contribute genuinely useful tabular features, and those features can lead to strong few-shot performance.

11. Ablations and Deeper Analysis

# Ablation of Weight Tuning versus Simple Rule Counting

In the full \textsf{FeatLLM} model, the rules generated by the language model are converted into binary features, and a linear model learns how much each rule should contribute to a class decision. Concretely, the logit for class $k$ is computed as $\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$, where $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ is the binary rule vector for sample $\mathbf{x}^i$. This means the model does not merely count how many rules fire; instead, it learns a separate importance weight for each rule. The positive projection of the weights is also important, because it ensures that a generated feature cannot reduce the likelihood of its class during learning.

The ablation called \textsf{-Tuning} removes this learned weighting step and replaces it with simple rule counting, so every binary feature contributes equally. The paper reports that this hurts performance across the datasets. The result is intuitive: not all rules extracted by the LLM are equally informative, and some are much more relevant to the target than others. A weighted linear model can emphasize the useful rules and suppress those that are weak or noisy, while simple counting treats them all the same and therefore loses a large part of the benefit of feature engineering.

# Ablation of Ensemble Averaging

FeatLLM does not rely on a single set of extracted rules. Instead, it repeats the full rule-generation and rule-application process multiple times, trains a separate lightweight model for each trial, and averages the resulting class probabilities. If the learned weights from the $T$ trials are denoted by $\{\mathbf{w}[t]\}_{t=1}^T$, then the final prediction is formed by averaging the probabilities produced by each trial. The ensemble is made diverse by using a sufficiently high temperature during LLM inference and by randomizing the order of the few-shot demonstrations.

The \textsf{-Ensemble} ablation removes this averaging step and keeps only one trial. The paper finds that this consistently degrades performance in every setting. The reason is that individual rule sets can be imperfect: some trials may produce incorrect rules, miss part of the signal, or focus on a subset of the relevant columns. Averaging lets correct rules from other trials compensate for those mistakes. In the paper's view, this is closely related to self-consistency: rules that repeatedly appear across trials are more likely to be reliable. The authors also note that applying the same idea to other LLM-based methods would be expensive, because it would require multiple per-sample LLM inferences and therefore substantially increase computational cost.

# Ablation of Feature Descriptions in the Prompt

A core part of the prompt is the feature-description block, which tells the LLM what each column means, what type of value it contains, and sometimes what categories or definitions apply. This information is not cosmetic. It gives the model the vocabulary it needs to formulate rules that are consistent with the data type and the task. In the paper's prompt design, feature descriptions also help the LLM avoid type mismatches when writing rules, which matters because the rules are later parsed and executed as program code.

When the authors ablate this component through \textsf{-Description}, performance drops. The effect is especially strong in low-shot settings, where there are very few labeled examples to compensate for missing context. In that regime, the prompt must carry more of the burden of telling the model what is relevant. Without feature descriptions, the LLM has less guidance about which columns are meaningful and how they should be interpreted, so the extracted rules become less useful for prediction.

# Ablation of the First Reasoning Step Based on Prior Knowledge

The reasoning instruction in \textsf{FeatLLM} deliberately splits the LLM's inference into two steps. First, the model is asked to infer the causal relationship or tendency between the task description and the features using general knowledge or common sense, without looking at example demonstrations. Only after this does it use the examples to derive class-specific rules. The paper frames this as a structured reasoning path that resembles chain-of-thought prompting, but is adapted to tabular rule extraction.

The \textsf{-Reasoning} ablation removes that first prior-knowledge step. The reported result is that this weakens performance, especially when the data are scarce. The reason given in the paper is that the first step helps the model focus on more significant features and avoid spurious correlations in irrelevant columns. Without it, the LLM has less of a chance to apply its pretrained knowledge as a filter, so rule generation becomes more vulnerable to accidental patterns in the few examples it sees.

# How the Contribution of Each Component Changes with the Number of Shots

One of the most interesting findings in the ablation study is that the importance of the different prompt and modeling components depends on how many labeled samples are available. When the number of shots is small, the feature descriptions and the reasoning instruction matter most. This makes sense in the framework of the paper: with only a few examples, the model needs extra help from the prompt to inject prior knowledge and to identify which features deserve attention.

As the number of shots increases, the effect of weight tuning becomes stronger. The paper interprets this as the point at which the linear model has enough labeled data to estimate rule importance more accurately. In other words, the binary rules are already informative, but more data lets the model learn which ones should matter more. By contrast, the ensemble mechanism helps in all settings, which is consistent with the idea that averaging over diverse trials makes the overall system more robust regardless of the amount of supervision.

# Training-Time and Inference-Time Comparison with Other Methods

The paper compares runtime on the Adult dataset using a training set of $16$ shots. In that experiment, one A100 GPU is the default hardware, except for TabLLM, which uses four A100 GPUs for model parallelism. Under this setup, \textsf{FeatLLM} shows a relatively low inference time, close to that of conventional tabular methods. By contrast, methods that require the LLM to make end-to-end predictions for every sample, such as in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM, incur much higher inference latency.

Training time is more nuanced. \textsf{FeatLLM} is still comparable to other LLM-based approaches, but it requires only $30$ API queries, and these can be parallelized. The paper emphasizes that this does not impose a major budget burden. This is important because the expensive part of \textsf{FeatLLM} is concentrated in feature discovery, not in repeated per-sample prediction. Once the rules are extracted and parsed, the final predictor is lightweight.

# Why FeatLLM's Inference Cost Is Closer to Standard Tabular Models than to Other LLM Methods

The reason \textsf{FeatLLM}'s inference cost resembles that of standard tabular models is that the LLM is removed from the prediction loop. The LLM is used to engineer rules and convert them into features, but after that, a simple model handles inference. In the paper's formulation, prediction is based on a dot product with learned weights followed by a softmax, which is much closer to a conventional linear classifier than to a black-box language model.

This design is exactly what separates \textsf{FeatLLM} from end-to-end LLM tabular methods. In those methods, each test sample must be serialized, prompted, and passed through the LLM again, so the per-sample cost stays high. \textsf{FeatLLM} moves the costly reasoning into a one-time feature-generation stage and then reuses the discovered features for cheap downstream prediction. That is why its inference profile is closer to logistic regression or other simple tabular learners than to prompt-based LLM systems.

# Measuring the Informativeness of Generated Features Using AUROC

To check whether the extracted rules are actually informative, the paper evaluates the new binary features using AUROC. For each generated feature, it measures how well that feature aligns with the target labels, and then computes the average AUROC across features. The authors also compute the ratio of useful features, where a useful feature is one whose AUROC exceeds $0.5$. In the paper's interpretation, features above that threshold carry meaningful signal for the target task.

This analysis shows that the majority of the rules produced by \textsf{FeatLLM} are relevant to the target attribute even before any tuning is applied. That is an important diagnostic result, because it means the LLM is not just generating arbitrary text rules; it is actually discovering criteria that have predictive value. The feature-quality study therefore supports the paper's central claim that the LLM can serve as an effective feature engineer rather than only as a direct predictor.

# Ratio of Useful Rules Before and After Linear-Model Tuning

The paper further examines how the set of useful rules changes after the linear model has been trained. Before tuning, many of the generated rules already have AUROC above $0.5$, indicating that they are informative on their own. After tuning, however, rules that are only weakly related to the data tend to receive very small weights, and these are effectively filtered out. The paper reports this using the "Before tuning" and "After tuning" columns in its feature-quality table.

This difference matters because it shows that the linear model is not merely a classifier on top of a fixed rule set; it is also acting as a filter. The model learns which rules deserve influence and which should be ignored. The paper sets a threshold of $0.1$ when inspecting the learned weights, based on the overall histogram of weights, and uses that to identify rules with little relevance. The resulting picture is that tuning refines the LLM's raw output into a more selective and more task-specific feature representation.

# How Tuning Filters Weak or Irrelevant Rules Through Learned Weights

The learned weights in \textsf{FeatLLM} are directly interpretable as rule importance scores. Because the model uses nonnegative weights, a rule can contribute positively to the class logit, but a weak rule can contribute very little. That is exactly why tuning is useful: it distinguishes rules that are repeatedly supported by the data from rules that are only weakly related to the target. In the paper's analysis, rules with low learned weights are treated as effectively filtered out.

This filtering mechanism is especially valuable in low-shot learning, where some extracted rules may be plausible in language but not truly predictive. The tuning stage allows the model to exploit the fact that not all prompt-derived knowledge deserves equal treatment. In this sense, the linear model is not just a downstream classifier; it is a learned selector over the candidate rules. The feature-quality analysis provides direct evidence that this selector is working as intended.

# Robustness to Spurious Correlations by Adding Unrelated Columns

To test whether \textsf{FeatLLM} can resist noisy correlations, the paper performs an experiment on the Heart dataset by randomly attaching columns from the Adult dataset that are irrelevant to the Heart task. The model is then evaluated as more unrelated columns are added. For fairness, the comparison uses the same $8$ labeled shots and does not include an unlabeled dataset, which excludes methods such as SCARF and STUNT from the experiment.

The result is that \textsf{FeatLLM} suffers the least degradation among the compared methods. The paper attributes this to the way rule extraction is guided by prior knowledge about the relationship between the task and the features. In practice, that means the LLM is less likely to generate rules based on irrelevant columns. The authors also observe that only about $1$-$2\%$ of the rules were based on noisy columns, which supports the idea that the prompt design acts as a filter against irrelevant information.

# Why the Reasoning Stage Helps Ignore Irrelevant Features

The first step of the reasoning instruction is central to this robustness. By asking the LLM to infer a causal relationship or tendency between the task and the features before looking at demonstrations, the prompt forces the model to engage with the task at a more conceptual level. That prior-knowledge step makes it less likely that the model will latch onto columns that are not genuinely connected to the target. In the paper's interpretation, this is a key reason why the framework resists spurious correlations.

This does not mean the reasoning stage is a perfect safeguard, but it does provide a meaningful bias toward relevant features. The experiment with unrelated Adult columns shows that the model can largely ignore those distractions when the task-feature relationship is clearly mismatched. That is consistent with the ablation study as well: removing the reasoning step hurts performance, which suggests that the step contributes not just to accuracy but also to robustness.

# Limits of This Robustness When Unrelated Columns Come from Closely Related Domains

The paper is careful to note that this robustness has limits. If the added columns come from a closely related dataset, \textsf{FeatLLM} can still learn spurious correlations and even misunderstand causal relationships between the task and the features. This is an important caveat, because it shows that the prior-knowledge filter is helpful but not foolproof. When irrelevant columns look semantically plausible, the LLM may be tempted to treat them as meaningful.

This limitation matters for interpreting the spurious-correlation experiment. The fact that unrelated columns from Adult are mostly ignored does not imply that the method is immune to all forms of noise. Instead, the results show that the framework is robust when the extra columns are obviously off-task, but can still be misled when the distractors are more subtle. That is a realistic and useful characterization of the method's behavior.

# Hyperparameter Analysis for the Number of Ensembles

The paper identifies the number of ensembles as one of the two main hyperparameters of \textsf{FeatLLM}. As this number increases, performance improves, but only up to a point. The reported behavior is that gains begin to converge around $20$ ensembles. This means that ensembling is important, but there are diminishing returns once enough diversity has been collected across trials.

The interpretation is straightforward: more trials increase the chance that at least some of them will generate good rules, and averaging across trials smooths out individual errors. At the same time, each additional trial costs extra LLM queries and extra feature extraction work. The convergence around $20$ shows that the method can get most of the benefit of ensembling without needing an unbounded number of repeated prompts.

# Hyperparameter Analysis for the Number of Rules per Class

The second major hyperparameter is the number of rules extracted per class in each LLM query. The paper evaluates different choices and reports that there is no statistically significant difference across the tested values, but it still chooses $10$ rules as the best compromise. That choice is based on the trade-off between prompt length and performance.

The reason for that trade-off is tied to the structure of the method. More rules increase the dimensionality of the generated feature space and can provide more information to the linear model, but they also make the prompt longer and can increase the risk of overfitting in a low-shot regime. Fewer rules keep the prompt shorter and simpler, but may leave out useful conditions. The selected value of $10$ reflects the paper's attempt to balance those competing pressures.

# Performance Trade-offs Among Prompt Length, Feature Dimensionality, Diversity, and Overfitting

Taken together, the hyperparameter analysis shows that \textsf{FeatLLM} is balancing several tensions at once. Bagging and repeated prompting improve diversity and help control prompt size, but they also introduce extra computation. More rules expand the feature representation, but they also enlarge the prompt and the downstream input dimension. More ensembles provide robustness, but only until the predictions become stable enough that further repetition adds little.

This is why the paper treats bagging and ensembling as practical tools rather than purely theoretical ones. When the tabular input is large, the prompt can become too long for a single LLM query, so bagging helps keep each trial within the available context window. At the same time, the authors argue that too many rules can lead to overfitting in the low-shot regime, while too few can underrepresent the task. The final method therefore uses a small number of carefully chosen rules, repeated across multiple diverse trials, to navigate the trade-off between expressiveness, robustness, and feasibility.

12. Limitations, Broader Impact, and Future Directions

# Why FeatLLM is designed for low-shot learning rather than large-data regimes

FeatLLM is explicitly built for the setting where labeled tabular data are scarce. The paper’s central idea is to use a large language model’s prior knowledge to extract rules from only a small number of labeled examples, and then to turn those rules into binary features that a simple downstream model can use. This design makes the method especially attractive when collecting labels is expensive or difficult, because the system can still learn useful decision criteria from a few demonstrations.

The same design also explains why the paper does not present FeatLLM as a general-purpose solution for large-data regimes. Its prompt-based rule extraction, fixed number of rules per class, and ensemble procedure are all shaped around the low-shot setting. The authors state directly that FeatLLM is designed to focus on the low-shot regime only, and they identify expanding it to larger sample sizes as future work. In other words, the method is optimized for data efficiency, not for replacing fully supervised tabular learners when abundant labeled data are already available.

The experiments and analyses reinforce this framing. The paper emphasizes performance in few-shot settings, and the ablation study shows that some components matter most when shots are small, especially the feature descriptions and reasoning instructions that help the language model use its prior knowledge effectively. This aligns with the overall philosophy of the method: FeatLLM is meant to extract maximum value from limited supervision, rather than scale in the same way as a conventional end-to-end tabular model trained on many labeled samples.

# Dependence on feature names and natural-language descriptions

FeatLLM depends strongly on the availability of meaningful feature names and accompanying natural-language descriptions. The prompt construction described in the paper includes task descriptions, feature descriptions, value types, and sometimes example category values. The serialized training samples also use feature names directly in text form. This means that the method is not operating purely on numeric arrays; it is operating on tabular data that have been translated into language.

This dependence is not incidental. The paper uses feature descriptions to guide the language model’s reasoning and to reduce type mismatches when generating rules. The two-step reasoning instruction first asks the model to understand the relationship between features and the task, then to derive rules from that understanding and the few-shot examples. That process only works well when the LLM can attach semantic meaning to the columns it sees. If feature names are unclear, missing, or poorly described, the prompt loses much of the structure that makes rule extraction possible.

For that reason, FeatLLM is best understood as a method that leverages metadata-rich tabular datasets. The paper’s formulation assumes natural-language feature names and separate definitions, which is a strength when such information exists but also a constraint when tables are sparse in semantic labeling. The method is therefore tightly coupled to the quality of the textual descriptions surrounding the data.

# Potential brittleness in rule generation and code execution

A central engineering challenge in FeatLLM is that the language model generates rules in natural language, but the rules must then be converted into executable logic. The paper notes that even when the response format is constrained, the LLM can introduce noisy syntactic changes. For example, symbols such as $>$ or $<$ may be replaced by phrases like “greater than” or “less than,” which makes straightforward parsing difficult.

To handle this, the authors do not build a complex handwritten parser. Instead, they use the LLM itself to generate code from the inferred rules, and then execute that code with Python’s `exec()` function. This choice takes advantage of the model’s ability to understand semantics even when the surface form changes, but it also makes the pipeline sensitive to how well the model follows the requested structure. The system depends on the model producing rules that can be faithfully translated into code and on the generated code correctly reflecting the intended conditions.

This is where the method can become brittle. Because rule generation, code generation, and feature construction are all chained together, an error at any stage can affect the downstream classifier. The paper tries to reduce this risk with response-format instructions, rule-format instructions, and a fixed number of rules per class, but the need for these safeguards shows that the process is not automatically robust. The more a workflow depends on the language model’s exact wording and formatting, the more careful the surrounding prompt design must be.

# Biases and misinformation embedded in LLM prior knowledge

The paper’s broader impact section highlights an important concern: the same prior knowledge that makes an LLM useful can also carry biases and misinformation. Since FeatLLM uses the model’s prior knowledge directly when generating rules, those hidden assumptions are not abstract background information. They can become part of the learned feature set and therefore influence predictions.

This is especially important because the paper notes that prior knowledge can directly affect predictions, even dominating over the observed labeled data. That is a major strength when the prior knowledge is accurate and relevant, but it becomes a risk when the model’s internal knowledge is incomplete, biased, or outdated. In FeatLLM, the language model is not merely summarizing text; it is helping define the criteria by which examples are judged. If that knowledge is flawed, the resulting rules may also be flawed.

The authors explicitly call out the need for future analyses of the societal biases and misinformation embedded in LLM prior knowledge. This is not only a technical issue but also a scientific one, because it affects whether the method can be trusted as a source of inference. A framework that embeds prior knowledge into feature engineering must confront the possibility that it is also embedding the model’s mistakes.

# Risks in sensitive domains such as healthcare and finance

The paper identifies finance and healthcare as particularly promising application areas because labels are often costly to obtain and domain knowledge is valuable. Those are exactly the kinds of settings where FeatLLM’s low-shot design could be useful. In such domains, the paper suggests that an LLM’s pretrained knowledge may complement the limited labeled data available to practitioners.

At the same time, these are also domains where the consequences of incorrect rules can be serious. Because FeatLLM uses rules derived from an LLM’s prior knowledge, any bias or misinformation in that prior knowledge can propagate directly into decisions. The broader impact discussion makes this dependency clear: the model’s knowledge can shape predictions even when only a small amount of labeled data is present. That means the model’s influence may be especially strong in precisely the contexts where caution matters most.

The paper does not claim that FeatLLM is a substitute for expert oversight. Instead, it frames the method as a data-efficient tool that can help in settings with labeling difficulty. The practical implication is that deployment in sensitive domains should be accompanied by careful attention to the source and quality of the model’s prior knowledge. The framework is promising, but the paper itself makes clear that its usefulness comes with responsibility.

# Interpretability benefits and current limits

One of FeatLLM’s main conceptual contributions is interpretability. Unlike end-to-end LLM prediction systems, FeatLLM extracts explicit rules and converts them into binary features. The downstream linear model then learns weights that reflect the importance of each rule. This means the method does not just output a label; it also creates a visible intermediate structure showing which conditions were considered and how much they contributed to the prediction.

The paper further supports this interpretation by showing that unimportant rules can be filtered out after tuning, and by analyzing the informativeness of generated features with respect to the target labels. This makes the workflow more transparent than a black-box prompt-to-label pipeline. In principle, users can inspect the rules, see which ones are retained by the linear model, and understand the rough logic used for classification.

Still, the interpretability is partial rather than complete. The paper shows that FeatLLM can sometimes learn spurious correlations, especially when closely related noisy columns are attached to the data. It also notes that rules based on noisy columns can still appear, even if they are relatively rare. So while the method improves interpretability by making the decision process explicit, those explicit rules are not guaranteed to be correct, causal, or semantically pristine. Interpretability here means visibility into the reasoning path, not automatic truth.

# Why API-only access makes the method practical for modern closed LLMs

A practical advantage of FeatLLM is that it only requires API-level access to the language model. This matters because the paper points out that many top-performing LLMs are available only through inference APIs and do not expose training codebases or tuning interfaces. By avoiding fine-tuning, FeatLLM becomes usable with models that cannot be modified directly.

This is a significant departure from methods that rely on parameter-efficient tuning. Those methods may achieve strong performance, but they depend on access to training or adaptation mechanisms that are not always available. FeatLLM instead uses in-context learning to extract rules and then trains only a simple downstream model. As a result, the method is more compatible with the current ecosystem of closed or restricted LLM services.

The paper also emphasizes that FeatLLM reduces inference cost because the LLM is not queried for each test sample. Once the rules are extracted and translated into features, prediction is handled by the simpler model. This makes the method more practical for real-world deployment than end-to-end LLM tabular predictors, especially when repeated sample-by-sample inference would be expensive.

# Future work on richer feature types beyond rules

The paper’s conclusion is clear that rules are only the starting point. FeatLLM currently generates rule-based features, but the authors state that a future goal is to explore various feature types beyond rules. This is an important direction because the rule-based representation is both powerful and restrictive: it captures explicit conditions, but it may not express every kind of structure that matters in tabular data.

Moving beyond rules could broaden the method’s range of applications while preserving its basic philosophy of LLM-assisted feature engineering. The paper does not specify what those richer feature types will be, but it clearly identifies this as an open direction. The key idea is to continue using the LLM to craft informative features, rather than limiting the system to a single symbolic format.

This future work also connects to the paper’s note that FeatLLM is currently focused on low-shot learning. If the framework can generate richer features, it may become more useful in settings where the tabular structure is more complex or where the task requires a more expressive representation than simple rule satisfaction.

# Extending the framework to larger datasets and broader task families

The paper explicitly says that FeatLLM is designed for low-shot learning and that expanding to datasets with a larger number of samples is a future goal. This extension matters because larger datasets raise different challenges from the few-shot regime. The current system relies on prompt engineering, a fixed number of rules, and repeated bagged trials. Those choices are well suited to scarce labels, but the paper suggests that they may need to evolve to handle larger-scale settings.

Broadening the task family is another natural direction identified by the authors. The conclusion mentions the possibility of applying the framework across a wider array of tasks, which implies that the current formulation is not yet meant to be universal. Since the present work focuses on classification tasks over tabular data, future extensions would need to test how well the idea transfers when the structure of the problem changes.

The paper’s own results suggest that the framework’s core strength is not tied to any single dataset but to a general principle: use the language model to infer criteria, then learn a lightweight predictor over those criteria. Extending that principle to more samples and more task types would test how far the approach can go while preserving its low-cost and data-efficient character.

# Improving interpretability, reliability, and safety of LLM-assisted feature engineering

The paper already takes steps toward reliability through ensemble learning and bagging. Multiple rule sets are generated, and their predictions are averaged so that incorrect rules from one trial can be compensated by correct rules from another. This helps because the model benefits from diversity across trials and from the self-consistency of rules that recur often.

Even so, the paper makes clear that reliability remains an open issue. The method can still infer spurious correlations or misunderstand causal relationships, especially when noisy but related columns are introduced. That means future work should continue improving not only the predictive performance of the framework but also the trustworthiness of the generated features. The broader impact discussion points directly to the need to analyze the biases and misinformation inside the LLM’s prior knowledge, which is closely tied to safety.

Interpretability and safety also belong together here. The value of FeatLLM is that it exposes rules and feature importances, but those same explanations can become misleading if the underlying knowledge is biased. Future work, then, is not only about making the rules more readable, but about making them more dependable and better aligned with the actual task. In this sense, the paper sets up a broader research agenda around LLM-assisted feature engineering that is human-readable, robust, and safe enough for real deployment.

# Final synthesis: what FeatLLM contributes to the evolution of tabular machine learning

FeatLLM reframes the role of large language models in tabular learning. Instead of using the LLM as a black-box predictor that must be queried for every sample, the paper uses it as a feature engineer that extracts rules from a small number of labeled examples. Those rules are converted into binary features, and a simple downstream model learns how to combine them. This is the paper’s core innovation.

The result is a method that is particularly well matched to low-shot tabular learning. It avoids fine-tuning, works with API-only access, reduces inference cost, and addresses prompt-size limitations through ensemble bagging. The empirical results show that this design can outperform competing methods across a broad set of datasets, including ones with many features. At the same time, the paper is candid about the method’s constraints: it is built for low-shot settings, it depends on meaningful feature descriptions, and it can inherit the biases and mistakes of the language model’s prior knowledge.

Taken together, these strengths and limitations make FeatLLM an important step in the evolution of tabular machine learning. The paper does not claim that LLMs should replace conventional models. Instead, it shows that LLMs can contribute a different kind of value: they can help discover features and rules that make tabular prediction more data-efficient. That shift from end-to-end prediction to structured feature engineering is the central contribution, and it points toward a broader future in which LLMs serve as tools for building better tabular learners rather than merely acting as the learners themselves.
