\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 2: 2. Background: Existing Approaches to Few-Shot Tabular Learning

# Conventional tabular baselines: logistic regression, random forests, and gradient-boosted trees

A natural starting point for few-shot tabular learning is the family of standard supervised models that have long been used on structured data. In the paper, these include logistic regression, random forests, and XGBoost, which represents gradient-boosted trees. These methods are familiar because they are simple to train, easy to deploy, and often strong on tabular problems when enough labeled data are available. In the few-shot setting, however, the learner only sees a small labeled subset of the data, so the central question is whether these conventional models can still generalize well when there are only $k$ labeled examples rather than a large training set.

The paper treats these models as important baselines because they do not rely on any external language model or special pretraining strategy. They learn directly from the labeled tabular samples, which makes them conceptually straightforward. At the same time, this simplicity also reveals their weakness in low-data regimes: with only a handful of examples, they may not recover the deeper patterns that connect the input features to the target label. The paper emphasizes that few-shot tabular tasks are especially vulnerable to spurious correlations, so a baseline that only fits the observed labels can easily miss the underlying structure. This is why conventional tabular methods remain relevant as reference points, but are often not enough on their own when labels are scarce.

# Semi-supervised and self-supervised methods for tabular data

Because labeled data can be expensive, one major line of work tries to compensate by exploiting unlabeled data. The paper describes semi-supervised approaches that use large quantities of unlabeled samples together with carefully designed augmentation procedures, as well as self-supervised approaches that pretrain models by corrupting or masking part of the data and then asking the model to reconstruct it. The intuition is that even without labels, the model can still learn useful representations of the data distribution, and those representations may transfer to the downstream prediction task.

Several methods in this family are mentioned in the paper as examples of prior work on tabular learning. Some methods use unlabeled datasets to improve training, while others rely on reconstruction objectives or contrastive learning objectives to pretrain a representation before the few-shot task begins. The paper also notes that such strategies can provide useful inductive biases, especially when the labeled set is very small. In principle, this reduces the chance that the model will simply memorize a few training examples or lock onto accidental correlations.

However, the paper also points out that these methods do not fully solve the few-shot problem. If the available unlabeled data are limited, mismatched, or hard to augment in a useful way, the benefit can be modest. Likewise, if the representation learned during pretraining does not align well with the target task, the model may not transfer effectively. This is one reason that self-supervised and semi-supervised tabular methods, while promising, have not become universally effective solutions for low-shot tabular prediction.

# Why augmentation and pretraining are harder for heterogeneous tabular features than for images or text

A key difficulty for tabular learning is that tables are heterogeneous. Unlike images, where pixels share a common structure, or text, where tokens belong to a sequential language format, tabular features can differ sharply from one another in type and meaning. Some columns are numeric, some are categorical, and others may have specialized definitions that only make sense in a particular domain. The paper stresses that this heterogeneity makes it difficult to design augmentations that work well across datasets.

This matters because many self-supervised methods depend on augmentations or corruption schemes. The challenge is that there is no universally good way to perturb table entries without potentially damaging the semantics of the data. A transformation that is harmless in one column may be meaningless or even misleading in another. The paper therefore argues that methods that rely on general-purpose augmentations, which are often effective in other domains, are much less reliable for tabular data. In few-shot settings, where every labeled example is already precious, poor augmentations can be especially harmful because they may obscure the true structure rather than reveal it.

The paper also suggests that the diversity of tabular domains makes pretraining difficult to standardize. Tabular datasets can come from healthcare, finance, biology, or synthetic tasks, and each domain may encode different kinds of feature relationships. That means a pretraining objective that works well on one collection of tables may not translate well to another. In short, tabular learning is not just a smaller-data version of image or text learning; its feature structure is fundamentally less uniform, and that makes generic augmentation and pretraining strategies harder to apply.

# Transfer learning for tables: TransTab, TabPFN, and related ideas

Another response to data scarcity is transfer learning. Instead of learning only from the target task, these methods train on many tabular datasets first and then transfer what they learned to a new task. The paper describes this family as using a broad range of tables beyond the target dataset, with the goal of learning priors that can support zero-shot or few-shot inference on new problems. This idea is appealing because the model can accumulate experience across tasks, much like a human who has seen many tables before encountering a new one.

The paper highlights TransTab as one example. TransTab uses transformer-based models and learns semantic relationships between columns through column names, not just through the raw table values. This is important because column names can carry meaningful hints about what each feature represents. By leveraging both the names and the values, the model can build a richer understanding of the table structure. The paper notes that knowledge learned from many different tables and columns can then help on unseen tasks, especially in zero-shot or few-shot regimes.

TabPFN is another representative approach. According to the paper, it mixes different types of distributions to create synthetic data for pretraining a Bayesian neural network. After this pretraining, inference on a target task is performed by providing both the training and test samples, without any additional task-specific training. The paper presents this as a strong example of how broad prior knowledge can improve few-shot performance. In contrast to methods that learn only from the current target dataset, TabPFN tries to bake in knowledge about many possible data distributions ahead of time.

# Language-interfaced tabular learning through serialization of tables into text

The paper next turns to a different strategy: using large language models as a bridge between language and tables. The basic idea is to serialize tabular data into text and then place that text into an LLM prompt. In this setup, the model receives not only the table values but also feature descriptions and a task description written in natural language. This allows the LLM to use its prior knowledge about the world and about the task context when making predictions. The paper emphasizes that this approach is attractive because LLMs are trained on extensive and diverse text corpora, so they may already know useful facts that are not present in the few labeled samples.

This interface between tables and language is central to recent LLM-based tabular methods. A table row can be rewritten as a natural-language description such as “feature $f_1$ is value $x_1$” and so on, followed by the answer label. The paper notes that earlier work adopts precisely this kind of serialization so that the LLM can process structured data as text. Once the table has been converted into language, the model can reason over feature names, feature definitions, and the task prompt in a way that resembles ordinary question answering.

At the same time, this approach changes the role of the LLM. Rather than being used only as a generic text generator, the model is asked to interpret tabular structure and infer how features relate to the target label. The paper frames this as an effort to bring prior knowledge into tabular learning, especially when the labeled data are scarce. But the cost of using language as the interface is that prediction now depends heavily on the quality of the prompt and on the LLM’s ability to process long serialized tables.

# In-context learning for tabular tasks

A particularly important variant of language-interfaced tabular learning is in-context learning. In this approach, the model is not fine-tuned at all. Instead, a few labeled examples are placed directly into the prompt, and the LLM is expected to infer the pattern from those demonstrations. The paper describes this as embedding few-shot training samples into the input prompt without tuning. The idea is that the examples provide enough context for the LLM to infer the task structure and then answer a new query.

The appeal of in-context learning is that it avoids the need for task-specific parameter updates. If the LLM already has strong general reasoning ability, then perhaps it can use the prompt examples as a temporary training set. In tabular problems, this means the model can inspect a small number of rows, note the feature names and values, and attempt to infer which patterns distinguish the classes. The paper positions this as one of the first ways to bring LLM reasoning into low-shot tabular prediction without explicit retraining.

But in-context learning also inherits the practical limitations of prompt-based inference. The model must process all of the relevant information every time it makes a prediction, and the prompt must remain within the model’s context window. As the number of features grows, the serialized table becomes longer, and the task becomes harder to fit into the prompt. This is one reason that, despite its elegance, in-context learning is not always a practical solution for large tabular datasets.

# Parameter-efficient tuning approaches such as LoRA and IA3

Another major direction is to fine-tune the LLM, but in a way that changes only a small part of the model. The paper mentions parameter-efficient tuning methods such as LoRA and IA3. These methods aim to make adaptation cheaper and more feasible than full fine-tuning by limiting the number of trainable parameters. In the tabular setting, the hope is that a pretrained LLM can be adapted to a specific task using only a few labeled examples and a lightweight tuning mechanism.

This strategy is attractive because it allows the model to retain much of its general knowledge while still specializing to the target dataset. The paper treats this as one of the main pathways by which LLMs have been applied to tabular learning. Instead of training a large model from scratch, the system can be adjusted in a controlled way to better reflect the target task. That makes parameter-efficient tuning a natural compromise between using a frozen model and fully retraining a large one.

Still, the paper notes that tuning is not always possible in practice. Some large models are available only through inference APIs, which means users cannot access their training code or modify their parameters. In those cases, even parameter-efficient methods may be infeasible. This practical constraint is a major reason the paper seeks a method that can work with API-only access and no model training.

# Representative LLM-tabular systems: TabLLM, TABLET, and related methods

The paper discusses several representative systems that combine LLMs with tabular learning. TabLLM is one such example. It uses parameter-efficient tuning, specifically a method like IA3, to train the LLM on few-shot tabular data. The paper notes that this approach depends on publicly available checkpoints, which makes it less flexible than methods that can work with any API-accessible LLM. TabLLM is therefore a useful example of the power of LLM-based tabular learning, but also of its dependence on model access.

TABLET is another representative system. According to the paper, TABLET adds additional hints to the prompt by using an external classifier together with rule sets and prototypes. This means the LLM is not asked to work entirely from raw serialized data; instead, the method supplies extra structure intended to improve inference. The general idea is that the prompt can be strengthened by including information that helps the LLM focus on the most relevant aspects of the task.

These methods show that the field has moved beyond simple prompt formatting. Researchers have tried tuning, prompt augmentation, and external hints to make LLMs better at tabular prediction. The paper places FeatLLM in this landscape as a different idea: rather than using the LLM to produce the final prediction for every sample, it asks the LLM to extract useful rules that can later be used by a simpler downstream model. That is a shift from end-to-end prediction toward feature engineering.

# Key limitations of prior LLM-based methods: per-sample inference cost, fine-tuning requirements, and prompt length constraints

The paper argues that the main weakness of existing LLM-based tabular systems is not just accuracy, but practicality. First, many methods require at least one LLM inference for every test sample. That means the model must repeatedly process the serialized table and make a fresh prediction each time. This is expensive, especially when the number of samples is large. Even if the LLM is accurate, the inference cost can become a serious obstacle in real applications.

Second, many methods require fine-tuning. This is a problem when the LLM is not openly trainable or when the training code is not accessible. The paper stresses that recent high-performance LLMs often allow only inference via API, so methods that assume direct parameter updates cannot always be used. This creates a gap between what is technically possible in research settings and what is actually available in deployment settings.

Third, prompt length is a fundamental constraint. Tabular datasets can contain many attributes, and when each feature must be serialized into natural language, the prompt can become very long. The paper notes that this becomes especially problematic for datasets with over $100$ attributes. Once the prompt exceeds the available context window, the method may become infeasible or suffer a drop in performance. This limitation is one of the clearest reasons the paper seeks a different strategy: instead of asking the LLM to reason over the entire table for every prediction, FeatLLM uses the LLM to extract rules once, then hands the resulting features to a simple downstream model.

Together, these limitations explain the motivation for FeatLLM. Earlier methods showed that LLMs can help on tabular tasks, but they often did so at the cost of heavy inference, tuning requirements, or prompt-size fragility. The background of the field, then, is a progression from conventional tabular baselines, to unlabeled-data methods, to transfer learning, and finally to LLM-based prompting and tuning. FeatLLM builds on that history by keeping the language model’s prior knowledge while moving the final prediction step into a lighter and more practical tabular pipeline.
