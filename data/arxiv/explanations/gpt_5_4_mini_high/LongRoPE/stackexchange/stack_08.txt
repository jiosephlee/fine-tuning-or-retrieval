\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What is the mathematical meaning of doing a 'second positional interpolation' after fine-tuning to 256k?
Question:
The progressive strategy in LongRoPE is:

1. search RoPE factors for $128\text{k}$ and $256\text{k}$,
2. fine-tune the model to $256\text{k}$,
3. do a second search on the fine-tuned $256\text{k}$ model to reach $2048\text{k}$ without further fine-tuning.

I am confused about how step 3 should be interpreted mathematically. After fine-tuning, the model weights have adapted to one modified RoPE schedule. Then the paper changes the RoPE schedule again.

Should I think of this second stage as composing two interpolation maps, or is it just searching a brand-new set of $\lambda_i$ values using the fine-tuned model as the evaluation target? Also, why is this easier than searching directly for $2048\text{k}$ on the original pre-trained model?
Answer:
In LongRoPE, the “second positional interpolation” is best understood as a **fresh search for a new RoPE rescaling scheme on top of the fine-tuned checkpoint**, not as literally composing two interpolation maps in the final deployed model.

A clean way to write what the paper is doing is this. Let $W_0$ be the original pretrained weights, and let $R_{\lambda}$ denote the RoPE map defined by the searched rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and threshold $\hat n$ in the paper’s Eq. (problem). First, they search a schedule for $256\text{k}$ and fine-tune the model under that schedule, producing weights $W_{256}$:
$$
W_{256} \approx \arg\min_W \mathcal{L}(W; R_{\lambda^{256}}, \text{long-context training data}).
$$
Then, for the second stage, they **freeze those fine-tuned weights** and search again for a new schedule $\lambda^{2048}$:
$$
\lambda^{2048} \approx \arg\min_{\lambda} \mathcal{L}(W_{256}; R_{\lambda}, \mathbf{X}),
$$
where $\mathbf{X}$ are the evaluation samples used by the evolutionary search. So the second stage is simply “search a brand-new set of $\lambda_i$ values, but evaluate them on the fine-tuned model,” exactly as you suspected.

This is why I would not think of it as composing two interpolation maps in any essential sense. The first schedule is used to adapt the weights during fine-tuning; the second schedule is used later for inference/search on the adapted model. The paper does not describe the final model as applying the $256\text{k}$ interpolation and then applying another interpolation on top of it. Rather, it uses the fine-tuned checkpoint as a better starting point and then **replaces** the RoPE factors with a new searched set for the larger window.

Why is this easier than searching directly for $2048\text{k}$ on the original model? The paper’s answer is basically a continuation argument. The original pretrained model is extremely far from the $2048\text{k}$ regime: many positions are unseen, the optimization is brittle, and direct long-context fine-tuning is expensive and hard to stabilize. By first extending to $256\text{k}$ and fine-tuning there, the model weights are already adapted to long-range behavior and to a nontrivial RoPE modification. Then the second search only needs to get from $256\text{k}$ to $2048\text{k}$, which is an additional $8\times$ extension, and the paper explicitly claims that LongRoPE can do about that much extension without further fine-tuning. So the second stage is easier both because the model is already “in the right neighborhood” and because the remaining extrapolation problem is much smaller than jumping straight from $4\text{k}$ to $2048\text{k}$.

So the short answer is: **it is a new search on the fine-tuned model, not a literal composition of two RoPE interpolations**. The progressive strategy works because fine-tuning at $256\text{k}$ makes the model less out-of-distribution, and the second search only has to solve the remaining extension from that better base.
