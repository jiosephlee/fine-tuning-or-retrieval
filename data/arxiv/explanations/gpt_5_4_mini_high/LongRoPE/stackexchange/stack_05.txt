\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why is a monotonic constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ considered reasonable, and could it exclude the true optimum?
Question:
LongRoPE imposes a non-decreasing monotonicity constraint on the searched interpolation factors across RoPE dimensions:

$\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$.

The motivation given is that lower dimensions are higher frequency and should require less interpolation, which sounds plausible. But this also seems like a strong prior over the search space.

My confusion is: if the whole point of the paper is that existing hand-designed interpolation rules are suboptimal, why is it safe to hard-code this monotonic shape? Could the best-performing solution in principle be non-monotonic across dimensions?

I would appreciate either an intuition or a more formal reason for why this constraint is not too restrictive.
Answer:
The important thing to notice is that LongRoPE is not claiming that $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ is a theorem about the best possible RoPE scaling. It is a search prior. The paper uses it because the RoPE dimensions are already ordered by frequency, with $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$, so smaller $i$ means higher frequency. In that setting, the authors adopt the natural Fourier/NTK intuition that higher-frequency components are the ones that are most sensitive to positional crowding and therefore should be interpolated less, while lower-frequency components can be interpolated more aggressively. Since $\lambda$ appears in the denominator of the angle, a larger $\lambda_i$ means more interpolation; the monotonic constraint is therefore saying “as frequency gets lower, interpolation can increase.”

So the constraint is “reasonable” in the same sense that it is reasonable to regularize a search with an inductive bias: it cuts away many configurations that are hard to justify physically and are very likely to be wasteful to evaluate. That matters a lot here because the search space is enormous, and the paper explicitly says the constraint is introduced to reduce search cost and avoid obviously poor mutations and crossovers. The authors are not pretending that arbitrary oscillating patterns across dimensions are impossible; they are saying that, empirically, the useful solutions they want live in a much smaller family where interpolation varies smoothly with frequency.

Could the true optimum be non-monotonic? In principle, yes. Once you impose any constraint, you exclude some candidates, so a globally optimal unconstrained solution could lie outside the monotone subset. The paper does not prove otherwise. What it is claiming is more modest: given the RoPE frequency ordering and the NTK motivation, monotonicity is a strong but plausible prior, and in practice it seems to preserve the kinds of solutions that matter while making the evolutionary search tractable. That is why it is best read as an efficiency-and-robustness heuristic, not as a statement of optimality.

Intuitively, this is very similar to how one would allocate a limited “resolution budget” across Fourier bands: keep the fast-varying bands as intact as possible, and allow the slow-varying bands to absorb more compression. LongRoPE’s monotonicity constraint encodes exactly that bias.
