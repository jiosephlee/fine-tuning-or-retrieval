\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why does short-context performance degrade after extreme RoPE interpolation, even for positions inside the original 4k window?
Question:
The paper says that after extending the model to $2048\text{k}$, performance within the original short context window drops, because positions inside $4\text{k}$ become "crowded." I don't fully understand this.

If the original model already handled positions $0$ through $4095$, why would those same positions become harder after interpolation? They are still numerically small positions.

Is the issue that under a large scale factor, the angular differences between nearby positions become too small, so the model can no longer distinguish local order as well? If so, can someone explain this using the RoPE formula directly, maybe in terms of how $\cos\left(\frac{n}{\lambda_i \beta^i}\right)$ changes more slowly with $n$ when $\lambda_i$ is large?
Answer:
Yes — the core issue is not the raw token index $n$, but the **angle that RoPE assigns to that index**.

In RoPE, position $n$ in dimension $i$ is encoded by a phase like

$$
\phi_i(n) = \frac{n}{\beta^i},
$$

so the embedding contains $\cos(\phi_i(n))$ and $\sin(\phi_i(n))$. After interpolation, that becomes

$$
\phi_i^{\text{interp}}(n) = \frac{n}{\lambda_i \beta^i}.
$$

So even if $n$ is still in the original range $0,\dots,4095$, the model is **not seeing the original short-context encoding anymore**. It is seeing a compressed version of it.

That is why the paper says the positions become “crowded.” Under a large scale factor, neighboring positions are mapped to very similar phases. The local separation between two adjacent tokens is

$$
\Delta \phi_i = \phi_i(n+1)-\phi_i(n)=\frac{1}{\lambda_i \beta^i},
$$

which is $\lambda_i$ times smaller than in the pretrained model. For extreme extension ratios like $512\times$, this shrinkage is severe. In other words, the model loses angular resolution: many distinct short-range positions now occupy a much narrower region of the RoPE space.

This matters because self-attention does not care about the integer label “position 1000” by itself; it cares about the **relative phase differences** between tokens. RoPE was trained so that certain phase changes correspond to one-token, two-token, and longer-distance offsets. If you divide all those phase changes by a huge factor, then nearby tokens become harder to distinguish, and the short-range positional patterns the model learned during pretraining no longer line up with what it is being fed.

So your intuition is basically correct: with a large $\lambda_i$, $\cos\!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda_i\beta^i}\right)$ and $\sin\!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda_i\beta^i}\right)$ vary more slowly with $n$, and that reduces the model’s ability to represent fine local order. The paper’s “crowding” language is just describing this compression of the positional geometry.

One subtle point is that the paper attributes the degradation especially to the **higher RoPE dimensions** during extreme interpolation. Those dimensions are already lower frequency, so when you compress them further, they can become nearly flat across the original $4\text{k}$ window. That is why LongRoPE later does an extra search for shorter contexts and reduces the allowed interpolation there: the original short-range resolution has to be partially restored.

So the short answer is: the positions are still numerically small, but after interpolation they are no longer encoded in the same way. Extreme interpolation makes the phase spacing too fine, the embeddings for nearby positions become too similar, and the model loses short-context precision even inside the original window.
