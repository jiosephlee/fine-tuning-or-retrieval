\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why is perplexity on only a few validation samples enough to guide such a huge evolutionary search?
Question:
The search procedure in LongRoPE evaluates candidate RoPE schedules by perplexity on a very small number of long validation samples, such as $5$ PG19 samples or $3$ Books3 samples depending on the target length.

I understand that full evaluation would be too expensive, but I am worried about overfitting the RoPE schedule to those few documents. Since the search space is enormous, it seems possible that evolution would exploit idiosyncrasies of the chosen samples rather than finding a generally good interpolation pattern.

Is there some reason to believe that perplexity under different RoPE schedules is stable enough across long documents that a tiny search set is sufficient? Or is this more of an empirical engineering choice?
Answer:
Short answer: it is mostly an empirical engineering choice, but it is not as fragile as “5 documents” sounds.

What LongRoPE is doing is not training a model to memorize those validation documents; it is using them as a noisy fitness signal to rank RoPE schedules. That matters because the search objective is global and content-independent: a candidate schedule changes the positional geometry for every sequence in the same way. So unlike fitting weights, there is not much scope for classic document-level overfitting. The thing being optimized is the next-token loss $\mathcal{L}$ under a particular RoPE rescaling, and the hope is that a schedule that preserves positional information well on a few very long sequences will also preserve it on other long sequences.

The other reason this can work is that each “sample” is not small in token count. A single PG19 or Books3 document spans many thousands or millions of tokens, so even 3–5 documents produce a large number of token-level loss terms. In other words, the search is not really based on 5 independent data points in the usual statistical sense; it is based on a very large number of next-token predictions aggregated over a few long contexts. That makes the perplexity estimate much less noisy than the raw document count suggests.

The paper also strongly regularizes the search space. It does not let evolution explore arbitrary schedules: the per-dimension scale factors $\lambda_i$ are constrained to be monotone, and the initial population is seeded with PI, NTK, and YaRN. So the algorithm is not “discovering” a schedule from scratch in an unconstrained combinatorial space; it is searching around already reasonable interpolation patterns. That greatly reduces the chance that evolution just exploits quirks of a few documents.

Importantly, the paper does not give a formal guarantee that 5 or 3 samples are enough. It is an empirical choice motivated by the cost of evaluating perplexity on extremely long inputs, and by the scarcity of such long texts. The evidence that this proxy is good enough is the downstream result: the schedules found from this tiny search set generalize to full PG19, Proof-pile, and Books3 evaluations, and they also transfer to passkey retrieval and standard short-context benchmarks. So the paper’s argument is essentially, “we used a cheap proxy, and the proxy worked in practice,” not “few-shot validation is theoretically sufficient.”

So your intuition about possible overfitting is reasonable in principle, but the paper’s combination of very long validation sequences, constrained search, and strong cross-dataset results is what makes the approach credible.
