\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# One Finetuning Method, Many AI Domains: How BOFT Works Across Language, Vision, and Image Generation

Foundation models are now strong enough that the real challenge is often not building them from scratch, but adapting them well. That is why parameter-efficient finetuning has become such an important topic: we want to reuse the same pretrained model across many tasks, without paying the cost of full retraining.

The BOFT paper makes a strong case that orthogonal finetuning can be part of that reusable foundation-model stack. More importantly, it shows this is not just a niche technique for one model family. BOFT was tested on large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models, and the same core method worked across all of them. That breadth is the main story of the paper.

## Why BOFT is interesting beyond the theory

BOFT stands for Orthogonal Butterfly, and it builds on Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT. The basic idea behind orthogonal finetuning is simple: instead of rewriting a pretrained weight matrix from scratch, you transform it with an orthogonal matrix. Because orthogonal transforms preserve angles, they help retain the structure learned during pretraining.

The problem with the original OFT is that a full orthogonal matrix is expensive to learn. A block-diagonal version reduces the number of parameters, but it also imposes a rigid sparsity pattern that may be too restrictive. BOFT keeps the orthogonal idea, but replaces the block-diagonal structure with a butterfly factorization. In practice, that means BOFT can represent dense orthogonal transformations while using far fewer parameters, with the butterfly case scaling like $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$.

That technical detail matters because it explains why BOFT can be used more broadly. It is not just “orthogonal finetuning, but smaller.” It is a way to make orthogonal finetuning practical enough to use across different architectures.

## The real contribution: it works across very different model families

The paper’s empirical section is what makes BOFT feel important. The authors do not limit themselves to one setting. They apply BOFT to:

- large vision transformers,
- large language models, and
- text-to-image diffusion models.

This is significant because these systems behave very differently. Vision transformers process images with attention-based architectures. Large language models operate over discrete tokens and long-range sequence dependencies. Diffusion models are generative systems with a very different optimization and sampling setup. A method that adapts well across all three is not just solving one benchmark problem. It is pointing at a more general finetuning principle.

The paper reports that BOFT outperforms current state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin in these adaptation settings. Just as importantly, this is the first time orthogonal finetuning is shown to be useful outside the original controllable text-to-image generation setting. That expansion matters because it suggests orthogonal finetuning is not tied to one specific generative workflow. It may be a generally useful way to adapt pretrained weights while preserving useful structure.

## Why breadth of applicability matters

A lot of parameter-efficient methods are good at one thing and less convincing elsewhere. Low-rank updates, prompts, and adapters each have their place, but they often come with different tradeoffs depending on the model family. The BOFT results are interesting because they suggest something more universal: a structured orthogonal update can be a good finetuning prior across multiple domains.

That points to a broader trend in foundation models. As models get larger, it becomes less realistic to train many separate task-specific models from scratch. Instead, the future likely belongs to shared backbones that can be adapted cheaply and reliably. For that to work, the adaptation method needs to be robust enough to handle different modalities and different downstream objectives.

BOFT is a good example of this direction. It tries to preserve pretrained knowledge through orthogonal transformations, while still giving the model enough flexibility to adapt. The butterfly structure adds a useful bias: it keeps the update structured, but not overly rigid. The result is a smaller parameter space that still appears expressive enough for real downstream tasks.

## A practical bonus: no inference penalty

One reason model finetuning remains attractive compared to adapters or prompt tuning is that it does not add inference latency. BOFT keeps that advantage. The learned orthogonal factors can be multiplied into the pretrained model after finetuning, so deployment does not require an extra runtime module.

That makes BOFT especially appealing for settings where you want both efficiency and clean inference-time behavior. You pay a bit more during training because the final orthogonal matrix is built from multiple factors, but you do not carry that cost into production.

## What this says about reusable foundation models

The big takeaway from BOFT is not just that a clever matrix factorization saves parameters. It is that reusable foundation models may benefit from stronger structural priors than we usually give them. If a single finetuning method can adapt language models, vision transformers, and diffusion systems, then the method is probably capturing something deeper than task-specific tricks.

BOFT suggests that the geometry of the weight update matters. By keeping the update orthogonal and structured, it preserves useful properties of the pretrained model while still allowing adaptation. In the paper’s view, that is exactly the kind of balance we want: enough flexibility to learn the new task, but enough regularity to avoid destroying what pretraining already gives us.

## Closing thoughts

BOFT is not just an incremental improvement over OFT. It is a sign that orthogonal finetuning can be turned into a practical, general-purpose finetuning strategy. The paper’s experiments across large language models, vision transformers, and text-to-image diffusion models make that point clearly.

If the next generation of AI systems is going to be built on a small number of reusable foundation models, then methods like BOFT matter a lot. They show that we may not need to retrain everything. We may just need better ways to adapt what we already have.
