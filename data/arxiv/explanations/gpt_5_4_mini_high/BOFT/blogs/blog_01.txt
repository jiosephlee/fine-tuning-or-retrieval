\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# A Smarter Way to Update Giant AI Models Without Retraining Them

Large foundation models are incredibly powerful, but they are also expensive to train and expensive to adapt. Once a model has been pretrained on massive data, the practical question is no longer “How do we build it from scratch?” but “How do we make it useful for my task without paying for another full training run?”

This paper is about that second problem. It looks at parameter-efficient finetuning, or PEFT: methods that adapt a pretrained model using only a small number of trainable parameters. The goal is simple in principle. Keep most of the model frozen, change only a small part, and still get strong downstream performance.

## Why not just retrain everything?

Full finetuning means updating all of a model’s weights. That works, but for modern foundation models it can be costly in both compute and memory. More importantly, it is often unnecessary. The pretrained model already contains a lot of useful structure. The challenge is to steer that structure toward a new task without destroying what the model already knows.

That is why PEFT has become so important. Instead of rewriting a model, these methods add a small, task-specific update.

There are three common families of PEFT methods:

- **Model finetuning**, where the architecture stays the same and only some parameters are updated.
- **Adapter tuning**, where small extra modules are inserted into the network.
- **Prompt tuning**, where the model is conditioned with extra learned tokens or prefixes.

Each option has trade-offs. Adapters and prompt tuning can be very efficient, but they add extra machinery at inference time. Model finetuning is attractive because it does not introduce inference latency: after training, the adapted weights can be used directly.

## The appeal of small, structured updates

The paper focuses on a more principled version of model finetuning. Instead of making unconstrained changes to the weights, it tries to preserve the geometry of the pretrained model.

The key idea is orthogonal finetuning. Rather than replacing a weight matrix $W^0$, the method learns an orthogonal matrix $R$ and transforms the pretrained weights as $RW^0$. Orthogonal matrices are special because they preserve angles between vectors. In plain terms, they rotate or reflect the representation without distorting it. That helps preserve the relationships learned during pretraining.

This is a cleaner idea than just using a very small learning rate. A small learning rate limits how far the weights move, but it does not give a principled notion of what should be preserved. Orthogonal finetuning does.

The original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) method is effective, but there is a catch: a dense orthogonal matrix is large. For a $d$-dimensional model, it can take $O(d^2)$ parameters. OFT reduces this cost with a block-diagonal structure, but that introduces a new problem. The model is forced to treat different index groups separately, which can limit expressiveness and impose an awkward sparsity pattern.

## Why the paper turns to butterfly structure

The main contribution of the paper is a new parameterization called **Orthogonal Butterfly** or **BOFT**.

The motivation comes from a simple observation: if a dense matrix is a way of moving information from one set of dimensions to another, then we can ask how to build that dense connectivity using a sequence of sparse transformations. The authors frame this as an information transmission problem on a graph.

That framing points naturally to butterfly structures, the same kind of structure used in the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform. Butterfly graphs are good at mixing information efficiently. They can connect many inputs to many outputs using only $O(d \log d)$ edges rather than a full dense matrix.

BOFT uses that idea to parameterize orthogonal matrices more efficiently. Instead of one large dense orthogonal matrix, it composes several sparse orthogonal factors arranged in a butterfly pattern. The result is still orthogonal, still structured, but much cheaper to train.

This matters because it gives a smoother middle ground between two extremes:

- a full orthogonal matrix, which is expressive but expensive;
- a block-diagonal matrix, which is cheap but rigid.

Butterfly structure offers a better trade-off. It is sparse enough to be efficient, but structured enough to remain expressive.

## What BOFT changes in practice

BOFT generalizes OFT. In fact, OFT appears as a special case of BOFT. The paper shows that if the butterfly depth and block sizes are chosen appropriately, BOFT can recover dense orthogonal transformations, while using far fewer trainable parameters than the original dense formulation.

A few practical details are worth noting.

First, BOFT uses identity initialization, so the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained model. That is the same basic idea as zero initialization in LoRA: begin with no effective change, then learn the update.

Second, the method includes a multiplicative version of dropout. Standard dropout works naturally for additive methods like LoRA, but not for multiplicative orthogonal updates. The paper adapts dropout to the BOFT setting by randomly replacing some butterfly components with identity matrices during training.

Third, after finetuning, the learned orthogonal factors can be folded into the pretrained model. So, like other weight-based finetuning methods, BOFT does not add inference latency.

## Why this is interesting beyond one model family

The paper does not stop at text-to-image generation, where OFT was originally studied. It applies BOFT to a broader set of tasks, including large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models.

That broader evaluation is important. It suggests that orthogonal finetuning is not just a niche trick for one kind of generative model. It may be a general-purpose way to adapt foundation models while preserving useful pretrained structure.

The paper also reports an appealing mathematical property: orthogonal updates preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. That helps explain why these methods can be stable in training and strong in generalization. BOFT keeps that benefit while using fewer parameters.

## The big takeaway

The core lesson of the paper is not just “butterfly matrices are efficient.” It is that **good finetuning is about changing a pretrained model in the right way, not just changing it as little as possible**.

BOFT shows one path forward:

- keep the update orthogonal so you preserve useful geometry;
- use butterfly factorization so the update is parameter-efficient;
- avoid rigid block boundaries that reduce expressiveness.

In a landscape where foundation models keep getting larger, that kind of structured adaptation is becoming more and more valuable. Instead of retraining giant models from scratch, we may increasingly rely on carefully designed updates that are small, efficient, and still expressive enough to do the job.
