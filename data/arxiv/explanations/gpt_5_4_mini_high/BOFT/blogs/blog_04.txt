\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# What Does Finetuning Have to Do With Information Flow? A Visual Guide to the Paper's Big Insight

## A matrix is more than a table of numbers

A useful way to read this paper is to stop thinking of a weight matrix as just a block of parameters and start thinking of it as a communication network.

In orthogonal finetuning, the pretrained weights $W^0$ are updated by left-multiplying an orthogonal matrix $R$, so the new layer becomes $R W^0$. The key idea is that $R$ does not just change numbers; it changes how information moves between dimensions. If an entry of $R$ is zero, then that direct route is unavailable. If it is non-zero, information can flow.

That simple viewpoint becomes powerful once you factor $R$ into a product of sparse matrices:

$$
R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1.
$$

Now the finetuning problem looks like routing messages through a network with multiple stages. Each factor is one communication step, and the final dense matrix is the result of repeatedly exchanging information across those steps.

## Why block-diagonal structure is not enough

The original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) method already tries to be parameter-efficient by making $R$ block-diagonal. This reduces the number of trainable parameters, but it also hard-codes a very specific assumption: dimensions are split into fixed groups, and each group is transformed separately.

From the information-flow perspective, that means the network has disconnected regions. Information can move around inside a block, but not freely across blocks. So even though the matrix is orthogonal, its sparsity pattern limits how much global mixing it can do.

That is the core limitation the paper highlights. A block-diagonal pattern may save parameters, but it is also a restrictive wiring scheme. It is easy to ask whether a given dimension should belong to one block or another; it is much harder to justify why the model should be forced to keep those groups separate in the first place.

The authors’ goal is therefore not just to make $R$ sparse. It is to make $R$ sparse in a way that still allows full information exchange.

## The network view makes the design problem clearer

Once you draw the factorization as a graph, the task becomes much easier to reason about.

Imagine a grid with $d$ rows and $m+1$ columns. The first column is the source layer, the last column is the destination layer, and each intermediate column represents one sparse factor. An edge between two adjacent columns means information can pass along that route. For the product to be dense, every source node must be able to reach every destination node through some path.

This graph view gives two practical design goals.

First, the graph should be densely connected in the end: every input dimension should be able to influence every output dimension. Second, the factorization should use as few trainable connections as possible, while still respecting orthogonality.

That is a nice abstraction, because it separates the question of *what pattern of mixing do we want?* from the question of *how do we enforce orthogonality?*

## Why butterfly connections are the right kind of sparsity

The paper’s answer comes from the butterfly structure used in the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform. A butterfly network is built from repeated local exchanges that double the reach of information at each stage. Early stages only mix nearby dimensions, but after enough stages, every source can influence every destination.

That is exactly what you want for a factorized orthogonal matrix.

Instead of one dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, BOFT builds $R$ as a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse orthogonal components. Each component has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries, so the overall parameter count drops to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ in the butterfly setting. Yet the composition still allows dense connectivity.

This is the main insight: butterfly sparsity is not “less structure” than block-diagonal sparsity. It is a different structure, and it is much better suited to global information flow.

You can think of block-diagonal updates as isolating groups, while butterfly updates repeatedly reshuffle and remix them. That repeated remixing is what makes the final matrix expressive.

## BOFT: orthogonal finetuning with better wiring

The method introduced in the paper is called Orthogonal Butterfly, or BOFT. It keeps the core orthogonal-fineturning idea from OFT: preserve geometric relationships in the pretrained weights by applying an orthogonal transformation. Because orthogonal matrices preserve angles, this helps maintain pretrained knowledge and also preserves the spectral norm of the weights.

But BOFT changes the parameterization of $R$.

Instead of learning a large dense orthogonal matrix or a block-diagonal one, BOFT learns a product of orthogonal butterfly components. The paper shows that OFT is actually a special case of BOFT, so BOFT gives a more general framework. In the extreme butterfly setting, it can represent a dense orthogonal matrix with far fewer trainable parameters than the original OFT.

There is also an important optimization detail: BOFT starts from the identity, so finetuning begins with the exact pretrained model. That keeps the update controlled, just like other PEFT methods that avoid drifting too far from the original model.

## Why this matters beyond the math

The information-transmission view is useful because it turns a reparameterization problem into a design problem about connectivity. Once you see the matrix as a network, the question becomes: what sparse topology lets information spread widely without wasting parameters?

The paper argues that the butterfly topology is a strong answer. It is expressive enough to recover many classic fast linear transforms, but still structured enough to act as a regularizer. That balance between expressivity and regularity is important in finetuning, where more freedom is not always better.

The empirical result is that BOFT is not just a neat parameterization trick. The authors apply it to large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models, and report strong gains over existing methods. So the graph view is not merely aesthetic; it leads to a practical finetuning method.

## The big takeaway

The paper’s main contribution is a shift in perspective.

Instead of asking, “How can we shrink an orthogonal matrix?”, it asks, “How should information move through a parameter-efficient orthogonal update?”

That change of viewpoint makes the answer much clearer. Block-diagonal sparsity saves parameters, but it can trap information inside isolated groups. Butterfly sparsity, by contrast, lets information spread globally through staged local mixing. BOFT takes advantage of that structure to build a more expressive and more efficient form of orthogonal finetuning.

In short, the paper shows that finetuning is not just about changing weights. It is about designing a good communication network inside the model.
