\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# A Smarter Way to Update Giant AI Models Without Retraining Them

Large foundation models are incredibly powerful, but they are also expensive to train and expensive to adapt. Once a model has been pretrained on massive data, the practical question is no longer “How do we build it from scratch?” but “How do we make it useful for my task without paying for another full training run?”

This paper is about that second problem. It looks at parameter-efficient finetuning, or PEFT: methods that adapt a pretrained model using only a small number of trainable parameters. The goal is simple in principle. Keep most of the model frozen, change only a small part, and still get strong downstream performance.

## Why not just retrain everything?

Full finetuning means updating all of a model’s weights. That works, but for modern foundation models it can be costly in both compute and memory. More importantly, it is often unnecessary. The pretrained model already contains a lot of useful structure. The challenge is to steer that structure toward a new task without destroying what the model already knows.

That is why PEFT has become so important. Instead of rewriting a model, these methods add a small, task-specific update.

There are three common families of PEFT methods:

- **Model finetuning**, where the architecture stays the same and only some parameters are updated.
- **Adapter tuning**, where small extra modules are inserted into the network.
- **Prompt tuning**, where the model is conditioned with extra learned tokens or prefixes.

Each option has trade-offs. Adapters and prompt tuning can be very efficient, but they add extra machinery at inference time. Model finetuning is attractive because it does not introduce inference latency: after training, the adapted weights can be used directly.

## The appeal of small, structured updates

The paper focuses on a more principled version of model finetuning. Instead of making unconstrained changes to the weights, it tries to preserve the geometry of the pretrained model.

The key idea is orthogonal finetuning. Rather than replacing a weight matrix $W^0$, the method learns an orthogonal matrix $R$ and transforms the pretrained weights as $RW^0$. Orthogonal matrices are special because they preserve angles between vectors. In plain terms, they rotate or reflect the representation without distorting it. That helps preserve the relationships learned during pretraining.

This is a cleaner idea than just using a very small learning rate. A small learning rate limits how far the weights move, but it does not give a principled notion of what should be preserved. Orthogonal finetuning does.

The original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) method is effective, but there is a catch: a dense orthogonal matrix is large. For a $d$-dimensional model, it can take $O(d^2)$ parameters. OFT reduces this cost with a block-diagonal structure, but that introduces a new problem. The model is forced to treat different index groups separately, which can limit expressiveness and impose an awkward sparsity pattern.

## Why the paper turns to butterfly structure

The main contribution of the paper is a new parameterization called **Orthogonal Butterfly** or **BOFT**.

The motivation comes from a simple observation: if a dense matrix is a way of moving information from one set of dimensions to another, then we can ask how to build that dense connectivity using a sequence of sparse transformations. The authors frame this as an information transmission problem on a graph.

That framing points naturally to butterfly structures, the same kind of structure used in the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform. Butterfly graphs are good at mixing information efficiently. They can connect many inputs to many outputs using only $O(d \log d)$ edges rather than a full dense matrix.

BOFT uses that idea to parameterize orthogonal matrices more efficiently. Instead of one large dense orthogonal matrix, it composes several sparse orthogonal factors arranged in a butterfly pattern. The result is still orthogonal, still structured, but much cheaper to train.

This matters because it gives a smoother middle ground between two extremes:

- a full orthogonal matrix, which is expressive but expensive;
- a block-diagonal matrix, which is cheap but rigid.

Butterfly structure offers a better trade-off. It is sparse enough to be efficient, but structured enough to remain expressive.

## What BOFT changes in practice

BOFT generalizes OFT. In fact, OFT appears as a special case of BOFT. The paper shows that if the butterfly depth and block sizes are chosen appropriately, BOFT can recover dense orthogonal transformations, while using far fewer trainable parameters than the original dense formulation.

A few practical details are worth noting.

First, BOFT uses identity initialization, so the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained model. That is the same basic idea as zero initialization in LoRA: begin with no effective change, then learn the update.

Second, the method includes a multiplicative version of dropout. Standard dropout works naturally for additive methods like LoRA, but not for multiplicative orthogonal updates. The paper adapts dropout to the BOFT setting by randomly replacing some butterfly components with identity matrices during training.

Third, after finetuning, the learned orthogonal factors can be folded into the pretrained model. So, like other weight-based finetuning methods, BOFT does not add inference latency.

## Why this is interesting beyond one model family

The paper does not stop at text-to-image generation, where OFT was originally studied. It applies BOFT to a broader set of tasks, including large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models.

That broader evaluation is important. It suggests that orthogonal finetuning is not just a niche trick for one kind of generative model. It may be a general-purpose way to adapt foundation models while preserving useful pretrained structure.

The paper also reports an appealing mathematical property: orthogonal updates preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. That helps explain why these methods can be stable in training and strong in generalization. BOFT keeps that benefit while using fewer parameters.

## The big takeaway

The core lesson of the paper is not just “butterfly matrices are efficient.” It is that **good finetuning is about changing a pretrained model in the right way, not just changing it as little as possible**.

BOFT shows one path forward:

- keep the update orthogonal so you preserve useful geometry;
- use butterfly factorization so the update is parameter-efficient;
- avoid rigid block boundaries that reduce expressiveness.

In a landscape where foundation models keep getting larger, that kind of structured adaptation is becoming more and more valuable. Instead of retraining giant models from scratch, we may increasingly rely on carefully designed updates that are small, efficient, and still expressive enough to do the job.



# Why Orthogonal Finetuning Helps AI Learn New Tasks Without Forgetting Old Ones

When we finetune a large foundation model, the goal is not to relearn everything from scratch. The goal is to adapt the model while keeping the useful structure it already learned during pretraining. That is exactly the motivation behind **orthogonal finetuning**.

The key idea is surprisingly simple: instead of freely rewriting a weight matrix, we **rotate** it with an orthogonal matrix. This preserves geometric relationships inside the layer, which helps the model keep its pretrained knowledge while still adapting to a new task.

## The basic idea: rotate the weights, don’t rebuild them

For a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, orthogonal finetuning replaces the usual forward pass with

$$
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
$$

where $\bm{R}$ is a learnable orthogonal matrix satisfying

$$
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}.
$$

If you think of a neural network layer as a set of directions in a high-dimensional space, then multiplying by an orthogonal matrix is like rotating or reflecting that space. It changes the orientation of the layer, but it does **not** stretch or distort it.

That matters because orthogonal transformations preserve:

- lengths of vectors,
- angles between vectors,
- and the overall spectral norm of the weight matrix.

So instead of letting finetuning freely change the geometry learned during pretraining, orthogonal finetuning keeps that geometry intact and only adjusts how it is used.

## Why preserving angles helps

The paper’s main intuition is that pretrained models already contain useful structure. In a layer, the relative angles between neurons can be thought of as part of that structure. Orthogonal finetuning preserves these pairwise angles, which means the model can adapt without destroying the relationships that pretraining established.

This gives orthogonal finetuning a strong inductive bias. The finetuned model is still flexible, but its updates are restricted to transformations that are geometrically well-behaved. That can help with:

- **training stability**, since orthogonal transformations preserve the spectral norm;
- **generalization**, because the model is not allowed to drift too far from the pretrained geometry;
- **knowledge retention**, because the pretrained representation is transformed rather than rewritten.

This is one reason the paper argues that orthogonal finetuning can be a more principled alternative to simply using a small learning rate.

## How this differs from LoRA

A popular parameter-efficient method like LoRA works in a very different way. LoRA adds a low-rank update to the pretrained weights. That is efficient, but it is an **additive** change:

$$
\bm{W}^0 \mapsto \bm{W}^0 + \Delta \bm{W}.
$$

Orthogonal finetuning is **multiplicative**:

$$
\bm{W}^0 \mapsto \bm{R}\bm{W}^0.
$$

The distinction is important. LoRA is designed to keep the update small in a low-rank subspace, while orthogonal finetuning is designed to preserve the geometry of the pretrained layer. In the paper, this geometric preservation is part of why orthogonal finetuning can be more stable and can generalize well.

There is also a trade-off. A full orthogonal matrix is expensive to learn, because a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix can still be very large. So the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) introduced a block-diagonal structure to reduce the number of trainable parameters. But block-diagonal sparsity comes with a cost: it limits expressiveness and imposes a rigid sparsity pattern.

## Making orthogonal finetuning practical with BOFT

The paper’s main contribution is to make orthogonal finetuning more parameter-efficient through **butterfly factorization**, leading to **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**.

Instead of learning one huge dense orthogonal matrix directly, BOFT writes it as a product of several sparse orthogonal matrices arranged in a butterfly pattern. This is inspired by the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform, where butterfly structures allow information to be mixed efficiently across many nodes.

The result is a much smaller parameterization:

- full OFT uses $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters for a dense orthogonal matrix,
- BOFT can reduce this to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ with butterfly structure.

Crucially, BOFT still stays within the orthogonal family, so it keeps the same geometric benefits as OFT while being far more efficient.

## Why this is useful in practice

The paper evaluates BOFT on large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models. The main message is not just that BOFT is cheaper, but that it is a good trade-off between **expressivity** and **regularity**.

That trade-off is the heart of orthogonal finetuning. If the update is too unconstrained, the model may overfit or drift away from the pretrained solution. If it is too rigid, it may not adapt well. Orthogonal finetuning tries to stay in the middle: flexible enough to learn a new task, but structured enough to preserve what was already learned.

## Takeaway

Orthogonal finetuning works because it changes a model in a way that respects the geometry of its pretrained representations. By learning an orthogonal transformation, the model can adapt while preserving angles, norms, and spectral properties that support stable training and generalization.

The paper’s BOFT method makes this idea practical by factorizing the orthogonal matrix into a butterfly structure, cutting the parameter cost without giving up the core benefit.

If you want a short version: **orthogonal finetuning does not ask a model to forget its old geometry and learn a new one from scratch. It asks the model to rotate what it already knows into a better shape for the new task.**



# From Fast Fourier Transforms to Better AI Finetuning: The Butterfly Trick

Large foundation models are powerful, but adapting them to new tasks is still expensive. Full finetuning can be heavy on parameters and memory, while many parameter-efficient methods trade away some flexibility. This paper takes a different path: it starts from **Orthogonal Finetuning** (OFT), a method that updates weights with an orthogonal transformation, and then asks a practical question: can we make that update much cheaper without losing the benefits?

The answer is **yes**, by using a structure borrowed from one of the most elegant algorithms in computing: the **butterfly pattern** behind the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform.

## Orthogonal finetuning, in plain terms

OFT updates a pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$ by multiplying it with a learnable orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$. In other words, instead of changing the weights arbitrarily, it rotates them in a way that preserves pairwise angles between neurons. That matters because it helps retain the pretrained model’s structure and can improve stability and generalization.

The issue is that a general $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix is still large: it has on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. OFT reduces this burden with a block-diagonal structure, but that comes with a cost. The blocks are independent, which makes the update less expressive and imposes a fairly rigid sparsity pattern.

So the challenge becomes: how do you get something that behaves like a dense orthogonal matrix, but is much cheaper to train?

## Dense behavior from sparse pieces

The paper’s key insight is to build a dense orthogonal matrix as a **product of several sparse orthogonal matrices**. This turns matrix design into an information-flow problem: if you think of matrix entries as connections between nodes, then multiplying sparse factors is like passing information through a layered graph.

For the final matrix to be dense, information must be able to flow from every source dimension to every output dimension. At the same time, the parameter budget should stay small. That means we want sparse factors that are still able to mix information broadly across coordinates.

This is where the butterfly structure comes in.

## Why the butterfly pattern works

The butterfly topology is famous because it powers the Cooley-Tukey FFT. In that algorithm, information is mixed recursively in a highly structured way, so a global transformation can be computed efficiently from many small local operations.

The same idea turns out to be useful for finetuning. A butterfly matrix is built from a product of sparse factors, and each factor only contains $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries. With about $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ such factors, you can create a dense transformation while using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters.

That is the crucial win: you get dense mixing without paying the full $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ cost.

The paper makes this work for orthogonal finetuning by ensuring that each butterfly factor is itself orthogonal. The result is a parameterization that stays within the orthogonal group, but is much more efficient than a fully dense orthogonal matrix.

## BOFT: Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning

The method the authors propose is called **Orthogonal Butterfly**, or **BOFT**. It applies the butterfly factorization to orthogonal finetuning, giving a general framework that includes OFT as a special case.

In BOFT, the finetuned layer is written as

$$
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}(m,b)\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x},
$$

where $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$ is a product of orthogonal butterfly components. The parameters $m$ and $b$ control the factorization structure. When $m=1$, BOFT reduces to block-diagonal OFT. When the butterfly structure is expanded enough, it can represent dense orthogonal updates.

This gives BOFT a useful middle ground. It is more flexible than the block-diagonal form, but still far more parameter-efficient than a fully dense orthogonal matrix.

The paper also keeps the finetuning setup practical. BOFT uses identity initialization so the model starts from the pretrained weights, and it introduces a multiplicative dropout variant that fits the multiplicative nature of the update. After finetuning, the learned orthogonal matrices can be merged into the model, so there is no extra inference latency.

## Why this is more than just compression

The butterfly structure is not only a way to save parameters. It also changes the inductive bias of the finetuning process.

Because butterfly matrices are structured, they form a smaller hypothesis class than an unconstrained orthogonal matrix. That can be beneficial: the model is still expressive enough to adapt, but it is also constrained enough to avoid overfitting. The paper argues that this is one reason BOFT generalizes well.

There is also a nice theoretical angle. Orthogonal updates preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weights, which is often associated with better stability. The paper further shows that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size, and that butterfly structures can recover classic fast transforms such as the FFT and Hadamard transform.

## What the experiments show

The authors evaluate BOFT on a broad set of tasks, including large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models. Across these settings, BOFT is reported to outperform existing methods by a substantial margin, while using parameters more efficiently than OFT.

That breadth matters. Orthogonal finetuning was originally studied in the context of controllable text-to-image generation, but this paper shows that the approach is much more general. The butterfly parameterization makes orthogonal finetuning practical across multiple model families.

## The main takeaway

The core idea in this paper is simple but powerful: **a dense transformation does not have to be parameterized as a dense matrix**. By composing many small sparse orthogonal transformations in a butterfly pattern, BOFT creates an efficient update that remains expressive, stable, and easy to apply across different foundation models.

If you like the elegance of FFT-style algorithms, BOFT is a great example of that mindset applied to modern AI finetuning: use structure, exploit sparsity, and let a clever factorization do the heavy lifting.



# What Does Finetuning Have to Do With Information Flow? A Visual Guide to the Paper's Big Insight

## A matrix is more than a table of numbers

A useful way to read this paper is to stop thinking of a weight matrix as just a block of parameters and start thinking of it as a communication network.

In orthogonal finetuning, the pretrained weights $W^0$ are updated by left-multiplying an orthogonal matrix $R$, so the new layer becomes $R W^0$. The key idea is that $R$ does not just change numbers; it changes how information moves between dimensions. If an entry of $R$ is zero, then that direct route is unavailable. If it is non-zero, information can flow.

That simple viewpoint becomes powerful once you factor $R$ into a product of sparse matrices:

$$
R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1.
$$

Now the finetuning problem looks like routing messages through a network with multiple stages. Each factor is one communication step, and the final dense matrix is the result of repeatedly exchanging information across those steps.

## Why block-diagonal structure is not enough

The original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) method already tries to be parameter-efficient by making $R$ block-diagonal. This reduces the number of trainable parameters, but it also hard-codes a very specific assumption: dimensions are split into fixed groups, and each group is transformed separately.

From the information-flow perspective, that means the network has disconnected regions. Information can move around inside a block, but not freely across blocks. So even though the matrix is orthogonal, its sparsity pattern limits how much global mixing it can do.

That is the core limitation the paper highlights. A block-diagonal pattern may save parameters, but it is also a restrictive wiring scheme. It is easy to ask whether a given dimension should belong to one block or another; it is much harder to justify why the model should be forced to keep those groups separate in the first place.

The authors’ goal is therefore not just to make $R$ sparse. It is to make $R$ sparse in a way that still allows full information exchange.

## The network view makes the design problem clearer

Once you draw the factorization as a graph, the task becomes much easier to reason about.

Imagine a grid with $d$ rows and $m+1$ columns. The first column is the source layer, the last column is the destination layer, and each intermediate column represents one sparse factor. An edge between two adjacent columns means information can pass along that route. For the product to be dense, every source node must be able to reach every destination node through some path.

This graph view gives two practical design goals.

First, the graph should be densely connected in the end: every input dimension should be able to influence every output dimension. Second, the factorization should use as few trainable connections as possible, while still respecting orthogonality.

That is a nice abstraction, because it separates the question of *what pattern of mixing do we want?* from the question of *how do we enforce orthogonality?*

## Why butterfly connections are the right kind of sparsity

The paper’s answer comes from the butterfly structure used in the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform. A butterfly network is built from repeated local exchanges that double the reach of information at each stage. Early stages only mix nearby dimensions, but after enough stages, every source can influence every destination.

That is exactly what you want for a factorized orthogonal matrix.

Instead of one dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, BOFT builds $R$ as a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse orthogonal components. Each component has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries, so the overall parameter count drops to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ in the butterfly setting. Yet the composition still allows dense connectivity.

This is the main insight: butterfly sparsity is not “less structure” than block-diagonal sparsity. It is a different structure, and it is much better suited to global information flow.

You can think of block-diagonal updates as isolating groups, while butterfly updates repeatedly reshuffle and remix them. That repeated remixing is what makes the final matrix expressive.

## BOFT: orthogonal finetuning with better wiring

The method introduced in the paper is called Orthogonal Butterfly, or BOFT. It keeps the core orthogonal-fineturning idea from OFT: preserve geometric relationships in the pretrained weights by applying an orthogonal transformation. Because orthogonal matrices preserve angles, this helps maintain pretrained knowledge and also preserves the spectral norm of the weights.

But BOFT changes the parameterization of $R$.

Instead of learning a large dense orthogonal matrix or a block-diagonal one, BOFT learns a product of orthogonal butterfly components. The paper shows that OFT is actually a special case of BOFT, so BOFT gives a more general framework. In the extreme butterfly setting, it can represent a dense orthogonal matrix with far fewer trainable parameters than the original OFT.

There is also an important optimization detail: BOFT starts from the identity, so finetuning begins with the exact pretrained model. That keeps the update controlled, just like other PEFT methods that avoid drifting too far from the original model.

## Why this matters beyond the math

The information-transmission view is useful because it turns a reparameterization problem into a design problem about connectivity. Once you see the matrix as a network, the question becomes: what sparse topology lets information spread widely without wasting parameters?

The paper argues that the butterfly topology is a strong answer. It is expressive enough to recover many classic fast linear transforms, but still structured enough to act as a regularizer. That balance between expressivity and regularity is important in finetuning, where more freedom is not always better.

The empirical result is that BOFT is not just a neat parameterization trick. The authors apply it to large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models, and report strong gains over existing methods. So the graph view is not merely aesthetic; it leads to a practical finetuning method.

## The big takeaway

The paper’s main contribution is a shift in perspective.

Instead of asking, “How can we shrink an orthogonal matrix?”, it asks, “How should information move through a parameter-efficient orthogonal update?”

That change of viewpoint makes the answer much clearer. Block-diagonal sparsity saves parameters, but it can trap information inside isolated groups. Butterfly sparsity, by contrast, lets information spread globally through staged local mixing. BOFT takes advantage of that structure to build a more expressive and more efficient form of orthogonal finetuning.

In short, the paper shows that finetuning is not just about changing weights. It is about designing a good communication network inside the model.



# BOFT vs. LoRA: Two Different Philosophies for Making Foundation Models Cheaper to Adapt

## The common goal: adapt a pretrained model without retraining it

As foundation models get larger, finetuning has become the practical way to adapt them to new tasks. Two popular parameter-efficient finetuning methods, LoRA and BOFT, solve the same problem but with very different assumptions.

LoRA treats adaptation as a small additive correction to the pretrained weights. BOFT, short for Orthogonal Butterfly, treats adaptation as a structured multiplicative transformation of the pretrained weights. That difference sounds subtle, but it changes how each method uses parameters, what it preserves from pretraining, and what kind of inductive bias it introduces.

## LoRA: learn a small additive update

LoRA updates a pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ by adding a low-rank matrix:
\[
W = W^0 + \Delta W.
\]
The key idea is that many downstream tasks do not require changing all of a model’s weights. Instead, a small update of low rank can be enough. This makes LoRA attractive because it is simple, widely used, and easy to train.

The strength of LoRA is flexibility. Since the update is additive, it can move the pretrained model in directions that are not tied to any particular geometric constraint. The trade-off is that LoRA does not explicitly preserve properties of the original weight matrix, such as pairwise angles between neurons or spectral norm.

## BOFT: learn a structured orthogonal transformation

BOFT takes a different route. Instead of adding a correction, it multiplies the pretrained weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix:
\[
W = RW^0, \qquad R^\top R = I.
\]

This matters because orthogonal transformations preserve lengths and pairwise angles. In the paper’s framing, that helps preserve the relational structure learned during pretraining. The update is not trying to rewrite the model from scratch. It is trying to rotate the pretrained representation in a way that keeps the original geometry intact.

That idea comes from Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), but OFT has a problem: a dense orthogonal matrix normally has too many parameters. BOFT fixes this by factorizing the orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse orthogonal butterfly components. In the butterfly setting, a dense orthogonal transform can be built with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a fully dense orthogonal matrix.

So, in practice, BOFT is a way to get the benefits of orthogonal finetuning without paying the full parameter cost.

## Why the butterfly structure is the key idea

The paper motivates BOFT with an information-transmission view. If you think of a matrix as a way for information to flow from input dimensions to output dimensions, then the goal is to build a dense transformation using as few trainable connections as possible.

The butterfly structure, borrowed from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform, is a good fit for this. It mixes information in stages, so local interactions can eventually create global connectivity. That gives BOFT a structured way to generate dense orthogonal matrices from sparse pieces.

This is also where BOFT differs from block-diagonal OFT. Block-diagonal structure saves parameters, but it forces the model to transform different groups of dimensions separately. BOFT is still sparse, but its butterfly pattern is much less rigid. The paper argues that this gives BOFT a smoother trade-off between regularity and expressiveness.

## Expressiveness, stability, and inductive bias

The main practical difference between LoRA and BOFT is not just how they use parameters, but what they assume about the update.

LoRA is a flexible additive correction. BOFT is a constrained multiplicative update. That constraint is not just a limitation. The paper argues that it creates a useful inductive bias.

Because BOFT is orthogonal, it preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. The authors connect this to better training stability and generalization. They also point out that butterfly structure can recover classic fast linear transforms such as the Fourier and Hadamard transforms, which suggests that the structure is expressive in a very particular and useful way.

The paper also shows that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size, and that smaller blocks with more butterfly factors can improve parameter efficiency. In other words, BOFT is not just a more compact version of OFT. It is a more structured one.

## What this means in practice

A useful way to think about the two methods is this:

LoRA says, “Keep the pretrained model fixed and add a small learned correction.”

BOFT says, “Keep the pretrained geometry and learn a structured rotation.”

That makes BOFT especially appealing when preserving the pretrained layer’s structure matters, or when you want an orthogonal finetuning method with much lower parameter cost than dense OFT. It is also attractive if you like the idea of a built-in structural prior, rather than an unconstrained low-rank patch.

The paper reports BOFT across a range of adaptation settings, including vision transformers, language models, and text-to-image diffusion models, and finds strong performance. It also notes one practical drawback: because BOFT composes multiple orthogonal factors, training has a bit more runtime overhead than OFT. On the other hand, after finetuning, the learned orthogonal matrices can be merged into the pretrained model, so there is no extra inference latency.

## Bottom line

LoRA and BOFT are both parameter-efficient, but they optimize for different things.

LoRA is the pragmatic additive baseline: simple, flexible, and widely adopted.

BOFT is the structure-preserving alternative: multiplicative, orthogonal, and designed to keep the pretrained representation intact while still being efficient.

If you want the shortest summary, it is this: LoRA learns a small correction, while BOFT learns a constrained rotation. For users who care about stability, geometry preservation, and a strong inductive bias, BOFT is a compelling alternative to the default low-rank playbook.



# One Finetuning Method, Many AI Domains: How BOFT Works Across Language, Vision, and Image Generation

Foundation models are now strong enough that the real challenge is often not building them from scratch, but adapting them well. That is why parameter-efficient finetuning has become such an important topic: we want to reuse the same pretrained model across many tasks, without paying the cost of full retraining.

The BOFT paper makes a strong case that orthogonal finetuning can be part of that reusable foundation-model stack. More importantly, it shows this is not just a niche technique for one model family. BOFT was tested on large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models, and the same core method worked across all of them. That breadth is the main story of the paper.

## Why BOFT is interesting beyond the theory

BOFT stands for Orthogonal Butterfly, and it builds on Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT. The basic idea behind orthogonal finetuning is simple: instead of rewriting a pretrained weight matrix from scratch, you transform it with an orthogonal matrix. Because orthogonal transforms preserve angles, they help retain the structure learned during pretraining.

The problem with the original OFT is that a full orthogonal matrix is expensive to learn. A block-diagonal version reduces the number of parameters, but it also imposes a rigid sparsity pattern that may be too restrictive. BOFT keeps the orthogonal idea, but replaces the block-diagonal structure with a butterfly factorization. In practice, that means BOFT can represent dense orthogonal transformations while using far fewer parameters, with the butterfly case scaling like $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$.

That technical detail matters because it explains why BOFT can be used more broadly. It is not just “orthogonal finetuning, but smaller.” It is a way to make orthogonal finetuning practical enough to use across different architectures.

## The real contribution: it works across very different model families

The paper’s empirical section is what makes BOFT feel important. The authors do not limit themselves to one setting. They apply BOFT to:

- large vision transformers,
- large language models, and
- text-to-image diffusion models.

This is significant because these systems behave very differently. Vision transformers process images with attention-based architectures. Large language models operate over discrete tokens and long-range sequence dependencies. Diffusion models are generative systems with a very different optimization and sampling setup. A method that adapts well across all three is not just solving one benchmark problem. It is pointing at a more general finetuning principle.

The paper reports that BOFT outperforms current state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin in these adaptation settings. Just as importantly, this is the first time orthogonal finetuning is shown to be useful outside the original controllable text-to-image generation setting. That expansion matters because it suggests orthogonal finetuning is not tied to one specific generative workflow. It may be a generally useful way to adapt pretrained weights while preserving useful structure.

## Why breadth of applicability matters

A lot of parameter-efficient methods are good at one thing and less convincing elsewhere. Low-rank updates, prompts, and adapters each have their place, but they often come with different tradeoffs depending on the model family. The BOFT results are interesting because they suggest something more universal: a structured orthogonal update can be a good finetuning prior across multiple domains.

That points to a broader trend in foundation models. As models get larger, it becomes less realistic to train many separate task-specific models from scratch. Instead, the future likely belongs to shared backbones that can be adapted cheaply and reliably. For that to work, the adaptation method needs to be robust enough to handle different modalities and different downstream objectives.

BOFT is a good example of this direction. It tries to preserve pretrained knowledge through orthogonal transformations, while still giving the model enough flexibility to adapt. The butterfly structure adds a useful bias: it keeps the update structured, but not overly rigid. The result is a smaller parameter space that still appears expressive enough for real downstream tasks.

## A practical bonus: no inference penalty

One reason model finetuning remains attractive compared to adapters or prompt tuning is that it does not add inference latency. BOFT keeps that advantage. The learned orthogonal factors can be multiplied into the pretrained model after finetuning, so deployment does not require an extra runtime module.

That makes BOFT especially appealing for settings where you want both efficiency and clean inference-time behavior. You pay a bit more during training because the final orthogonal matrix is built from multiple factors, but you do not carry that cost into production.

## What this says about reusable foundation models

The big takeaway from BOFT is not just that a clever matrix factorization saves parameters. It is that reusable foundation models may benefit from stronger structural priors than we usually give them. If a single finetuning method can adapt language models, vision transformers, and diffusion systems, then the method is probably capturing something deeper than task-specific tricks.

BOFT suggests that the geometry of the weight update matters. By keeping the update orthogonal and structured, it preserves useful properties of the pretrained model while still allowing adaptation. In the paper’s view, that is exactly the kind of balance we want: enough flexibility to learn the new task, but enough regularity to avoid destroying what pretraining already gives us.

## Closing thoughts

BOFT is not just an incremental improvement over OFT. It is a sign that orthogonal finetuning can be turned into a practical, general-purpose finetuning strategy. The paper’s experiments across large language models, vision transformers, and text-to-image diffusion models make that point clearly.

If the next generation of AI systems is going to be built on a small number of reusable foundation models, then methods like BOFT matter a lot. They show that we may not need to retrain everything. We may just need better ways to adapt what we already have.
