\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Foundation Models and the Need for Parameter-Efficient Adaptation

# Why foundation models are powerful but expensive to train from scratch

Recent systems such as ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion show how powerful large foundation models have become. Their success comes from their broad generalization ability: a single pretrained model can support many different downstream uses. At the same time, the paper emphasizes that this strength has been accompanied by a dramatic growth in model size. GPT-3, for example, has around $175$ billion parameters. Once models reach this scale, training them from scratch becomes prohibitively expensive for most researchers and practitioners.

This tension between capability and cost motivates the central problem of the paper. If we want to keep benefiting from the knowledge stored in these large pretrained models, we need ways to adapt them to new tasks without paying the full cost of retraining. The paper treats this as a practical necessity for continued progress in machine learning: broad adoption of foundation models makes downstream adaptation, rather than from-scratch training, the key challenge.

# Downstream adaptation as a central problem in modern machine learning

The paper frames modern machine learning as increasingly centered on adaptation. Instead of building a new model for every task, we begin with a foundation model and adjust it to fit a downstream objective. This is especially important because foundation models are now used across different domains, including vision, language, and text-to-image generation. The paper’s own empirical study spans large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models, underscoring how general this adaptation problem has become.

The broader point is that the value of a foundation model lies not only in pretraining, but also in how efficiently it can be repurposed. If adaptation is too expensive, then the promise of pretraining is limited. The paper therefore focuses on methods that preserve most of the pretrained model while changing only a small part of it. This is the motivation behind parameter-efficient finetuning, or PEFT.

# The three main PEFT families: model finetuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning

The paper organizes PEFT into three major families. The first is model finetuning. In this approach, the model architecture stays unchanged, and only a subset of the model parameters is updated. The second is adapter tuning, where extra trainable parameters are inserted into the original model. The third is prompt tuning, where additional trainable prefix tokens are attached to the input.

These three families reflect different strategies for keeping adaptation cheap. Model finetuning changes the model directly but tries to limit how much of it is trainable. Adapter tuning introduces new components while leaving the main pretrained model mostly intact. Prompt tuning pushes the adaptation into the input representation itself. The paper positions its contribution within the model finetuning family, because that family is especially simple and avoids extra inference cost.

# Why model finetuning is attractive: simplicity and no added inference latency

Among the PEFT methods discussed in the paper, model finetuning is highlighted as a particularly appealing choice. Its appeal comes from two related advantages. First, it is conceptually simple: the pretrained model is kept in place, and only a controlled subset of its parameters or reparameterization is updated. Second, it introduces no inference latency. Because no extra adapter modules or prompt tokens need to be carried through the forward pass, the finetuned model can be used just like the original architecture.

This simplicity matters in practice. The paper emphasizes that model finetuning is not only efficient, but also directly compatible with existing models and deployment settings. That makes it a natural starting point for the authors’ work. Their goal is not to invent a new adaptation paradigm, but to make model finetuning more parameter-efficient while preserving its simplicity and lack of inference overhead.

# The core challenge of preserving pretrained knowledge during adaptation

The paper’s key conceptual premise is that finetuning should preserve the knowledge learned during pretraining. A naive way to do this is to use a small learning rate, so that the finetuned model does not drift too far from the pretrained model. The paper describes this as an ad hoc solution: it may help, but it does not explicitly encode what should be preserved.

A more principled approach is to preserve relational information in the weight matrices, especially the pairwise angles between neurons. This is the motivation behind orthogonal finetuning. By using orthogonal transformations, the method preserves those angles exactly. The paper argues that this is a better way to maintain the semantic structure encoded in the pretrained network, because it constrains adaptation in a way that is aligned with the geometry of the weights rather than relying only on a small optimization step.

# Additive updates versus multiplicative updates in finetuning

The paper contrasts two broad ways of updating pretrained weights. In additive methods, the new weights are obtained by adding a learned correction to the old weights. LoRA is the main example discussed: it updates the pretrained matrix by adding the product of two low-rank matrices. In multiplicative methods, the pretrained weights are transformed by a learned matrix on the left. Orthogonal finetuning, or OFT, follows this multiplicative route.

This difference is important because it changes what kind of structure is being imposed on adaptation. Additive low-rank updates try to be efficient by restricting the correction matrix. Multiplicative orthogonal updates try to preserve geometry while still allowing the model to move within a constrained family of transformations. The paper treats both low-rank structure and sparse structure as major structured-matrix families that can be used to achieve parameter efficiency. BOFT is the paper’s answer to how sparse structure can be used in the orthogonal setting.

# Low-rank structure and sparse structure as two routes to parameter efficiency

The paper explains parameter efficiency through two broad structural ideas. Low-rank structure, as used in LoRA, reduces the number of trainable parameters by limiting the rank of the update. Sparse structure reduces the number of trainable parameters by limiting how many entries of a matrix are non-zero. OFT already uses sparsity, but in the form of a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix. This helps reduce parameters, yet it also imposes a fixed sparsity pattern.

The distinction is important because the structure is doing more than saving parameters. It also shapes the inductive bias of the model. A low-rank update and a sparse update are not equivalent: they represent different ways of constraining how information can flow through the weights. The paper’s central idea is to keep the orthogonal-fineturning philosophy of OFT while replacing its block-diagonal sparsity with a more expressive sparse factorization.

# High-level overview of OFT and BOFT

Orthogonal finetuning, or OFT, reparameterizes a pretrained linear layer by learning an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and applying it to the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. If the original forward pass is $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, OFT changes it to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n$. The orthogonal constraint is important because it preserves pairwise angles between neurons. To keep $\bm{R}$ orthogonal during training, the paper uses Cayley parameterization, and to reduce the parameter count, OFT uses a block-diagonal structure.

BOFT, short for Orthogonal Butterfly, keeps the orthogonal-fineturning idea but changes how the orthogonal matrix is parameterized. Instead of a single large orthogonal matrix or a block-diagonal one, BOFT composes a dense orthogonal matrix from multiple sparse orthogonal factors arranged in a butterfly structure. This gives a more general orthogonal finetuning framework, and OFT becomes a special case within it. The paper emphasizes that BOFT can be much more parameter-efficient: with butterfly factorization, a $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix can be represented with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters, rather than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters needed by a full dense orthogonal matrix.

# The paper’s main contributions and what makes BOFT new

The paper’s first contribution is conceptual: it introduces an information transmission framework for thinking about orthogonal finetuning. In that framework, building a dense orthogonal matrix is treated as a transmission problem on a grid-structured graph. This viewpoint helps the authors reason about which sparsity patterns can still support dense connectivity while respecting orthogonality.

The second contribution is the BOFT parameterization itself. Inspired by butterfly structures from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm, the authors design a sparse factorization that yields an efficient orthogonal parameterization. This is not just a parameter-saving trick; it also gives a structured path from identity matrices toward the full orthogonal group, creating a smoother interpolation than the block-diagonal OFT design.

The third contribution is theoretical and interpretive. The paper argues that BOFT can significantly reduce trainable parameters while still maintaining good expressivity and training stability. It also notes an intriguing weight interpolation property arising from the matrix factorization. In addition, the paper presents an expressivity result showing that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size.

Finally, the paper makes a methodological contribution by showing that orthogonal finetuning can be used beyond the original text-to-image setting where OFT was introduced. BOFT is applied to a wide range of downstream tasks in vision and language, including large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models. The paper reports that BOFT outperforms current state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin, which supports its claim to be a generic and effective model finetuning method.

Taken together, these contributions explain why BOFT is new. It is not merely another efficient reparameterization. It is a structured orthogonal finetuning framework that replaces block-diagonal sparsity with butterfly factorization, preserves the benefits of orthogonal updates, and extends the applicability of orthogonal finetuning to a broader set of adaptation tasks.

Geometric Foundations of Orthogonal Finetuning

# Review of linear layers and weight matrices in neural networks

A linear layer in a neural network is represented by a weight matrix that maps an input vector to an output vector. In the notation used in the paper, a pretrained linear layer has weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, an input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and an output vector $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The forward pass is written as
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
This is the basic object that Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, aims to adapt. The key point is that the model does not change its architecture; instead, the pretrained weight matrix itself is adjusted in a structured way.

This matrix view is important because it turns finetuning into a question about how to modify a learned linear map while preserving useful information. The paper argues that broad progress on foundation models requires efficient adaptation rather than retraining from scratch. OFT belongs to the family of model finetuning methods, where the pretrained architecture remains fixed and only selected parameters are adapted. The geometric viewpoint becomes central because the way the matrix is changed determines what is preserved from pretraining.

# Viewing columns of a weight matrix as neurons

The paper treats the columns of the weight matrix as the neurons of a layer. If we write the $i$-th column of $\bm{W}^0$ as a vector $\bm{w}_i^0$, then each such column is one neuron in the layer, understood as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$. This is the level at which OFT operates: instead of changing each neuron independently, it applies the same transformation to all of them in the layer.

This shared treatment matters because neurons are not just isolated parameters. Their arrangement inside the layer carries information about how the model represents and combines features. When OFT transforms the matrix, it is not merely changing numbers one by one. It is moving the entire set of neuron vectors together through a common geometric transformation. That is why the method is naturally described in terms of geometry rather than simple parameter updates.

# Pairwise angles between neurons as relational information

A central idea in the paper is that the pairwise angles between neurons encode relational information. If two columns of a weight matrix have a particular angle between them, that angle reflects how the corresponding neurons are positioned relative to one another. OFT is motivated by the desire to preserve this internal structure during finetuning, rather than allowing the layer to drift arbitrarily away from what was learned during pretraining.

This is why the paper describes OFT as a principled alternative to ad hoc choices such as using a small learning rate. A small learning rate may keep the finetuned model close to the pretrained one in a loose numerical sense, but it does not explicitly preserve the geometry of the learned representation. OFT is designed around the stronger requirement that the pairwise angles among neurons remain unchanged, so that the relational structure of the layer is retained while the model adapts to a downstream task.

# Orthogonal matrices and their geometry-preserving properties

Orthogonal matrices are the mathematical objects that make this kind of preservation possible. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ satisfies
\[
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}.
\]
The paper relies on the fact that applying the same orthogonal matrix to the neurons in a layer preserves their pairwise angles. This is exactly the kind of transformation needed when the goal is to adapt a model without destroying the geometric relationships learned during pretraining.

The intuition is straightforward. If two vectors are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix, their relative orientation is maintained. In OFT, the orthogonal matrix is learned, but its orthogonality is preserved throughout training. As a result, the layer can change in a controlled way, but the transformation remains geometrically disciplined. This is what makes orthogonal finetuning fundamentally different from unconstrained weight updates: the adaptation is not arbitrary, but restricted to a family of transformations that preserve the layer’s internal geometry.

# Why preserving neuron angles can help retain pretrained semantics

The paper’s motivation for preserving angles is that the pretrained model already contains useful semantic knowledge. If the geometry of the neurons is maintained, then the relationships encoded during pretraining can be largely preserved while the model is adapted to a new task. In other words, OFT aims to alter the model just enough to fit downstream data, but not so much that it loses the structure that made pretraining effective.

This is why the paper presents orthogonal transformation as a principled notion of similarity between the pretrained and finetuned models. Rather than merely hoping that the new model stays close to the old one, OFT explicitly preserves pairwise angular relations among neurons. The result is a finetuning mechanism that respects the organization of the pretrained representation. The paper argues that this kind of structural preservation is a better way to retain knowledge than a purely numerical constraint such as a small update size.

# The OFT update rule: replacing $W^0$ with $R W^0$

The OFT update rule is simple and elegant. For a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, OFT introduces a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and changes the forward pass from
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
\]
to
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
This means that the pretrained weights are not replaced from scratch. Instead, they are transformed by a learned orthogonal map on the left.

This update rule captures the central geometric idea of OFT. Every neuron in the layer is transformed by the same matrix, so the layer changes coherently. The model keeps the pretrained weights as its starting point and learns how to rotate or otherwise orthogonally transform that space to suit the downstream task. The paper emphasizes that this is a multiplicative update, in contrast to the additive low-rank update used in LoRA. That difference is not just algebraic; it reflects a different philosophy of finetuning.

# Identity initialization as a way to start from the exact pretrained model

To make finetuning begin from the exact pretrained model, OFT initializes the orthogonal matrix as the identity matrix. If $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, then
\[
(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top = (\bm{W}^0)^\top,
\]
so the model initially behaves exactly like the original pretrained network. This is the geometric counterpart of the usual goal in finetuning: start with no deviation from pretraining and let training gradually introduce task-specific changes.

The paper describes this as zero initialization. In the OFT setting, zero initialization does not mean setting the weight matrix itself to zero. Rather, it means starting from the identity transformation, which produces no change at the beginning of training. This is important because it gives the optimization process a stable and interpretable starting point: the finetuned model begins as the original model and only moves away from it when the data justify that movement.

# Skew-symmetric matrices and the Cayley parameterization

The paper enforces orthogonality during training using the Cayley parameterization. In this approach, the learnable object is a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, meaning
\[
\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top.
\]
The orthogonal matrix is then defined by
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
This construction is taken from the paper’s discussion of OFT and is used to ensure that the learned transformation remains orthogonal.

The reason this is useful is that it turns the orthogonality constraint into a parameterization problem. Instead of optimizing over all matrices and then trying to force orthogonality afterward, OFT optimizes through a representation that produces an orthogonal matrix by design. The paper follows prior work in using this parameterization so that orthogonality is maintained throughout finetuning. In the identity case, if $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$, then $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, which is consistent with the identity initialization described above.

# How Cayley transforms enforce orthogonality during training

The Cayley transform is the mechanism that keeps the optimization on the orthogonal manifold. Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, the matrix $\bm{R}$ produced by
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}
\]
is orthogonal. This means that the training process does not need to repeatedly project a general matrix back onto the orthogonal set; the parameterization itself guarantees the constraint.

This matters for finetuning because the geometry-preserving behavior of OFT depends on orthogonality holding throughout training, not just at the end. The paper’s framing is that orthogonality preserves pairwise angles between neurons, and therefore preserves the relational information from pretraining. The Cayley parameterization is what makes that statement operational. It allows the model to be trained in a way that respects the desired geometric structure at every step.

# Conceptual comparison between OFT and LoRA

The paper contrasts OFT with LoRA as two different parameter-efficient finetuning methods. LoRA updates the pretrained weight matrix by adding a product of two low-rank matrices. OFT, by contrast, updates the pretrained weights multiplicatively by applying a learnable orthogonal matrix on the left. So while LoRA is built around low-rank structure, OFT is built around orthogonal geometry.

This difference leads to a different adaptation philosophy. LoRA is designed to make a compact additive correction, whereas OFT aims to preserve the geometric relations among neurons by transforming them without distorting their pairwise angles. The paper notes that OFT has shown strong generalization and more stable training than LoRA in the settings studied, though it can involve more trainable parameters because orthogonal matrices are high-dimensional. In the broader context of the paper, that tradeoff motivates the later move toward more parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterizations, but the foundational geometric idea remains the same: adapt the pretrained model through an orthogonality-preserving transformation rather than an unconstrained update.

Why OFT Still Uses Too Many Parameters

# Parameter cost of a dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix

Orthogonal Finetuning, or OFT, is built around a simple idea: instead of changing a pretrained linear layer by adding a new matrix, it multiplies the pretrained weights by a learnable orthogonal matrix. Concretely, if the pretrained layer is $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, then OFT changes the forward pass from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is orthogonal. The motivation is principled: an orthogonal transformation preserves pairwise angles between neurons, so the relational structure learned during pretraining can be largely preserved during finetuning.

The difficulty is that a dense orthogonal matrix is still a large object. Even though orthogonality imposes structure, a fully dense $\bm{R}$ still requires on the order of $d^2$ parameters in the usual parameterization used for finetuning. OFT enforces orthogonality during training with the Cayley parameterization, $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, but this does not remove the underlying issue that the matrix is high-dimensional. In other words, OFT is conceptually elegant, but a dense orthogonal matrix is still expensive to train when $d$ is large.

# Why naive OFT scales poorly in high dimensions

The paper’s concern is not that OFT is ineffective. On the contrary, OFT is presented as a principled finetuning method that preserves useful pretraining information and has demonstrated good generalizability. The problem is scale. Foundation models are large, so the internal linear layers that OFT modifies are also high-dimensional. Once $d$ becomes large, the parameter cost of learning a dense orthogonal matrix becomes difficult to justify for downstream adaptation.

This is why the paper treats parameter efficiency as a central obstacle rather than a minor implementation detail. If finetuning a large model requires learning a matrix whose size grows quadratically with the dimension, then the method may become unwieldy even if it has attractive theoretical properties. The paper’s basic message is that orthogonality alone is not enough: we also need a way to represent orthogonal updates using far fewer trainable parameters.

# Block-diagonal orthogonal matrices as the original OFT solution

The original OFT answers this problem by introducing a block-diagonal structure. Instead of learning one large dense orthogonal matrix, it parameterizes $\bm{R}$ as $\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is a smaller orthogonal block and $br = d$. This immediately lowers the number of trainable parameters, because the model no longer needs to describe all entries of a dense matrix.

The appeal of this design is straightforward. Each block can still be made orthogonal, for example by using the same Cayley-style construction that preserves orthogonality throughout training. At the same time, the overall number of parameters is much smaller than for a fully dense matrix. The original OFT therefore trades away some flexibility in exchange for a lighter parameterization. The paper makes clear that this was an effective first step, but not the final answer.

# How block size controls the parameter-efficiency trade-off

In block-diagonal OFT, the block size is the knob that controls the trade-off between parameter efficiency and flexibility. The smaller the size of the diagonal blocks is, the more parameter-efficient OFT becomes. This is because the model is then learning many small orthogonal transformations rather than one large one, which reduces the total number of trainable parameters.

But the same knob also controls how restrictive the model becomes. Larger blocks are closer to a dense orthogonal matrix and therefore more expressive, while smaller blocks are cheaper but more constrained. The paper summarizes this trade-off by noting that block-diagonal OFT with block number $r$ uses on the order of $bd$ parameters, where $b$ is the block size. This is far less than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ cost of a fully dense orthogonal matrix, but it also means that the model’s ability to mix information across dimensions is limited by how the blocks are chosen.

# Independent transformations across blocks and the resulting inductive bias

The most important consequence of the block-diagonal design is that different blocks are transformed independently. The dimensions of a neuron are divided into $r$ groups, and each group is handled by a separate orthogonal matrix. This means the model is not learning one unified transformation over the entire layer; it is learning several isolated transformations that do not directly interact.

That independence creates a structural prior, or inductive bias. The model is being told in advance that dimensions within a block should interact, while dimensions in different blocks should not. The paper views this as a strong assumption built into the finetuning method itself. Although such a prior may sometimes work empirically, it is not obviously justified by the structure of the pretrained weights or by any natural semantic grouping of the dimensions.

# Why grouping dimensions by index is hard to justify

A key criticism in the paper is that the block-diagonal structure groups dimensions by their indices. In other words, the coordinate positions of a neuron are split into consecutive chunks, and those chunks are then transformed separately. The paper argues that this is hard to justify: there is no clear reason why dimensions that happen to be adjacent in the index order should form a meaningful group, or why dimensions in different groups should be prevented from interacting.

This is why the authors say that a dense orthogonal matrix is desirable. A dense matrix does not impose an arbitrary partition of the coordinates, so it avoids baking in index-based assumptions about how the representation should be organized. The criticism is not merely aesthetic. It reflects the paper’s view that the fixed grouping imposed by block diagonals is an unnatural restriction when the goal is to adapt a pretrained model in a principled but flexible way.

# Reduced expressivity caused by fixed sparsity patterns

The block-diagonal solution is parameter-efficient precisely because it is sparse, but that sparsity comes with a cost in expressivity. The paper emphasizes that the block-diagonal sparsity pattern is fixed, so the orthogonal transformation is applied independently in each block. As a result, the set of matrices the model can represent is much smaller than the full orthogonal group.

This matters because the goal of orthogonal finetuning is not just to save parameters. It is to find a finetuned model that stays close enough to the pretrained model to preserve useful knowledge while still being flexible enough to adapt to new tasks. A fixed sparsity pattern can restrict that flexibility too much. In the language of the paper, it reduces the expressiveness of the method and can introduce undesirable inductive biases. The model becomes cheaper, but also less capable of representing the kinds of global transformations that some tasks may require.

# Why block-diagonal OFT cannot recover many classic dense linear transforms

The paper makes this limitation especially concrete by comparing block-diagonal OFT to classic linear transforms. It argues that the butterfly structure can easily recover many such transforms, but that the block-diagonal structure in OFT cannot recover any of them. This is a strong statement, and it highlights the difference between merely being sparse and being structured in a useful way.

The reason is that many classic transforms, such as the discrete Fourier transform, the discrete cosine transform, the discrete sine transform, and the Hadamard transform, rely on global mixing across dimensions. A block-diagonal matrix, by construction, cannot perform that kind of global mixing because each block only acts inside its own small coordinate group. So even though the matrix is orthogonal, its rigid sparsity pattern prevents it from matching the structure of these widely used dense transforms. This is one of the main reasons the paper sees block-diagonal OFT as an incomplete solution.

# The central design goal: dense orthogonal updates with far fewer parameters

The paper’s central design goal is therefore clear: it wants dense orthogonal updates, but without the quadratic parameter cost of a full dense orthogonal matrix. The key idea is to represent a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices. In this way, the method can preserve the advantages of orthogonality while trading computation for parameter efficiency.

This is the motivation behind BOFT, the paper’s Orthogonal Butterfly method. Rather than relying on block-diagonal sparsity, BOFT uses butterfly factorization to build an orthogonal matrix from sparse components in a way that is far more parameter-efficient. The paper shows that this gives a smoother interpolation between the fully expressive orthogonal case and the highly regular identity-like case, while also providing a more principled structural prior than block diagonals. In short, the block-diagonal workaround in original OFT solved the most obvious parameter problem, but it did so by imposing a rigid and somewhat arbitrary sparsity pattern. BOFT is introduced to keep the orthogonal-finetuning philosophy while removing that bottleneck.

Matrix Factorization as Information Transmission

# Factoring a Dense Transform as $R = B_m \cdots B_1$

The paper begins from a simple but important observation: a dense linear transform does not have to be represented directly as one large matrix. Instead, it can be built as a product of several smaller factors,
\[
R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1.
\]
This is not just a notational convenience. It changes the way we think about parameterization. If we insist on learning a single dense $d \times d$ matrix, then we are faced with $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ entries. But if we can break that matrix into structured factors, then we may be able to reduce the number of trainable parameters while still obtaining a matrix that behaves like a dense transform.

In the setting of orthogonal finetuning, this factorization is especially meaningful because the matrix $R$ is not just any transform. It is the learnable orthogonal update applied to a pretrained weight matrix. The central question is therefore not only whether $R$ can be dense, but whether it can be dense and parameter-efficient at the same time. The paper’s key move is to interpret this factorization problem through the lens of information transmission.

# Unrolling Matrix Multiplication into a Grid-Structured Graph

To study the factorization $R = B_m \cdots B_1$, the paper “unrolls” the multiplication into a graph with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes. Each column of the grid corresponds to one stage in the multiplication, and each row corresponds to one coordinate index. In this view, the product is not merely algebraic composition; it is a sequence of information exchanges across levels of a graph.

This graph viewpoint is useful because it turns matrix multiplication into a spatial picture. The first level represents the input coordinates, the last level represents the output coordinates, and the intermediate levels represent the state after each multiplication by one factor. Each factor $B_i$ then becomes the connectivity matrix between two adjacent levels. The entire factorization can be read as a layered network in which information moves from left to right through successive sparse transforms.

The paper emphasizes that this graph is obtained by unrolling the multiplication itself. That means the structure of the product is not imposed afterward; it is revealed by the factorization. As a result, questions about sparsity, expressivity, and parameter efficiency become questions about graph connectivity.

# Interpreting Nonzero Matrix Entries as Edges That Transmit Information

In the graph view, a nonzero entry of a matrix is interpreted as a directed edge. If the $(j_1,j_2)$ entry of $B_i$ is nonzero, then there is a directed edge from node $j_2$ in level $i$ to node $j_1$ in level $i+1$. If the entry is zero, then that direct transmission path does not exist. This simple interpretation is the foundation of the whole framework.

The intuition is that each factor $B_i$ controls what information can flow one step farther through the network. A sparse matrix means fewer edges, so fewer immediate transmission routes. A dense product matrix means that, after composing several sparse stages, information from many sources can still reach many destinations. Thus, sparsity at the level of factors does not necessarily imply sparsity at the level of the product.

The paper’s perspective is particularly powerful because it separates local and global structure. A factor can be sparse, but the product can still be dense if the sparse factors are arranged so that their combined edges allow information to propagate across the entire grid. This is the key idea behind efficient parameterization.

# Dense Matrices as All-to-All Reachability Between Source and Destination Nodes

A dense matrix corresponds, in this language, to all-to-all reachability between the source nodes in the first level and the destination nodes in the last level. If $R$ is dense, then every input coordinate can influence every output coordinate through some path in the graph. The paper treats this as the operational meaning of density.

This is a useful shift in viewpoint because density is normally a statement about entries, but here it becomes a statement about connectivity. Instead of asking whether $R_{ij}$ is zero or nonzero in isolation, we ask whether there exists a path from source node $j$ to destination node $i$ through the layered graph. In other words, a dense matrix is one where every source-destination pair is connected by at least one route through the factorization.

The paper also makes clear that this reachability is not abstract bookkeeping. It is directly tied to whether the factorization can represent a dense transform. If some source node cannot reach some destination node, then the corresponding entry in the product must be zero. So all-to-all reachability is the graph-theoretic condition that captures matrix density.

# How Reachability Determines the Sparsity Pattern of a Product Matrix

The sparsity pattern of the product matrix is determined by what information can flow through the chain of factors. If a path exists from a source node to a destination node, then the product may have a nonzero entry there. If no such path exists, then that entry is forced to be zero. This is why the paper treats matrix multiplication as a problem of information transmission rather than purely algebraic manipulation.

The main point is that the product matrix inherits its sparsity from the cumulative connectivity of the factors. Even if each individual factor is sparse, the product can become dense if the factors are arranged so that paths proliferate across layers. Conversely, if the factors are too restrictive, then some source-destination pairs remain disconnected, and zeros persist in the product.

This reachability perspective is what makes factorization design possible. Once the product matrix is viewed as the result of layered graph connectivity, one can ask which graph patterns are sufficient to guarantee density. The paper uses this question to motivate the search for sparse patterns that still permit dense outputs.

# Reading Missing Connections from Partial Products

One of the most intuitive aspects of the framework is that missing connections in partial products can be read directly from the unrolled graph. The paper gives a concrete example: if we consider a partial product such as $R = B_4 B_3 B_2 B_1$, and there is no path from node $1$ in the first level to node $3$ in the fifth level, then the $(3,1)$ entry of that partial product must be zero.

This is a powerful idea because it shows that sparsity is not merely a property of the final product. It can be traced through intermediate stages. If we truncate the factorization at an earlier point, we obtain a smaller graph with fewer levels, and the same correspondence between paths and nonzero entries still holds. The paper explicitly notes that the same relationship applies to $R = B_3 B_2 B_1$ and $R = B_2 B_1$ as well.

In this sense, the framework lets us “read off” zeros from missing connections. If the graph has no route from a given source to a given destination, then the associated entry cannot appear in the matrix. This makes the factorization problem much more intuitive: instead of thinking about a large matrix all at once, we can inspect how its nonzero entries are assembled through successive layers.

# Why Orthogonality Constrains Allowable Edge Patterns

The paper is not studying arbitrary sparse factorizations. It is specifically interested in orthogonal factorizations, because orthogonal updates preserve the pairwise angles of neurons and help retain pretrained knowledge. Orthogonality, however, imposes additional constraints on what edge patterns are possible.

A key point is that each factor must be full rank if it is to be orthogonal. In the graph view, this means that between two adjacent levels there must be enough edges to form a bijection between the nodes. The paper states that this requires $d$ edges between adjacent levels, which are necessary for orthogonality. These edges are not considered trainable in the same way as the extra degrees of freedom, because they are simply the minimum structural requirement for the matrix to be orthogonal.

Orthogonality also creates dependencies among edges. The paper points out that in a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrix, if one entry is forced to be zero, then another entry must also become zero. This means that allowable sparsity patterns are not arbitrary. A missing edge can force another missing edge, and conversely, certain patterns of nonzeros are constrained by the orthogonality condition. The graph picture is therefore more subtle than ordinary sparsity: orthogonality couples the edges together.

# Necessary Edges Versus Trainable or Free Edges

The paper distinguishes between edges that are necessary because of orthogonality and the additional edges that actually contribute to parameterization. Necessary edges are the ones needed just to make the factor full rank and orthogonal. In the paper’s phrasing, these are not counted as part of the effective trainable edge budget, because they do not represent extra flexibility.

The trainable, or free, edges are the ones that matter for parameter efficiency. These are the edges beyond the minimum orthogonality-imposed structure. The goal is to minimize the number of such free edges while still allowing the final product to be dense. This is why the paper says that the “minimum free edges” criterion is one of the central desiderata.

This distinction matters because a factorization can look sparse on paper but still hide many effective degrees of freedom if the orthogonality constraints force elaborate dependencies. By separating necessary structure from trainable structure, the information transmission framework provides a more precise way to measure efficiency. It is not enough to count zeros; one has to ask how many degrees of freedom remain after orthogonality is enforced.

# The Two Main Desiderata: Dense Connectivity and Minimum Free Edges

The paper identifies two main desiderata for a good sparse orthogonal factorization. First, it must achieve dense connectivity: every node in the first level should have at least one path to every node in the last level. Second, it should use as few free edges as possible, subject to the orthogonality constraint. These two goals capture the basic tension in the problem.

Dense connectivity ensures expressivity. If the graph is not sufficiently connected, then the product matrix cannot become dense, and some source-destination pairs will remain unreachable. But minimizing free edges ensures parameter efficiency. If too many extra edges are allowed, then the factorization loses the advantage of being sparse. The entire challenge is to satisfy both conditions simultaneously.

The framework makes this trade-off explicit. Rather than choosing a sparsity pattern ad hoc, we can ask whether a given graph structure has the right balance of connectivity and compactness. In the paper, this is exactly how the butterfly structure enters the picture: it is a pattern that can connect all nodes efficiently while keeping the number of edges low.

# Trading Computation for Memory Through Repeated Sparse Multiplications

A central practical idea in the paper is that parameter efficiency can be achieved by trading computation for memory. If we represent an orthogonal matrix as a product of sparse matrices, then we reduce the number of trainable parameters, but we must perform the multiplication repeatedly during each training iteration. This means the factorized representation is more computationally involved than a single unconstrained matrix.

The paper is explicit that this repetition is part of the cost of factorization. The product has to be formed from its sparse components each time the model is updated. In exchange, however, we avoid storing and optimizing a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix. This trade-off is exactly what makes the approach parameter-efficient.

From the paper’s perspective, this is a good bargain when large foundation models are being adapted to downstream tasks. Training from scratch is too expensive, and even direct finetuning of a full matrix can be parameter-heavy. A sparse factorization offers a way to preserve expressive power while reducing the number of learned quantities, even if it requires additional computation during training.

# Why This Framework Is Especially Useful for Studying OFT-Style Updates

The information transmission view is especially useful for orthogonal finetuning, or OFT, because OFT is already based on updating a pretrained weight matrix by multiplying it on the left with an orthogonal matrix. In OFT, the forward pass changes from
\[
z = (W^0)^\top x
\]
to
\[
z = (R W^0)^\top x,
\]
where $R$ is orthogonal and initialized as the identity matrix. Since the whole method revolves around the structure of $R$, understanding how to parameterize $R$ efficiently is the main technical challenge.

The original OFT uses a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix to reduce parameters. The paper argues that this achieves efficiency at the cost of a rigid sparsity pattern, where different groups of coordinates are transformed separately. The information transmission framework makes this limitation easy to see: block-diagonal structure restricts connectivity in a way that can be too rigid if one wants a dense orthogonal transform. In other words, the graph picture reveals why block-wise independence may be an awkward inductive bias.

This is why the framework matters. Once orthogonal finetuning is translated into a graph problem, the search for a better parameterization becomes the search for a better connectivity pattern. The paper’s broader contribution is to show that sparse factorization can be designed in a principled way, and that the resulting structure can preserve the virtues of orthogonal updates while using far fewer effective parameters.

Butterfly Structures and Efficient Dense Connectivity

# The Cooley-Tukey FFT and the Origin of Butterfly Graphs

The paper’s butterfly idea begins with the radix-$2$ Cooley-Tukey algorithm for the fast Fourier transform. The key observation is that a large $N$-point discrete Fourier transform can be broken recursively into two smaller $\frac{N}{2}$-point transforms. This divide-and-conquer structure is not just a computational trick; it also induces a distinctive pattern of information exchange. When the recursion is drawn as a graph, the connections between stages form the characteristic “butterfly” shape.

This origin matters because it gives a concrete model of how local interactions can produce global mixing. In the paper’s framing, the same pattern that lets the FFT efficiently redistribute frequency information also suggests how a matrix factorization can transmit information across many coordinates. A butterfly structure is therefore not an arbitrary sparsity pattern. It is a recursive communication pattern that was already known to be effective in the FFT, and the paper leverages that same pattern to build parameter-efficient orthogonal matrices.

# Why Butterfly Networks Are Efficient Communication Topologies

From the information-transmission perspective adopted in the paper, a dense matrix can be viewed as a complete communication pattern: every source coordinate should be able to influence every output coordinate. A butterfly network is efficient because it achieves this dense connectivity using only sparse connections at each stage. Rather than connecting every source directly to every receiver, it routes information through a sequence of intermediate levels, where each level performs a structured local exchange.

The paper emphasizes that butterfly graphs can connect $d$ source nodes and $d$ receiver nodes with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges. This is the essential efficiency claim. Each stage uses only a linear number of connections, but the number of stages grows only logarithmically with the dimension. As a result, the overall graph remains sparse at each stage while still allowing information from any input node to reach any output node through a suitable path.

This makes butterfly networks especially attractive for the paper’s goal of parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning. In that setting, one wants a dense orthogonal transformation, but one does not want to pay the full cost of a dense $d\times d$ parameterization. Butterfly topology gives a way to keep each factor sparse while still allowing the product of all factors to become dense.

# Defining a Butterfly Factor

The paper defines the basic building block as a butterfly factor. For a power-of-$2$ size $k \geq 2$, the butterfly factor is a $k\times k$ matrix of the form

$$
\bm{B}^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
$$

where each $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. The important feature here is that each quadrant is diagonal. That means the factor is sparse, but not randomly sparse. Its nonzero entries are arranged so that corresponding coordinates in the two halves of the matrix can mix in a coordinated way.

This definition is the atomic version of the butterfly pattern. The matrix is small enough to be simple, but rich enough to express a structured exchange between two halves of a vector. Because the nonzero entries are organized in diagonal subblocks, each coordinate interacts only with a limited set of partners at this stage. The full expressiveness comes not from a single factor, but from multiplying many such factors together in a recursive pattern.

# Recursive Construction of Butterfly Matrices

The paper then builds a full butterfly matrix recursively. For $d=2^N$, the $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix is defined as

$$
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(d/2) & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(d/2)
\end{bmatrix}
=
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d/2)\cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2).
$$

This recursive definition mirrors the recursion of the FFT itself. Each stage splits the problem into smaller pieces, mixes them, and then combines them again. The factorization therefore does not rely on one large dense matrix. Instead, it assembles global connectivity from a sequence of sparse stages.

Intuitively, the recursion is what makes the butterfly pattern powerful. A single sparse stage can only move information locally, but repeated sparse stages at different scales gradually spread information throughout the entire vector. By the end of the recursion, each output coordinate can depend on every input coordinate, even though each intermediate matrix remains sparse. This is exactly the kind of structure the paper wants for dense orthogonal finetuning: dense end-to-end connectivity, but with sparse intermediate factors.

# Butterfly Components as Structured Sparse Block Matrices

To describe the repeated factors used in the recursion, the paper introduces the butterfly component

$$
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\cdots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)).
$$

This is a block-diagonal matrix of size $d\times d$ with block size $k$. Each block is itself a butterfly factor. So although the matrix as a whole is large, its structure is built from many small, repeated sparse patterns.

The smallest nontrivial case is $k=2$. In that setting, the paper notes that orthogonality is easy to guarantee using a Cayley transform or an equivalent $2$-dimensional rotation parameterization for each block. Once the $k=2$ case is orthogonal, the other butterfly components are obtained by permuting the nonzero pattern of that base case. This is important because it means the paper can build orthogonal matrices from many simple pieces, rather than needing to directly parameterize a full dense orthogonal matrix.

Seen this way, a butterfly component is a structured sparse matrix whose pattern is not fixed in the rigid sense of simple block partitioning. Instead, the pattern is recursively derived and systematically reused across scales. That is what makes the butterfly family much more expressive than a plain block-diagonal decomposition.

# The Block-Butterfly Generalization Used in the Paper

The paper also generalizes the butterfly construction to a block version. In the block-butterfly setting, each scalar entry in the butterfly factor is replaced by a $b\times b$ matrix. The resulting component is denoted $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$, and the paper uses this to define the BOFT family.

This block generalization serves two purposes. First, it allows the model to work with larger internal blocks while retaining the butterfly topology. Second, it gives a direct way to control the trade-off between expressiveness and parameter efficiency. To keep the construction orthogonal, the paper parameterizes each $2b\times 2b$ block matrix as orthogonal in the base case $k=2$, and then uses block-wise permutations of that pattern for the larger components.

The resulting BOFT parameterization is

$$
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
$$

with each factor orthogonal. The paper notes several special cases: when $m=1$, BOFT reduces to the block-diagonal OFT form; when $b=d$, it recovers the original full OFT parameterization. This makes the butterfly generalization especially useful because it subsumes the older orthogonal finetuning designs rather than replacing them with an unrelated mechanism.

# How Butterfly Factorization Achieves Dense Connectivity with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ Structure

The central advantage of butterfly factorization is that it produces a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of sparse orthogonal matrices. In the paper’s graph view, the requirement for density is that every node in the first level can reach every node in the last level. Butterfly connectivity meets this requirement by layering sparse interactions over multiple stages so that information can propagate across the whole system.

The parameter count reflects this structure. The paper states that BOFT($m,b$) uses $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters for a linear layer of size $d\times n$. When the pure butterfly setting is used, namely $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, the number of parameters becomes $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$. That is the key scaling result: the matrix becomes dense in effect, but the parameterization grows only linearly in $d$ up to a logarithmic factor.

This is the crucial contrast with a single dense orthogonal matrix, which requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. Butterfly factorization achieves dense connectivity without paying that full cost because it spreads the work across many sparse factors. Each factor contributes a limited amount of trainable structure, but the product of all factors is rich enough to represent a dense transformation.

# Comparison with a Single Dense Matrix and with Block-Diagonal Sparsity

The paper contrasts butterfly factorization with both the fully dense orthogonal case and the block-diagonal case used in earlier OFT. A single dense orthogonal matrix is expressive, but its parameter cost is high. A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix is more parameter-efficient, but it imposes a very rigid sparsity pattern: different groups of coordinates are transformed independently, and the grouping is fixed by index.

That rigidity is a major limitation. The paper points out that the block-diagonal structure does not provide a natural way to approximate classic linear transforms, and it introduces an arbitrary assumption that coordinates should be partitioned into groups based on their indices. In other words, it reduces parameters by cutting the matrix into disconnected pieces, but that can hurt expressiveness and impose undesirable inductive biases.

Butterfly factorization offers a smoother compromise. It still uses sparsity, but the sparsity is organized recursively rather than by static blocks. This gives BOFT a more gradual interpolation between the identity matrix and the full orthogonal group. In the paper’s view, that makes butterfly structure a better fit for finetuning, where one wants enough flexibility to adapt the model while still preserving useful pretraining structure.

# Why Butterfly Structure Can Recover Many Classic Fast Linear Transforms

One of the strongest reasons the paper favors butterfly structure is that it can recover many classic fast linear transforms. The paper explicitly notes that, together with permutations, the butterfly structure can perfectly recover transforms such as the fast Fourier transform and the Hadamard transform. In the discussion of inductive bias, it also mentions the discrete Fourier transform, the discrete sine transform, and the discrete cosine transform.

This is not merely a coincidence of notation. These transforms all have a recursive, stage-wise structure that matches the way butterfly factors mix information. Because the butterfly pattern is built from repeated local exchanges at multiple scales, it can represent the same kind of hierarchical mixing that these fast transforms use. The block-diagonal pattern, by contrast, cannot reproduce such global recursive mixing, because it keeps the coordinate groups isolated from one another.

So the ability to recover classic fast transforms serves as evidence that butterfly factorization is not an ad hoc sparsity pattern. It is a structured family with a strong mathematical and algorithmic lineage. That lineage is one of the reasons the paper argues that BOFT carries a useful inductive bias for generalization.

# Prior Uses of Butterfly Matrices in Numerical Methods and Machine Learning

The paper places its contribution in the context of earlier work on butterfly matrices. In numerical methods, butterfly parameterizations have been used to parameterize orthogonal matrices, avoid pivoting in Gaussian elimination, and improve efficiency. They have also been used in kernel approximation and in fast matrix-vector multiplication. These examples show that the butterfly idea has long been associated with structured efficiency.

In machine learning, butterfly matrices have appeared in work on stabilizing recurrent neural networks, learning fast linear transforms, and enabling efficient training of neural networks. The paper also cites uses in data-sparse matrix approximation and network transmission. What BOFT adds is a new finetuning strategy: rather than using butterfly matrices simply to train a model from scratch or to approximate a linear transform, it uses them to build an efficient orthogonal update on top of a pretrained foundation model.

This distinction is important. BOFT is not just another butterfly-parameterized network. It combines the butterfly structure with orthogonal finetuning, so the learned transformation remains orthogonal while being far more parameter-efficient than a full dense orthogonal matrix. That is the specific role butterfly structures play in the paper: they are the mechanism that makes orthogonal finetuning practical at scale.

BOFT: Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning

# Making each butterfly component orthogonal

BOFT begins with a simple but important requirement: if the finetuning transform is built from several factors, then each factor must itself be orthogonal so that their product remains orthogonal. The paper starts from the butterfly matrix viewpoint and then turns it into an orthogonal parameterization. In the scalar setting, a butterfly factor is written as
\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathrm{diag}(\bm d_1) & \mathrm{diag}(\bm d_2)\\
\mathrm{diag}(\bm d_3) & \mathrm{diag}(\bm d_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in \mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\]
where each $\bm d_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. A larger butterfly matrix is then assembled recursively from these factors. The key intuition is that the butterfly construction does not try to make a single large dense orthogonal matrix directly. Instead, it builds the matrix from a sequence of structured pieces whose sparsity patterns are arranged so that information can pass through the whole space in stages.

This staged construction is what makes orthogonal factorization feasible. The paper defines the full butterfly matrix recursively as a product of butterfly components, and then requires each multiplicative component to be orthogonal. Once that is true, the final product is orthogonal automatically. In other words, BOFT turns a difficult global constraint into a collection of local constraints. Rather than searching over all dense orthogonal matrices, it searches over products of sparse orthogonal matrices whose patterns are carefully chosen to compose into a much richer transform.

# Orthogonal $2 \times 2$ rotations and their blockwise generalization

The smallest nontrivial butterfly unit is a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal transform. In the paper, this can be realized either as a $2$-dimensional rotation or through the Cayley parameterization. This matters because a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal block is the atomic unit from which the larger butterfly structure is built. Once the smallest stage is orthogonal, the higher stages can be viewed as rearrangements and repetitions of the same local orthogonal mixing pattern.

BOFT then generalizes this idea from scalar entries to blocks. In the block butterfly setting, each scalar entry in the butterfly factor is replaced by a $b \times b$ matrix, so the smallest orthogonal unit becomes a $2b \times 2b$ block matrix. The paper notes that the non-zero pattern of every butterfly component is a block-wise permutation of the pattern of the smallest component. That means the same orthogonality idea used for the base $2 \times 2$ stage can be lifted to the block setting: if the base block is orthogonal, then the larger components can be parameterized orthogonally as well.

# Using Cayley parameterization inside butterfly blocks

To keep each component orthogonal during optimization, BOFT uses the Cayley parameterization. The paper writes an orthogonal matrix as
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, meaning $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. This is a useful device because orthogonality is enforced by construction rather than by an external constraint or projection step. As the parameters change, the resulting matrix stays on the orthogonal manifold.

BOFT initializes these Cayley parameters at zero, which means $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ at the start. Then $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, so the finetuned model initially matches the pretrained model exactly. This identity initialization is important because it gives a clean starting point for finetuning: BOFT does not perturb the pretrained network at the beginning, but instead learns a gradual orthogonal deviation from it. Within each butterfly block, the Cayley transform provides the orthogonal parameterization that makes this possible.

# Constructing the final BOFT transform $\bm{R}(m,b)$

The full BOFT transform is a product of multiple orthogonal butterfly components. The paper writes this as
\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component. The notation $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a shorthand for the stage-$i$ butterfly factor at dimension $d$ and block size $b$. Because each factor is orthogonal, their product is orthogonal as well. So $\bm{R}(m,b)$ stays inside the orthogonal group while still being built from sparse pieces.

The role of the two main design parameters is easy to interpret. The number of factors $m$ controls how many rounds of butterfly mixing are applied, while the block size $b$ controls the local granularity of each factor. Smaller blocks mean more sparsity and fewer parameters per factor; more factors mean more opportunities for information to travel across the full dimension. BOFT is therefore not just a single matrix form, but a family of orthogonal transforms indexed by how deep and how coarse the butterfly decomposition is.

# The BOFT forward pass for a pretrained linear layer

BOFT is inserted into a pretrained linear layer by left-multiplying the pretrained weight matrix with the orthogonal transform. If the pretrained layer is $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, then the original forward pass is
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\]
for input $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$. BOFT changes this to
\[
\bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
So instead of adding a separate adapter or a low-rank correction, BOFT reparameterizes the existing weight matrix by an orthogonal transformation.

This is a layer-shared update: the same orthogonal matrix is applied to all neurons in the layer, and the paper emphasizes that a neuron corresponds to a column vector of $\bm{W}^0$. The motivation is to preserve the geometric relations among neurons, especially their pairwise angles, while still allowing the model to adapt to a downstream task. In this sense, BOFT is a multiplicative finetuning method: it changes the weight matrix by composing it with an orthogonal factor rather than by adding a new module.

# Notation for $b$, $m$, and BOFT variants

The paper uses BOFT$(m,b)$ to denote the method with $m$ butterfly factors and block size $b$. The corresponding orthogonal transform is $\bm{R}(m,b)$. When the authors refer to a specific factor, they write $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, which indicates the $i$-th orthogonal butterfly component at dimension $d$ with block size $b$. This notation is designed to make the structure of the factorization explicit: one can read off both the depth of the butterfly composition and the local block structure of each stage.

This notation also helps track how BOFT interpolates between different orthogonal parameterizations. At one extreme, the model has only one factor and behaves like a block-diagonal orthogonal update. At the other extreme, sufficiently many butterfly stages can produce a dense orthogonal transform. The parameters $m$ and $b$ therefore control the position of BOFT along a spectrum between simplicity and expressivity.

# Special case: BOFT with one factor equals block-diagonal OFT

If $m=1$, BOFT reduces to a single orthogonal butterfly component. In the paper, this is exactly the block-diagonal OFT form with block size $b$. So the shallowest BOFT is not a separate method from OFT; it is the block-diagonal OFT parameterization already used there. This is one reason the paper presents BOFT as a general framework rather than a completely unrelated design.

This special case is important conceptually because it shows that BOFT strictly contains the earlier block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning approach. The butterfly structure is not replacing OFT’s orthogonal update; it is extending it. When $m=1$, the structure is still sparse and block-separated, but the broader BOFT formalism already describes it.

# Special case: one full block equals unconstrained OFT

If $m=1$ and the block size is set to $b=d$, then the block-diagonal restriction disappears because the entire matrix is a single block. In that case, BOFT becomes the original OFT with a full dense orthogonal matrix. The paper explicitly identifies this as another special case of its framework.

This limit is useful because it shows that BOFT is not tied to block sparsity. The same notation can represent a fully dense orthogonal update when the block size is large enough, while also representing a sparse block-diagonal update when the block size is small. So BOFT spans both the low-parameter and high-expressivity ends of the orthogonal finetuning spectrum within one unified construction.

# Special case: enough butterfly factors yields a dense orthogonal matrix

The main advantage of the butterfly construction is that it can produce a dense orthogonal matrix without needing a full $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameterization. The paper states that BOFT with enough factors, specifically BOFT$\big(\log \frac{2d}{b}, b\big)$, uses the block butterfly matrix $\bm{B}^{b/2}(d)$ as $\bm{R}$ and yields a dense orthogonal matrix. In the pure butterfly setting, taking $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ gives the classic butterfly depth.

The intuition is that each butterfly stage exchanges information across a larger scale. Early stages mix nearby coordinates, later stages mix progressively farther-apart coordinates, and after enough stages every coordinate can influence every other coordinate. This is the same basic logic that makes the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform efficient: local interactions, repeated recursively, can create global mixing. BOFT borrows this structure to get dense orthogonal behavior from sparse orthogonal factors.

# Effective parameter count and why BOFT scales as $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$

The paper summarizes the effective trainable parameter count of BOFT$(m,b)$ for a linear layer of size $d \times n$ as
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm.
\]
This is the key efficiency statement behind the method. The count is far smaller than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters needed for a full dense orthogonal matrix in original OFT. It is also more flexible than a plain block-diagonal scheme because the butterfly structure can achieve dense connectivity with only logarithmically many factors.

In the pure butterfly case, where $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, the scaling becomes $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$. The paper contrasts this with the original OFT and with block-diagonal OFT, emphasizing that BOFT gets a much better tradeoff between expressivity and parameter efficiency. The important point is not just that there are fewer parameters, but that the parameters are organized in a way that still allows dense orthogonal mixing when enough factors are stacked.

# How BOFT generalizes OFT into a broader orthogonal finetuning framework

BOFT generalizes OFT by replacing a single orthogonal update with a structured product of orthogonal butterfly factors. This gives a larger family of admissible transforms while preserving the core OFT principle: the finetuning update should remain orthogonal so that the geometry of the pretrained representation is not arbitrarily distorted. In the paper’s framing, BOFT creates a smoother interpolation between regularity and expressivity than the block-diagonal structure alone.

This broader framework is the real conceptual contribution of BOFT. The block-diagonal structure of OFT reduces parameters, but it also hard-codes a fixed sparsity pattern that the paper argues is limiting. BOFT instead uses butterfly factorization, which can recover many classic fast linear transforms and can also represent dense orthogonal matrices. As a result, BOFT acts like a structured subset of the orthogonal group: it is restrictive enough to provide an inductive bias, but flexible enough to include OFT as a special case and to move beyond it when the downstream task needs more expressive power.

Training BOFT in Practice

# Identity Initialization of Butterfly Components

BOFT is built to behave like the pretrained model at the start of finetuning, and that goal is reflected in how every orthogonal factor is initialized. In the BOFT parameterization, the learned orthogonal matrix is not stored as one dense object; instead, it is formed as a product of multiple orthogonal butterfly components. The paper initializes each of these components to the identity matrix, and it does so by initializing the skew-symmetric matrix in the Cayley transform as zero. This is the same identity-start idea used in orthogonal finetuning more generally: the model should begin from the exact pretrained solution and only move away from it as training learns a useful downstream adaptation.

This initialization matters because BOFT is a multiplicative update. Rather than adding a new trainable correction to the pretrained weight matrix, BOFT transforms the pretrained weights by left-multiplying them with an orthogonal matrix. If the factors are all identity matrices at initialization, then the product is also the identity matrix, so the entire BOFT module begins in the most conservative possible state. In practical terms, the finetuned layer initially behaves exactly like the original pretrained layer, which keeps the adaptation from introducing any unintended disturbance before learning begins.

# Exact Preservation of the Pretrained Model at Step Zero

The exact preservation property follows directly from the forward pass used in BOFT. For a pretrained linear layer with weights $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, the original output is $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. BOFT changes this to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is the learned orthogonal matrix composed of butterfly factors. When every butterfly component is initialized as the identity, the product $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is exactly $\bm{I}$, so the BOFT layer reduces to
$$
(\bm{I}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
$$
Thus, the initial model is not merely close to the pretrained model; it is identical to it.

This exact match is important because it makes the finetuning process well grounded. The paper’s general motivation for orthogonal finetuning is to preserve the relational structure of neurons, especially pairwise angles, so that pretrained semantic knowledge is largely retained. Starting at the identity is the cleanest way to respect that principle. The model can then learn the downstream task by gradually moving away from the initial point, rather than being forced to recover the pretrained behavior after an arbitrary perturbation.

# Why Standard Dropout Does Not Directly Fit BOFT

The paper points out that conventional Dropout, while natural in some reparameterized methods, does not directly match BOFT’s structure. The reason is that BOFT updates weights multiplicatively rather than additively. In methods like LoRA, the trainable update is an added low-rank term, so dropping parts of that additive correction is conceptually straightforward. BOFT does not work that way. Its trainable object is an orthogonal transform that acts on the pretrained weights as a product, so a naive application of standard Dropout does not align with the way the parameters actually enter the model.

This mismatch is not just a cosmetic issue. A dropout mechanism has to respect the algebraic form of the update if it is to be useful and well defined. In BOFT, the update is not a separate residual branch that can be masked independently; it is embedded into the orthogonal factorization itself. For that reason, the paper introduces a dropout variant specifically designed for multiplicative orthogonal updates rather than borrowing the standard additive intuition unchanged.

# Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT

To regularize BOFT while respecting its multiplicative structure, the paper proposes multiplicative Dropout. The key idea is simple: instead of dropping activations or additive residuals, BOFT drops parts of the orthogonal factorization itself by replacing selected pieces with identity matrices. Since the orthogonal matrix is built from $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, and those components can be permuted into block-diagonal orthogonal form, the paper applies dropout at two levels. It randomly selects a fraction of the butterfly components, denoted by $p_1$, and within each selected component it randomly selects a fraction of the diagonal blocks, denoted by $p_2$, then replaces those chosen blocks by identity matrices.

This makes the dropout operation compatible with the BOFT parameterization. The model still lives inside the orthogonal framework, but part of the learned structure is temporarily suppressed during training. Intuitively, this means the model does not depend too heavily on every factor or block at once. The paper presents this as a way to regularize the multiplicative decomposition while keeping the update scheme aligned with the orthogonal structure that BOFT is designed to exploit.

# Choosing the Number of Factors $m$ and Block Size $b$

BOFT exposes two main structural knobs: the number of factors $m$ and the block size $b$. These choices directly control how the orthogonal matrix is factorized and how many trainable parameters the method uses. The paper states that BOFT$(m,b)$ takes $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters for finetuning a linear layer of size $d \times n$. This makes the budget explicit: larger blocks and more factors increase the parameter count, while smaller blocks reduce it.

The special cases make the meaning of these knobs especially clear. When $m=1$, BOFT reduces to the block-diagonal OFT design with block size $b$. When $m=1$ and $b=d$, it reduces to the original OFT with an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix. At the other end of the spectrum, when the butterfly matrix is used with $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, BOFT achieves $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, which is a substantial reduction from the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters of a full dense orthogonal matrix. The paper’s broader point is that BOFT can reach dense orthogonal matrices through factorization while allowing the user to tune the balance between structure and capacity.

# Expressivity, Regularity, and Parameter Budget

The paper does not treat parameter reduction as the only objective. Instead, it emphasizes a balance between expressivity and regularity. Expressivity refers to how richly the orthogonal transform can vary; regularity refers to how strongly the structure constrains the hypothesis class. BOFT is designed to sit between these two extremes. Because it uses butterfly factorization, it can generate dense orthogonal matrices while still imposing a structured prior that is much less arbitrary than a fully unconstrained matrix.

This trade-off is one of the main practical reasons BOFT is useful. The paper argues that the butterfly structure is more favorable than the block-diagonal sparsity pattern used by OFT, because the butterfly pattern can recover many classic fast linear transforms such as the discrete Fourier transform, the discrete cosine transform, and the Hadamard transform, while the block-diagonal pattern cannot recover any of them. In the paper’s approximation experiment, BOFT generally achieves better parameter efficiency than OFT, and smaller $b$ with larger $m$ tends to be more efficient. At the same time, the paper stresses that greater expressivity does not automatically mean better finetuning performance. The goal is not to maximize freedom, but to find a structured subset of the orthogonal group that is rich enough for the downstream task while still regular enough to preserve generalization.

# Computation Versus Memory in Sparse Factorization

BOFT reduces parameter storage by replacing one dense orthogonal matrix with a product of sparse orthogonal factors, but that reduction is not free. The paper explicitly notes that the matrix multiplication must be performed repeatedly in each training iteration. In other words, BOFT trades computation time for space. Instead of storing and directly optimizing a large dense orthogonal matrix, the method stores multiple sparse factors and composes them when the forward pass is evaluated.

This is the practical cost of factorization. The benefit is that the number of trainable parameters can be brought down dramatically, especially when the butterfly structure is used. The drawback is that training requires repeated sparse multiplications, so the runtime overhead is slightly larger than in OFT. The paper presents this as an acceptable trade-off for parameter efficiency, especially given that the model is still easy to deploy after training. The crucial point is that the factorization is only a training-time burden; it does not become a permanent inference-time cost.

# Training Stability Under Orthogonal Updates

One of the paper’s main reasons for preferring orthogonal finetuning over other reparameterizations is stability. The motivating intuition is that orthogonal transformations preserve the pairwise angles between neurons, so the finetuned model stays structurally close to the pretrained model in a meaningful geometric sense. This is part of the reason orthogonal finetuning is viewed as preserving pretrained knowledge better than a less constrained update would.

The paper also highlights a spectral advantage. If $\bm{W}^0$ has singular value decomposition $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, then multiplying by an orthogonal matrix on the left gives $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, the largest singular value of the pretrained matrix is preserved. The paper states that this preservation of spectral norm is beneficial for training stability and generalization. In practice, BOFT inherits this same advantage because it is still an orthogonal finetuning method; the only difference is that the orthogonal matrix is parameterized more efficiently through butterfly factorization.

# Merging BOFT Into Pretrained Weights

BOFT is designed so that the learned orthogonal transformation can be absorbed into the pretrained weights after finetuning. This is possible because the finetuned layer is just the pretrained weight matrix left-multiplied by the learned orthogonal matrix. Once training is finished, the product $\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0$ can be formed explicitly and treated as the new weight matrix of the layer.

This merging step is important in practice because it means BOFT does not require a special runtime representation after training ends. The learned factors do not have to remain separate modules during deployment. Instead, they can be combined into standard weights, which makes the finetuned model easy to store and use in ordinary inference pipelines.

# Why BOFT Adds No Inference Latency

The main practical advantage of BOFT over many other parameter-efficient methods is that it adds no inference latency. This follows from the same merging property described above. Although the model is trained using multiple sparse orthogonal factors, these factors do not need to be applied separately at inference time. After finetuning, the orthogonal matrices can be directly multiplied into the pretrained model, and the resulting model is just an ordinary weight matrix.

This is why the extra structure in BOFT is a training-time structure rather than a deployment-time burden. The paper explicitly contrasts this with methods whose added modules remain active at inference. BOFT instead keeps the inference path simple while still benefiting from a structured and parameter-efficient finetuning process during training. In practical terms, that means the method gives you the advantages of a richer orthogonal parameterization without paying for that structure every time the model is used.

Expressivity, Stability, and Inductive Bias

# Simulation-based approximation of random orthogonal matrices

A central way the paper probes the expressive power of BOFT is by asking a simple but revealing question: how well can a parameterization approximate a random dense orthogonal matrix? This is a useful test because the target itself is not tied to any particular downstream task; it isolates the geometry of the parameterization. In the paper’s simulation, the target is a random dense orthogonal matrix of size $512 \times 512$, and the approximation error is averaged over $10$ random seeds. The horizontal axis measures the number of effective trainable parameters, so the plot directly reveals how much expressivity can be obtained per parameter.

The paper interprets the curves in a very intuitive way. For each fixed block size, the leftmost point corresponds to BOFT($1,b$), which is exactly block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. As the number of butterfly factors increases, the parameterization can use more stages of sparse orthogonal mixing, and the approximation quality improves more efficiently than in the block-diagonal setting. The paper highlights examples such as BOFT($9,2$), which is more expressive than BOFT($1,16$) while using far fewer parameters, and BOFT($6,4$), which achieves a similar approximation error to BOFT($2,16$) with fewer parameters. The lesson is not merely that “more parameters help,” but that the right structure lets the model spend parameters where they matter.

This simulation therefore serves as evidence for the paper’s main thesis about expressivity. BOFT does not rely on large dense blocks to become expressive; instead, it achieves a dense transformation by composing sparse orthogonal pieces. In other words, the approximation curves suggest that depth in the factorization can substitute for width in the individual blocks, and this is exactly what makes BOFT more parameter-efficient than OFT.

# How BOFT achieves better parameter-efficiency than OFT in expressivity tests

To understand why BOFT is parameter-efficient, it helps to compare it directly with the original orthogonal finetuning framework. In OFT, the new weight matrix is obtained by left-multiplying the pretrained matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. To reduce the parameter count, OFT constrains $\bm{R}$ to be block-diagonal. This is parameter-efficient, but it comes with a severe structural limitation: the coordinates are partitioned into fixed groups, and each group is transformed independently. The paper points out that this grouping is based purely on index positions, which is not a natural semantic division of neuron dimensions.

BOFT changes the tradeoff by replacing one large orthogonal matrix with a product of several sparse orthogonal matrices. This is the key step that improves parameter-efficiency without forcing the model into a rigid block structure. Because the orthogonal matrix is represented as a factorization, the model can generate dense interactions among coordinates over multiple stages, rather than demanding that every interaction be stored directly in one dense matrix. The paper emphasizes that this is a classic space-time tradeoff: one pays a bit more computation during training by repeatedly multiplying factors, but one saves a large amount of parameter space.

The resulting scaling is much more favorable. In the butterfly setting, the paper notes that one can obtain a $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix using $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse factors, each with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries, yielding $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. By contrast, a dense orthogonal matrix uses $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, and block-diagonal OFT still relies on a fixed sparsity pattern that cannot fully recover dense orthogonality unless the blocks become as large as the entire matrix. So the expressivity tests are not just empirical plots; they illustrate the mechanism by which BOFT gets more out of each parameter.

# The expressivity theorem: BOFT is more expressive than OFT at the same block size

The paper makes the expressivity claim precise through a theorem stating that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. This is an important statement because it separates two ideas that are easy to confuse. Parameter-efficiency alone does not guarantee expressivity, and expressivity alone does not guarantee efficiency. The theorem says that the butterfly construction gives BOFT a strictly richer family of orthogonal transformations than the block-diagonal structure used in OFT, even when the block size is held fixed.

The theorem also indicates how dense orthogonal matrices can be approximated or represented using butterfly matrices. In particular, the paper states that, for the butterfly matrix to approximate all orthogonal matrices of size $d$, one can multiply butterfly matrices in the form $\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}_{d-1,2}^\top(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}_{1,2}^\top(d)$, where each $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$ is itself a butterfly matrix. The role of the transposes here is to enrich the representational family by letting the composition move through a larger portion of the orthogonal group.

The conceptual point is that a factorized butterfly construction can cover a much broader set of orthogonal transformations than a block-diagonal matrix with the same block size. A block-diagonal matrix is limited by its disconnected blocks; butterfly factorizations create a chain of structured interactions that can eventually couple all coordinates. The theorem therefore formalizes what the approximation experiments suggest: the butterfly parameterization is not merely cheaper, but also more capable.

# Generalizing BOFT with repeated butterfly and transposed-butterfly products

The paper does not stop at a single butterfly factorization. It introduces a generalized orthogonal matrix
$\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)=\bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)$,
which is built by repeating products of BOFT factors and their transposes. This is the paper’s way of widening the family of orthogonal matrices that can be expressed while keeping the same basic structural idea: use factorized orthogonal pieces to build a larger orthogonal whole.

This generalization is important because it shows that BOFT is not tied to one particular butterfly depth or one particular block size. Instead, it provides a framework in which one can tune the depth and the local block structure jointly. When $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and $l=d-1$, the paper states that this construction can represent the entire orthogonal group. That is a very strong representational statement: the butterfly-based framework is not merely an approximation trick, but a compositional route to full orthogonal expressivity.

The intuition is that each additional factor adds another structured stage of mixing. Rather than trying to describe every possible orthogonal transformation at once, the generalized construction accumulates expressive power through repeated orthogonal components. This is why BOFT can be viewed as a framework rather than a single fixed architecture. The paper uses this generalization to show that butterfly-based finetuning can interpolate between more restricted and more expressive orthogonal families.

# The kaleidoscope hierarchy and representation of the full orthogonal group

The repeated composition of butterfly matrices and transposed butterfly matrices is referred to in the paper as a kaleidoscope hierarchy. The name is fitting because the construction works by composing repeated structured transformations in a layered way, much as a kaleidoscope repeatedly reflects and recombines a small pattern into a richer whole. In the paper’s setting, these repeated compositions increase the expressive reach of the orthogonal parameterization without abandoning structure.

The most striking claim here is that, with the choice $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and $l=d-1$, the generalized construction can represent the entire orthogonal group. This means that the butterfly-based family is not inherently a restricted family of orthogonal matrices; instead, it can be made universal within the orthogonal group by stacking enough appropriately structured pieces. The paper presents this as a natural extension of BOFT, and it connects directly to earlier work on kaleidoscope-style matrix composition.

At the same time, the paper uses this result carefully. Full representational power is mathematically appealing, but it is not automatically the best choice for finetuning. The fact that a family can represent all orthogonal matrices does not mean every downstream task benefits from such an expansive hypothesis class. The kaleidoscope hierarchy therefore serves as a theoretical upper bound on expressivity, while BOFT itself is positioned as a more practical point on the spectrum between simplicity and universality.

# Why more expressivity is not always better for finetuning

One of the paper’s most important conceptual points is that more expressivity is not automatically better in finetuning. This may seem counterintuitive at first, since expressivity is usually treated as a virtue in model design. But finetuning is different from training from scratch. The model already contains useful pretrained knowledge, and the central challenge is to adapt that knowledge without destroying it. A parameterization that is too unconstrained can move too far away from the pretrained solution and lose the benefit of pretraining.

The paper makes this point explicitly by contrasting orthogonal finetuning with full finetuning. Full finetuning is, in principle, maximally expressive, but the paper notes that it often yields unsatisfactory performance. This is a reminder that downstream success depends on more than the ability to represent many functions. It also depends on having the right structural prior, so that the optimization process searches in a favorable part of the hypothesis space.

BOFT is designed around this tradeoff. It enlarges the finetuning parameter space with a structured orthogonal factorization, but it does not abandon regularity. The butterfly construction gives a smaller hypothesis class than arbitrary dense updates, yet one that is still expressive enough to capture useful transformations. The paper’s message is that good finetuning is not about maximizing freedom; it is about balancing freedom with structure in a way that preserves useful pretrained behavior.

# Smooth interpolation between identity-like updates and highly expressive orthogonal transforms

The paper describes BOFT as constructing a smoother interpolation between identity matrices and highly expressive orthogonal matrices. This is a subtle but important idea. Identity-like updates correspond to regularity: they preserve the pretrained model almost exactly. Highly expressive orthogonal transforms correspond to the ability to make meaningful task-specific changes. A good finetuning method should connect these two extremes without abrupt jumps.

BOFT does this by using multiple orthogonal butterfly components, each of which can be initialized at the identity. Since the components are multiplied together, the overall orthogonal matrix starts exactly from the identity when the factor parameters are initialized accordingly. As training proceeds, the model gradually moves away from the pretrained solution, but it does so through structured orthogonal transformations rather than through arbitrary dense perturbations. This creates a smoother path from the original model to the adapted model.

The paper contrasts this with the block-diagonal structure in OFT. Block-diagonal sparsity imposes a rigid partition of the coordinates, while butterfly structure spreads interaction across stages. That makes the transition from regularity to expressivity less abrupt. In the paper’s view, this smoother interpolation is part of what makes BOFT a better finetuning framework: it preserves the pretrained model at initialization, yet it can grow into richer orthogonal transformations as needed.

# Weight interpolation behavior induced by matrix factorization

Because BOFT represents the update as a product of factor matrices, the learned weight matrix is not a single unconstrained object. Instead, it is assembled from smaller orthogonal pieces, and this factorization gives the update a distinctive interpolation behavior. The paper highlights this as an intriguing consequence of the method. The overall weight transformation changes through coordinated changes in the factors, rather than through one monolithic dense parameter matrix.

The most immediate form of interpolation is between the pretrained model and the learned orthogonal update. Since BOFT is identity-initialized, the finetuned weights begin at the pretrained configuration and then move continuously within the orthogonal family. The factorization makes this movement structured: each factor remains orthogonal, and the product remains orthogonal, so the interpolation never leaves the class of admissible transformations. That is a very different kind of interpolation from unconstrained weight updates.

This behavior matters because it ties together expressivity and regularity. The factorized representation gives BOFT enough flexibility to adapt, but the multiplicative structure keeps the adaptation coherent. The paper’s discussion suggests that this is one reason BOFT can be both stable and expressive: the matrix factorization organizes how the learned transformation evolves, rather than letting it drift arbitrarily.

# Spectral norm preservation under orthogonal finetuning

A major reason orthogonal finetuning is attractive is that it preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. The paper explains this through singular value decomposition. If the pretrained weight matrix is written as
$\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$,
then left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ gives
$\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$.
Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, this operation does not change the singular values, and in particular it does not change the largest singular value.

The paper emphasizes this as a strong spectral property of orthogonal finetuning. In the BOFT setting, the learned orthogonal matrix is still orthogonal, so the same conclusion holds. This is an important difference from many other parameter-efficient methods, where the update can alter the scale and conditioning of the weights in less controlled ways. Orthogonal finetuning keeps the update on a geometrically constrained manifold.

From a conceptual point of view, this matters because spectral behavior is closely tied to how signals propagate through a network layer. If the norm of the transformation is preserved, the layer avoids introducing uncontrolled amplification or collapse purely through the finetuning update. The paper presents this as one of the main theoretical advantages of BOFT and OFT over additive low-rank updates.

# Why preserving the spectrum can improve training stability and generalization

The paper connects spectral norm preservation to training stability and generalization. The argument is not that spectrum alone determines performance, but that preserving the spectral norm protects the model from undesirable distortions during adaptation. Since the update is orthogonal, the finetuned weight matrix retains the same largest singular value as the pretrained one. That makes the finetuning process more controlled.

The paper notes that such spectral preservation has been shown to greatly benefit training stability and generalization. Intuitively, if the update does not arbitrarily change the scale of the linear transformation, optimization can proceed in a more predictable regime. This is especially useful when adapting large pretrained models, where preserving useful pretrained structure is a major concern. BOFT inherits this benefit because its factorization still produces an orthogonal update.

This helps explain why orthogonal finetuning can be stable even when it is expressive. The key is that the model is not free to wander anywhere in parameter space. It is constrained to transformations that keep the spectrum intact, so adaptation happens inside a geometrically well-behaved family. In the paper’s view, this combination of controlled geometry and structural flexibility is one of the core strengths of BOFT.

# BOFT as learning a bilinear similarity matrix

The paper also gives a different interpretation of BOFT: it can be seen as learning a bilinear similarity. For a given neuron vector $\bm{w}_i^0$ from the pretrained weight matrix, BOFT effectively uses the form $\bm{w}_i^0 \bm{R}\bm{x}$. This is a bilinear expression in the pretrained neuron and the input vector, with $\bm{R}$ acting as the learned similarity matrix. Rather than updating the neuron itself arbitrarily, BOFT learns how that neuron should interact with the input under an orthogonally constrained transformation.

This viewpoint is useful because it reframes finetuning as a structured similarity-learning problem. The pretrained weights are not discarded; instead, they define one side of the bilinear form, and the orthogonal matrix shapes the interaction. The paper emphasizes that $\bm{R}$ comes with strong regularity because it must remain orthogonal. So BOFT is not simply learning any bilinear map, but a constrained one that preserves the geometry of the pretrained layer.

This interpretation also helps connect BOFT to the broader language of representation learning. The finetuning process is not just modifying coefficients; it is altering the way inputs and pretrained neurons are compared and combined. That makes BOFT conceptually close to other frameworks that learn structured similarities, while keeping its distinctive orthogonal constraint.

# Connections to metric learning and bilinear forms

The paper explicitly notes that BOFT’s bilinear viewpoint connects to distance metric learning and to bilinear forms. This connection is mathematically natural. A bilinear form is any expression of the form $\bm{u}^\top \bm{A}\bm{v}$, and BOFT’s update has exactly this kind of structure once one views $\bm{w}_i^0 \bm{R}\bm{x}$ as a similarity between a pretrained neuron and an input. The matrix $\bm{R}$ plays the role of a learned transformation that shapes the interaction.

The metric-learning connection comes from the fact that learned similarity matrices are often used to measure how close or compatible two vectors are under a task-specific geometry. BOFT does something similar, except the similarity matrix is required to be orthogonal. That restriction gives the learned similarity a special regularity, since the geometry of the transformation remains constrained throughout training.

The paper does not claim that BOFT is identical to standard metric learning. Rather, it uses this comparison to help the reader understand what the orthogonal update is doing. BOFT is learning a similarity structure under a tight geometric constraint, and that is why the bilinear-form perspective is illuminating. It shows that orthogonal finetuning can be understood as a principled way to reshape interactions without abandoning the pretrained representation.

# Structured inductive bias from butterfly transforms

A major reason the paper favors butterfly factorization is that it brings a useful structured inductive bias. The butterfly structure appears in many fast linear transforms, including the discrete Fourier transform, the discrete sine and cosine transforms, and the Hadamard transform. The paper argues that this shared pattern is not accidental: butterfly structure captures a form of multiscale mixing that is useful in many settings.

This matters because structured inductive bias can improve generalizability. If a parameterization naturally aligns with patterns that frequently arise in efficient linear transforms, then it may favor solutions that are both expressive and well-organized. The paper specifically argues that the butterfly structure can easily recover many classic linear transforms, whereas the block-diagonal structure in OFT cannot recover any of them. That is a strong indication that butterfly factorization is not just parameter-efficient, but structurally more suitable for a wide range of transformations.

The paper’s point is not that every downstream task needs a Fourier-like transform. Rather, it is that the existence of such recoverable structure suggests a meaningful prior. BOFT constrains the orthogonal group in a way that is already aligned with well-known efficient transforms, and this may explain why it generalizes well while remaining parameter-efficient.

# Implicit bias introduced by sparse matrix factorization

Finally, the paper suggests that the sparse factorization itself may introduce an implicit inductive bias. This is a more subtle idea than the explicit structural prior of orthogonality. Even among orthogonal matrices, the particular way BOFT parameterizes them can influence which solutions are easier to reach during optimization. Because the orthogonal matrix is built from sparse components, the optimization landscape is not the same as it would be for an unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix.

The paper does not give a full characterization of this implicit bias, but it points to the broader literature on implicit bias in optimization to motivate the idea. The important takeaway is that parameterization matters not only because it changes the number of parameters, but also because it changes how the model is learned. A sparse factorization can favor certain structured solutions over others, even before considering any explicit regularization.

In BOFT, this implicit bias works together with the explicit orthogonality constraint and the butterfly structure. The result is a finetuning method that is both expressive and regularized in a way that appears favorable for downstream adaptation. That combination is the paper’s main theoretical message: BOFT is not just a cheaper way to represent orthogonal matrices, but a carefully structured parameterization that shapes expressivity, stability, and inductive bias at the same time.

Empirical Validation Across Vision, Language, and Generation

# Goals of the empirical study: accuracy, parameter efficiency, generalization, and convergence

The empirical program in this paper is designed to answer a simple but important question: if orthogonal finetuning is made more parameter-efficient, does it still retain the practical benefits that made OFT attractive in the first place? The paper treats this as more than a raw accuracy question. It evaluates BOFT along several dimensions at once: how well it performs on downstream tasks, how many trainable parameters it requires, how well it generalizes, and how stable its optimization is during training.

This broader lens matters because BOFT is not just another way to fit a model more tightly to a task. It is intended to preserve the central idea of orthogonal finetuning while reducing parameter cost through butterfly factorization. So the experiments are meant to verify whether the new parameterization actually delivers the promised trade-off: smaller trainable modules, but still the kind of controlled, stable adaptation that orthogonal updates are supposed to provide. In the paper’s framing, success means that BOFT should be able to compete with or exceed existing parameter-efficient finetuning methods while using a smaller effective parameter budget and without sacrificing the favorable optimization behavior associated with orthogonal updates.

The paper also uses experiments to test whether the butterfly parameterization gives a better balance between expressivity and regularity than the block-diagonal structure used in the original OFT. That is an important empirical question, because the theoretical motivation says BOFT should be able to generate dense orthogonal matrices with far fewer parameters than a full orthogonal matrix, but still avoid some of the overly rigid constraints of block-diagonal sparsity. The experiments are therefore meant to validate not just final task performance, but also the underlying claim that the new structure is a better practical hypothesis class for finetuning.

# Applying orthogonal finetuning beyond its original diffusion-model setting

A major empirical contribution of the paper is that it moves orthogonal finetuning beyond the setting in which it was originally introduced. The original OFT work focused on controllable text-to-image diffusion models, and the paper explicitly positions BOFT as a way to test whether orthogonal finetuning is useful as a general adaptation strategy rather than a technique specialized to one generative domain.

This expansion in scope is central to the paper’s significance. If a finetuning method works only in one niche, it is harder to view it as a general tool for foundation models. BOFT is evaluated with that broader goal in mind. The paper applies it to large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models. In other words, the experiments cover both discriminative and generative settings, and they span both vision and language. That breadth is important because it suggests that the benefits of orthogonal updates are not tied to one architecture family or one modality.

The paper’s conclusion from this cross-domain study is that orthogonal finetuning can function as a generic finetuning method. BOFT is presented not as a model-specific trick, but as a reusable adaptation mechanism that can be inserted into large pretrained systems across modalities. The experiments are what support this broader claim: the method is shown to work outside the original diffusion-model context and to remain competitive in very different downstream settings.

# Adapting large vision transformers to downstream vision tasks

One part of the empirical study applies BOFT to large vision transformers on downstream vision tasks. The paper does not frame this as a narrow one-off test; rather, it treats vision transformers as a representative family of large pretrained models whose adaptation can reveal whether the method is broadly useful.

The significance of these experiments is that vision transformers are a demanding testbed for parameter-efficient finetuning. A method that simply adds parameters or overfits to a small adaptation set would not be persuasive. BOFT is evaluated here because its orthogonal updates are supposed to preserve pretrained structure while still allowing task-specific adaptation. The paper argues that the structured butterfly factorization should help it find a smaller but useful hypothesis class inside the orthogonal group, which is exactly the kind of bias that may be beneficial in downstream vision tasks.

The paper’s empirical message is that BOFT performs strongly in this setting and contributes to the claim that orthogonal finetuning is not restricted to generation tasks. Its performance on vision transformers supports the idea that the method can be plugged into a modern foundation model and used as a general-purpose adaptation mechanism. The fact that the same finetuning idea carries over from diffusion models to vision transformers is one of the clearest pieces of evidence for BOFT’s cross-domain relevance.

# Adapting large language models to downstream NLP tasks

The paper also evaluates BOFT on large language models for downstream natural language processing tasks. This is an especially important test because language models are one of the main settings in which parameter-efficient adaptation has become practically essential. The paper’s introduction emphasizes the scale of contemporary foundation models and the difficulty of retraining them from scratch, so demonstrating effectiveness on language tasks helps establish BOFT as a serious contender in a major application area.

The language-model experiments are important for another reason as well. They test whether the advantages of orthogonal finetuning are specific to continuous-valued image-generation pipelines or whether they transfer to the discrete and highly structured settings common in NLP. The paper’s general argument is that the answer is yes: the same orthogonal factorization idea remains useful even when the downstream task is language-based rather than visual.

By including large language models, the paper broadens the scope of orthogonal finetuning from a specialized generative setup to a much wider adaptation landscape. This matters because a general finetuning method should be able to tolerate different data distributions, different model architectures, and different task objectives. The paper’s results are presented as evidence that BOFT satisfies that expectation. In the authors’ summary, the method outperforms current state-of-the-art approaches by a considerable margin across adaptation applications ranging from computer vision to natural language processing.

# Adapting text-to-image diffusion models to controllable generation tasks

The original motivation for orthogonal finetuning came from text-to-image diffusion models, and the paper continues to use this family as an important part of its empirical study. This is the setting where OFT had already shown promising generalization and convergence, so it provides a natural baseline for assessing whether BOFT preserves the strengths of orthogonal updates while improving parameter efficiency.

In these experiments, the key question is whether the butterfly factorization can reduce the number of trainable parameters without weakening the finetuning behavior that made OFT attractive. Because BOFT is built as a generalization of OFT, the diffusion-model setting is a direct test of whether the new parameterization remains faithful to the original orthogonal finetuning principle. The paper treats this as an especially important validation, since the method must be able to match the behavior of OFT where OFT was already known to work.

The paper’s conclusion is that BOFT does not merely preserve the diffusion-model use case; it extends it in a more efficient form. The experiments support the claim that orthogonal finetuning can be made substantially more parameter-efficient while retaining the benefits of the underlying orthogonal update. This is part of why the paper presents BOFT as a generic finetuning framework rather than as a diffusion-specific refinement.

# Baselines used for comparison, including LoRA, OFT, and other PEFT methods

The paper situates BOFT within the broader landscape of parameter-efficient finetuning methods. Among the most relevant baselines are LoRA and the original OFT. This comparison is natural because LoRA and OFT represent two different structured ways to adapt a pretrained weight matrix: LoRA uses an additive low-rank update, while OFT uses a multiplicative orthogonal update. BOFT is introduced precisely as a way to make orthogonal finetuning more efficient, so OFT is the most direct point of comparison.

The paper also discusses the wider family of PEFT methods, including adapters and prompt tuning, as part of the broader context for adaptation. Even when those methods are not the central focus of the orthogonal argument, they form part of the paper’s backdrop because the real question is whether BOFT can compete with the main practical alternatives for adapting large foundation models. The experiments are framed around this broader challenge: parameter-efficient methods should be judged not just by elegance, but by whether they are competitive in real downstream finetuning scenarios.

Within this comparison framework, LoRA is especially useful as a baseline because it has become a widely used and simple parameter-efficient method. OFT is especially useful because BOFT is derived from it. The paper’s empirical narrative is that BOFT improves upon the orthogonal finetuning line by reducing parameter cost, while still fitting comfortably within the same general family of finetuning strategies. That makes the comparisons conceptually clean: BOFT is not inventing an unrelated adaptation paradigm, but refining an existing one.

# Comparing methods under similar parameter budgets

A central theme of the experiments is fair comparison under comparable parameter budgets. This is crucial because BOFT’s main claim is parameter efficiency. If it achieved good results simply by using more trainable parameters than the baselines, the result would not be very informative. The paper therefore emphasizes the need to compare methods in a way that reflects their actual adaptation cost.

This is also why the paper spends so much effort on parameterization. BOFT is designed so that the orthogonal matrix can be represented with a product of sparse orthogonal factors. For a linear layer of size $d \times n$, BOFT($m,b$) uses $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters. When the butterfly matrix is used, that becomes $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$, compared with $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a full dense orthogonal matrix in the original OFT. The point of the experiments is to show that this lower budget is not merely theoretical: it still gives strong downstream performance.

The paper also highlights that BOFT can interpolate between different levels of structure by changing $m$ and $b$. That makes parameter-budget comparisons especially meaningful, because BOFT can be instantiated in ways that range from highly constrained to more expressive. The empirical study uses this flexibility to assess how much performance can be obtained at a given cost, and whether smaller block sizes with deeper butterfly factorization can outperform more conventional block-diagonal choices at similar or even lower parameter counts.

# Observed gains in performance and parameter efficiency for BOFT

The paper’s main empirical conclusion is that BOFT outperforms current state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin while also being more parameter-efficient. This is the core result that ties the entire chapter together. The model is not simply making a theoretical improvement in compression or structure; it is actually delivering stronger finetuning behavior across tasks.

One especially important finding is that BOFT generally offers better parameter efficiency than OFT. The paper includes a simulation in which a random dense orthogonal matrix is approximated using different BOFT configurations, and the results show that BOFT with smaller block size and larger depth is typically more parameter-efficient. The paper uses examples like BOFT($9,2$) versus BOFT($1,16$) and BOFT($6,4$) versus BOFT($2,16$) to illustrate that deeper butterfly factorization can reach similar approximation quality with fewer parameters. This supports the idea that the butterfly structure is a stronger parameterization than the original block-diagonal scheme.

The empirical message is therefore twofold. First, BOFT can use fewer effective parameters than OFT for the same or better expressivity. Second, this efficiency translates into strong downstream performance in real finetuning tasks. The paper treats this as validation of the whole factorization approach: the butterfly structure is not only mathematically elegant, but practically useful.

# Evidence of stronger generalization and more stable training with orthogonal updates

The paper repeatedly connects orthogonal updates with generalization and training stability. This is not presented as an abstract claim alone; it is linked to empirical behavior. In the discussion of OFT, the paper notes that orthogonal finetuning has demonstrated stronger generalization and consistently more stable training than LoRA in the original text-to-image setting. BOFT is designed to preserve that same orthogonal update mechanism while reducing parameter cost, so its empirical success is interpreted as evidence that these favorable properties survive the move to the butterfly parameterization.

The paper also gives a spectral explanation for why orthogonal finetuning tends to be stable. Since multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, the finetuned model keeps an important part of the pretrained geometry intact. That preservation is associated in the paper with training stability and generalization. The empirical results across vision, language, and generation are then presented as consistent with this intuition: BOFT does not disrupt the pretrained model in the way that a less structured update might.

Generalization is also supported by the structural bias of the butterfly parameterization. The paper argues that BOFT induces a meaningful inductive bias because the butterfly structure resembles fast linear transforms such as the discrete Fourier transform, the discrete cosine transform, and the Hadamard transform. Empirically, the method’s good performance across modalities is taken as evidence that this bias is helpful rather than harmful. In that sense, the experiments support the claim that BOFT is not just compact, but also well matched to the needs of downstream finetuning.

# Why the experiments support BOFT as a generic finetuning method across modalities

Taken together, the experiments make a broader conceptual point: BOFT behaves like a general finetuning mechanism rather than a specialized trick for one kind of model. That claim is supported by the breadth of the evaluation itself. The same orthogonal butterfly idea is applied to vision transformers, language models, and diffusion models, and it performs well in each setting. This cross-modal consistency is a strong sign that the method is capturing something fundamental about parameter-efficient adaptation.

The paper’s framework helps explain why this might be true. By using orthogonal transformations, BOFT preserves important structure in the pretrained weights. By using butterfly factorization, it avoids the rigidity and limited expressivity of a purely block-diagonal design. The experiments show that this combination is effective enough to compete with current methods while remaining lightweight. In other words, BOFT appears to strike a useful balance between keeping what the pretrained model already knows and allowing enough flexibility for the downstream task.

The paper is careful to note that BOFT is not perfect. It introduces a slightly larger training-time overhead than OFT because the final orthogonal matrix is built as a product of multiple orthogonal factors. But the key empirical point is that this extra training cost does not come with inference latency, since the learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied directly into the pretrained model after finetuning. That practical property, together with the strong results across vision, language, and generation, is what allows the paper to argue that BOFT is a generic and promising parameter-efficient finetuning method.

Limitations, Related Directions, and Future Work

# Summary of the BOFT Framework and Its Main Advantages

The central idea of BOFT is to make orthogonal finetuning more parameter-efficient without giving up the geometric benefits of orthogonality. In the paper, a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ is adapted by learning an orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ and replacing the forward pass $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ with $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. The orthogonal update is meant to preserve pairwise angles between neurons, so the pretrained structure is not destroyed while the model is adapted to a downstream task. BOFT keeps this basic orthogonal-finetuning philosophy, but makes it much more compact by parameterizing $\bm{R}$ as a product of sparse orthogonal butterfly components.

This matters because a dense orthogonal matrix is expensive to learn directly. BOFT addresses that cost by replacing the single dense matrix with a factorized construction. In the paper’s notation, BOFT($m$,$b$) uses $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters for a $d\times n$ linear layer, and in the butterfly case with $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, the parameter count drops to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$. That is a major improvement over a full dense orthogonal matrix, which uses $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. BOFT also generalizes the original OFT: when $m=1$, it reduces to the block-diagonal version of OFT, and when the block size is large enough, it recovers the unconstrained dense orthogonal case. In this sense, BOFT is not just a smaller version of OFT; it is a more general orthogonal finetuning framework.

The paper argues that BOFT’s main advantage is not only parameter efficiency, but also a better balance between expressivity and regularity. The butterfly structure gives a structured subset of the orthogonal group that is dense enough to be useful, yet constrained enough to act as an inductive bias. The authors emphasize that this kind of structure can be beneficial for generalization and can prevent overfitting. They also report that BOFT extends orthogonal finetuning beyond the original text-to-image setting and performs strongly on vision, language, and diffusion-model adaptation tasks.

# Training-Time Overhead from Multiplying Multiple Sparse Orthogonal Factors

The most direct limitation of BOFT is that parameter efficiency is achieved by paying some computation cost during training. Because the orthogonal transform is represented as a product of multiple sparse factors, the effective matrix must be formed by repeated multiplication during each training iteration. This is exactly the trade-off the paper highlights: the number of trainable parameters goes down, but the model has to spend more work composing the factorized update.

The paper is careful to frame this as a runtime issue rather than a representational issue. The factorization is useful because it replaces one large dense orthogonal matrix with many smaller sparse orthogonal components, each of which is easier to store and optimize. However, the composition itself is not free. Since the final transform is a product of factors, the training loop has to account for that extra structure. The paper therefore notes that BOFT’s training runtime overhead is slightly larger than OFT’s.

This is an important practical point because it shows that parameter efficiency does not automatically mean computation efficiency during optimization. BOFT reduces the number of effective parameters, but the price is that learning happens through a deeper multiplicative parameterization. For a student reading the paper, the key lesson is that structured finetuning often involves a trade-off between space and time: a method can be cheaper in parameters while still being somewhat more expensive to train.

# Why Inference Remains Efficient After Merging the Learned Transform

Although BOFT adds some overhead during training, the paper stresses that inference remains efficient. The reason is simple: after finetuning, the learned orthogonal matrix can be directly multiplied into the pretrained model. Once this merge happens, the factorized update no longer needs to be carried as a separate computation graph. The final adapted model behaves like an ordinary network with updated weights.

This is one of the most attractive properties of BOFT as a finetuning method. The model does not need to keep applying a special adapter or extra module at test time. The learned transformation is absorbed into the original pretrained weights, so there is no additional inference latency. In practical terms, this means BOFT is designed for adaptation without making deployment slower.

That no-latency property is closely tied to the philosophy of model finetuning in general. The paper contrasts finetuning with adapter tuning and prompt tuning, but the important point here is that BOFT preserves the deployment simplicity of finetuning while introducing a much more structured update. So the runtime burden is concentrated in training, not in inference.

# Open Question: Is Butterfly the Most Efficient Information-Transmission Topology?

A major conceptual contribution of the paper is the information transmission view of orthogonal finetuning. In that view, a dense orthogonal matrix is treated as a communication problem on a grid-structured graph: each factor in the matrix product corresponds to connections between adjacent levels of nodes, and the final matrix is dense only if every source node can transmit information to every receiver node. This makes sparsity pattern design feel like network design.

The butterfly structure is attractive because it solves this transmission problem very efficiently. The paper explains that butterfly graphs, inspired by the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform, can connect $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges. That is why they are such a good fit for BOFT. But the paper is also explicit that butterfly may not be optimal. It asks, in effect, whether there is an even better topology for transmitting information while respecting orthogonality.

This is one of the main open questions left by the paper. The information transmission framework is broad enough to compare many sparse factorizations, not just butterfly ones. The challenge is to find a topology that still guarantees dense connectivity and orthogonality, while using as few nontrivial edges as possible. The paper’s own language makes this point clear: butterfly is efficient, but it is not proven to be the most efficient possible arrangement.

# Searching for Better Sparse Orthogonal Factorizations Using Graph and Networking Ideas

Because the paper recasts matrix factorization as a graph problem, it naturally points toward graph design as a future research direction. The authors explicitly suggest drawing inspiration from computer networking, where the efficiency of a topology for transmitting information is a central question. In that spirit, future work could look for other sparse orthogonal factorizations that satisfy the same dense-connectivity requirement but use a different communication pattern than butterfly graphs.

The appeal of this direction is that it does not begin from a fixed algebraic form and then try to compress it. Instead, it begins from the communication objective itself. The graph perspective says that each factor matrix contributes edges only between adjacent levels, and that orthogonality adds additional constraints on which edges can coexist. That means topology search is not just a matter of making the matrix sparse; it is a constrained design problem in which the sparsity pattern must still allow full information flow.

This opens the door to a broader search over structured matrices. The paper already shows that the butterfly structure can generate dense orthogonal matrices with relatively few parameters, but it also suggests that different network topologies might do even better. In that sense, the information transmission framework is not just a proof device; it is a design language for future finetuning methods. The main open problem is to identify the most useful communication graphs for dense orthogonal factorization under the orthogonality constraint.

# Relationship to Butterfly-Based Sparse Training Methods

The paper places BOFT in the context of prior butterfly-based methods, but it also draws a sharp distinction between BOFT and those earlier approaches. Many butterfly-based works focus on reparameterizing weights directly with butterfly structures and then training neural networks from scratch. Their goal is often to make linear transforms or entire networks more efficient to train or to approximate fast transforms. BOFT is not doing that.

Instead, BOFT uses butterfly factorization to parameterize the orthogonal update in orthogonal finetuning. The pretrained weight matrix is still there, and the butterfly structure is used to learn a transform that acts on it. This is a very different use of the same underlying matrix family. In other words, BOFT is not primarily a sparse-training method; it is a finetuning method that borrows the butterfly idea to make orthogonal adaptation cheaper.

The paper also mentions a related finetuning approach in which pretrained weights are projected onto a butterfly variant and the projected components are then optimized. BOFT differs from that strategy as well, because BOFT applies a layer-shared orthogonal transformation to the frozen pretrained weights. The difference may sound subtle, but it changes the whole adaptation philosophy. BOFT is not trying to relearn the model from a butterfly-constrained parameterization; it is trying to preserve pretrained knowledge while updating it through a structured orthogonal map.

# Contrast Between BOFT’s Layer-Shared Orthogonal Transformation and Direct Sparse Weight Training

BOFT’s defining feature is that it uses a layer-shared orthogonal transformation. The same orthogonal matrix is applied to the neurons of a layer, so the update acts as a coherent transformation of the pretrained representation. Mathematically, the model output depends on the product $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, not on a new weight matrix learned from scratch. This is why the paper can interpret orthogonal finetuning as learning a bilinear similarity of the form $\bm{w}_i^0 \bm{R} \bm{x}$.

Direct sparse weight training is conceptually different. There, the sparse matrix itself is the object being learned. By contrast, BOFT treats the sparse structure as a way to parameterize a transformation that acts on the pretrained weights. This distinction matters because the orthogonal constraint preserves pairwise angles and the spectral norm of the pretrained weights, while direct sparse training does not aim to preserve those geometric properties in the same way.

So even when both approaches use butterfly parameterizations, they are optimizing different things. Direct sparse training is about learning a structured weight matrix. BOFT is about learning a structured orthogonal update on top of an existing model. The paper’s core claim is that this latter approach is a better fit for downstream adaptation, because it keeps the pretrained knowledge intact while still allowing a controlled change in the model’s behavior.

# When BOFT Is Likely to Be a Strong PEFT Choice in Practice

The paper suggests that BOFT is especially appealing when one wants parameter-efficient finetuning without adding inference latency. That combination is valuable in large foundation models, where training from scratch is too costly and downstream adaptation must be efficient. Because BOFT merges into the pretrained model after training, it keeps deployment simple while still reducing the number of effective trainable parameters.

BOFT also looks attractive when the goal is to preserve the pretrained model’s geometry. The paper emphasizes that orthogonal finetuning preserves pairwise angles and spectral norm, and argues that this can help training stability and generalization. BOFT keeps these benefits while reducing the parameter cost of the orthogonal update. This makes it a strong choice when regularization matters and when the downstream task does not require the full freedom of unconstrained finetuning.

The paper’s experiments support this view across several settings, including large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models. It also highlights that more expressiveness is not always better. Full finetuning is maximally flexible, but that does not guarantee the best downstream performance. BOFT is useful precisely because it narrows the hypothesis class in a structured way, giving a better balance between flexibility and regularity. In practice, that means BOFT is likely to be strongest when a task benefits from a principled structural bias rather than unrestricted weight changes.

# Broader Implications for Structured Finetuning of Future Foundation Models

The broader message of the paper is that the future of foundation-model adaptation may depend on structured finetuning, not just on reducing the number of trainable parameters. BOFT shows that sparse factorization can be used in a very different way from low-rank methods like LoRA: not as an additive low-rank correction, but as a structured orthogonal transformation that preserves important geometric features of the pretrained model. This widens the design space for parameter-efficient adaptation.

The paper also makes a stronger conceptual point: structured finetuning is not only about compression, but also about inductive bias. The butterfly structure is attractive because it is expressive enough to recover many classic linear transforms, while still constraining the hypothesis class. The result is a finetuning method that can be more stable and more generalizable than an unstructured update. That idea is likely to matter more, not less, as foundation models continue to grow.

Finally, the paper leaves the reader with a clear research agenda. Improve the training runtime overhead, search for better information-transmission topologies, and explore other structured factorization schemes for dense orthogonal updates. Those directions all fit naturally into the same framework. BOFT is therefore not just a specific method; it is also an example of how ideas from structured matrices, graph design, and network topology can shape the next generation of finetuning methods for very large models.
