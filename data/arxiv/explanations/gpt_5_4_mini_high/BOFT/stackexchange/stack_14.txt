\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What can and cannot be concluded from the paper's information-transmission framework?
Question:
The paper says the framework is useful because, for designing the factorization, 'we only care about the non-zero trainable elements of \(R\) and their specific values do not matter.' This is the part I find most questionable. The actual numerical values of the orthogonal factors seem crucial for optimization and for how well the final matrix approximates a useful transformation. Is the graph view meant only to design sparsity patterns that make dense support possible, rather than to analyze approximation quality or training dynamics? I would like to understand the exact scope of what this framework explains and what it does not.
Answer:
You are reading the framework correctly: in this paper, the “information transmission” view is mainly a combinatorial way to reason about the *support pattern* of the factorization, not a full theory of the learned numerical values.

What the authors are doing is turning the question “can I build a dense orthogonal matrix with few trainable parameters?” into a reachability problem on a layered graph. In that abstraction, a zero entry means “no edge,” a nonzero entry means “an edge,” and the key question is whether information can flow from every node on the left to every node on the right through the sequence of sparse factors. That is why they say “we only care about the non-zero trainable elements of $\bm{R}$ and their specific values do not matter”: they mean that, for the purpose of *designing the sparsity pattern*, the exact values are irrelevant. What matters is whether the pattern can support dense connectivity while respecting orthogonality and using as few free edges as possible.

So yes, the graph view is primarily a tool for architecture design. It tells you things like “the block-diagonal pattern cannot mix all dimensions,” or “a butterfly pattern can connect all inputs to all outputs with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges.” It is therefore useful for comparing candidate factorizations, counting effective parameters, and motivating why butterfly structure is better than the original block-diagonal OFT for expressivity-per-parameter.

But your concern is also correct: the graph view does *not* capture everything that matters in optimization. The paper does not claim that the numerical values of the orthogonal factors are unimportant for training or final performance. On the contrary, those values are learned by gradient descent through the Cayley parameterization, and they determine the actual transformation applied to the pretrained weights. The graph only says what is *possible* at the level of connectivity and support; it does not tell you which learned orthogonal matrix will be best, how easy it will be to optimize, or how the loss landscape will behave.

The paper is explicit about this limitation. In the “Expressivity of BOFT” discussion, the authors say that butterfly matrices can recover many classic transforms and are more expressive than OFT with the same block size, but they also say that “how well our orthogonal butterfly matrix can approximate a general orthogonal matrix remains unknown.” They further note that “better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance in finetuning.” So the framework supports claims about representational capacity and parameter efficiency, not a guarantee of optimization quality or task performance.

A good way to think about it is that the information-transmission view explains the *architecture-level feasibility* of a sparse factorization, much like a network topology tells you whether communication between endpoints is possible. It does not determine the actual message content. In this paper, that “message content” is the learned matrix values, and those still matter a great deal. The framework simply gives a principled way to choose a sparse orthogonal parameterization that is dense enough to be expressive, but small enough to be parameter-efficient.

So the exact scope is: it explains support patterns, dense connectivity, and parameter counting under orthogonality constraints; it motivates butterfly factorization; and it helps compare hypothesis classes. It does *not* by itself explain approximation error for a specific target matrix, training dynamics, or why one particular learned set of orthogonal values will generalize better than another.
