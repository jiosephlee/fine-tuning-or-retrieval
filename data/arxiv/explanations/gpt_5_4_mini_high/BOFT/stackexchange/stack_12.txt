\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why might a more expressive finetuning family perform worse than a more constrained one?
Question:
The paper says that full finetuning, despite being more expressive, often performs worse than a structured method like BOFT, and that the key trade-off is expressivity versus regularity. I understand the general bias-variance intuition, but I am struggling to connect it to this particular parameterization. What inductive bias does the butterfly structure introduce beyond simply using fewer parameters? Is the claim mainly about preserving pretrained geometry and singular values, or is there a more specific reason why FFT-like sparse mixing patterns might generalize better on downstream tasks?
Answer:
The paper’s point is not that “more expressivity is bad” in the abstract, but that for finetuning a pretrained model, unrestricted expressivity can be too much freedom. Full finetuning can change the weight space in arbitrary ways, which makes it easy to fit downstream idiosyncrasies while also easier to destroy useful structure learned during pretraining. BOFT deliberately restricts the update to a structured orthogonal transformation, $R W^0$, so the finetuned model stays close to the pretrained geometry: orthogonal left-multiplication preserves pairwise angles and the spectral norm of $W^0$, which the paper explicitly links to training stability and generalization.

The butterfly structure adds a more specific inductive bias than just “fewer parameters.” It does not merely shrink the parameter count; it constrains *how* information can mix across dimensions. Instead of allowing an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix, BOFT builds $R$ as a product of sparse orthogonal factors in a butterfly pattern. That means the model performs hierarchical, stage-wise mixing: each factor only connects a small set of coordinates, but after several stages every coordinate can influence every other one. In the paper’s information-transmission view, this is exactly the desirable middle ground between the overly rigid block-diagonal OFT and the overly unconstrained full orthogonal group.

This is why the authors emphasize that the butterfly parameterization can recover classic fast linear transforms such as the Fourier, cosine, and Hadamard transforms, whereas the block-diagonal structure cannot. So the inductive bias is not just “sparsity” in general, but a particular kind of structured sparsity that favors multiscale, FFT-like global mixing with strong parameter sharing. That shared structure reduces the hypothesis class to a “smoother” subset of orthogonal transformations, which can generalize better on downstream tasks with limited data.

So the short answer is: BOFT works better not because it is more expressive than full finetuning, but because it is *more regular*. The butterfly factorization gives a very specific prior about how useful transformations look: globally mixing, but composed from local, well-conditioned steps that preserve pretrained geometry. That is the paper’s intended trade-off between expressivity and regularity.
