\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What do the two BOFT hyperparameters \(m\) and \(b\) actually control?
Question:
The notation in the paper confused me. It defines \(R(m,b)\) using block butterfly components \(\tilde{B}_{(d,i)}^b\), and then says BOFT with \(R(m,b/2)\) is denoted as BOFT\((m,b)\). It also states that BOFT\((1,b)\) reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size \(b\), and BOFT\((\log (2d/b), b)\) yields a dense orthogonal matrix. I am not sure whether \(b\) is the butterfly block size, half-block size, or the size of the orthogonal submatrix being parameterized. Can someone translate this notation into a simple description of what increasing \(m\) or \(b\) does to density, expressivity, and parameter count?
Answer:
The cleanest way to read BOFT is as a depth-versus-block-size tradeoff. The hyperparameter $m$ is the number of butterfly factors multiplied together, so it controls how many rounds of mixing happen across the coordinates. The hyperparameter $b$ controls the size of the local orthogonal blocks used inside each factor. In other words, $m$ is “how many stages,” while $b$ is “how big each stage’s local block is.”

That is why the special cases in the paper look the way they do. When $m=1$, BOFT collapses to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, so it is still sparse unless $b=d$. When $b=d$, BOFT$(1,d)$ becomes the original full OFT with an unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix. And when $m=\log(2d/b)$, the butterfly composition is deep enough to produce a dense orthogonal matrix. So increasing $m$ generally makes the matrix denser and more expressive by allowing information to propagate through more butterfly stages; increasing $b$ also increases expressivity because each factor itself contains larger orthogonal blocks, but it makes each factor less sparse.

The parameter count makes this tradeoff very explicit. The paper states that BOFT$(m,b)$ uses $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters for a $d\times n$ linear layer. So both $m$ and $b$ increase parameters linearly. If you hold one fixed and increase the other, you get more capacity but also more cost. If you keep the overall budget similar, you can trade a larger $b$ for a smaller $m$, or vice versa. Empirically, the paper notes that smaller $b$ and larger $m$ are often more parameter-efficient, because the extra butterfly stages give global mixing without making each stage too expensive.

About the notation confusion: the authors first define a block-butterfly component $\tilde B^b(d,k)$, then later say they denote BOFT with $R(m,b/2)$ as BOFT$(m,b)$. That is just a notational conversion, not a different hyperparameter. The user-facing $b$ in BOFT$(m,b)$ is best understood as the effective block size of the orthogonal submatrices, the same quantity you would compare to the block size in block-diagonal OFT. The internal $b/2$ is only there because of how the butterfly factor is recursively written.

So in one sentence: $m$ controls the number of mixing layers, $b$ controls the size of each local orthogonal block, and the combination $m(b-1)$ is what drives the parameter count, density, and expressivity tradeoff.
