\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why is the token-level importance ratio considered invalid in GRPO if PPO also uses per-step ratios?
Question:
I am reading the GSPO paper, and one of its main claims is that GRPO is unstable because it uses token-level importance ratios of the form $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}$. The paper says this is a misapplication of importance sampling and can lead to collapse.

What confuses me is that PPO in reinforcement learning is also usually described with per-timestep action ratios. So is the paper saying that PPO-style per-step ratios are fine in ordinary RL but not in autoregressive language modeling, or is it claiming something more general is wrong here?

I would appreciate a precise explanation of what breaks in GRPO specifically. Is the issue that the reward is only sequence-level, that each token distribution contributes only one sampled token, or that the same advantage is copied to all tokens?
Answer:
The paper is not claiming that “per-step ratios are always wrong.” It is claiming something more specific: in GRPO, the token-level ratio

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
$$

is the wrong object for the objective GRPO is trying to optimize. The reason is that GRPO has only a sequence-level reward $r(x,y_i)$, and then it copies the same group-normalized advantage $\widehat A_i$ onto every token in the response. So the update is effectively weighting each token separately even though the supervision signal is for the whole sequence. In the paper’s view, that is a mismatch between the unit of reward and the unit of optimization.

This is why PPO is not a counterexample. In ordinary PPO, the per-timestep ratio appears in the standard policy-gradient form for trajectories, where each term is associated with a state-action pair and usually its own advantage estimate. Roughly speaking, PPO is optimizing something like

$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_t w_t \widehat A_t\right],
$$

which is a local, stepwise approximation to the policy gradient. That is different from GRPO’s situation, where all tokens in a response share the same $\widehat A_i$. In that setting, multiplying each token by its own $w_{i,t}$ is not a clean importance-sampling correction for the sampled response as a whole.

The paper’s key point is that if the reward is sequence-level, then the natural importance ratio is the ratio of whole-sequence likelihoods,

$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}=\prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}(\theta),
$$

or, in their normalized form,

$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}.
$$

That is exactly why GSPO switches to sequence-level clipping and sequence-level weighting: it aligns the importance ratio with the level at which the reward is defined.

So the instability the paper attributes to GRPO is not “token ratios are mathematically illegal in autoregressive models.” It is that, in GRPO specifically, token ratios are being used as if they were a valid off-policy correction for a sequence-level objective, even though each next-token distribution contributes only one sampled token and the same advantage is broadcast to all positions. The paper argues that this creates high-variance gradients, which get worse as the response gets longer and clipping starts distorting different tokens differently. That is the mechanism they say can eventually lead to collapse.

One extra clue from the paper is that it introduces GSPO-token as a variant for finer-grained token-wise advantages, but even there the sequence-level likelihood still underlies the ratio. That reinforces the main message: the problem is not “token-wise learning signals are impossible,” but “the correction factor should match the level at which the reward and optimization are defined.”

### Does having only one sampled token per position make importance sampling invalid, or just high-variance?
Question:
The GSPO paper argues that GRPO's token-level importance weights do not perform proper distribution correction because at each position $t$ there is only one sampled token $y_{i,t}$ from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{i,<t})$.

My prior understanding was that importance sampling can still be unbiased with a single sample, although the variance may be terrible. So I am not sure whether the paper is making a stronger statement than that.

Is the GRPO estimator actually biased because of how the token-level ratios are used, or is the main problem only variance and clipping interaction? If there is a real theoretical invalidity, could someone point to the exact mathematical reason?
Answer:
A single sample per position does not, by itself, make importance sampling invalid. In ordinary importance sampling, one sample is enough for an unbiased estimator; the usual problem is variance, not correctness.

What matters is whether the weight is the density ratio of the *same random variable* whose expectation you want. In this paper, the thing being optimized is sequence-level: a whole response $y=(y_1,\dots,y_T)$ with a sequence reward $r(x,y)$. For that object, the correct importance weight is the full sequence ratio
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}
=
\prod_{t=1}^T
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}.
$$
That is the Radon-Nikodym derivative that turns an expectation under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ into one under $\pi_\theta$.

GRPO does something different: it puts the local token ratio
$$
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}
$$
inside a sum over tokens, while the advantage $\widehat A_i$ is the same sequence-level scalar for all tokens in the response. So each token term is only “corrected” at that single conditional distribution, but the prefix distribution that generated the later tokens is still the old one. In other words, GRPO is not estimating the target sequence-level expectation with the correct weight; it is a heuristic surrogate that mixes together partially corrected token terms. That is the theoretical mismatch the paper is pointing at.

So the right diagnosis is: it is not merely “high variance because there is only one token sample per position.” The deeper issue is that the token-level ratios are the wrong importance weights for a sequence-level objective, so the estimator is biased relative to the intended sequence-policy gradient. Clipping then makes things worse by truncating an already-misaligned weight signal, which adds further bias and can amplify instability.

If you want the intuition in one sentence: in an autoregressive model, changing token $1$ changes the distribution of the prefixes seen by tokens $2,3,\dots$, so correcting each token independently does not correct the distribution of the whole trajectory. That is why GSPO moves the ratio to the sequence level.

### Why is GSPO's normalized sequence ratio still called an importance ratio?
Question:
In standard importance sampling, I would expect the sequence-level correction factor to be $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}$. But GSPO defines

$s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)} \right)^{1/|y_i|}$.

This looks like a geometric mean of token ratios rather than the actual sequence likelihood ratio. I understand the practical reason given in the paper: variance reduction and length normalization. But after taking the $1/|y_i|$ power, is it still correct to interpret $s_i(\theta)$ as an importance weight in the usual sense?

Or is GSPO really optimizing a surrogate objective that is inspired by importance sampling but no longer equal to a standard off-policy correction?
Answer:
Strictly speaking, no: after the $1/|y_i|$ power, $s_i(\theta)$ is no longer the usual importance weight for off-policy correction. The exact sequence-level importance ratio would be

$$
w(y_i)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}
=\exp\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
$$

GSPO instead uses

$$
s_i(\theta)=w(y_i)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right),
$$

which is the geometric mean of the token-wise ratios. That normalization changes the quantity, so it is no longer the Radon-Nikodym derivative you would use for standard importance sampling.

What the paper is doing is using “importance ratio” in a looser, algorithmic sense: it is still a ratio between the current policy and the behavior policy, and it still measures how far an old-policy sample has drifted under the new policy. But GSPO is not trying to give an unbiased off-policy estimator of an expectation under $\pi_\theta$; it is defining a clipped surrogate objective whose job is to provide a stable training signal. The paper explicitly says the length normalization is there “to reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range.” So the normalization is a deliberate departure from exact importance sampling, not a cosmetic rewrite.

So the clean interpretation is: GSPO is “importance-ratio-inspired,” not standard importance sampling. The true sequence ratio is the exact correction factor; the normalized ratio is a length-normalized proxy that preserves the idea of relative policy mismatch while making the optimization numerically and statistically better behaved.

### How does length normalization change the optimization target in GSPO for responses of different lengths?
Question:
GSPO defines its ratio as

$s_i(\theta) = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})} \right)$.

The paper says this puts responses of different lengths into a unified numerical range. But I am confused about the theoretical effect. If two responses have the same full likelihood ratio but different lengths, then their normalized ratios $s_i(\theta)$ will be different.

Does this mean GSPO intentionally treats long and short responses differently in its trust region? If so, is that only a numerical stabilization trick, or is there a deeper reason this is the right thing to do when rewards are sequence-level?
Answer:
Yes: in GSPO the length normalization is a deliberate part of the optimization objective, not just a cosmetic rescaling.

The key observation is that

$$
s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right)
$$

is the geometric mean of the token-level likelihood ratios. So GSPO is not clipping the raw sequence ratio $\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i\mid x)$; it is clipping the average log-likelihood shift per token. That is exactly why responses of different lengths can be compared in one numerical range.

This changes the meaning of the trust region in an important way. With the unnormalized joint ratio, even tiny per-token changes accumulate multiplicatively, so longer responses are much more likely to look “far” from the old policy simply because they have more factors in the product. Length normalization removes that effect. If two responses have the same total likelihood ratio but different lengths, then the longer one must have had smaller average token-level changes, and GSPO intentionally regards that as less off-policy. In other words, a fixed clipping window in GSPO is meant to have roughly the same meaning across lengths.

So this is more than a numerical stabilization trick, although stability is one of the paper’s stated reasons. The paper explicitly says that normalization is used “to reduce the variance” and to keep $s_i(\theta)$ in a unified range, because otherwise “the importance ratios of responses with different lengths will require varying clipping ranges.” The deeper motivation is that GSPO is a sequence-level method: the reward is assigned to the whole response, so the optimization is also done at the sequence level, and the relevant notion of policy drift is the average per-token drift rather than the raw product over tokens.

The trade-off is that this is no longer the exact importance-sampling ratio for the full sequence; it is a length-normalized surrogate. GSPO is choosing a better-conditioned surrogate trust region that matches its sequence-level reward structure, rather than trying to preserve exact sequence likelihood correction.

### Why does the paper say the unit of reward should match the unit of optimization?
Question:
A central motivation in the paper is that because the reward $r(x,y)$ is assigned to the whole response, the optimization and clipping should also happen at the sequence level rather than the token level.

I do not fully understand this principle. In policy-gradient methods, it seems common to take a trajectory-level return and use it to update every action in the trajectory. So why is it suddenly a problem here to optimize token log-probabilities using the same response-level advantage?

Is the mismatch problem specifically about using token-level off-policy ratios with a sequence-level reward, or are the authors arguing that any token-level objective is conceptually wrong when the verifier score is sequence-level?
Answer:
The paper is **not** saying that it is wrong to use a sequence-level reward to update token probabilities. In fact, that is exactly what standard policy gradient does: if a whole response $y$ gets one scalar reward $r(x,y)$, then the gradient of the sequence log-likelihood decomposes as
$$
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}),
$$
so the same sequence-level signal naturally gets distributed over all token log-probabilities. That part is completely normal.

What GSPO is objecting to is something more specific: **the off-policy correction and clipping should be done at the same level as the random variable being rewarded**. In the paper’s setting, the sampled object is the whole response $y$, and the verifier gives one score $r(x,y)$ for that whole response. If you are doing importance sampling for that objective, the mathematically natural weight is the **sequence ratio**
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}
=
\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}.
$$
GRPO instead uses token ratios
$$
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}
$$
inside a clipped surrogate, even though the reward is attached to the whole sequence. The paper’s claim is that this is a mismatch: those tokenwise ratios are not the right importance weights for a sequence-level objective, and with clipping they inject noisy, uneven corrections token by token. The authors argue that this noise accumulates with length and can destabilize training.

So the issue is **not** “token-level gradients are conceptually wrong whenever the reward is sequence-level.” That would contradict ordinary REINFORCE-style updates. The issue is **using token-level off-policy ratios and tokenwise clipping to optimize a sequence-level reward**. GSPO keeps token-level backpropagation, but it moves the importance ratio and clipping to the sequence level, which is what the paper means by matching the “unit of reward” and the “unit of optimization.”

You can see this from their own GSPO-token variant: they explicitly allow token-wise advantages when finer-grained supervision is desired, but the sequence-level ratio is still the core importance weight. That is a strong hint that the paper is not rejecting token-level optimization per se; it is rejecting the **token-level importance-sampling surrogate** in the sequence-reward setting.

### If GSPO clips entire responses, why do the experiments report a much larger fraction of clipped tokens?
Question:
The paper emphasizes that GSPO performs sequence-level clipping, while GRPO clips at the token level. Later it says that GSPO has a much larger fraction of clipped tokens than GRPO, even by two orders of magnitude.

I am confused about what exactly is being measured. If a response is clipped in GSPO, are all tokens inside that response counted as clipped for the figure? Or does the implementation still have some hidden token-level mask?

I am trying to understand how to compare token clipping statistics between an algorithm that clips responses and an algorithm that clips individual tokens.
Answer:
Yes: for GSPO, the “fraction of clipped tokens” should be read as a token-weighted statistic over responses whose **sequence-level** importance ratio was clipped.

The key point is that GSPO does not clip individual tokens with separate decisions the way GRPO does. In GSPO the clipping test is made on the response-level scalar
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|},
$$
and if that scalar falls outside the clipping window, then the clipped surrogate is applied to the **whole response**:
$$
\min\!\left(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\right).
$$
Since every token in that response shares the same sequence-level weight, all of its tokens are effectively part of the clipped sample for logging purposes. So the natural “fraction of clipped tokens” for GSPO is something like
$$
\frac{\sum_i |y_i|\,\mathbf{1}\{s_i \text{ clipped}\}}{\sum_i |y_i|},
$$
not a hidden token mask inside the response.

That is why the figure can show a much larger clipped-token fraction for GSPO even though the algorithm is sequence-level: one clipped long response contributes many clipped tokens. By contrast, GRPO clips at the token level, so its statistic is
$$
\frac{\sum_{i,t}\mathbf{1}\{w_{i,t} \text{ clipped}\}}{\sum_{i,t}1},
$$
which can be much smaller.

So the paper is not saying GSPO has secretly reverted to token-level clipping. It is saying that when you measure clipping in terms of tokens, clipping entire responses causes many more tokens to be excluded from gradient estimation, and that this is still empirically more stable and efficient than GRPO’s tokenwise clipping.

### How can GSPO learn useful credit assignment if every token in a response gets the same advantage?
Question:
In the main GSPO objective, all tokens in the same sampled response $y_i$ share the same advantage $\widehat{A}_i$, and the gradient in the paper shows that all token log-probability gradients are weighted equally within that response.

Intuitively, though, only a few tokens may be responsible for whether the verifier gives a high or low score. For example, in a math solution the final answer token may matter much more than many earlier reasoning tokens.

Why does pushing all tokens in a good response up, and all tokens in a bad response down, not create a very noisy learning signal? Is GSPO basically relying on the same logic as REINFORCE with sequence return, or is there some language-model-specific reason this works better than it sounds?
Answer:
Yes — GSPO is basically using the same credit-assignment logic as sequence-level REINFORCE, and the paper is quite explicit that this is intentional.

The key point is that GSPO is not trying to say that every token was equally responsible for the verifier’s score. Rather, it is optimizing a reward that is only defined at the sequence level, $r(x,y_i)$, so the gradient estimator also has to act on the whole sampled response. In an autoregressive language model, the probability of a response factorizes as $\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)=\prod_t \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$, so increasing the probability of the whole successful trajectory necessarily means increasing the probability of the individual token choices that made up that trajectory. That is exactly why the GSPO gradient contains the sum of token log-probability gradients multiplied by the same sequence advantage $\widehat{A}_i$.

So the “same advantage for every token” should be read as a Monte Carlo policy-gradient estimator for a sequence-level objective, not as a statement of fine-grained causal attribution. This is the same broad logic as REINFORCE with a sequence return: if a sampled sequence turns out good, then all actions in that sampled trajectory get nudged up; if it turns out bad, they get nudged down. It is coarse credit assignment, but it is still a valid one for the objective being optimized.

Why is this not just hopelessly noisy? The paper’s answer is that the noise problem was much worse in GRPO’s token-level importance ratios, because those ratios try to correct off-policy mismatch separately at each token, even though each token is only a single next-token sample. GSPO avoids that by moving the importance ratio and clipping to the sequence level, which is the same level at which reward is assigned. In the paper’s view, that alignment is what makes the learning signal more stable, not more magical. The updates are still coarse, but they are less distorted by spurious token-wise ratio fluctuations.

There is also an important practical intuition here for language models specifically. A correct mathematical solution or program is not usually credited to one token in isolation; it is the result of a whole chain of decisions, and changing early reasoning tokens changes everything downstream. So from the model’s perspective, pushing up all tokens in a successful response is not as silly as it first sounds: those tokens jointly define a trajectory that led to high reward. Over many samples, the model learns which kinds of trajectories tend to be rewarded, and the internal parameters redistribute probability mass toward those patterns.

If you do want finer-grained credit assignment, the paper actually anticipates that and introduces GSPO-token as a variant where token-level advantages can be used. But the core GSPO method intentionally stays at the sequence level because the paper argues that this matches the reward structure and is much more stable than token-level correction.

### What exactly is the stop-gradient trick doing in GSPO-token?
Question:
The GSPO-token variant defines

$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})]}$,

where $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ means stop-gradient. The paper says this quantity is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$, but its gradient produces the desired token-wise form.

I am having trouble seeing why this construction is necessary. Why not just plug $s_i(\theta)$ directly into each token term? How does the stop-gradient placement make the gradient become

$s_i(\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$

instead of introducing extra terms from differentiating $s_i(\theta)$ itself?
Answer:
The stop-gradient is there to make the forward value look like a sequence-level importance ratio while making the backward pass look like a token-wise policy-gradient term.

If you wrote the token-level variant naively as
$$
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t},
$$
then autodiff would differentiate $s_i(\theta)$ itself. Since
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
$$
depends on the whole response, its gradient is
$$
\nabla_\theta s_i(\theta)
=
s_i(\theta)\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_u \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,u}\mid x,y_{i,<u}).
$$
So the gradient of the naive loss becomes
$$
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t}\,\nabla_\theta s_i(\theta)
=
\frac{s_i(\theta)}{|y_i|^2}\left(\sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t}\right)\left(\sum_u \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,u}\mid x,y_{i,<u})\right).
$$
That is not token-wise in the sense the paper wants: every token advantage multiplies the gradient of every token log-probability.

The GSPO-token construction
$$
s_{i,t}(\theta)=\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]}
$$
changes only the backward pass. Numerically, the ratio is $1$, so $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is still equal to $s_i(\theta)$ in the forward pass. But in backpropagation, $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ is treated as a constant, and $\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(\cdot)]$ is also treated as a constant denominator. Therefore
$$
\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta)
=
\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
$$
so the gradient of
$$
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t s_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t}
$$
is exactly
$$
\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t}\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
$$
which is the paper’s desired token-wise form. Since $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ has the same numerical value as $s_i(\theta)$, this matches the expression in the paper.

So the point of the stop-gradient is not to change the objective value; it is to prevent the sequence-level ratio from backpropagating through itself and coupling all token gradients together. It effectively says: “use the sequence-level importance weight as a fixed scalar weight, but attach each token’s advantage only to that token’s own log-likelihood gradient.” That is exactly why the paper introduces this construction.

### Why does expert-routing volatility in Mixture-of-Experts models destabilize GRPO more than GSPO?
Question:
The paper argues that in Mixture-of-Experts language models, the activated experts for the same sampled response can change significantly after an update. It then says this makes GRPO's token-level ratios unstable, while GSPO remains stable because it only cares about sequence likelihood.

What I do not understand is that sequence likelihood is still the product of the same token probabilities. If routing changes a lot, why would the token likelihoods be unstable but the overall sequence likelihood remain stable enough to use safely?

Is the idea that the token-level ratios fluctuate wildly from position to position, while the average log-ratio over the whole response is much smoother? Or is there some other reason sequence-level clipping is less sensitive to routing changes?
Answer:
Yes — that is essentially the point, but with one important nuance: the paper is not claiming that the sequence likelihood is somehow immune to expert-routing changes. It is claiming that a sequence-level ratio is much less *sensitive* to those changes than token-level ratios, because the volatility is averaged across the whole response rather than injected separately at every token.

In GRPO, the update uses the token ratio
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
$$
for each position. In an MoE model, small parameter changes can switch which experts are routed to a token, so these conditional probabilities can jump even when the sampled response is the same. That means some $w_{i,t}$ can become very large or very small just because the routing path changed, and GRPO then uses those noisy values directly in the gradient at each token. Since this happens at every position, the instability accumulates over long responses.

GSPO instead uses one ratio for the whole response,
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
$$
So yes, this is exactly an average of per-token log-ratios, i.e. a geometric mean of the token ratios. That averaging is what makes it more stable. A few routing-induced spikes at individual tokens are diluted by the rest of the sequence, especially after the explicit length normalization by $1/|y_i|$. The sequence ratio can still move, but it typically moves much more smoothly than the raw token ratios.

The other key point is clipping. In GRPO, clipping is applied token by token, so a single unstable token can be clipped or not clipped independently of the rest of the response. That creates a very uneven and noisy learning signal. In GSPO, clipping is done once per response, so the whole sample is treated consistently: either the response is considered sufficiently on-policy, or it is downweighted as a unit. Since the reward in the paper is also sequence-level, this matches the granularity of the task much better.

So the short answer is: yes, GSPO is less sensitive because it uses the average log-ratio over the response, not because the underlying token probabilities stop changing. The paper’s claim is that in MoE models the routing volatility makes token-level importance weights especially noisy and invalid as a training signal, while sequence-level likelihood remains a much more robust summary of whether the sampled response is still likely under the updated policy.

### How should I interpret GSPO's tiny clipping ranges compared with GRPO's much larger ones?
Question:
In the experiments, GSPO uses clipping ranges around $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$, while GRPO uses values around $0.2$ and $0.27$. The paper says these are not directly comparable because the underlying ratios are defined differently.

I still do not have intuition for what the GSPO numbers mean. Since $s_i(\theta)$ is a length-normalized geometric mean of per-token likelihood ratios, what does clipping at something like $1 \pm 3 \times 10^{-4}$ correspond to in practice?

For example, is there a simple connection between this threshold and average per-token log-probability change, or approximate KL divergence?
Answer:
The cleanest way to read GSPO’s clipping range is: it is a bound on the **average log-likelihood change per token**, not on the raw sequence likelihood ratio.

In the paper,
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
$$
So if GSPO clips $s_i$ to $1\pm\varepsilon$, that is equivalent to clipping the **mean log-ratio**
$$
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
$$
to approximately
$$
\log(1-\varepsilon)\le \cdots \le \log(1+\varepsilon).
$$
For small $\varepsilon$, this is basically
$$
-\varepsilon \lesssim \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} \lesssim \varepsilon.
$$

So with $\varepsilon=3\times 10^{-4}$, the interpretation is “the average token in the response is allowed to move by only about $3\times 10^{-4}$ nats in log-probability,” i.e. a roughly $0.03\%$ multiplicative change per token on average. That sounds tiny, but because it is averaged over the whole sequence, the corresponding **unnormalized** sequence likelihood ratio can still change noticeably on long responses. If the change were spread uniformly across a length-$L$ response, the total ratio would be about
$$
(1+\varepsilon)^L \approx e^{L\varepsilon}.
$$
So the same $3\times 10^{-4}$ per-token tolerance becomes a few percent over hundreds of tokens.

That is why the paper says the GSPO and GRPO clipping values are not directly comparable. GRPO clips the **token-level** ratio $w_{i,t}$, while GSPO clips the **geometric mean over the whole sequence**. A GSPO value of $3\times 10^{-4}$ is not “smaller in spirit” than GRPO’s $0.2$; it is just a different quantity on a different scale.

On the KL side, there is no exact KL threshold in the paper, because GSPO uses clipping rather than an explicit KL penalty. But the clipped quantity is closely related to a per-token log-ratio, and under the old policy its expectation over sequences is tied to a sequence KL:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}\right]
= -\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\right).
$$
So the right intuition is that GSPO’s clipping range corresponds to a very small **average per-token KL/log-probability drift**, not to a small raw sequence probability change.

In short: GSPO’s $10^{-4}$-level clipping is tiny because it is applied to a length-normalized geometric mean, and its practical effect is “keep the average log-probability shift per token extremely close to zero,” while still allowing a longer sequence to accumulate a meaningful total change.

### What happens when the group-normalized reward has zero standard deviation?
Question:
Both GRPO and GSPO define the response advantage as

$\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}$.

If all responses in the group get the same verifier score, then the denominator is $0$. This seems especially possible for binary rewards or easy prompts where all samples are either correct or incorrect.

The paper does not explain how this case is handled in practice. Is an $\epsilon$ added to the denominator, are these groups skipped, or is there another trick? I am asking because this seems like an important implementation detail for stability.
Answer:
The paper itself does not say how this corner case is handled. It gives the normalized advantage as

$$
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)},
$$

but it does not mention an $\epsilon$, a special-case branch, or skipping degenerate groups.

Conceptually, though, a group with zero standard deviation carries no learning signal. If all $G$ responses receive the same reward, then every numerator is also zero because each $r(x,y_i)$ equals the group mean. So the “right” advantage is effectively $0$ for every response in that group: there is no relative preference within the group, and GRPO/GSPO are designed to learn from relative differences, not absolute reward levels.

So in practice, implementations usually do one of two equivalent things:

$$
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r_i-\mu}{\sigma+\epsilon}
$$

with a tiny $\epsilon$, or they explicitly set the whole group’s advantages to zero when $\sigma=0$. Either way, the gradient contribution from such a group is zero, which is exactly what you want in the degenerate case.

So the short answer is: the paper does not specify the guard, but mathematically such groups should produce no update; the implementation detail is just how that zero-gradient case is enforced numerically.

### Should I think of GSPO as an unbiased off-policy estimator or as a trust-region surrogate objective?
Question:
The paper motivates GSPO using off-policy data reuse: a rollout batch is split into several mini-batches and used for multiple gradient updates, so the optimizer must correct for the gap between $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $\pi_\theta$.

But once the full sequence likelihood ratio is replaced by the normalized quantity $s_i(\theta)$ and then clipped, I am not sure how to interpret the objective anymore. It does not look like a standard unbiased importance-sampling estimator of an expectation under $\pi_\theta$.

So what is the right mental model here? Is GSPO still theoretically an off-policy policy-gradient estimator, or is it better viewed as a practical trust-region-style surrogate whose justification is mainly empirical and heuristic?
Answer:
Short answer: GSPO is better thought of as a PPO-style clipped surrogate objective at the sequence level, not as a strictly unbiased off-policy policy-gradient estimator.

The reason is visible in the paper’s own definition. The exact importance-sampling correction from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ to $\pi_\theta$ for a sequence-level quantity would use the full likelihood ratio
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y \mid x)}.
$$
If GSPO stopped there and did not clip, you could at least view it as a standard off-policy reweighting identity for a sequence-level expectation. But GSPO does two things that move it away from an unbiased estimator: it replaces that ratio by the length-normalized quantity
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|},
$$
and then it clips it. The first change is not the exact Radon–Nikodym derivative for importance sampling; it is a variance-reducing, length-normalized surrogate. The second change is exactly the kind of conservative truncation used in PPO, which is intentionally biased but much more stable.

So the right mental model is: GSPO is motivated by off-policy reuse, but its objective is a practical trust-region-like surrogate designed to keep updates small and sequence-level rather than token-level. The paper’s claim is not “we have an unbiased estimator of the true on-policy objective”; it is “token-level ratios are ill-posed and noisy, while a sequence-level clipped ratio is stable and better aligned with sequence-level rewards.”

This also matches the broader PPO intuition. PPO itself is not usually read as an unbiased policy-gradient estimator; it is a clipped surrogate that trades exactness for stability. GSPO follows the same philosophy, but moves the ratio from token level to sequence level, which the paper argues is especially important for long responses and MoE models.

So if you want one sentence: GSPO is an off-policy-inspired, sequence-level clipped surrogate, not a clean unbiased importance-sampling estimator.
