\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 5: The GSPO Algorithm: Sequence-Level Policy Optimization

# Sequence-Level Importance Ratios as the Core Idea

Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO, is built around a simple but important change in perspective: when the reward belongs to an entire generated response, the policy correction should also be done at the level of the entire response. The paper argues that this is the central flaw in GRPO, where the importance ratio is applied token by token even though the reward is attached to the whole sequence. GSPO replaces that token-level correction with a sequence-level correction, so the unit of optimization matches the unit of reward.

This matters because the training data in RL for language models is off-policy. Responses are generated by an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, but the model being updated is the current policy $\pi_\theta$. Some correction is needed to account for this mismatch. GSPO keeps that idea, but it asks a different question from GRPO: instead of asking how each token changed, it asks how the likelihood of the whole response changed. That is the sequence-level importance ratio, and it is the core design choice that makes GSPO coherent at the response level.

# The GSPO Objective and Group-Normalized Advantages

GSPO optimizes a clipped response-level objective over a group of responses to the same query. If $x$ is a query and $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ is a group of $G$ responses sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)$, then GSPO uses

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min \left( s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\,
\mathrm{clip}\left(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon\right)\widehat{A}_i \right)
\right].
\]

This objective has the same overall structure as the clipped policy objectives used in prior work, but everything important is now defined at the response level. Each response $y_i$ is assigned one importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ and one advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. The final objective averages these clipped terms across the group.

The advantage used by GSPO is the same group-based normalization introduced in GRPO. The reward for each query-response pair is computed by a verifier as $r(x,y_i)\in[0,1]$, and then the rewards inside the response group are normalized by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation:

\[
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}
{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}.
\]

This normalization makes the response’s contribution relative to the other responses generated for the same query. In other words, GSPO does not ask whether a response is good in some absolute sense alone; it asks how good it is compared to the other responses in the same group. That group-relative score then becomes the advantage used in optimization. When the response is later differentiated, all tokens in that response inherit this same response-level advantage.

# Defining the Sequence Ratio from Policy Likelihoods

The sequence-level importance ratio in GSPO is based on the likelihood of the entire response under the current policy and the old policy. Since the policy is autoregressive, the likelihood of a response $y_i$ is

\[
\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}),
\]

where $|y_i|$ is the number of tokens in the response. GSPO defines the importance ratio as

\[
s_i(\theta) =
\left(
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}
\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\left(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
\right).
\]

This ratio summarizes how much the current policy differs from the old policy on the sampled response as a whole. The paper emphasizes that this has a clear theoretical meaning in the context of language generation: the ratio measures how far the sampled response under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ has moved relative to the current policy $\pi_\theta$. Because the reward is also sequence-level, this ratio is the natural quantity to use in clipping and optimization.

The key distinction from GRPO is that GSPO does not assign a separate correction to each token. Instead, a single sequence-level correction controls the entire response. That difference is not merely cosmetic. It changes which samples are treated as reliable for gradient estimation, and it changes how the policy update is weighted across the tokens inside one response.

# Why Length Normalization Is Needed

The exponent $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ is a deliberate length normalization. The paper states two reasons for it. First, it reduces variance. Second, it keeps $s_i(\theta)$ in a unified numerical range. Without this normalization, a few token-level likelihood changes could cause the sequence ratio to fluctuate dramatically, and responses of different lengths would not be comparable under the same clipping range.

This is especially important because the raw sequence likelihood is a product across tokens. As the response becomes longer, the unnormalized likelihood ratio can become extremely sensitive to small changes at individual tokens. By averaging the log-ratios across the tokens and then exponentiating, GSPO turns that product into a quantity that is much easier to compare across response lengths. The paper’s point is not that length normalization changes the objective into something unrelated, but that it makes the sequence-level ratio numerically workable and more stable during training.

This also explains why the same clipping range cannot be reused blindly across responses of very different lengths. A long response and a short response should not be expected to produce importance ratios on the same raw scale unless the ratio is normalized by length. GSPO uses this normalization precisely so that a single clipping rule can be applied more consistently.

# Geometric-Mean Interpretation of the Normalized Sequence Ratio

The normalized sequence ratio in GSPO has a direct geometric interpretation. Since

\[
s_i(\theta) =
\exp\left(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
\right),
\]

it is the exponential of the average log token ratio. Equivalently, it is the geometric mean of the token-level likelihood ratios along the response.

This is useful intuition because it tells you what information GSPO keeps from the token-level likelihoods. It does not collapse the response into a single token or let one token dominate the update on its own. Instead, it summarizes the whole response by combining all token-level changes multiplicatively and then normalizing by length. That makes the ratio a compact response-level indicator of how the current policy differs from the old one on the sampled sequence.

The paper’s motivation for this construction is that the response-level ratio aligns with sequence-level reward and is therefore a more meaningful quantity for policy optimization than the token-level ratio used in GRPO. GSPO is not trying to estimate a token-wise correction for a reward that was never defined token by token. It is trying to represent the overall change in likelihood of the entire sampled response.

# Sequence-Level Clipping and Excluding Off-Policy Responses

GSPO keeps the clipped objective form, but clipping is applied to the entire response ratio $s_i(\theta)$ rather than to each token ratio separately. The objective uses

\[
\min\left(
s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\,
\mathrm{clip}\left(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon\right)\widehat{A}_i
\right),
\]

so the response is treated as one unit when deciding whether it is too far from the old policy. If the ratio falls outside the clipping region, the clipped value is used instead, which limits the effect of overly off-policy responses on the gradient.

This is exactly the alignment the paper argues for. The reward is assigned to the whole response, the ratio is computed for the whole response, and the clipping rule is also applied to the whole response. In that sense, GSPO excludes overly off-policy samples at the response level rather than trying to exclude individual tokens within an otherwise sampled response. The paper contrasts this directly with GRPO, where token-level importance ratios can inject high-variance noise into the update.

The gradient analysis in the paper reinforces this picture. When clipping is omitted for simplicity, the gradient of GSPO is weighted by the same sequence-level ratio for all tokens in a response:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\left(
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}
\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\widehat{A}_i
\cdot
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

The key point is that GSPO avoids token-specific importance weights inside a response. Every token in the response contributes through the same response-level multiplier. That is what makes the update uniform at the response level and avoids the unequal token weighting that the paper identifies as a source of instability in GRPO.

# Why GSPO Clipping Ranges Differ from GRPO's

The paper notes that the clipping ranges used in GSPO and in earlier token-level algorithms such as GRPO differ by orders of magnitude. This is not because one method is simply more conservative than the other in a direct, comparable sense. It is because the importance ratios themselves are defined differently, so their numerical scales are different.

GRPO clips token ratios of the form

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) =
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})},
\]

whereas GSPO clips the length-normalized sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$. Since these ratios are not on the same scale, the clipping thresholds are not directly comparable. The paper’s experimental setting reflects this difference: GSPO uses very small numeric clipping ranges, while GRPO uses much larger ones. The important point is that the clipping ranges have to be interpreted relative to the ratio definition they are designed for.

So when one sees that GSPO’s clipping numbers are much smaller than GRPO’s, that should not be read as a weakness or an inconsistency. It simply reflects the fact that GSPO’s ratio is a normalized response-level quantity, while GRPO’s ratio is a token-level quantity. Because the underlying quantities differ, the same numeric range would not mean the same thing.

# Conceptual Alignment Between Sequence Reward and Sequence Optimization

The deepest idea in GSPO is the alignment between what is rewarded and what is optimized. The paper explicitly states that the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward. Since the verifier assigns a reward to the whole query-response pair $(x,y)$, the policy update should also operate on the whole response $y$ rather than on individual tokens.

GSPO implements this alignment directly. The advantage is computed from response-level rewards within a group, the importance ratio is computed from the likelihood of the full response, and the clipping rule filters out responses that are too far from the old policy. Nothing in the update tries to split the reward into a tokenwise signal that was never observed. This is why GSPO is presented as a more principled use of importance sampling in language-model RL.

In the paper’s framing, this alignment is not just elegant; it is also practical. It is tied to the improved training stability, better sample efficiency, and stronger performance reported for GSPO. But even before looking at experiments, the algorithm’s internal logic is clear: reward the response as a whole, compare the response as a whole, and optimize the response as a whole. That is the central design principle of GSPO.
