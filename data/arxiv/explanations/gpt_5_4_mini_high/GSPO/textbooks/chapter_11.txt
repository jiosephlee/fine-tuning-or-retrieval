\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 11: Mixture-of-Experts Training and the End of Routing Replay

# What makes MoE models different from dense models

The paper treats Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, models as a special case of large language model training because of their sparse activation nature. In the paper’s setting, this sparsity matters most during reinforcement learning, where the model is not just being evaluated once, but repeatedly updated from rollout data. For a given response, only certain experts are activated, so the computation used to assign probability to that response depends on which experts are currently active. This is already different from the dense-model case the paper contrasts with, where such routing-dependent instability does not appear in the same way.

That difference becomes important because RL training in the paper is off-policy: responses are generated by an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and then used to update a current policy $\pi_\theta$. In dense models, this off-policy gap is already something clipping is meant to manage. In MoE models, the paper argues, the gap is even harder to handle because the policy update can also change which experts are activated. So the training problem is not only about comparing two policies; it is also about comparing two different activated networks for the same response.

# Expert-activation volatility across policy updates

The paper introduces the term expert-activation volatility to describe a specific instability in MoE reinforcement learning. After one or more gradient updates, the experts activated for the same response can change significantly. This means that the model’s internal path for the same query-response pair is not fixed across updates. The policy has not only shifted in its output probabilities; it may also be using a different subset of experts to produce those probabilities.

The paper emphasizes that this volatility becomes visible even after a single RL update. In other words, the routing pattern is not something that only drifts slowly over the course of training. Instead, it can change in a way that is large enough to matter immediately when computing the next optimization step. That is why the authors treat expert-activation volatility as a core cause of instability, rather than as a minor implementation detail.

# Why changing expert routes destabilizes token-level importance ratios

GRPO, the baseline discussed in the paper, uses token-level importance ratios of the form $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$. In principle, such a ratio is supposed to correct for the fact that samples came from the old policy rather than the current one. The paper’s criticism is that, in MoE models, this token-level ratio is being asked to do more than it can reliably do. If the activated experts change between $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$, then the numerator and denominator are no longer comparable in the way importance sampling intends.

This is why the paper says the token-level importance weights in GRPO are misapplied and effectively invalidated in MoE settings. Rather than acting as a clean correction for distribution mismatch, they become a source of high-variance noise. Because the ratio is computed for every token, that noise can accumulate over long responses. The paper also notes that clipping can amplify the problem rather than solve it, which is one reason the instability can eventually lead to catastrophic model collapse.

# The observed scale of routing changes in the Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base setting

The paper does not present expert-activation volatility as a theoretical possibility only. It gives a concrete empirical example using the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model. After each RL gradient update, and for the same rollout sample, roughly $10\%$ of the activated experts under the new policy $\pi_\theta$ differ from those under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. That is a substantial routing shift for the same response.

The significance of this number is not just that some experts change, but that they change repeatedly as training proceeds. Since the same response can be routed differently after every update, the mismatch between old and current policy is continually being introduced into the gradient estimate. The paper uses this observation to argue that token-level importance ratios are being computed in a setting where their underlying assumptions are already breaking down in practice.

# How this problem worsens with deeper MoE architectures

The paper explicitly notes that expert-activation volatility becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models. That matters because deeper architectures have more layers in which routing can differ, so the instability has more opportunity to accumulate. The result is that token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can fluctuate even more drastically, which further invalidates them as a stable training signal.

This is one reason the paper frames the problem as especially serious for large-scale RL. As models grow larger and deeper, the same response may be evaluated through increasingly different activated pathways after each update. In that setting, the token-level objective in GRPO becomes more fragile, and the instability is no longer easy to dismiss as a small numerical artifact. It becomes a structural training problem.

# Routing Replay: caching and replaying old expert routes

Before GSPO, the paper says the team used Routing Replay as a workaround. The idea is simple in concept even though it is operationally involved: cache the experts activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and then replay those routing modes when evaluating the current policy $\pi_\theta$ for the purpose of computing the importance ratios. In other words, the old and current policies are forced to share the same activated network when the ratio is measured.

This replay makes the comparison much more controlled. If both policies use the same activated experts for a given token, then the token-level probability ratio is no longer confounded by changes in the route itself. The paper describes this as restoring stability to the token-level importance ratios and ensuring that the same activated network is optimized consistently across gradient updates. In the authors’ previous MoE training setup, this was the mechanism that made GRPO converge normally.

# Why Routing Replay helps GRPO converge

Routing Replay helps because it patches the specific place where GRPO becomes unstable on MoE models. GRPO’s optimization objective is built around token-level advantages and token-level ratios. Once the activated experts become inconsistent across old and current policies, those ratios become noisy and unreliable. By replaying the old routing modes, the ratio is measured against a fixed computational path, so the gradient signal is less distorted.

The paper is clear that this was not a cosmetic improvement. Figure-level evidence is used to support the claim that Routing Replay is an essential technique for the normal convergence of GRPO training on MoE models. In the paper’s framing, the workaround does not merely improve performance slightly; it is what keeps the training process from becoming unstable when token-level optimization is used on sparse models.

# The memory, communication, and capacity costs of Routing Replay

Although Routing Replay stabilizes training, the paper argues that it comes with real costs. Reusing routing modes requires extra memory and communication overhead, because the activated experts from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ must be cached and then reused during optimization. That extra bookkeeping makes the training system more complex.

The paper also says that Routing Replay can limit the actual capacity of the MoE model. The reason is that reusing routing modes imposes an artificial constraint on how the model can behave during training. Instead of allowing the model to use its full sparse-expert flexibility, the optimization process is forced to remain tied to previously cached routes. So while the workaround makes GRPO workable, it does so by adding complexity and by reducing how naturally the model can express its capacity.

# Why GSPO is robust to routing volatility at the sequence-likelihood level

GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay by changing the unit of optimization. Instead of defining importance ratios token by token, GSPO defines the ratio at the level of the whole response:
$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
$
The paper’s key claim is that this sequence-level ratio has a clear theoretical meaning: it measures how far the entire response under the old policy deviates from the current policy, and it does so in a way that matches the sequence-level reward.

This matters for MoE training because GSPO is not trying to make a token-level correction that is sensitive to each local routing choice. The paper notes that the MoE model still maintains its language modeling capability, so the sequence likelihood does not fluctuate as drastically as the token-level path might suggest. By working at the sequence level, GSPO becomes robust to the routing volatility that breaks token-level importance ratios. The length normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ also helps reduce variance and keeps the ratio in a unified numerical range.

# How GSPO enables stable MoE RL without special routing constraints

With sequence-level optimization, GSPO can compute importance ratios conventionally and optimize stably even when expert routes vary. The paper’s conclusion is that GSPO is fully capable of normal convergence on MoE models without Routing Replay. This is not because routing volatility disappears, but because GSPO no longer depends on token-level ratios that are invalidated by that volatility.

The broader consequence is that GSPO simplifies the training system while also making it more robust. The model no longer needs special routing constraints to make RL work, so it can use its full capacity without artificial limitations. In the paper’s view, this is the deeper reason GSPO is a better fit for large-scale MoE reinforcement learning: it aligns the optimization objective with the sequence-level reward and avoids the instability introduced by token-level off-policy correction. That is why the authors describe GSPO as resolving the expert-activation volatility issue fundamentally, rather than merely working around it.
