\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 2: Core Foundations: Policies, Rewards, and Off-Policy Optimization

# Autoregressive language models as policies

The paper treats an autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$ as a policy $\pi_\theta$. This is a useful way to think about language generation because the model does not choose a whole response all at once. Instead, it produces one token at a time, and each new token depends on the query and on all previously generated tokens. If $x$ is the query and $y$ is the response, then the policy assigns a likelihood to the full response by multiplying the conditional likelihoods of its tokens:
\[
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t}).
\]
Here $|y|$ is the number of tokens in the response, and $y_{<t}$ means the tokens before position $t$.

This factorization is the mathematical expression of autoregressive generation. It says that a response is a sequence of linked decisions, where each decision is made in the context of what has already been generated. In the paper’s notation, this is the basic object being optimized during RL training: not a static prediction, but a policy over complete sequences. That perspective is essential for understanding why later parts of the paper distinguish carefully between token-level quantities and sequence-level quantities.

# Queries, responses, datasets, and verifier-based rewards

The paper uses $x$ to denote a query and $\mathcal{D}$ to denote the set of queries used for training. A response $y$ is generated for a query $x$, and the pair $(x,y)$ is then evaluated by a verifier $r$. The verifier produces a scalar reward
\[
r(x,y)\in [0,1].
\]
This reward is attached to the query-response pair as a whole, not to individual tokens. That detail matters, because it means the feedback signal the model receives is about the quality of the entire response.

A key point in the paper is that the reward is sequence-level. The verifier does not say that one token is good and another is bad; it scores the completed response. This is why the paper later argues that the optimization objective should also operate at the response level. When multiple responses are sampled for the same query, their rewards can be compared against one another. In the paper’s group-based setting, this comparison is turned into an advantage signal by normalizing the rewards within the group. For a group of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ to the same query, the paper defines
\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}.
\]
This turns raw verifier scores into a relative measure of how well each response performed compared with the other responses to the same query.

# Token likelihood versus sequence likelihood

Because the model is autoregressive, it is natural to talk about token likelihoods. The likelihood of each token is the conditional probability of that token given the query and the previous tokens, such as $\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})$. This is the local quantity used at a particular step of generation. It tells you how probable one token is in one context.

The sequence likelihood is the product of all those token likelihoods, $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$. In other words, it measures how probable the entire response is under the policy. The paper’s central observation is that this quantity has a much clearer meaning when the object being optimized is the whole response. If a response is the unit that receives the reward, then the sequence likelihood is the natural object to compare between the old policy and the current policy. The paper later turns this idea into a sequence-level importance ratio. At the intuition level, the sequence likelihood asks a single question: how much does the model, as a whole, prefer this complete response?

This distinction between token likelihood and sequence likelihood becomes more important for long responses. Since the sequence likelihood is a product over many token probabilities, changes at many token positions accumulate across the whole response. That accumulation is one reason the paper is careful about which level of likelihood is used in the optimization objective. A local tokenwise quantity may be easy to write down, but it is not always the right match for a reward that is assigned only after the entire response has been generated.

# What it means to optimize a response-level reward

To optimize a response-level reward means that the learning signal is attached to the entire response, and the model is encouraged to increase the probability of responses that receive better verifier scores. The paper emphasizes a simple guiding principle: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of the reward. Since the verifier judges the full query-response pair, the optimization procedure should also treat the full response as the object being rewarded or discouraged.

This is exactly what the group-based setup is trying to accomplish. When multiple responses are sampled for the same query, the paper forms a relative score for each response by comparing it with the others in the group. That relative score becomes the advantage used in optimization. In GRPO, for example, all tokens in a response share the same group-based advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. The idea is that if a response is better than the other responses sampled for the same query, then the entire response should be reinforced; if it is worse, the entire response should be downweighted.

This is a very different picture from token-by-token reward assignment. Instead of pretending that each token has an independent reward, the paper treats the response as a single decision object composed of many token decisions. The reward is still ultimately about the whole response, and the optimization signal should respect that structure. This matching between reward granularity and optimization granularity is one of the paper’s main conceptual anchors.

# On-policy and off-policy learning in language-model RL

In RL, on-policy learning means that the data being used for optimization come from the same policy that is currently being updated. Off-policy learning means that the data were generated by an older policy, while the model being optimized has already changed. The paper uses the notation $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for the policy that generated the rollout data, and $\pi_\theta$ for the policy being optimized. Once $\theta$ changes, the samples collected under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ become off-policy relative to the current model.

This distinction is not just a formal detail. The paper explains that when a large rollout batch is generated and then reused across several gradient updates, the policy can drift away from the policy that produced the data. As the update proceeds, the same response becomes less representative of the current model. The farther the current policy moves from the old one, the more severe the off-policy mismatch becomes. That is why the paper pays so much attention to how old-policy samples are weighted and how aggressively they are allowed to influence the update.

The paper also notes that the mismatch is particularly troublesome for long responses. A response is not a single action but a long chain of token choices, so any discrepancy between the old and current policies can accumulate across many positions. This is one reason the paper argues that stable RL training for language models requires a careful treatment of off-policy data rather than a naive reuse of rollout samples.

# Why rollout batches are split into mini-batches during training

The paper explains that as models become larger, sparser, and capable of longer responses, RL training requires large rollout batches to make efficient use of hardware. A large batch keeps the training system busy and helps produce enough samples for learning. But once those samples have been collected, they are usually not consumed all at once. Instead, the batch is partitioned into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates.

This partitioning improves sample efficiency, because the same rollout data can be reused for several updates. But it also creates the off-policy situation described above. The first mini-batch is processed using one version of the policy, and later mini-batches are processed after the model has already been updated. So even though the rollouts were generated together, the optimization sees them through gradually changing parameters. The paper presents this as a fundamental practical feature of large-scale language-model RL, not as an unusual corner case.

This is why clipping and importance weighting matter. Without some mechanism to control how much old data can influence the current update, the mini-batch training procedure would make the learning signal increasingly stale. In the paper’s framing, mini-batching is necessary for efficiency, but it also makes the off-policy problem unavoidable.

# The basic principle of importance sampling

The paper invokes importance sampling as the mathematical tool for correcting a mismatch between a behavior distribution and a target distribution. If $f$ is a function of a random variable $z$, then the basic principle is
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)]
=
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_\text{beh}}\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}f(z)\right].
\]
Here $\pi_\text{tar}$ is the target distribution whose expectation we want, and $\pi_\text{beh}$ is the behavior distribution from which the samples were actually drawn. The ratio $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ tells us how to reweight each sample so that, on average, the behavior samples reproduce the target expectation.

The intuition is simple: a sample that is more likely under the target than under the behavior distribution should count more, while a sample that is less likely under the target should count less. The weight does not change the sample itself; it changes how much the sample contributes to the estimate. In the language-model setting of the paper, this principle is used to compare quantities under the current policy with samples collected under the old policy. The same idea appears whether the ratio is defined for tokens or for whole responses.

# When importance reweighting is valid and why sample averaging matters

The paper emphasizes that importance reweighting is not just about computing a ratio. It also depends on averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution. In the paper’s wording, the correction works effectively when there are multiple samples, with $N \gg 1$, from the behavior distribution. The reason is that the weighted average is what approximates the target expectation. A single weighted sample can still be extremely noisy, but many weighted samples can collectively compensate for the distribution mismatch.

This point matters directly for the paper’s critique of token-level importance weights. GRPO applies the importance weight at each token position, but each such weight is based on a single sampled token from the next-token distribution. According to the paper, that is not the setting in which importance sampling is meant to operate effectively. The weight is then asked to correct a mismatch using a very small amount of information, and the result is high-variance noise in the gradient estimate. The paper argues that this noise accumulates over long sequences and becomes a serious source of instability.

The broader lesson is that importance reweighting should be used at the same level as the quantity being estimated. If the reward is attached to a full response, then the correction should also be applied at the response level. That is why the paper’s sequence-level approach is so important: it keeps the statistical correction aligned with the object being optimized, rather than distributing the correction across tokenwise fragments that do not represent the reward signal on their own.

# Clipping as a safeguard against overly off-policy updates

The paper describes clipping as a safeguard that keeps the policy update within a proximal region of the old policy. In PPO and GRPO, the importance ratio is clipped to an interval such as $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$, and the objective uses the smaller of the unclipped and clipped terms. The purpose is to prevent samples that are too far from the current policy from dominating the gradient update. In effect, clipping says that if a sample has become too off-policy, its influence should be limited.

This safeguard is especially important in the setting of repeated mini-batch updates from the same rollout batch. As the policy drifts, some old samples may no longer be reliable indicators of what the current model should do. Clipping keeps those samples from causing overly aggressive updates. The paper also argues that clipping is not merely a numerical trick; it is part of the mechanism that allows off-policy data to be used safely during RL training.

At the same time, the paper’s main claim is that clipping should match the level of the reward and the likelihood ratio. If the reward is sequence-level, then clipping should be sequence-level as well. That is why the paper moves away from tokenwise clipping and toward response-level clipping. In the paper’s view, this alignment between reward granularity, importance weighting, and clipping granularity is what makes the optimization stable and meaningful for language-model RL.
