\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 3: PPO and GRPO: The Starting Point

# Review of the PPO objective

The paper begins from Proximal Policy Optimization, or PPO, as a standard clipped policy-gradient method for training language models with reinforcement learning. In the paper’s notation, an autoregressive language model with parameters $\theta$ is treated as a policy $\pi_\theta$. A query is denoted by $x$, and a generated response by $y$. The overall likelihood of a response under the policy is written as $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})$, where $|y|$ is the number of tokens in the response. A query-response pair $(x,y)$ can be evaluated by a verifier, producing a reward $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$.

PPO is introduced as a way to update the policy using samples generated from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The key idea is that the update should stay within a proximal region of the old policy, rather than changing too abruptly. The paper expresses this through a clipped objective:
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\min\left(w_t(\theta)\widehat{A}_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_t\right)\right].
\]
The objective averages over tokens in the sampled response, and the clipping mechanism limits how much a token’s contribution can grow or shrink. In the paper’s presentation, the KL regularization term is omitted for brevity, because it is not the focus of the discussion.

This formulation captures the central PPO intuition used throughout the paper: training is driven by samples produced by an earlier policy, but the optimizer is discouraged from trusting samples that would require too large a shift in probability under the current policy. The clipping term is therefore a safeguard against overly aggressive updates.

# Token-level importance ratios in PPO

A defining feature of PPO in the paper is that the importance ratio is computed at the token level. For the token $y_t$, the ratio is
\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}.
\]
This ratio compares how likely the current policy thinks a token is, relative to the old policy that generated the sample. Because PPO uses samples from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ while optimizing $\pi_\theta$, this ratio serves as the correction factor that connects the sampled data to the updated model.

The paper emphasizes that PPO applies this ratio at every token position. The objective then takes the minimum of the unclipped term and the clipped term, so that if the ratio moves too far from $1$, the contribution is constrained. The clipping range is controlled by $\varepsilon$. In effect, PPO tries to preserve the usefulness of the old sample while preventing a single token from causing an excessively large gradient step.

In the language used by the paper, PPO is therefore token-centric: each token contributes through its own importance ratio and its own advantage estimate. This makes PPO a strong baseline for policy optimization, but it also creates a dependence on accurate token-level advantage estimates, which becomes crucial in the discussion that follows.

# Advantage estimation with a learned value model

PPO in the paper uses an advantage estimate $\widehat{A}_t$ for each token, and that advantage is estimated by another value model. This is important because the advantage provides the learning signal that tells the policy whether a token is better or worse than expected. In the objective, the advantage multiplies the importance ratio, so the sign and magnitude of the advantage directly control how the policy is updated.

The paper does not expand the value-model training procedure here, but it makes clear why the value model is central: the effectiveness of PPO depends on the reliability of the value estimate. If the value model is inaccurate, then the advantage signal is also unreliable, and the policy update may be guided in the wrong direction. In other words, PPO’s policy optimization quality is tied to the quality of a separate learned component.

This reliance on the value model is not a small implementation detail in the paper’s view. It is part of the reason why PPO is difficult to scale in the setting of large language models, especially when responses become longer and tasks become more complex. The advantage estimate must remain reliable across these harder settings, which the paper treats as a major practical obstacle.

# Why value models are costly and difficult to scale for LLMs

The paper highlights the value model as a major source of cost in PPO. In practice, the value model is usually similar in size to the policy model, so it adds substantial memory and computational burden. This matters especially in large-scale language model training, where resource demands are already high.

Beyond cost, the paper points out a second problem: the algorithm’s effectiveness hinges on the reliability of the value estimate. That means one must not only afford the value model, but also ensure that it remains trustworthy. The paper argues that this becomes harder as responses grow longer and tasks become more complex. So the challenge is both practical and algorithmic: the value model is expensive, and scaling it robustly is difficult.

This is the backdrop against which the paper later motivates a value-model-free alternative. PPO is not presented as flawed in the abstract; rather, it is presented as a strong baseline whose dependence on a learned value model becomes increasingly burdensome in the large-language-model regime.

# The motivation for Group Relative Policy Optimization

Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO, is introduced in the paper as a way to bypass the value model entirely. The motivation is straightforward: if the model can compare multiple responses to the same query directly, then it may not need a separate value function to estimate token-level advantage. GRPO is designed to exploit this idea.

The paper describes GRPO as operating on a group of responses generated for the same query. Instead of estimating an advantage with a learned value model, GRPO uses the relative quality of each response within the group. This is the key conceptual shift from PPO. Rather than asking an external value model what a token or response should be worth, GRPO asks how one sampled response compares to the other sampled responses for the same query.

This group-based perspective is what makes GRPO attractive in the paper’s discussion. It removes the most expensive extra component from PPO, and it replaces it with a reward-normalization procedure computed directly from the sampled responses themselves.

# Group-based sampling over multiple responses per query

In GRPO, the paper samples $G$ responses for each query $x$. These responses are written as $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$ and are drawn from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)$. The use of multiple responses per query is central to the method, because GRPO needs a group in order to define relative advantage.

The intuition is that the model is not judged in isolation on a single response. Instead, each response is compared against other responses to the same query. That makes the learning signal relative rather than absolute. If one response is better than the others in its group, it should receive a positive advantage; if it is worse, it should receive a negative one.

This sampling structure also helps explain why GRPO can avoid the value model. The group itself supplies the baseline for comparison. The method does not need a separately learned estimate of expected return, because it can compare the rewards observed within the group and compute a normalized score directly from them.

# Normalized group rewards as response-level advantages

GRPO defines its response-level advantage by normalizing each response’s reward relative to the other rewards in the same group. The paper’s formula is
\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{G}\right)}{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{G}\right)}.
\]
This is a standardized score: the reward of response $y_i$ is centered by subtracting the group mean, and then scaled by the group standard deviation.

The meaning of this normalization is easy to interpret. If a response’s reward is above the average reward in the group, then its normalized advantage is positive. If it is below average, the normalized advantage is negative. The standard deviation rescales the differences so that the advantages are measured in a common unit across groups. In the paper’s implementation, all tokens in the same response share the same advantage value, namely $\widehat{A}_i$.

This is one of the most important structural differences from PPO. PPO assigns a separate advantage estimate to each token, whereas GRPO assigns one response-level advantage to every token in the response. The reward is still delivered to the entire query-response pair, so this response-level treatment matches the granularity of the reward more closely than token-specific value estimation would.

# The GRPO objective and its token-level clipping

The paper writes the GRPO objective as
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\min\left(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t},\ \mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_{i,t}\right)\right],
\]
where the token-level importance ratio is
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
In GRPO, the token advantages are all the same within a response: $\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i$.

The structure of the objective is therefore a hybrid of two ideas. On one hand, the advantage signal is response-level and comes from the group reward normalization. On the other hand, the optimization is still carried out token by token using token-level importance ratios and token-level clipping. This means that GRPO retains the clipped surrogate style of PPO while replacing the value model with a group-based advantage estimate.

The paper presents this as the defining mathematical form of GRPO. Its appeal is that it keeps the familiar clipped-update structure while removing the need for a value network.

# Strengths of GRPO: avoiding a value model

From the paper’s perspective, the primary strength of GRPO is that it avoids the value model altogether. This is a significant simplification, because it eliminates the memory and computation cost associated with a separate value network. It also removes the need to train and maintain a reliable value estimator.

Another advantage is conceptual simplicity. GRPO uses the relative quality of sampled responses to define advantage, which fits naturally with the idea of evaluating multiple responses to the same query. This makes it especially appealing in large-language-model reinforcement learning, where generating multiple responses is already part of the training process.

At the same time, the paper treats GRPO as a baseline rather than a final solution. Its value-model-free design is clearly useful, but the paper later argues that the token-level importance ratios in GRPO create stability problems when training very large models. For the purposes of understanding the paper, however, the important point is that GRPO is a practical step beyond PPO because it removes the value model bottleneck.

# The paper’s setup for comparing GSPO against GRPO

The paper’s empirical comparison between the new GSPO method and GRPO is built on a specific training setup. The authors experiment with a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base. They report both training reward curves and model performance curves on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces. The AIME'24 metric is average Pass@1 over 32 samplings, LiveCodeBench uses the 202410-202502 split and average Pass@1 over 8 samplings, and CodeForces is measured by Elo Rating.

The RL training procedure partitions each batch of rollout data into four mini-batches for gradient updates. For GSPO, the left and right clipping ranges in its objective are set to $3\mathrm{e}{-4}$ and $4\mathrm{e}{-4}$, respectively. For GRPO, the corresponding clipping ranges are set to $0.2$ and $0.27$, which the paper says were carefully tuned to ensure a fair comparison.

The paper also notes that GRPO requires the Routing Replay training strategy for normal convergence of MoE RL, while GSPO does not. This setup detail is important because it shows that the comparison is not only about reward curves or benchmark scores, but also about whether the method can train stably in the first place. The paper’s broader claim is that GSPO improves upon GRPO in stability, efficiency, and performance under this carefully controlled comparison.
