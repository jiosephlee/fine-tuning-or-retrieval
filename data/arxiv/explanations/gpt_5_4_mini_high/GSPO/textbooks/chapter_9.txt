\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 9: Empirical Results: Stability, Efficiency, and Performance

The experiments in this paper are designed to test GSPO under realistic large-model RL conditions. The authors fine-tune a cold-start model initialized from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, split each rollout batch into four mini-batches for gradient updates, and compare GSPO against a carefully tuned GRPO baseline. For GSPO, the left and right clipping ranges are set to $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$, while GRPO uses $0.2$ and $0.27$. The paper tracks both training reward and downstream benchmark performance, so the empirical story is not just about whether optimization runs, but whether it keeps improving and whether that improvement transfers to actual capabilities.

# Reward-curve behavior under GSPO versus GRPO

The first empirical signal the paper emphasizes is the behavior of the training reward curves. In RL for language models, the reward curve is a direct window into whether the optimization process is moving in a controlled direction or becoming erratic. GSPO is reported to proceed stably throughout training, which means the reward signal remains usable across the run rather than breaking down as optimization continues.

This matters because the paper’s central claim is that sequence-level optimization avoids the instability caused by token-level importance ratios in GRPO. The reward curves therefore serve as a practical confirmation of that claim. Instead of seeing unstable progress or abrupt failure, the authors report a training trajectory that remains coherent enough to support continued learning.

# Evidence of stable training throughout long RL runs

A major takeaway from the experiments is that GSPO remains stable over long reinforcement learning runs. The paper explicitly states that GSPO training proceeds stably throughout, and this is a central result rather than a side observation. In the setting studied here, stability is not a convenience; it is a prerequisite for being able to push RL further without triggering collapse.

The importance of this result is clearer when viewed against the background of the paper’s motivation. The authors argue that large language model RL can fail catastrophically when the optimization signal is too noisy or too misaligned with the reward structure. GSPO’s stable behavior throughout training shows that the sequence-level formulation is not only theoretically cleaner, but also robust enough to sustain long runs in practice.

# Continuous gains from increasing training compute

One of the most important scaling findings in the paper is that GSPO continues to improve as training compute increases. The authors report that GSPO can deliver continuous performance improvement through increasing the training compute, which is exactly what one wants from a scalable RL algorithm. Rather than plateauing immediately or becoming unstable as more optimization steps are added, the method keeps turning extra compute into better results.

This is significant because scale only helps if the learning process remains healthy at larger budgets. A method that works on a small run but degrades when compute grows is not truly scalable. By showing that performance continues to improve with more compute, the paper argues that GSPO has the right behavior for large-scale RL training of language models.

# Benefits of regularly updating the query set

The paper also reports that GSPO continues to benefit when the query set is regularly updated. This is one of the training axes along which performance keeps improving, alongside compute and generation length. In the authors’ experiments, refreshing the query set does not interrupt stability; instead, it accompanies the continued upward trend in performance.

This result suggests that GSPO is not tied to a fixed set of prompts in a brittle way. As the training queries change, the algorithm can keep extracting useful learning signal. In the context of the paper, that is another sign that GSPO is suited to large-scale RL setups, where the training distribution may be expanded or refreshed over time.

# Benefits of extending generation length

A third scaling axis highlighted in the experiments is generation length. The paper reports that GSPO shows continuous performance improvement when generation length is extended. This is especially relevant because longer responses are a harder setting for RL training: there are more tokens involved, more opportunities for instability, and a stronger need for a learning rule that stays meaningful over the whole sequence.

GSPO’s sequence-level design is well matched to this setting, and the empirical results reflect that match. Instead of becoming less effective as responses grow longer, the method keeps improving. The paper uses this to argue that GSPO is not just stable in short-horizon optimization, but also capable of supporting the longer reasoning processes that are important in modern language model RL.

# Higher training efficiency under the same compute budget

The paper reports that GSPO achieves superior training efficiency over GRPO under the same training compute and consumed queries. This is one of the clearest empirical wins in the study, because it shows that GSPO is not merely stable; it is also a better use of resources. The authors describe GSPO as achieving better training accuracy under the same budget, which means it extracts more learning progress from each unit of compute.

A particularly interesting observation concerns clipping. GSPO clips entire responses rather than individual tokens, and the paper notes a difference of two orders of magnitude in the fractions of clipped tokens between GSPO and GRPO. Even after adjusting clipping ranges, that disparity in magnitude remains. Yet GSPO still attains higher training efficiency. The paper interprets this counter-intuitive result as evidence that GRPO’s token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient for sample exploitation, while GSPO’s sequence-level approach provides a more reliable learning signal.

# Higher benchmark performance under the same consumed queries

The downstream benchmark curves are the other half of the empirical picture. The paper evaluates the model on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces, reporting average Pass@1 over $32$ samplings for AIME'24, average Pass@1 over $8$ samplings for LiveCodeBench (202410-202502), and Elo rating for CodeForces. Under the same consumed queries, GSPO achieves better benchmark performance than GRPO.

This is important because it shows that the gains are not confined to the training objective. Better reward behavior would be less compelling if it failed to translate into external capability improvements. The reported benchmark results indicate that GSPO’s more efficient training dynamics carry through to real evaluation performance, which is exactly what one expects from a method that aligns optimization at the sequence level with sequence-level reward.

# How the results support GSPO as a scalable RL algorithm

Taken together, the empirical findings support the paper’s broader claim that GSPO is a scalable RL algorithm for large language models. The method is stable throughout long training runs, continues to improve as compute is increased, remains effective when the query set is updated, and still works when generation length is extended. At the same time, it uses training compute more effectively than GRPO and produces stronger benchmark results under the same consumed queries.

The scalability claim is strengthened further by the practical simplifications GSPO brings. In the MoE setting discussed elsewhere in the paper, GSPO eliminates the need for Routing Replay, which GRPO required for normal convergence. The authors also argue that GSPO’s sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision discrepancies between training and inference engines, pointing toward simpler RL infrastructure. The empirical results therefore support not just better learning behavior, but a more practical large-scale training workflow.

# Connection to improvements reported for the latest Qwen3 models

The paper closes the empirical story by connecting GSPO directly to the latest Qwen3 models. The authors state that the merits of GSPO contributed to the remarkable improvements in those models, and that GSPO has been successfully applied to their RL training. In other words, the gains reported in the experiments are presented as having immediate model-development impact rather than remaining a purely academic result.

This connection gives the empirical section its broader significance. GSPO is not only shown to be stable, efficient, and performant in a controlled comparison with GRPO; it is also described as part of the training recipe behind stronger Qwen3 systems. That makes the experiments more than a benchmark exercise: they are evidence that the sequence-level formulation can serve as a practical foundation for scaling RL in frontier language models.
