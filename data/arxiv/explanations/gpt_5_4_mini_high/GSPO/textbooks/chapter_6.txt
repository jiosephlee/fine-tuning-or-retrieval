\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 6: Gradient-Level Comparison Between GSPO and GRPO

# Deriving the GSPO gradient without clipping

To understand why GSPO behaves differently from GRPO, it is useful to begin with the GSPO objective in its unclipped form. The paper defines GSPO at the sequence level as
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i
\right],
\]
where the advantage is computed from the group of responses to the same query,
\[
\widehat{A}_i
=
\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}
{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)},
\]
and the importance ratio is based on sequence likelihood,
\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\left(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\right).
\]
When clipping is omitted, differentiating this objective is straightforward because the expectation is taken over samples drawn from the fixed old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$.

The key step is that the derivative of $s_i(\theta)$ can be written through the derivative of its logarithm. The paper derives
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i \cdot \nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)
\right],
\]
and then expands $\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)$ as the average of token-level log-likelihood gradients:
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\widehat{A}_i
\cdot
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]
This expression is the starting point for the paper's interpretation of GSPO: the entire response contributes a single scalar sequence ratio, and that one scalar multiplies the aggregate gradient of all tokens in the response.

# How the sequence ratio scales all tokens in a response equally

The most important feature of the GSPO gradient is that every token in a given response receives the same multiplicative scale factor, namely the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$. In the final gradient expression, the token-wise gradients appear only inside the average
\[
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
\]
and this whole average is multiplied by the same scalar $s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i$. So although the response contains many tokens, GSPO does not assign each token its own off-policy correction weight. Instead, the correction is computed once for the full response and then applied uniformly to all token gradients belonging to that response.

This is exactly what the paper means when it says that GSPO performs sequence-level clipping, rewarding, and optimization. The sequence likelihood ratio reflects how far the whole response $y_i$ under the current policy deviates from the old policy, and because the reward is also defined for the whole query-response pair $(x,y_i)$, the gradient update is aligned with the same unit. The paper also notes that length normalization is used in
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}},
\]
so that the importance ratio stays within a unified numerical range and is less sensitive to the length of the response. This matters because, without normalization, likelihood changes from a few tokens could produce large fluctuations in the response-level ratio, and responses of different lengths would not be comparable in a stable way.

# Deriving the GRPO gradient without clipping

GRPO begins from a different starting point. Instead of assigning a sequence-level importance ratio to the full response, it defines a token-level ratio
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]
while using the same group-based advantage for all tokens in a response,
\[
\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i.
\]
Ignoring clipping for the moment, the paper writes the GRPO objective as
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t}
\right].
\]
Since $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ is the same across the tokens in a response, the only token-specific part is the importance ratio.

Differentiating this objective yields
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\widehat{A}_i
\cdot
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]
This is the critical contrast with GSPO. In GRPO, each token gradient is multiplied by its own ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, so the tokens in the same response are not scaled uniformly. Even though the response as a whole has a single advantage $\widehat{A}_i$, the per-token correction is different at each position.

# How GRPO assigns different weights to different tokens

The paper's gradient comparison makes it clear that GRPO does not treat a response as a single optimization unit. Instead, it treats every token as if it needed its own off-policy correction factor. That means the gradient contribution from one token can be amplified while another token in the same response can be reduced, even though both tokens belong to the same rewarded sequence and share the same group advantage $\widehat{A}_i$.

This distinction is not just a matter of notation. In the paper's words, the fundamental difference between GSPO and GRPO is how they weight the gradients of the log likelihoods of tokens. GSPO uses one sequence-level weight for the whole response, while GRPO uses a separate token-level weight at each position. Those token weights are not negligible. The paper emphasizes that they can vary within the ranges associated with clipping, and their unequal contributions accumulate as training progresses. So, even before considering clipping, GRPO already builds in a source of within-response imbalance that GSPO avoids.

The intuitive consequence is that GRPO can end up emphasizing some tokens in a response much more than others, not because the reward says they matter more, but because their current token-level likelihood ratios happen to differ. This is precisely the kind of mismatch the paper argues against: when the reward is sequence-level, the optimization should not fracture that reward into separately weighted token updates.

# The role of token-wise likelihood ratios in gradient noise

The paper identifies the token-wise importance ratio as the core source of instability in GRPO. Its argument is that importance sampling is meant to correct a distributional mismatch by reweighting samples from a behavior distribution. But this logic relies on averaging over many samples from that distribution so that the weighting can actually play a corrective role. GRPO does not do that at the level of token likelihoods. Instead, it applies
\[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\]
to a single sampled token from each next-token distribution. The paper argues that this makes the weight a poor distribution-correction mechanism and turns it into a source of high-variance noise.

That noise is especially problematic because the token-level ratios appear inside a sum over all tokens in the response. As the response becomes longer, the gradient accumulates more of these noisy token-specific factors. The paper states that the noise progressively accumulates with increased response length and is further amplified by clipping. In other words, the more tokens there are, the more opportunities there are for the mismatch between token-level weighting and sequence-level reward to distort the update.

This is why the paper views token-wise likelihood ratios as fundamentally ill-suited for the problem it is trying to solve. They do not merely add detail; they inject variability into the gradient at the wrong granularity. The result is not a cleaner approximation to the objective, but a noisier and less reliable update direction.

# Why unequal token weighting can accumulate harmful effects

Once token-level weights are introduced, the differences among tokens are not isolated. They compound across the entire response and across many training steps. The paper stresses that these unequal weights can accumulate and lead to unpredictable consequences as training proceeds. Because every response contributes many token terms, a small imbalance at each position can become a larger distortion once aggregated over long sequences and repeated updates.

This accumulation is the reason the paper connects GRPO's gradient structure to catastrophic model collapse. The instability does not appear as a single isolated error. Rather, the training noise builds up over time until the model collapses, and the paper describes this collapse as often irreversible. It also reports that once collapse occurs, resuming training is unavailing, even if one reverts to a previous checkpoint, tunes the clipping range, extends generation length, or changes the RL queries.

From the gradient perspective, the problem is clear: if different tokens in the same response are repeatedly scaled by different importance ratios, the updates no longer reflect a coherent response-level judgment. The model is trained by many slightly inconsistent signals, and those inconsistencies are exactly what the paper identifies as the source of instability in large-scale RL training.

# Positive and negative advantage regions under clipping

Clipping is meant to keep updates within a proximal region of the old policy, but the paper shows that it interacts differently with positive and negative advantages. In GRPO, the clipped objective is
\[
\min\left(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t},
\,
\mathrm{clip}\left(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\right)\widehat{A}_{i,t}
\right),
\]
and because $\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i$, the sign of $\widehat{A}_i$ determines how the clipping behaves.

For $\widehat{A}_i>0$, the effective token weights can vary in the range $(0,1+\varepsilon]$. For $\widehat{A}_i<0$, they can vary in the range $[1-\varepsilon,+\infty)$. The paper points out that these unequal weights are not negligible. In practice, this means that the gradient does not simply scale all tokens by one clipped factor. Instead, the behavior of the clipping depends both on the token-level ratio and on the sign of the response advantage, so different tokens within the same response may be clipped in different ways.

GSPO uses the same clipping idea, but at the sequence level. So rather than clipping each token separately according to its own ratio, GSPO clips the response as a whole according to its sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$. That is an important conceptual difference: the sign of the advantage still matters, but the clipping decision is made for the response-level quantity that matches the reward structure.

# Interpreting GSPO as a more coherent response-level credit assignment mechanism

The paper's central principle is simple: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward. Because the verifier gives reward to the entire query-response pair $(x,y_i)$, GSPO treats the entire response as the unit of credit assignment. The group-based advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ summarizes how well a response did relative to other responses to the same query, and the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ scales the update for that whole response.

This makes GSPO more coherent than GRPO at the level of the gradient. In GSPO, all tokens in a response are optimized together, using one response-level importance ratio and one response-level advantage. The gradient therefore says, in effect, "this entire response was good or bad relative to the group, so scale every token in it by the same response-level correction." That is exactly what the paper means by sequence-level rewarding and optimization.

GRPO breaks that coherence by assigning separate importance ratios to each token. Even though the reward is still sequence-level, the correction is not. That mismatch is the reason the paper argues GRPO's objective is ill-posed. GSPO removes that mismatch by making the optimization unit and the reward unit the same.

# What the gradient analysis suggests about training stability

The gradient formulas explain why the paper expects GSPO to train more stably than GRPO. GSPO replaces many token-specific ratio factors with a single sequence-level ratio, so the response contributes a smoother and more unified gradient signal. Since the same scalar multiplies all token log-likelihood gradients in the response, GSPO avoids the within-response imbalance that comes from token-wise importance ratios.

By contrast, GRPO's gradient contains token-specific ratios that can differ substantially from one position to the next. The paper argues that these unequal weights introduce high-variance noise, which becomes worse as sequences get longer and which can be amplified by clipping. That is the mechanism the paper connects to instability and collapse. The gradient analysis therefore does more than compare formulas: it gives a structural explanation for why GRPO can become unstable and why GSPO is more robust.

The paper's empirical findings are consistent with this interpretation. GSPO is reported to train stably, improve continuously with more compute, and perform well on the benchmarks considered. It also resolves stability problems in MoE RL training that previously required extra machinery such as Routing Replay. From the viewpoint of the gradients, this outcome is not surprising: when the optimization signal is aligned with the reward at the sequence level, the update is simpler, less noisy, and more stable.
