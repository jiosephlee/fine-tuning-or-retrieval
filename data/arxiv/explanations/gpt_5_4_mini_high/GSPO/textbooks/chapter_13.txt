\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 13: Synthesis, Limitations, and Future Directions

# The paper's central lesson about matching reward level and optimization level

The most important conceptual lesson in the paper is that the level at which a reward is defined should match the level at which optimization is performed. In the setting studied here, the verifier assigns reward to an entire query-response pair $(x, y)$, not to individual tokens in the response. That means the natural object being judged is the full sequence. The paper argues that when an algorithm optimizes token-by-token while the reward is sequence-level, it introduces a mismatch between what is being scored and what is being updated. GSPO is designed to remove that mismatch by making the optimization objective sequence-level as well.

This lesson is presented not as a minor implementation detail, but as the core reason GRPO becomes unstable at scale. GRPO attaches a response-level advantage to each token while still weighting each token with its own token-level importance ratio. The paper argues that this combination is fundamentally ill-posed when responses are long and models are large. As training proceeds, the small discrepancies at the token level accumulate across long sequences, and the clipping mechanism can amplify rather than tame the resulting noise. GSPO is the paper’s answer to that problem: if the reward is for the whole response, then the importance correction and clipping should also be for the whole response.

# Why sequence-level importance correction is more principled in this setting

The paper motivates sequence-level importance correction using the basic principle of importance sampling. Importance sampling is meant to estimate an expectation under a target distribution by reweighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution. In the paper’s notation, a response $y$ sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ should be corrected by the ratio $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)$. This is the ratio that directly compares how likely the entire sampled response is under the current policy versus the behavior policy. Because the response is the sampled object, the paper argues that this is the principled unit of correction.

The contrast with GRPO is sharp. GRPO uses token-level ratios of the form $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$, but each token is only one sample from a next-token distribution. The paper’s key critique is that this does not fulfill the corrective role that importance sampling is supposed to play. Instead of properly aligning distributions, it introduces high-variance noise into the gradient estimate. This matters even more as response length grows, because the noise compounds over many tokens. GSPO’s sequence-level ratio is therefore more principled not only because it matches the reward level, but also because it avoids treating single-token fluctuations as if they were sufficient to correct a full-sequence mismatch.

The paper also emphasizes that sequence-level correction is especially meaningful when combined with sequence-level clipping. Clipping is meant to exclude overly off-policy samples from gradient estimation. If the object being updated is the whole response, then clipping the whole response is the consistent choice. GSPO also uses length normalization in the sequence ratio, namely the exponent $1/|y_i|$, to reduce variance and keep ratios in a common numerical range across responses of different lengths. Without this normalization, a few token-level likelihood changes could create extreme sequence ratios, and longer or shorter responses would not be comparable under the same clipping rule.

# Summary of GSPO's advantages over GRPO

The paper presents GSPO as a direct improvement over GRPO in stability, efficiency, and performance. Empirically, GSPO trains stably across the paper’s experiments, while GRPO is described as vulnerable to severe instability and even catastrophic model collapse. GSPO also achieves better training efficiency, meaning that it obtains stronger performance improvements under the same training compute and the same consumed queries. On the benchmark side, the paper reports stronger curves on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces.

A major practical advantage is that GSPO appears to continue improving as training compute increases, the query set is refreshed, and generation length is extended. That is important because the paper’s broader motivation is to support scaled RL training for large language models, especially on difficult tasks that require deeper and longer reasoning. Another notable advantage is that GSPO stabilizes RL training for large Mixture-of-Experts models. In the paper’s discussion of MoE systems, GRPO needed an additional Routing Replay strategy to converge normally, but GSPO removed that need. That is not a small convenience: it means the algorithm itself is more robust to the dynamic expert-routing behavior that makes MoE training difficult.

The gradient analysis in the paper helps explain why these advantages arise. GRPO assigns different token-level weights within a response, and those weights can vary across tokens and across training steps in ways that are not negligible. GSPO instead weights all tokens in a response equally through the same sequence-level ratio. This removes one instability source: token-level gradient weights no longer fluctuate independently within the same response. The paper’s experiments on clipping fractions reinforce this picture. Even though GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens, it still trains more efficiently, which the paper interprets as evidence that GRPO’s token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and less effective for sample exploitation.

# How GSPO changes the design space for LLM RL algorithms

GSPO changes how one thinks about RL infrastructure for language models. The paper’s argument is not simply that a different objective performs better, but that the entire design space becomes simpler when the optimization is organized around sequences rather than tokens. In the paper’s discussion of infrastructure, this shows up in the observation that sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision discrepancies between training engines and inference engines. Because of that, GSPO can potentially use likelihoods returned by the inference engine directly, rather than requiring recomputation with the training engine. The paper highlights this as especially useful in partial rollout, multi-turn RL, and training-inference disaggregated frameworks.

The same simplification appears in the MoE setting. GRPO’s token-level importance ratios are sensitive to expert-activation volatility, because after a gradient update the activated experts for the same response can change substantially. The paper reports that in a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, roughly $10\%$ of the experts activated under the new policy differed from those under the old policy after each update for the same rollout sample. That kind of change undermines the validity of token-level importance ratios. GSPO sidesteps the problem by using the sequence likelihood, which the paper argues is much less sensitive to such routing fluctuations. As a result, GSPO expands the design space for RL algorithms by making stable training possible without elaborate workarounds like Routing Replay.

# Potential limitations and unanswered theoretical questions

Although the paper provides strong empirical evidence for GSPO, it does not present a complete theoretical account of every aspect of the algorithm. The main stability claims are grounded in experiments and gradient intuition, but the paper does not offer a full convergence theory or a formal characterization of exactly when sequence-level clipping is sufficient to prevent collapse. The paper’s central claim is that the sequence-level formulation is better aligned with importance sampling and with sequence-level reward, and the experimental results support that claim, but many details of the stability mechanism remain open in a broader theoretical sense.

Another limitation is that the algorithm’s practical behavior still depends on design choices such as the clipping range and the sequence-length normalization. The paper notes that the clipping ranges for GSPO and prior algorithms typically differ by orders of magnitude because the importance ratios themselves are defined differently. That means one cannot simply transplant a clipping setting from GRPO to GSPO. The paper shows careful tuning in its experiments, but it does not provide a universal rule for choosing clipping thresholds across different tasks, response lengths, or model scales. Likewise, length normalization is motivated and empirically useful, but the paper does not claim that the specific normalization used in GSPO is uniquely optimal.

A further open issue is the tradeoff between simplicity and granularity. GSPO deliberately works at the sequence level, which is a strength when the reward is sequence-level. But the paper also introduces GSPO-token to support finer-grained advantage customization in settings like multi-turn RL. This suggests a limitation of the plain sequence-level objective: some tasks may benefit from token-wise adjustment even if the correction itself is sequence-level. In other words, GSPO solves a major mismatch, but it does not eliminate the need to think carefully about how advantage information should be distributed within a response.

# Open questions on variance, bias, and clipping design

The paper makes a strong case that token-level importance ratios in GRPO create high-variance noise, while sequence-level ratios in GSPO are more stable. What remains open is a more detailed understanding of the variance behavior under different conditions. The paper emphasizes that length normalization reduces variance and keeps the ratio in a unified numerical range, but it does not fully map out how variance scales with response length, model size, or the structure of the generated text. Because the sequence likelihood is a product over tokens, even the normalized ratio can still be sensitive to many small changes, and the paper leaves open how this sensitivity should be best managed in general.

Clipping is another place where bias and variance considerations matter, even though the paper focuses mainly on empirical stability. The whole purpose of clipping is to exclude overly off-policy samples, but doing so inevitably changes which samples contribute to the gradient. The paper shows that GSPO’s clipping works well in practice and that the fraction of clipped tokens differs dramatically from GRPO, yet it does not provide a formal study of how clipping shapes the bias-variance tradeoff. That makes clipping design a clear area for future work. The key question is not just how much to clip, but how to choose the clipping rule so that it remains compatible with the sequence-level interpretation of the objective.

The paper also implies a second open question: how should one think about the scale of clipping when the definition of the importance ratio itself changes? Because GSPO’s ratio is based on sequence likelihood rather than token likelihood, the numeric scale of the ratio changes substantially. The paper observes that the corresponding clipping ranges are therefore very different from those used in GRPO. This means that future research may need to develop more principled guidance for clipping design that takes the unit of optimization into account.

# Possible extensions to richer reward structures and multi-turn interaction

The paper explicitly points to multi-turn RL as a setting where more fine-grained advantage adjustment may be useful. For that purpose, it introduces GSPO-token, a token-level objective variant that preserves the sequence-level importance ratio while allowing token-wise advantage customization. Importantly, the paper shows that when all token advantages are set equal, GSPO-token is numerically identical to GSPO in the optimization objective, clipping condition, and theoretical gradient. This is a useful design property: the framework can support richer reward structures without abandoning the sequence-level correction principle that makes GSPO stable.

This suggests a natural path for future work inside the paper’s framework. If a task provides rewards or preferences at a finer granularity than the whole response, one can ask how to assign token-level or turn-level advantages while still maintaining the stability gains of sequence-level weighting. The paper does not fully develop such extensions, but it clearly identifies the need for them in scenarios like multi-turn RL. The challenge is to preserve the core GSPO insight, namely that the importance ratio should remain sequence-based even when the advantage signal becomes more detailed.

Another possible direction is to generalize the group-based advantage computation itself. In the paper, the advantage is built from normalized rewards across multiple responses to the same query. That construction is central to both GRPO and GSPO because it removes the need for a value model. Future work could explore how different reward structures interact with this group normalization while still preserving the stability benefits of GSPO. The paper does not work out these possibilities, but it makes clear that richer interaction structures will likely require matching refinements in the advantage signal rather than a return to token-level importance correction.

# Broader implications for scaling RL in large language models

The broader message of the paper is that scaling RL for language models is not only a matter of more compute or bigger models. It also requires algorithmic choices that respect the structure of generation and reward. GSPO is presented as evidence that sequence-level optimization can support stable large-scale RL, especially when responses are long, models are sparse, and training is off-policy across mini-batches. In that sense, the paper argues that sequence-level policy optimization can become a foundational building block for the next stage of language-model training.

The practical significance is reinforced by the paper’s discussion of Qwen3. GSPO is described as having contributed to the latest Qwen3 models, and the paper frames this as evidence that stable RL algorithms can directly enable performance gains in frontier systems. The architectural simplifications are also important: GSPO can reduce dependence on auxiliary stabilization methods like Routing Replay and may simplify the interface between training and inference systems. That matters for future RL infrastructure because it suggests that some of the complexity in current pipelines is not inevitable, but rather a consequence of using an optimization objective that is not well matched to the reward structure.

Taken together, the paper’s conclusion is that large-scale RL for language models should be built on a principled relationship between reward, importance correction, and optimization level. GSPO is the paper’s concrete demonstration of that idea. It does not settle every theoretical question, but it shows that when the optimization unit matches the reward unit, stable and scalable training becomes far more plausible. For future research, that is the main lesson to carry forward.
