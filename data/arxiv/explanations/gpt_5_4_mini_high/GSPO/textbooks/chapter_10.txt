\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 10: Clipping Behavior and What It Reveals

# Sequence-level clipping versus token-level clipping

The paper’s key change from GRPO to GSPO is not merely a different clipping threshold, but a different unit of clipping. In GRPO, clipping is applied at the token level through the token importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}$. Each token in a response is treated as its own optimization site, and each token can be independently clipped if its ratio moves outside the interval $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$. In GSPO, by contrast, the importance ratio is defined on the entire response as $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$, and clipping is performed on that response-level quantity.

This difference matters because the paper ties the optimization unit to the reward unit. The verifier reward $r(x,y)$ is assigned to the whole query-response pair, so GSPO’s response-level clipping matches the way the reward is defined. In that sense, GSPO does not try to make a token-by-token correction for an inherently sequence-level evaluation signal. Instead, it asks a simpler question: is this whole response sufficiently close to the old policy to be trusted for gradient estimation? If not, the entire response is clipped.

The paper emphasizes that this is not just a stylistic difference. It is the consequence of using a sequence-level importance ratio that has a clear interpretation, namely how far a sampled response has moved from the old policy. Because this ratio is computed from the full sequence likelihood, the clipping mechanism naturally operates at the same level as the reward and the policy update.

# Observed differences in clipping fractions across the two algorithms

The most striking empirical observation in the paper is that GSPO clips far more tokens than GRPO. In the reported experiments, the fraction of clipped tokens differs by roughly two orders of magnitude between the two methods. Importantly, the paper notes that changing the clipping ranges does not remove this large gap. So the difference is not just a consequence of one algorithm using a tighter threshold than the other. It reflects the structure of the objectives themselves.

This is surprising at first glance, because one might expect a method that clips more to be discarding more training signal and therefore to learn less efficiently. The paper shows the opposite: GSPO, despite clipping many more tokens, still achieves better training efficiency and better benchmark performance than GRPO under the same training compute and consumed queries.

The fact that the clipping-fraction gap remains even after adjusting the clipping ranges is part of the point. It suggests that what is being counted as “clipped” is not merely a threshold artifact. Rather, GSPO and GRPO are responding differently to the same off-policy data because their importance ratios are defined differently. A token-level ratio and a sequence-level ratio do not produce the same clipping statistics, even when both are tuned carefully.

# Why GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens indirectly through response rejection

GSPO clips whole responses, so a single sequence-level decision can affect every token in that response. If the response-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ lies outside the clipping region, then the response’s contribution is clipped as a unit. When clipping statistics are measured in terms of tokens, this means that an entire response can show up as a large block of clipped tokens. This is the sense in which GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens indirectly.

The paper frames this behavior as a feature rather than a flaw. GSPO is designed to exclude overly off-policy samples from gradient estimation at the response level. Because the reward is attached to the entire response, once the response-level likelihood has drifted too far, the whole sequence is treated as less trustworthy for learning. This is consistent with the paper’s central principle that the optimization unit should match the reward unit.

There is also an important numerical aspect. The paper notes that the clipping ranges in GSPO and prior algorithms such as GRPO typically differ in order of magnitude because the importance ratios themselves are defined differently. Since the sequence-level ratio averages log-likelihood changes over the whole response, it naturally lives on a different scale from a token-level ratio. So GSPO’s apparently more aggressive clipping cannot be compared to GRPO’s clipping by looking only at raw threshold values.

# The counter-intuitive link between more clipping and better efficiency

One of the paper’s most interesting claims is that clipping more can actually improve training efficiency. In the reported experiments, GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens, which means fewer tokens are available for gradient estimation. Yet GSPO still produces better training accuracy and better benchmark performance than GRPO under the same compute budget.

The paper interprets this counter-intuitive result as evidence that not all training signal is equally useful. If a large fraction of the gradient contributions are coming from samples that are too far off-policy, then keeping them in the optimization step may add more noise than value. GSPO’s stronger clipping behavior seems to remove those unreliable contributions more effectively, leaving a learning signal that is more trustworthy even if it is smaller in raw quantity.

This is why the paper argues that higher clipping is not inherently bad. What matters is whether clipping is filtering out harmful gradient estimates or unnecessarily throwing away useful ones. GSPO’s result suggests that, at least for long-response language model RL in this setting, response-level clipping does a better job of selecting the parts of the rollout batch that are worth learning from.

# What this suggests about noise in GRPO's token-level gradients

The paper uses the clipping statistics to reinforce its broader critique of GRPO. In GRPO, the token-level importance ratio is computed from a single token sample at each position. The paper argues that this does not fulfill the intended role of importance sampling, which is to correct for a mismatch between a target distribution and a behavior distribution using averaging over multiple samples. Because the GRPO weight is applied at each token position rather than at the sequence level, it introduces high-variance noise into the gradients.

The empirical clipping gap is consistent with that diagnosis. If GRPO’s token-level estimates were truly providing clean correction, then one would expect the clipping behavior to translate into effective learning. Instead, the paper finds that GRPO’s token-level gradients are noisy and inefficient, especially as response length grows. The clipping mechanism does not fix that noise; the paper says it can actually exacerbate instability.

This is the key conceptual reading of the clipping results. GSPO’s willingness to clip many more tokens is not a sign of excessive conservatism. It is evidence that the algorithm is filtering out off-policy behavior at the right level, while GRPO’s smaller clipped fraction does not imply cleaner training. The paper’s position is that GRPO can retain tokens whose individual weights are numerically acceptable but whose cumulative effect still injects instability into the optimization process.

# Why fewer but cleaner gradients can outperform more but noisier gradients

The paper’s results show that sample count alone is not a good measure of training quality. GSPO may use fewer tokens for gradient estimation because more of them are clipped, but the resulting gradients are more effective. That is why the method can outperform GRPO in both training efficiency and final performance.

The reason is that a gradient estimate is only valuable if it points in a useful direction. In the paper’s view, token-level GRPO gradients are polluted by weight fluctuations that accumulate over long sequences. A token may contribute to the update simply because its ratio happens to fall within the clipping band, not because it is a particularly reliable learning signal. By contrast, GSPO’s sequence-level clipping and sequence-level importance ratio make the update depend on a more coherent measure of how the whole response compares to the old policy.

The paper’s experimental conclusion is therefore not that clipping is good in general, but that cleaner clipping can beat noisier inclusion. Even though GSPO excludes more of the rollout tokens from gradient estimation, the remaining signal is more aligned with the reward and more stable across training. In the paper’s terminology, it is a more reliable and effective learning signal.

# How clipping statistics can be used diagnostically in RL training

The paper’s clipping analysis does more than explain GSPO’s performance. It also shows that clipping statistics can serve as a diagnostic for the quality of the RL objective. If a method clips a large fraction of tokens and still learns well, that suggests the clipped samples were not the source of useful gradient information. If a method clips less but performs worse, that suggests the retained gradients may be noisy or misaligned with the reward.

This is especially important because the paper observes that the difference in clipped-token fractions between GSPO and GRPO remains large even after tuning the clipping ranges. That persistence tells us the discrepancy is structural. It reflects how the algorithms define importance ratios, how they distribute reward across tokens or sequences, and how they handle off-policy data. In that sense, clipping statistics are not just bookkeeping; they reveal whether the learning signal is being measured at the right level.

The paper also uses these statistics to support a broader diagnosis of GRPO. Since token-level importance ratios can fail to correct distribution mismatch and can accumulate noise over long responses, a pattern of clipping behavior that looks modest may still hide unstable optimization. GSPO’s sequence-level clipping makes the problem visible in a different way: it sharply filters entire responses, and the resulting statistics help show that the method is learning from a more coherent subset of the data. For this reason, clipping behavior is not merely a side detail of the algorithm. In the paper, it is one of the clearest windows into whether the RL update is stable, efficient, and properly aligned with the reward.
