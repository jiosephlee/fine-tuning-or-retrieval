\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 8: Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol

# The cold-start model based on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base

The paper evaluates GSPO starting from a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base. This matters because the goal of the experiments is not to test GSPO in isolation on a toy setting, but to examine whether it can reliably support large-scale reinforcement learning for a strong language model. By beginning from a relatively capable base model, the evaluation focuses on the stability and efficiency of the reinforcement learning stage itself, which is exactly where the paper argues GRPO becomes fragile.

The phrase “cold-start” indicates that the model is not trained from scratch during the reported RL runs. Instead, it begins from a pretrained foundation and is then optimized further with the RL procedure under study. That setup is important for the paper’s claims about training dynamics: the central question is whether GSPO can continue improving an already competent model without the collapse behavior that the authors associate with GRPO. In other words, the base model gives the reinforcement learning algorithm a realistic and demanding starting point, where instability would be especially costly.

This choice of starting point also aligns with the paper’s emphasis on scaling. The model under study is not a small experimental proxy, but a large model from the Qwen3 family. The authors use this setting to show that their method is relevant for the regime where response lengths are long, compute budgets are large, and training stability becomes a decisive issue.

# Rollout generation and four-way mini-batch partitioning

The training procedure described in the paper generates rollout data and then partitions each batch of rollout data into four mini-batches for gradient updates. This detail is important because it reveals how the optimization is actually carried out in practice. The model first produces responses under an older policy, and then those generated responses are reused across multiple updates rather than discarded immediately. This makes the training more sample-efficient, but it also creates the central off-policy setting that motivates the paper.

Partitioning a rollout batch into mini-batches is a standard practical strategy for improving hardware utilization and making large RL runs tractable. The paper emphasizes that this is not a cosmetic choice: once the same sampled responses are used across several gradient steps, the policy being optimized is no longer exactly the policy that produced the samples. That mismatch is a core reason why importance ratios and clipping matter so much.

The paper’s comparison is therefore not just about an abstract objective function. It is about what happens when the objective is used in a realistic training loop with batched rollouts and repeated updates. The four-way partitioning is part of that realism, and it helps explain why the authors frame the problem in terms of training stability rather than only final benchmark performance. A method that looks acceptable on paper can still fail when its gradients are repeatedly applied to stale samples; the experimental design is meant to expose that behavior.

# Why the training regime is naturally off-policy

The paper argues that the RL regime is naturally off-policy because responses are generated by an old policy, denoted $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, while gradient updates are applied to the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$. This mismatch is not accidental; it is inherent in the way rollout-based RL is run at scale. A large batch of samples is collected first, then reused for optimization. Once optimization begins, the policy parameters change, but the samples do not. As a result, the data being used for learning increasingly reflects an earlier version of the model.

The paper connects this directly to the need for clipping. Clipping is meant to limit the influence of samples that have become too “off-policy” relative to the current model. In the authors’ view, the core issue is not merely that the samples are stale, but that the old and current policies can drift enough during optimization that naïvely trusting token-level ratios becomes unstable. This is why the experimental setting is important: it creates the exact environment in which the difference between token-level and sequence-level importance ratios becomes consequential.

The off-policy nature is especially pronounced when responses are long or when the model is large and sparse, because then even modest parameter updates can alter the probability structure of later tokens or the routing behavior in MoE models. The paper uses this to motivate GSPO’s sequence-level formulation, but the experimental protocol itself already reflects that reality. The data are not collected from a stationary policy; they are repeatedly reused under a changing one.

# Benchmarks: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces

The paper evaluates GSPO on three benchmarks: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces. These benchmarks are chosen to probe the types of capabilities that large-scale RL is meant to strengthen, especially mathematical reasoning and programming. Their inclusion is consistent with the paper’s broader narrative that scaling RL should improve performance on tasks requiring deep reasoning rather than only superficial pattern matching.

AIME'24 is reported using average Pass@1 over 32 samplings. This means the model is evaluated multiple times per question, and the metric reflects the chance that the top sampled answer is correct under repeated sampling. The use of many samplings indicates that the evaluation is not based on a single deterministic completion. Instead, it measures how often the model can surface a correct solution when given repeated opportunities, which is appropriate for reasoning tasks where sampling can expose latent capability.

LiveCodeBench, covering the 202410-202502 range, is reported using average Pass@1 over 8 samplings. Like the AIME evaluation, this metric focuses on whether the model can produce a correct first answer among sampled completions. Because LiveCodeBench targets programming tasks, it offers a complementary view of model improvement. The paper uses it to show that GSPO’s benefits are not limited to one type of reasoning problem.

CodeForces is evaluated using Elo rating. Unlike Pass@1, Elo is not a simple per-problem success rate. It is a relative performance measure that summarizes competitive strength across tasks and opponents in a ranking-like framework. The paper includes this benchmark to show that GSPO’s gains can translate into a more global measure of programming performance. Together, these three benchmarks cover both mathematical and coding ability, giving the evaluation a broader basis than a single task family would provide.

# Evaluation metrics: Pass@1 and Elo rating

The two main metrics in the paper are Pass@1 and Elo rating, and they serve different purposes. Pass@1 is a direct measure of whether a model’s first sampled solution is correct. Because the evaluations use multiple samplings, the reported Pass@1 is averaged over those samples. This metric is especially intuitive for reasoning tasks: if a model can often generate one correct answer among several attempts, then it has likely learned something genuinely useful during RL training.

Elo rating serves a different role. It is used for CodeForces, where relative performance against a pool of competitors or problem-solving strength is more informative than a raw accuracy-style measure. Elo compresses a range of outcomes into a single score that reflects broader competitive skill. In the context of the paper, this helps demonstrate that GSPO is not merely improving isolated sample correctness, but supporting stronger end-to-end coding performance.

The use of both metrics is important because the paper’s claims are about training efficiency and final capability. Pass@1 can reveal how training changes the model’s probability of producing correct responses, while Elo can reflect whether those gains matter in a more competitive programming setting. By reporting both, the evaluation avoids relying on a single perspective.

# Clipping ranges used for GSPO and GRPO

A key part of the experimental protocol is that the clipping ranges are set differently for GSPO and GRPO. For GSPO, the left and right clipping ranges are set to $3\times 10^{-4}$ and $4\times 10^{-4}$, respectively. For GRPO, the corresponding ranges are set to $0.2$ and $0.27$. The paper notes that these values were carefully tuned to ensure a fair comparison.

The fact that the ranges differ by orders of magnitude is not presented as an accident, but as a consequence of the different definitions of importance ratio in the two methods. GSPO uses sequence-level likelihood ratios, so the numerical scale of its ratio is different from the token-level ratios used in GRPO. Therefore, the clipping values must be chosen on the scale appropriate to each algorithm. Comparing them fairly requires tuning each method within its own natural range rather than forcing the same raw numbers onto both.

This detail is central to understanding the paper’s evaluation philosophy. The authors are not claiming superiority by using a weak baseline setting for GRPO. Instead, they state that GRPO’s clipping parameters were tuned carefully, and the difference in performance remains. That framing strengthens the experimental claim that GSPO is not just benefitting from a favorable hyperparameter choice, but from a better-matched objective.

# Fairness considerations in hyperparameter tuning

The paper is explicit that the comparison with GRPO is intended to be fair. The clipping ranges for GRPO were carefully tuned, and the same overall training compute and consumed queries are used in the comparison. This matters because RL training can be very sensitive to hyperparameters, especially clipping. A poorly tuned baseline could collapse simply because the chosen settings are bad, which would not be a meaningful comparison.

The authors also emphasize that GSPO achieves better training accuracy and benchmark performance under the same training compute and consumed queries. That is a strong fairness criterion because it compares methods at equal resource budgets rather than allowing one method more data or more optimization steps. In this way, the evaluation focuses on sample efficiency and optimization quality, not just final score after unlimited training.

Another fairness-related point is that the paper contrasts GSPO with GRPO in the same practical training regime, including rollout generation and repeated mini-batch updates. The result is meant to show that GSPO is robust in the very setting where GRPO struggles, not only in a simplified or idealized setup. The tuning and resource matching together support the paper’s claim that GSPO genuinely improves the RL process.

# The role of query refresh and generation-length extension during training

The paper reports that GSPO can deliver continuous performance improvement through increasing training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending the generation length. These choices are part of the experimental story because they probe whether the algorithm can keep benefiting from additional training rather than saturating or destabilizing.

Regularly updating the query set, or query refresh, means that training does not remain fixed on an unchanging collection of prompts. Instead, the model is exposed to new queries over time. This is important because it tests whether the optimization method can generalize across a shifting set of problems and continue extracting useful learning signals as training proceeds. The paper presents GSPO as capable of handling this regime stably.

Extending generation length is equally significant. Longer generations increase the difficulty of RL because they enlarge the window over which instability can accumulate. The paper explicitly links long responses with the problems of token-level importance ratios and with the need for a better sequence-level method. In the experiments, GSPO is described as continuing to improve when generation length is extended, which supports the authors’ argument that its sequence-level formulation is better suited for long-form reasoning training.

Taken together, query refresh and generation-length extension are not incidental training tricks. They are part of the protocol used to show that GSPO scales in the way a successful large-model RL algorithm should: it remains stable, keeps improving with more compute, and continues to work as the task distribution and response length become more demanding.
