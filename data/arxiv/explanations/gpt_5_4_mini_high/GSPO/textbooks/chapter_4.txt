\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 4: Why GRPO Becomes Unstable

# The Mismatch Between Reward Granularity and Optimization Granularity

The paper’s central criticism of GRPO starts from a simple but decisive observation: the reward is defined for an entire response, while GRPO optimizes the policy through token-level importance ratios. In the paper’s notation, a query $x$ receives a response $y$, and a verifier assigns a sequence-level reward $r(x,y) \in [0,1]$. That reward is attached to the whole query-response pair, not to individual tokens. GRPO, however, still breaks the response into token positions and applies a separate importance ratio to each token during optimization.

This mismatch matters because the algorithm is trying to correct for off-policy data at a finer granularity than the signal it is trying to learn from. The paper argues that this is not just a small implementation detail. It is the root of the instability problem. When the objective is built from token-level corrections but the reward exists only at the response level, the optimization signal is no longer naturally aligned with what was actually evaluated.

# Why GRPO Uses a Single Response-Level Advantage for Every Token

GRPO avoids a value model by using a group of responses to the same query and turning their relative rewards into an advantage. If a query $x$ produces $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$, then GRPO defines the advantage of response $y_i$ as

$$
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}.
$$

The key point is that this is a response-level advantage. Every token in the response $y_i$ inherits the same value $\widehat{A}_i$. In the paper’s notation, $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$ for all token positions $t$.

This design is attractive because it removes the need for a separate value model, which would otherwise add considerable memory and compute cost. But it also means that token-level learning is driven by a single scalar reward signal that was computed for the whole response. The only thing that differentiates one token from another in the gradient is the token-level importance ratio. That makes the correctness of those ratios absolutely central to the stability of the method.

# The Paper’s Claim: Token-Level Importance Ratios Are Misapplied

The paper argues that GRPO misapplies the basic idea of importance sampling. In importance sampling, one wants to estimate an expectation under a target distribution by reweighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution. The paper states the principle as

$$
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}}\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z)\right].
$$

The important thing here is that the ratio is a ratio of distributions, and the paper emphasizes that this correction becomes meaningful when one averages over many samples, with $N \gg 1$, from the behavior distribution. The ratio is supposed to correct a distributional mismatch, not simply rescale a single noisy sample.

GRPO instead uses the token-level ratio

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}.
$$

According to the paper, this is problematic because each token is only one sampled realization from the next-token distribution at that position. That single sample is not enough for the importance weight to play its intended distribution-correction role. Rather than producing a principled correction, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient estimate.

# Importance Sampling Over Distributions Versus Single Sampled Tokens

The paper’s sequence-level viewpoint makes the flaw clearer. For a full response $y$, the likelihood under a policy $\pi_\theta$ is

$$
\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
$$

This is the natural object to compare when the reward is attached to the entire response. A sequence-level ratio compares how probable the whole response is under the current policy versus the old policy, which makes direct sense as a measure of distribution shift. The paper explicitly says that the sequence-level importance weight has a clear theoretical meaning because it reflects how far the sampled response deviates from the current policy.

By contrast, a token-level ratio compares only one sampled token at a particular position. The paper’s critique is not that token probabilities are useless in general, but that using them as importance weights for response-level reinforcement learning is ill-posed. The ratio is being asked to do a job it was not designed to do. That is why the paper argues that the sequence, not the token, should be the unit of correction and optimization.

# How Token-Level Noise Accumulates Over Long Responses

The instability in GRPO becomes worse as responses get longer because the gradient contains many token-level terms, each carrying its own noisy importance ratio. The paper’s gradient expression for GRPO shows this accumulation explicitly:

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i
\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
$$

The paper’s argument is that these token-wise weights are not negligible. They can vary in ways that matter, and their effects do not cancel cleanly. Instead, as the response length grows, the noisy deviations accumulate across more and more token positions. The result is training noise that becomes progressively more severe with longer generations.

This is why the paper emphasizes that the problem is not merely statistical in the ordinary sense. It is structural. A method that applies an unreliable importance ratio at every token in a long sequence is exposed to repeated noise injection at exactly the scale where the reward signal is most coarse.

# How Clipping Can Amplify Rather Than Fix Instability

Clipping is meant to make policy updates safer by excluding samples that are too far off-policy. In PPO and GRPO, the clipped objective limits the effect of large importance ratios so that training remains within a proximal region of the old policy. But the paper argues that clipping cannot repair a fundamentally misapplied importance weight.

The reason is that clipping acts on the same token-level ratios that are already producing high-variance noise. If the underlying ratio does not correctly represent a useful distribution correction, then clipping only truncates the noise rather than turning it into a reliable signal. The paper goes further and states that the noise introduced by the token-level weights is exacerbated by the clipping mechanism, eventually contributing to model collapse.

The paper also reports that once collapse occurs, simple remedies are ineffective. Reverting to an earlier checkpoint, tuning clipping ranges, extending the generation length, or changing the RL queries does not reliably restore training. This is an important part of the diagnosis: clipping is not just insufficient, but in the paper’s empirical observations it does not prevent the failure mode that token-level importance ratios create.

# Why Instability Becomes Worse with Larger Models and Longer Generations

The paper argues that the instability is especially acute when model size, sparsity, and response length increase. Larger models and longer responses require large rollout batch sizes to keep hardware utilization high during RL, and those large batches are commonly partitioned into mini-batches for gradient updates. That means training is already happening in an off-policy setting, because the samples come from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ while the parameters being optimized are $\theta$.

This problem becomes even more pronounced in Mixture-of-Experts models. The paper reports expert-activation volatility: after one or more gradient updates, the experts activated for the same response can change significantly under the new policy. For the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, the paper observes that roughly $10\%$ of the experts activated under $\pi_\theta$ differ from those under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for the same rollout sample after each update. In deeper MoE models, this effect becomes even more prominent.

That volatility makes the token-level importance ratios fluctuate drastically, further invalidating them. In the paper’s discussion, this is one reason why GRPO training of MoE models required an extra stabilization method called Routing Replay. Even then, the need for such a workaround is treated as evidence that the original token-level design is fragile.

# Empirical Symptoms: Collapse, Irreversibility, and Failed Recovery Attempts

The paper describes GRPO instability in very strong terms. The failure is not just a slowdown or a modest loss of performance. It can lead to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. Once collapse happens, the training run is often unrecoverable.

The paper emphasizes that recovery attempts do not reliably work. Even if one returns to a previous checkpoint and carefully retunes hyperparameters such as clipping ranges, the collapsed training dynamics do not necessarily recover. The same is true if one changes the generation length or switches the RL queries. This irreversibility is one of the most persuasive empirical signs that the instability is not a minor tuning issue but a deeper algorithmic flaw.

The paper’s experiments are presented against this background. GRPO can fail in exactly the regimes where large-scale RL is supposed to help most: larger models, longer reasoning trajectories, and more difficult tasks. That makes the instability especially damaging, because it blocks the very scaling behavior RL is supposed to enable.

# The Design Principle: The Optimization Unit Should Match the Reward Unit

The paper’s diagnosis culminates in a simple design principle: the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. If the reward is granted to the entire response, then the correction and optimization should also be carried out at the response level. That is why the paper treats sequence-level importance weights as the correct object for RL training in this setting.

This principle explains the motivation for GSPO, which replaces token-level importance ratios with a sequence-level ratio based on the likelihood of the full response. The paper presents this not as a cosmetic change, but as a way to restore alignment between reward, importance weighting, and optimization. In the paper’s view, that alignment is what GRPO lacks, and that lack is what makes GRPO unstable.

So the core lesson is not merely that GRPO uses the wrong clipping range or that it needs a better tuning recipe. The deeper issue is that it optimizes tokens while evaluating responses. Once the reward unit and the optimization unit diverge, the gradient becomes noisy, the noise accumulates with sequence length, and the training process can collapse.
