\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 1: Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models

# Why post-training with RL matters for modern language models

Reinforcement learning has become a pivotal paradigm for scaling language models. In the framing of this paper, RL is not a minor training tweak but a central mechanism for pushing model capability beyond what is achieved by the initial model alone. The reason is that language models can be treated as policies, meaning they produce responses in a way that can be optimized using reward signals. Once this optimization is applied at scale, the model can improve at tasks that require more than shallow pattern matching.

The paper emphasizes that large-scale RL enables language models to tackle sophisticated problems by undertaking deeper and longer reasoning processes. This is important because many of the most valuable language-model applications are not single-step predictions, but responses that require sustained coherence across many tokens. RL gives the training process a way to reward entire responses, rather than only local next-token behavior, so the model can be pushed toward outputs that satisfy a verifier over the full query-response pair.

A useful way to think about the paper’s setup is that a query $x$ is given to the model, the model produces a response $y$, and a verifier assigns a reward $r(x,y)$ in $[0,1]$. RL then becomes the mechanism for adjusting the policy $\pi_\theta$ so that responses with higher reward become more likely. In this sense, RL is the part of the training pipeline that connects language generation to explicit task success.

# RL-driven gains in math, coding, and long-form reasoning

The paper specifically points to competition-level mathematics and programming as examples of tasks that become more tractable through large-scale RL. These are not simple one-step classification problems; they demand extended reasoning, intermediate deductions, and the ability to keep track of structure over a long response. The paper’s claim is that RL helps models learn to carry out these longer reasoning trajectories more effectively.

This emphasis on reasoning length matters because the model is not merely producing an answer token by token in isolation. For a difficult math problem or a programming task, the response often needs to unfold through many steps before arriving at a correct conclusion. RL is presented as a way to improve these multi-step behaviors by rewarding the quality of the full sequence, not just individual next-token choices.

The paper later evaluates this idea using AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces. These benchmarks serve as concrete examples of the kinds of reasoning-heavy tasks the paper associates with RL scaling. The broader point is that RL is tied to improvements in domains where deeper reasoning and longer responses are essential.

# The scaling perspective: compute, model size, and long rollouts

The paper’s motivation is closely tied to scaling. As model size grows, as sparsity increases in Mixture-of-Experts models, and as response length increases, RL training requires a larger rollout batch size in order to keep hardware utilization high. This is a practical scaling issue: if you want to train a very large model efficiently, you need to generate enough data per iteration to make the computation worthwhile.

At the same time, large rollout batches are usually split into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates to improve sample efficiency. This creates an off-policy learning setting, because the responses were sampled from an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ rather than the current policy $\pi_\theta$ that is being optimized. Once data becomes off-policy, the training algorithm must decide how to account for the mismatch between the behavior policy that generated the data and the current policy that is being updated.

This is why clipping appears in PPO-style methods and in GRPO. Clipping is meant to stop overly off-policy samples from dominating the gradient. In other words, the algorithm tries to protect training from updates that are too far from the policy that produced the data. The paper treats this as a necessary practical safeguard, but also argues that clipping alone does not solve the deeper instability problem in GRPO.

The empirical results reinforce the scaling story. The paper reports that GSPO can continue improving as training compute increases, the query set is updated, and generation length is extended. That is an important signal: the method is designed not merely to work at a fixed scale, but to keep benefiting as more training resources are devoted to it.

# Training stability as the main bottleneck

The paper argues that stability is the first requirement for scaling RL successfully. Before one can hope to get the benefits of more compute or larger models, the training dynamics must remain robust. If training becomes unstable, then extra compute does not help; it simply accelerates failure. For that reason, stability is presented as the main bottleneck in large-scale RL.

The paper contrasts two major algorithms in this respect. PPO uses a value model, but that model is expensive and difficult to scale, especially as responses become longer and tasks become more complex. GRPO avoids the value model by using group-relative advantages, which removes one source of burden. However, the paper argues that GRPO introduces a more serious problem: its objective is ill-posed because of how it uses importance sampling weights.

This is the key conceptual issue. In the paper’s view, GRPO does not simply have a tuning problem or a small numerical instability. Rather, its design misuses importance weights in a way that makes high-variance noise accumulate during training. That noise becomes more severe as responses get longer, and the clipping mechanism can amplify the problem instead of resolving it. So the training pipeline becomes increasingly fragile exactly when the model and tasks are becoming more ambitious.

# Catastrophic model collapse in existing RL algorithms

The paper uses strong language to describe what can happen under GRPO: catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. The collapse is not treated as a temporary dip in performance that can be recovered by more training. Instead, once it happens, the model may not recover even if training is restarted from an earlier checkpoint. The paper says that resuming training is unavailing even when one carefully changes clipping ranges, extends generation length, or switches RL queries.

The explanation given is that GRPO applies an importance weight at each token position, but this weight is based on a single token sample from the next-token distribution. Importance sampling, in principle, is meant to correct for differences between a behavior distribution and a target distribution by reweighting samples. The paper emphasizes that this correction depends on averaging over many samples. Applying the weight token by token in the GRPO setting does not perform the intended correction and instead injects noisy gradient estimates.

Because the response is long, that noise can accumulate across many tokens. The paper argues that this accumulation is one reason the instability becomes so severe in large models and long-response tasks. In practice, the result is that the training trajectory can abruptly fail and then remain unrecoverable. This is why the paper treats stability not as a secondary nuisance but as a fundamental obstacle to scaling RL for language models.

# GSPO as the paper's response to GRPO instability

GSPO, or Group Sequence Policy Optimization, is proposed as the paper’s answer to the instability of GRPO. The central idea is simple but important: the importance ratio should be defined at the sequence level, not the token level. Instead of comparing individual token probabilities under $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, GSPO compares the full sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ to $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)$.

This choice is not presented as a cosmetic change. The paper argues that sequence-level importance weighting has a clear theoretical meaning: it measures how far the sampled response $y$ deviates from the old policy’s distribution. Just as importantly, it matches the fact that reward is assigned to the entire response. The paper’s guiding principle is that the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. If the reward is sequence-level, then the correction and optimization should also be sequence-level.

GSPO therefore performs sequence-level clipping, rewarding, and optimization. It also uses group-based advantage estimation, where the reward of each sampled response is normalized relative to the group of responses generated for the same query. This keeps the training signal aligned with the whole response rather than forcing token-by-token weights that the paper views as unstable. In the paper’s analysis, GSPO avoids the unequal token weighting that makes GRPO unpredictable over time.

# What the paper claims GSPO improves

The paper makes three main claims about GSPO: stability, efficiency, and performance. First, GSPO is said to train stably where GRPO can collapse. Second, it is reported to achieve better training efficiency, meaning that it makes better use of the compute and sampled queries available during training. Third, it improves benchmark performance, including on the math and coding evaluations reported in the experiments.

A particularly important claim is that GSPO stabilizes RL training for large Mixture-of-Experts models. The paper explains that MoE models introduce expert-activation volatility: after gradient updates, the set of activated experts for the same response can change substantially. Under GRPO, this makes token-level importance ratios fluctuate badly and can break convergence. Previous workarounds required a Routing Replay strategy, which caches and reuses routing modes to keep the activated experts consistent across updates. GSPO is presented as eliminating the need for this workaround.

This matters because removing Routing Replay simplifies both training and infrastructure. The paper notes that replaying routing modes adds memory and communication overhead and can reduce the effective capacity of the MoE model. GSPO avoids these costs by relying on sequence-level likelihoods, which are less sensitive to token-level routing volatility. In the paper’s view, this is not only a stability improvement but also an infrastructure simplification.

The paper also argues that GSPO is more forgiving of precision discrepancies between training engines and inference engines. Since GSPO uses sequence-level likelihoods rather than token-level likelihoods, it may be possible to use likelihoods returned by the inference engine directly for optimization instead of recomputing them with the training engine. That potential simplification is part of the paper’s broader claim that GSPO can streamline RL systems.

Taken together, the paper’s message is that GSPO provides a more stable foundation for scaling RL in large language models. It is positioned as a theoretically grounded response to the instability of GRPO, and as an algorithm that supports continued gains in compute, response length, and model scale. The paper concludes that these properties helped contribute to the improved results in the latest Qwen3 models.
