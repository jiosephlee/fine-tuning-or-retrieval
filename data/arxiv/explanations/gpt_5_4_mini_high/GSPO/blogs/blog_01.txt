\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# Why AI Training Breaks: The Stability Problem GSPO Tries to Fix

Large language models get better at hard reasoning when we train them with reinforcement learning. That is the basic idea behind systems that learn to solve math, coding, and planning tasks by trying answers, getting rewards, and updating the model toward better responses.

But there is a catch: this kind of training can become unstable fast. In the worst cases, the model does not just get a little worse. It can collapse, and once that happens, recovery can be extremely difficult.

The GSPO paper is about fixing exactly that problem.

## Reinforcement learning for LLMs is powerful, but fragile

In RL training for language models, the model generates several candidate responses to the same query. A verifier or reward function scores each response, and the model is updated to prefer the better ones.

That sounds straightforward, but the training loop is a little messy in practice. To use hardware efficiently, we usually generate a large batch of responses, then split it into smaller mini-batches for optimization. By the time the model is updated on a mini-batch, the responses were actually produced by an older version of the model. So training becomes partly off-policy: we are learning from data that came from a previous policy, not the one we are updating right now.

That is why RL algorithms for LLMs use importance sampling and clipping. The importance weight tries to correct for the mismatch between the old policy and the current one, while clipping limits how far the update can move.

The problem, according to this paper, is that the common GRPO design applies this correction at the token level, and that turns out to be the wrong place.

## What goes wrong in GRPO

GRPO was attractive because it removes the need for a large value model. Instead of estimating a value for each token, it compares multiple responses to the same query and uses the relative reward within the group as the advantage.

But GRPO still uses token-level importance ratios:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

The paper argues that this is a misuse of importance sampling. The ratio is computed from a single sampled token at each position, so it does not really correct the distribution mismatch in a meaningful way. Instead, it injects high-variance noise into the gradients.

Why is that such a big deal? Because language responses are long. When you apply a noisy correction at every token, the noise accumulates over the whole sequence. Longer responses mean more opportunities for those errors to pile up. Clipping does not fully solve this; in fact, the paper argues that clipping can make the instability worse once the underlying estimate is already noisy.

The authors report that this can lead to catastrophic model collapse. Once the model collapses, they found that simply going back to an earlier checkpoint, changing clipping ranges, extending generation length, or switching queries did not reliably recover training.

In short, the issue is not just that GRPO is imperfect. The paper claims the objective itself is ill-posed.

## The key idea behind GSPO: match the reward to the unit of optimization

The paper’s main principle is simple: if the reward is assigned to the whole response, then the optimization should also operate at the whole-response level.

That is the motivation for Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO.

Instead of using token-level importance ratios, GSPO uses a sequence-level ratio based on the likelihood of the entire response:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]

You can think of this as the average log-likelihood change per token, or equivalently a length-normalized sequence score. That length normalization matters because it keeps responses of different lengths in a comparable numerical range and reduces variance.

GSPO still works with groups of responses to the same query. It computes a relative advantage inside the group, just like GRPO does, but it clips and optimizes at the response level instead of the token level. In other words, if a response is too far from the old policy, GSPO excludes that whole response from the gradient estimate rather than trying to salvage it token by token.

That change sounds small, but it is the core fix.

## Why GSPO is more stable in practice

The paper’s gradient analysis makes the difference clear. In GRPO, different tokens in the same response are weighted differently by their token-level ratios. Those weights can drift in unpredictable ways over training.

In GSPO, all tokens in a response contribute equally to the update, because the response-level ratio controls the whole gradient. That removes one source of instability.

The empirical result is that GSPO trains stably where GRPO can struggle. On a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, the authors report steady training, better efficiency, and stronger benchmark performance on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces under the same training budget. They also report that GSPO keeps improving as they increase compute, refresh the query set, and extend generation length.

That last point matters. Long-form reasoning is exactly where RL should help most, but it is also where unstable training hurts most. GSPO is designed to make that scaling less brittle.

## A useful side effect for Mixture-of-Experts models

The paper also highlights an important win for Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, models. These models are sparse: different tokens activate different experts. During GRPO training, the paper found that the set of active experts can change a lot after each update, even for the same response. That makes token-level likelihood ratios even less reliable.

To work around this, the authors previously used a technique called Routing Replay, which reuses the old routing pattern when computing the ratios. That helps stabilize training, but it adds memory and communication overhead.

GSPO removes that dependency. Because it works at the sequence level, it is less sensitive to token-by-token routing changes and can train MoE models stably without that extra workaround.

The paper also notes a potential infrastructure benefit: sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision differences between training and inference engines, so GSPO can simplify some RL systems.

## The takeaway

GSPO is not a completely different RL recipe. It is a correction to the unit of optimization.

GRPO tries to stabilize training with token-level importance weights, but the paper argues that this does not match the structure of the reward and introduces noisy gradients, especially for long responses and sparse MoE models. GSPO moves the correction to the sequence level, aligns the optimization with the reward, and in the paper’s experiments leads to more stable and more efficient training.

If you want the shortest summary possible, it is this: when the reward is for the whole answer, the training signal should be, too.
