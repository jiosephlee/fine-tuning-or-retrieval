\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# From Token-by-Token to Whole Answers: The Big Idea Behind GSPO

Large language model reinforcement learning often looks simple on the surface: generate an answer, score it, and nudge the model toward better behavior. In practice, though, the details of how that nudge is computed matter a lot.

The GSPO paper argues that a major source of instability in recent RL training comes from a mismatch between the unit of reward and the unit of optimization. The reward is assigned to the entire response, but previous methods, especially GRPO, still apply the policy correction one token at a time. GSPO changes that. It moves the optimization target from tokens to full sequences, so the thing being corrected is the same thing being rewarded: the whole answer.

## Why the mismatch matters

Suppose a model answers a question with a long response. A verifier gives that response a single reward, based on whether the whole solution is good. That makes sense, because the quality of the answer depends on the full sequence.

But in GRPO, the importance ratio used for off-policy correction is computed at each token:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

This is meant to correct for the fact that the response was sampled from an older policy. The problem, as the paper explains, is that a single token is only one draw from a next-token distribution. That is not enough to reliably act as an importance-sampling correction for the full generation process.

The result is high-variance training noise. Over long responses, those token-level fluctuations can pile up. The clipping mechanism that is supposed to keep updates stable can then make things worse, especially in large models. The paper reports that this can even lead to catastrophic model collapse, from which training is hard to recover.

## The basic idea behind GSPO

GSPO, short for Group Sequence Policy Optimization, fixes the mismatch by moving the importance ratio to the sequence level. Instead of asking how much each token changed between the old and new policy, GSPO asks how much the probability of the whole response changed.

The sequence-level importance ratio is

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}.
\]

The paper uses length normalization here, which helps keep the scale of the ratio comparable across responses of different lengths and reduces variance.

Once you have a sequence-level ratio, the optimization also becomes sequence-level. GSPO clips and weights entire responses, not individual tokens. That means the update is aligned with the unit that actually received the reward.

In plain terms, GSPO says: if the verifier judged the whole answer, then the policy update should also treat the whole answer as the unit of change.

## Group-based advantages still matter

GSPO keeps one of the useful ideas from GRPO: no separate value model is needed. Instead, it generates a group of responses for the same query and computes a normalized advantage from their rewards:

\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}.
\]

So the model still learns from relative quality within a group of samples. What changes is how the policy update is applied. The advantage is still computed from sequence-level outcomes, but now the probability correction matches that same sequence-level view.

That alignment is the core technical shift in the paper.

## Why sequence-level clipping is more stable

Clipping is supposed to stop updates from drifting too far from the data that generated them. In PPO and GRPO, clipping is applied to token-level importance ratios. GSPO applies it to the sequence ratio instead.

This may sound like a small implementation detail, but the paper argues it is important for stability. Token-level ratios can swing wildly, especially on long outputs. Sequence-level ratios are more directly tied to the probability of the actual sampled response, so they behave more like a proper off-policy correction signal.

The paper also includes a gradient analysis showing a key difference: GRPO gives different weights to different tokens in the same response, based on token-level importance ratios. GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally through the sequence ratio, which removes one major source of instability.

## Why this matters for long reasoning and MoE models

The paper emphasizes that these issues become more severe as models get larger and responses get longer. That is exactly the regime where RL is being used to push models toward deeper reasoning.

It is also especially relevant for Mixture-of-Experts models. The paper reports that expert activation can change a lot from one update to the next, which makes token-level likelihood ratios even less reliable. Earlier, the authors had to use a workaround called Routing Replay to stabilize GRPO for MoE training. GSPO removes that need. Because it works at the sequence level, it is less sensitive to token-by-token routing volatility and can train MoE models stably without that extra machinery.

The paper also points out a practical infrastructure benefit. Sequence-level likelihoods are more forgiving of precision differences between training and inference systems. In some settings, that can make RL pipelines simpler because the likelihoods produced by an inference engine may be usable directly, without recomputing token-level values in the training engine.

## The bigger picture

GSPO is not trying to reinvent reinforcement learning for language models from scratch. The key insight is narrower, but important: the optimization object should match the reward object.

If the reward is for the entire answer, then the policy update should reason about the entire answer. GSPO follows that principle by using sequence likelihood, sequence-level clipping, and sequence-level optimization. According to the paper, that simple alignment improves stability, efficiency, and performance compared with GRPO, and it helps make large-scale RL training more workable in practice.

In short, GSPO’s main idea is not more complexity. It is less mismatch.

## Takeaways

The paper’s argument can be summarized in a few lines:

- GRPO uses token-level importance ratios even though the reward is sequence-level.
- That mismatch introduces noisy, unstable updates, especially for long responses.
- GSPO replaces token-level correction with sequence-level correction.
- The result is a cleaner match between reward and optimization, with better stability in large-scale RL, including MoE training.

That is the central shift: from correcting words one by one to optimizing whole answers as whole answers.
