\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# Why Mixture-of-Experts Models Love GSPO

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models are attractive because they scale model capacity without activating every parameter on every token. In practice, though, that sparse activation pattern makes reinforcement learning much harder than it looks. The paper on Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO, shows that this is exactly where a sequence-level objective pays off: it makes RL training for MoE models far more stable, and it removes a lot of the extra machinery that older methods needed.

## The core MoE problem: routing can move around

An MoE model does not route every token through the same computation path. Instead, a router selects which experts handle each token. That means two very similar policies can still activate different experts for the same response.

During RL training, this matters a lot. Each gradient update changes the policy slightly, but for MoE models those small changes can shift the routing pattern noticeably. The paper describes this as **expert-activation volatility**. On the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, after each RL update, about 10% of the experts activated for the same rollout sample differed between the new policy and the old policy. That is a big deal, because it means the computation path itself is changing while we are trying to optimize it.

Once routing starts changing, token-level importance ratios become unstable. In other words, the model is no longer comparing like with like from one update to the next.

## Why GRPO struggles here

GRPO was designed to avoid the value model used by PPO by comparing multiple responses to the same query and using a group-relative advantage. That makes it simpler than PPO, but it still uses **token-level** importance ratios. The paper argues that this is the wrong unit for MoE RL.

The problem is that token-level importance weights are supposed to correct the mismatch between the policy that generated the data and the policy being optimized. But when the routing pattern changes, the likelihood of a token can fluctuate for reasons that are not really about the quality of the response. The paper says these weights then become high-variance noise rather than a meaningful correction.

For dense models this is already awkward. For MoE models it gets worse, because routing volatility can make the token-level ratios swing sharply from one update to the next. Over time, that instability can prevent normal convergence.

## The old workaround: Routing Replay

Before GSPO, the paper describes a practical workaround called **Routing Replay**. The idea is straightforward: if the routing decisions are part of what makes the token-level ratios unstable, then force the new policy to reuse the old policy’s routing pattern when computing those ratios.

More concretely, the old activated experts are cached from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and those same routing modes are replayed under $\pi_{\theta}$ when calculating the token-level importance ratios. This ensures that the two policies are evaluated through the same activated network, which restores some stability to the ratio computation.

This helped GRPO converge properly on MoE models. But it came with tradeoffs. Routing Replay adds memory overhead and communication overhead, and it can also limit the effective capacity of the MoE model because the routing is no longer free to behave naturally during optimization.

So while Routing Replay made MoE RL workable, it also made the infrastructure more complicated.

## What GSPO changes

GSPO takes a different approach: it moves the optimization target from the token level to the **sequence level**.

Instead of defining importance ratios for individual tokens, GSPO uses the full-response likelihood ratio

\[
s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}},
\]

with length normalization to keep the scale stable across different response lengths. The key idea is simple: the reward is assigned to the entire response, so the optimization should also operate on the entire response.

For MoE models, this matters because the full sequence likelihood is much less sensitive to small routing changes than token-level likelihoods are. The paper notes that as long as the model still maintains its language modeling ability, the sequence likelihood does not fluctuate nearly as violently as individual token probabilities. That makes the sequence-level ratio a much more stable quantity for RL updates.

GSPO also applies clipping at the sequence level, so it excludes overly off-policy responses rather than trying to micro-manage token-level discrepancies. In effect, it treats the whole response as the unit of optimization, which matches the way the reward is defined.

## Why this helps MoE training so much

This design change removes the root cause of the MoE instability that plagued GRPO. Since GSPO does not depend on token-level importance ratios, it is no longer fragile to expert-activation volatility. That means it can train MoE models stably without Routing Replay.

This is a practical win, not just a theoretical one. The paper reports that GSPO:

- converges normally on MoE RL without Routing Replay,
- avoids the extra memory and communication overhead of replaying routing,
- allows the model to use its full capacity without artificial routing constraints,
- and improves training efficiency and performance compared to GRPO.

The paper also notes a broader infrastructure benefit: because GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods, it is more tolerant of precision differences between training and inference engines. That opens the door to using likelihoods from the inference engine directly in some settings, rather than recomputing them with the training engine.

## The practical takeaway

For MoE models, GSPO is appealing because it aligns the optimization target with the structure of the model and the structure of the reward. Instead of fighting routing instability with extra machinery, it sidesteps the problem by moving to sequence-level policy optimization.

That is why the paper frames GSPO as more than just another RL objective. For sparse models in particular, it simplifies the training stack, stabilizes learning, and removes a workaround that older token-level methods needed just to stay on track. In the paper’s experiments, that translated into stable RL training for large MoE models and contributed to the latest Qwen3 results.

If you are scaling RL on MoE systems, that is the kind of change that matters: less infrastructure complexity, fewer failure modes, and a cleaner path to stable training.
