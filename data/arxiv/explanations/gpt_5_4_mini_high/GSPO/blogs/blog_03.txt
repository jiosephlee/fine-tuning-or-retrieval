\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# GSPO vs. GRPO: What Changed and Why It Matters for Better LLMs

Reinforcement learning has become one of the main ways to push large language models toward stronger reasoning. It helps models solve harder math and coding problems by learning to spend more time thinking through a response. But once you scale this up to very large models and long answers, training stability becomes the real challenge.

That is the context for Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO, and the newer Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO. The paper behind GSPO makes a simple but important argument: a small change in *where* you apply policy correction can have a big effect on training stability, efficiency, and final model quality.

## Why GRPO was useful in the first place

PPO-style reinforcement learning is powerful, but it depends on a value model. That extra model adds memory and compute cost, and it is hard to make reliable at scale.

GRPO was attractive because it removed that burden. Instead of learning a value function, it compares multiple responses to the same query and uses their relative rewards to estimate advantage. In practice, that makes RL much simpler to run.

The catch is that GRPO still keeps a token-level view of policy correction. It computes an importance ratio for each token and clips those ratios during optimization. That detail turns out to matter a lot.

## The core problem: token-level correction does not match sequence-level reward

The paper argues that the instability in GRPO comes from a mismatch between the unit of correction and the unit of reward.

In RL for language models, a reward is usually assigned to the entire response, not to individual tokens. But GRPO applies its importance correction token by token. That means each token is treated as if it were a meaningful standalone sample for correcting distribution shift, even though a token is just one step in a long generated sequence.

Why is that a problem? Because token-level ratios are noisy. Over long responses, that noise accumulates. Then clipping, which is meant to keep updates safe, can end up amplifying the instability instead of fixing it.

The paper claims this can lead to catastrophic model collapse, especially for large models and long-generation tasks. Once that happens, recovery is difficult even if you go back to an earlier checkpoint and retune hyperparameters.

## What GSPO changes

GSPO keeps the same overall group-based reward idea as GRPO, but moves the policy correction to the sequence level.

Instead of asking, “How much did the probability of this token change?” GSPO asks, “How much did the probability of this entire response change?” It then applies clipping to the whole response, not to each token separately.

This is the key design shift:

- GRPO uses token-level importance ratios.
- GSPO uses a sequence-level importance ratio based on the full response likelihood.

That sounds like a small change, but it fixes the mismatch between reward and optimization. Since the reward is given to the whole response, the correction is now applied to the whole response too.

The paper also normalizes by response length when computing the sequence-level ratio. That helps keep the values in a more consistent range and reduces variance across different response lengths.

## Why sequence-level clipping is more stable

Clipping is there to keep updates from drifting too far from the old policy. In GRPO, clipping happens at the token level, which means many small decisions are being clipped independently. The paper argues that this produces unstable and noisy gradients.

GSPO clips entire responses instead. That makes the filtering decision easier to interpret: if a sampled response is too far off-policy, it gets downweighted as a whole.

A striking result in the paper is that GSPO actually clips a much larger fraction of tokens than GRPO, yet still trains more efficiently. That seems counterintuitive at first, but it fits the broader story: GSPO is not trying to squeeze learning signal out of every token. It is trying to get a cleaner signal from the responses that matter.

## What this means in practice

According to the paper, GSPO improves both training stability and efficiency compared with GRPO. The authors report better training curves and better benchmark performance on tasks such as AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces, using a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base.

More importantly, the improvements are not just about final scores. The paper emphasizes that GSPO supports continuous improvement as training compute increases, the query set is refreshed, and generation length grows. That matters because RL scaling only helps if training remains stable as the workload becomes harder.

## A big win for Mixture-of-Experts models

One of the most interesting claims in the paper is that GSPO helps solve a stability problem specific to Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, models.

MoE models are sparse: different experts are activated for different tokens. The paper observes that after an RL update, the activated experts for the same response can change noticeably. That makes token-level likelihood ratios in GRPO fluctuate even more, which further undermines stability.

The authors previously used a workaround called Routing Replay, where the old routing pattern is cached and reused during optimization. That helps GRPO converge on MoE models, but it adds memory and communication overhead and can constrain the model’s capacity.

GSPO avoids that workaround. Because it operates on sequence likelihood rather than token-by-token routing behavior, it is less sensitive to expert-activation volatility. In the paper’s telling, that makes GSPO a more natural fit for MoE RL training.

## Why this could simplify RL infrastructure

GSPO may also reduce engineering complexity.

In practice, RL systems often use different engines for training and inference, and those engines can have small precision differences. With token-level methods, those differences can be a problem because the optimization depends on exact token probabilities.

GSPO uses only sequence-level likelihoods, which are more tolerant of those discrepancies. The paper suggests this could make it easier to reuse likelihoods from the inference engine directly, instead of recomputing them with the training engine. That is especially useful in partial rollout and multi-turn RL setups, or in systems where rollout and training are disaggregated.

So GSPO is not just an algorithmic tweak. It also points toward simpler infrastructure.

## The main takeaway

GRPO helped make RL for language models more practical by removing the value model. GSPO keeps that advantage, but changes the level at which policy correction happens.

That shift from token level to sequence level is the whole story. It aligns the optimization objective with the reward signal, reduces noisy gradient updates, improves stability on long responses, and works better for MoE models.

The paper’s broader lesson is worth remembering: in RL, the level at which you measure “importance” matters. If the reward is for the whole answer, it often makes more sense to optimize the whole answer too.

According to the authors, that seemingly small change helped deliver the gains seen in the latest Qwen3 models.
