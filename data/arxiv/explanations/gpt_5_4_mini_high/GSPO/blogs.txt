# Why AI Training Breaks: The Stability Problem GSPO Tries to Fix

Large language models get better at hard reasoning when we train them with reinforcement learning. That is the basic idea behind systems that learn to solve math, coding, and planning tasks by trying answers, getting rewards, and updating the model toward better responses.

But there is a catch: this kind of training can become unstable fast. In the worst cases, the model does not just get a little worse. It can collapse, and once that happens, recovery can be extremely difficult.

The GSPO paper is about fixing exactly that problem.

## Reinforcement learning for LLMs is powerful, but fragile

In RL training for language models, the model generates several candidate responses to the same query. A verifier or reward function scores each response, and the model is updated to prefer the better ones.

That sounds straightforward, but the training loop is a little messy in practice. To use hardware efficiently, we usually generate a large batch of responses, then split it into smaller mini-batches for optimization. By the time the model is updated on a mini-batch, the responses were actually produced by an older version of the model. So training becomes partly off-policy: we are learning from data that came from a previous policy, not the one we are updating right now.

That is why RL algorithms for LLMs use importance sampling and clipping. The importance weight tries to correct for the mismatch between the old policy and the current one, while clipping limits how far the update can move.

The problem, according to this paper, is that the common GRPO design applies this correction at the token level, and that turns out to be the wrong place.

## What goes wrong in GRPO

GRPO was attractive because it removes the need for a large value model. Instead of estimating a value for each token, it compares multiple responses to the same query and uses the relative reward within the group as the advantage.

But GRPO still uses token-level importance ratios:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

The paper argues that this is a misuse of importance sampling. The ratio is computed from a single sampled token at each position, so it does not really correct the distribution mismatch in a meaningful way. Instead, it injects high-variance noise into the gradients.

Why is that such a big deal? Because language responses are long. When you apply a noisy correction at every token, the noise accumulates over the whole sequence. Longer responses mean more opportunities for those errors to pile up. Clipping does not fully solve this; in fact, the paper argues that clipping can make the instability worse once the underlying estimate is already noisy.

The authors report that this can lead to catastrophic model collapse. Once the model collapses, they found that simply going back to an earlier checkpoint, changing clipping ranges, extending generation length, or switching queries did not reliably recover training.

In short, the issue is not just that GRPO is imperfect. The paper claims the objective itself is ill-posed.

## The key idea behind GSPO: match the reward to the unit of optimization

The paper’s main principle is simple: if the reward is assigned to the whole response, then the optimization should also operate at the whole-response level.

That is the motivation for Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO.

Instead of using token-level importance ratios, GSPO uses a sequence-level ratio based on the likelihood of the entire response:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]

You can think of this as the average log-likelihood change per token, or equivalently a length-normalized sequence score. That length normalization matters because it keeps responses of different lengths in a comparable numerical range and reduces variance.

GSPO still works with groups of responses to the same query. It computes a relative advantage inside the group, just like GRPO does, but it clips and optimizes at the response level instead of the token level. In other words, if a response is too far from the old policy, GSPO excludes that whole response from the gradient estimate rather than trying to salvage it token by token.

That change sounds small, but it is the core fix.

## Why GSPO is more stable in practice

The paper’s gradient analysis makes the difference clear. In GRPO, different tokens in the same response are weighted differently by their token-level ratios. Those weights can drift in unpredictable ways over training.

In GSPO, all tokens in a response contribute equally to the update, because the response-level ratio controls the whole gradient. That removes one source of instability.

The empirical result is that GSPO trains stably where GRPO can struggle. On a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, the authors report steady training, better efficiency, and stronger benchmark performance on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces under the same training budget. They also report that GSPO keeps improving as they increase compute, refresh the query set, and extend generation length.

That last point matters. Long-form reasoning is exactly where RL should help most, but it is also where unstable training hurts most. GSPO is designed to make that scaling less brittle.

## A useful side effect for Mixture-of-Experts models

The paper also highlights an important win for Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, models. These models are sparse: different tokens activate different experts. During GRPO training, the paper found that the set of active experts can change a lot after each update, even for the same response. That makes token-level likelihood ratios even less reliable.

To work around this, the authors previously used a technique called Routing Replay, which reuses the old routing pattern when computing the ratios. That helps stabilize training, but it adds memory and communication overhead.

GSPO removes that dependency. Because it works at the sequence level, it is less sensitive to token-by-token routing changes and can train MoE models stably without that extra workaround.

The paper also notes a potential infrastructure benefit: sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision differences between training and inference engines, so GSPO can simplify some RL systems.

## The takeaway

GSPO is not a completely different RL recipe. It is a correction to the unit of optimization.

GRPO tries to stabilize training with token-level importance weights, but the paper argues that this does not match the structure of the reward and introduces noisy gradients, especially for long responses and sparse MoE models. GSPO moves the correction to the sequence level, aligns the optimization with the reward, and in the paper’s experiments leads to more stable and more efficient training.

If you want the shortest summary possible, it is this: when the reward is for the whole answer, the training signal should be, too.



# From Token-by-Token to Whole Answers: The Big Idea Behind GSPO

Large language model reinforcement learning often looks simple on the surface: generate an answer, score it, and nudge the model toward better behavior. In practice, though, the details of how that nudge is computed matter a lot.

The GSPO paper argues that a major source of instability in recent RL training comes from a mismatch between the unit of reward and the unit of optimization. The reward is assigned to the entire response, but previous methods, especially GRPO, still apply the policy correction one token at a time. GSPO changes that. It moves the optimization target from tokens to full sequences, so the thing being corrected is the same thing being rewarded: the whole answer.

## Why the mismatch matters

Suppose a model answers a question with a long response. A verifier gives that response a single reward, based on whether the whole solution is good. That makes sense, because the quality of the answer depends on the full sequence.

But in GRPO, the importance ratio used for off-policy correction is computed at each token:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

This is meant to correct for the fact that the response was sampled from an older policy. The problem, as the paper explains, is that a single token is only one draw from a next-token distribution. That is not enough to reliably act as an importance-sampling correction for the full generation process.

The result is high-variance training noise. Over long responses, those token-level fluctuations can pile up. The clipping mechanism that is supposed to keep updates stable can then make things worse, especially in large models. The paper reports that this can even lead to catastrophic model collapse, from which training is hard to recover.

## The basic idea behind GSPO

GSPO, short for Group Sequence Policy Optimization, fixes the mismatch by moving the importance ratio to the sequence level. Instead of asking how much each token changed between the old and new policy, GSPO asks how much the probability of the whole response changed.

The sequence-level importance ratio is

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}.
\]

The paper uses length normalization here, which helps keep the scale of the ratio comparable across responses of different lengths and reduces variance.

Once you have a sequence-level ratio, the optimization also becomes sequence-level. GSPO clips and weights entire responses, not individual tokens. That means the update is aligned with the unit that actually received the reward.

In plain terms, GSPO says: if the verifier judged the whole answer, then the policy update should also treat the whole answer as the unit of change.

## Group-based advantages still matter

GSPO keeps one of the useful ideas from GRPO: no separate value model is needed. Instead, it generates a group of responses for the same query and computes a normalized advantage from their rewards:

\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}.
\]

So the model still learns from relative quality within a group of samples. What changes is how the policy update is applied. The advantage is still computed from sequence-level outcomes, but now the probability correction matches that same sequence-level view.

That alignment is the core technical shift in the paper.

## Why sequence-level clipping is more stable

Clipping is supposed to stop updates from drifting too far from the data that generated them. In PPO and GRPO, clipping is applied to token-level importance ratios. GSPO applies it to the sequence ratio instead.

This may sound like a small implementation detail, but the paper argues it is important for stability. Token-level ratios can swing wildly, especially on long outputs. Sequence-level ratios are more directly tied to the probability of the actual sampled response, so they behave more like a proper off-policy correction signal.

The paper also includes a gradient analysis showing a key difference: GRPO gives different weights to different tokens in the same response, based on token-level importance ratios. GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally through the sequence ratio, which removes one major source of instability.

## Why this matters for long reasoning and MoE models

The paper emphasizes that these issues become more severe as models get larger and responses get longer. That is exactly the regime where RL is being used to push models toward deeper reasoning.

It is also especially relevant for Mixture-of-Experts models. The paper reports that expert activation can change a lot from one update to the next, which makes token-level likelihood ratios even less reliable. Earlier, the authors had to use a workaround called Routing Replay to stabilize GRPO for MoE training. GSPO removes that need. Because it works at the sequence level, it is less sensitive to token-by-token routing volatility and can train MoE models stably without that extra machinery.

The paper also points out a practical infrastructure benefit. Sequence-level likelihoods are more forgiving of precision differences between training and inference systems. In some settings, that can make RL pipelines simpler because the likelihoods produced by an inference engine may be usable directly, without recomputing token-level values in the training engine.

## The bigger picture

GSPO is not trying to reinvent reinforcement learning for language models from scratch. The key insight is narrower, but important: the optimization object should match the reward object.

If the reward is for the entire answer, then the policy update should reason about the entire answer. GSPO follows that principle by using sequence likelihood, sequence-level clipping, and sequence-level optimization. According to the paper, that simple alignment improves stability, efficiency, and performance compared with GRPO, and it helps make large-scale RL training more workable in practice.

In short, GSPO’s main idea is not more complexity. It is less mismatch.

## Takeaways

The paper’s argument can be summarized in a few lines:

- GRPO uses token-level importance ratios even though the reward is sequence-level.
- That mismatch introduces noisy, unstable updates, especially for long responses.
- GSPO replaces token-level correction with sequence-level correction.
- The result is a cleaner match between reward and optimization, with better stability in large-scale RL, including MoE training.

That is the central shift: from correcting words one by one to optimizing whole answers as whole answers.



# GSPO vs. GRPO: What Changed and Why It Matters for Better LLMs

Reinforcement learning has become one of the main ways to push large language models toward stronger reasoning. It helps models solve harder math and coding problems by learning to spend more time thinking through a response. But once you scale this up to very large models and long answers, training stability becomes the real challenge.

That is the context for Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO, and the newer Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO. The paper behind GSPO makes a simple but important argument: a small change in *where* you apply policy correction can have a big effect on training stability, efficiency, and final model quality.

## Why GRPO was useful in the first place

PPO-style reinforcement learning is powerful, but it depends on a value model. That extra model adds memory and compute cost, and it is hard to make reliable at scale.

GRPO was attractive because it removed that burden. Instead of learning a value function, it compares multiple responses to the same query and uses their relative rewards to estimate advantage. In practice, that makes RL much simpler to run.

The catch is that GRPO still keeps a token-level view of policy correction. It computes an importance ratio for each token and clips those ratios during optimization. That detail turns out to matter a lot.

## The core problem: token-level correction does not match sequence-level reward

The paper argues that the instability in GRPO comes from a mismatch between the unit of correction and the unit of reward.

In RL for language models, a reward is usually assigned to the entire response, not to individual tokens. But GRPO applies its importance correction token by token. That means each token is treated as if it were a meaningful standalone sample for correcting distribution shift, even though a token is just one step in a long generated sequence.

Why is that a problem? Because token-level ratios are noisy. Over long responses, that noise accumulates. Then clipping, which is meant to keep updates safe, can end up amplifying the instability instead of fixing it.

The paper claims this can lead to catastrophic model collapse, especially for large models and long-generation tasks. Once that happens, recovery is difficult even if you go back to an earlier checkpoint and retune hyperparameters.

## What GSPO changes

GSPO keeps the same overall group-based reward idea as GRPO, but moves the policy correction to the sequence level.

Instead of asking, “How much did the probability of this token change?” GSPO asks, “How much did the probability of this entire response change?” It then applies clipping to the whole response, not to each token separately.

This is the key design shift:

- GRPO uses token-level importance ratios.
- GSPO uses a sequence-level importance ratio based on the full response likelihood.

That sounds like a small change, but it fixes the mismatch between reward and optimization. Since the reward is given to the whole response, the correction is now applied to the whole response too.

The paper also normalizes by response length when computing the sequence-level ratio. That helps keep the values in a more consistent range and reduces variance across different response lengths.

## Why sequence-level clipping is more stable

Clipping is there to keep updates from drifting too far from the old policy. In GRPO, clipping happens at the token level, which means many small decisions are being clipped independently. The paper argues that this produces unstable and noisy gradients.

GSPO clips entire responses instead. That makes the filtering decision easier to interpret: if a sampled response is too far off-policy, it gets downweighted as a whole.

A striking result in the paper is that GSPO actually clips a much larger fraction of tokens than GRPO, yet still trains more efficiently. That seems counterintuitive at first, but it fits the broader story: GSPO is not trying to squeeze learning signal out of every token. It is trying to get a cleaner signal from the responses that matter.

## What this means in practice

According to the paper, GSPO improves both training stability and efficiency compared with GRPO. The authors report better training curves and better benchmark performance on tasks such as AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces, using a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base.

More importantly, the improvements are not just about final scores. The paper emphasizes that GSPO supports continuous improvement as training compute increases, the query set is refreshed, and generation length grows. That matters because RL scaling only helps if training remains stable as the workload becomes harder.

## A big win for Mixture-of-Experts models

One of the most interesting claims in the paper is that GSPO helps solve a stability problem specific to Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, models.

MoE models are sparse: different experts are activated for different tokens. The paper observes that after an RL update, the activated experts for the same response can change noticeably. That makes token-level likelihood ratios in GRPO fluctuate even more, which further undermines stability.

The authors previously used a workaround called Routing Replay, where the old routing pattern is cached and reused during optimization. That helps GRPO converge on MoE models, but it adds memory and communication overhead and can constrain the model’s capacity.

GSPO avoids that workaround. Because it operates on sequence likelihood rather than token-by-token routing behavior, it is less sensitive to expert-activation volatility. In the paper’s telling, that makes GSPO a more natural fit for MoE RL training.

## Why this could simplify RL infrastructure

GSPO may also reduce engineering complexity.

In practice, RL systems often use different engines for training and inference, and those engines can have small precision differences. With token-level methods, those differences can be a problem because the optimization depends on exact token probabilities.

GSPO uses only sequence-level likelihoods, which are more tolerant of those discrepancies. The paper suggests this could make it easier to reuse likelihoods from the inference engine directly, instead of recomputing them with the training engine. That is especially useful in partial rollout and multi-turn RL setups, or in systems where rollout and training are disaggregated.

So GSPO is not just an algorithmic tweak. It also points toward simpler infrastructure.

## The main takeaway

GRPO helped make RL for language models more practical by removing the value model. GSPO keeps that advantage, but changes the level at which policy correction happens.

That shift from token level to sequence level is the whole story. It aligns the optimization objective with the reward signal, reduces noisy gradient updates, improves stability on long responses, and works better for MoE models.

The paper’s broader lesson is worth remembering: in RL, the level at which you measure “importance” matters. If the reward is for the whole answer, it often makes more sense to optimize the whole answer too.

According to the authors, that seemingly small change helped deliver the gains seen in the latest Qwen3 models.



# Why Mixture-of-Experts Models Love GSPO

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models are attractive because they scale model capacity without activating every parameter on every token. In practice, though, that sparse activation pattern makes reinforcement learning much harder than it looks. The paper on Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO, shows that this is exactly where a sequence-level objective pays off: it makes RL training for MoE models far more stable, and it removes a lot of the extra machinery that older methods needed.

## The core MoE problem: routing can move around

An MoE model does not route every token through the same computation path. Instead, a router selects which experts handle each token. That means two very similar policies can still activate different experts for the same response.

During RL training, this matters a lot. Each gradient update changes the policy slightly, but for MoE models those small changes can shift the routing pattern noticeably. The paper describes this as **expert-activation volatility**. On the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, after each RL update, about 10% of the experts activated for the same rollout sample differed between the new policy and the old policy. That is a big deal, because it means the computation path itself is changing while we are trying to optimize it.

Once routing starts changing, token-level importance ratios become unstable. In other words, the model is no longer comparing like with like from one update to the next.

## Why GRPO struggles here

GRPO was designed to avoid the value model used by PPO by comparing multiple responses to the same query and using a group-relative advantage. That makes it simpler than PPO, but it still uses **token-level** importance ratios. The paper argues that this is the wrong unit for MoE RL.

The problem is that token-level importance weights are supposed to correct the mismatch between the policy that generated the data and the policy being optimized. But when the routing pattern changes, the likelihood of a token can fluctuate for reasons that are not really about the quality of the response. The paper says these weights then become high-variance noise rather than a meaningful correction.

For dense models this is already awkward. For MoE models it gets worse, because routing volatility can make the token-level ratios swing sharply from one update to the next. Over time, that instability can prevent normal convergence.

## The old workaround: Routing Replay

Before GSPO, the paper describes a practical workaround called **Routing Replay**. The idea is straightforward: if the routing decisions are part of what makes the token-level ratios unstable, then force the new policy to reuse the old policy’s routing pattern when computing those ratios.

More concretely, the old activated experts are cached from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and those same routing modes are replayed under $\pi_{\theta}$ when calculating the token-level importance ratios. This ensures that the two policies are evaluated through the same activated network, which restores some stability to the ratio computation.

This helped GRPO converge properly on MoE models. But it came with tradeoffs. Routing Replay adds memory overhead and communication overhead, and it can also limit the effective capacity of the MoE model because the routing is no longer free to behave naturally during optimization.

So while Routing Replay made MoE RL workable, it also made the infrastructure more complicated.

## What GSPO changes

GSPO takes a different approach: it moves the optimization target from the token level to the **sequence level**.

Instead of defining importance ratios for individual tokens, GSPO uses the full-response likelihood ratio

\[
s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}},
\]

with length normalization to keep the scale stable across different response lengths. The key idea is simple: the reward is assigned to the entire response, so the optimization should also operate on the entire response.

For MoE models, this matters because the full sequence likelihood is much less sensitive to small routing changes than token-level likelihoods are. The paper notes that as long as the model still maintains its language modeling ability, the sequence likelihood does not fluctuate nearly as violently as individual token probabilities. That makes the sequence-level ratio a much more stable quantity for RL updates.

GSPO also applies clipping at the sequence level, so it excludes overly off-policy responses rather than trying to micro-manage token-level discrepancies. In effect, it treats the whole response as the unit of optimization, which matches the way the reward is defined.

## Why this helps MoE training so much

This design change removes the root cause of the MoE instability that plagued GRPO. Since GSPO does not depend on token-level importance ratios, it is no longer fragile to expert-activation volatility. That means it can train MoE models stably without Routing Replay.

This is a practical win, not just a theoretical one. The paper reports that GSPO:

- converges normally on MoE RL without Routing Replay,
- avoids the extra memory and communication overhead of replaying routing,
- allows the model to use its full capacity without artificial routing constraints,
- and improves training efficiency and performance compared to GRPO.

The paper also notes a broader infrastructure benefit: because GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods, it is more tolerant of precision differences between training and inference engines. That opens the door to using likelihoods from the inference engine directly in some settings, rather than recomputing them with the training engine.

## The practical takeaway

For MoE models, GSPO is appealing because it aligns the optimization target with the structure of the model and the structure of the reward. Instead of fighting routing instability with extra machinery, it sidesteps the problem by moving to sequence-level policy optimization.

That is why the paper frames GSPO as more than just another RL objective. For sparse models in particular, it simplifies the training stack, stabilizes learning, and removes a workaround that older token-level methods needed just to stay on track. In the paper’s experiments, that translated into stable RL training for large MoE models and contributed to the latest Qwen3 results.

If you are scaling RL on MoE systems, that is the kind of change that matters: less infrastructure complexity, fewer failure modes, and a cleaner path to stable training.



# How GSPO Could Simplify the Hidden Plumbing of AI Training

Reinforcement learning for large language models is often discussed in terms of capability gains: better reasoning, stronger math, improved code generation. But behind those gains sits a lot of plumbing. Rollouts are generated in one system, optimization happens in another, and the two often need to agree on fine-grained likelihood calculations that are expensive to reproduce and easy to get subtly wrong.

The GSPO paper is interesting not just because it improves stability and performance, but because it may simplify that infrastructure layer. Its main idea is simple: instead of doing policy optimization at the token level, it moves the important ratio and clipping logic to the sequence level. That shift has several practical consequences for large-scale RL pipelines.

## Why the current setup is awkward

In many RL setups for language models, rollout generation and training are handled by different engines. The inference stack may use systems like SGLang or vLLM, while the training stack may use Megatron or another training engine. In theory, both should compute the same likelihoods for a sampled response. In practice, they often differ slightly because of implementation details and numerical precision.

That matters because algorithms like GRPO rely on token-level importance ratios. If the token probabilities used for optimization do not match the ones used during rollout, the update can become noisy or unstable. The paper notes that the common workaround is to recompute likelihoods on the training side, rather than trusting the values returned by the inference engine.

This extra recomputation is not just a nuisance. It adds latency, increases engineering complexity, and makes it harder to build cleanly disaggregated systems where rollout and training are separated for efficiency.

## GSPO moves the comparison to the sequence level

GSPO changes the unit of optimization. Instead of comparing current and old policies token by token, it uses a sequence-level importance ratio based on the likelihood of the whole response:

\[
s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.
\]

The paper also uses length normalization, which keeps the ratio in a more controlled numeric range and reduces variance.

From an infrastructure point of view, this matters because a sequence-level likelihood is much more forgiving than a token-level one. Small discrepancies in individual token probabilities are less likely to derail the overall ratio. That creates a plausible path to using the likelihoods produced by the inference engine directly for optimization, instead of forcing a second pass through the training engine just to recompute token log-probabilities.

The paper is careful here: it says GSPO makes this possible, not that every deployment can immediately drop recomputation. But the direction is clear. If your optimizer only needs a sequence-level score, the training system has fewer reasons to mirror the inference system token by token.

## Less back-and-forth between systems

That shift can simplify the boundary between rollout generation and optimization in several ways.

First, it reduces the amount of data that has to be re-evaluated. Instead of preserving or recomputing per-token likelihood traces, the system only needs the sequence likelihood for each response. That is a smaller and cleaner interface.

Second, it reduces sensitivity to precision mismatches across engines. The paper specifically points out that sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of the differences between training and inference engines than token-level likelihoods.

Third, it fits better with disaggregated architectures. The paper mentions that this is particularly useful for partial rollout and multi-turn RL, where separating rollout and training is common and the bookkeeping can get complicated quickly.

In practical terms, GSPO could let teams keep the fast inference stack doing what it is good at, while avoiding a costly round-trip through the trainer for likelihood recomputation.

## A cleaner story for Mixture-of-Experts training

The infrastructure benefits are even more interesting for Mixture-of-Experts models. The paper identifies a specific failure mode in GRPO: after gradient updates, the experts activated for the same response can change substantially. This “expert-activation volatility” makes token-level importance ratios fluctuate and can destabilize training.

To work around this, the authors previously used a strategy called Routing Replay. The idea is to cache the expert routing pattern from the old policy and replay it when computing token-level ratios under the new policy. That keeps the comparison consistent, but it also adds memory and communication overhead, and it can limit how much of the model’s capacity is actually usable.

GSPO avoids this workaround. Because it operates on sequence likelihood rather than token-by-token likelihood, it is less sensitive to changes in which experts were activated for individual tokens. The paper reports that this allows MoE RL training to converge normally without Routing Replay.

That is a big deal from an engineering standpoint. A successful RL algorithm is not just one that learns well; it is one that does not force the systems team to build elaborate stabilizers around it.

## What gets simpler if GSPO holds up

If GSPO continues to work well in practice, the biggest wins may be operational rather than just algorithmic. The likely simplifications include:

- fewer token-level likelihood recomputations across training and inference engines,
- less dependence on matching the exact behavior of two different runtime stacks,
- no need for Routing Replay in MoE RL,
- cleaner handling of long responses and multi-turn training setups,
- and a smaller surface area for subtle numerical and routing bugs.

That does not mean the infrastructure problem disappears. RL for language models is still a distributed, high-throughput system with lots of moving parts. But GSPO pushes several of those parts in the same direction: response-level scoring, response-level clipping, and response-level optimization.

## The bigger takeaway

The most interesting thing about GSPO may be that it aligns the algorithm with the way the system is already organized. Rewards are assigned to whole responses, so the paper argues that optimization should also happen at the response level. That simple shift not only improves stability in the paper’s experiments, but also makes the training stack easier to reason about.

In large-scale RL, that kind of simplification matters. Sometimes progress comes from a smarter objective. Sometimes it comes from removing the glue code that the objective forced you to write in the first place.



# The Algorithm Powering Qwen3: What GSPO Says About the Future of RL for LLMs

Reinforcement learning has become one of the main ways to push language models beyond next-token prediction and into deeper reasoning. That matters most for tasks like competition math and coding, where the model has to produce long, structured responses and keep improving from feedback rather than just imitation.

The new paper on **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** makes a clear claim: if you want to scale RL for large language models, especially modern reasoning and coding systems, the optimization algorithm itself has to be more stable. According to the paper, GSPO is not just a small tweak to existing RL methods. It is a sequence-level redesign that improves training stability, training efficiency, and final performance, and it helped drive the latest improvements in **Qwen3**.

## Why reinforcement learning matters more now

For large models, RL is increasingly the tool used to refine reasoning behavior after pretraining. The paper points out that with enough RL compute, language models can learn to solve harder problems through longer and deeper reasoning traces. That is one reason RL has become so important for frontier systems.

But there is a catch: scaling RL only works if training stays stable. If the algorithm becomes noisy or collapses during training, then more compute does not help. It can actually make things worse.

That stability issue is where GSPO enters.

## The problem with token-level importance weights

To understand GSPO, it helps to recall how prior methods work.

PPO uses a value model to estimate advantages, but that is expensive and hard to scale. GRPO removes the value model by comparing multiple sampled responses to the same query and using their relative rewards as advantages. That is attractive, because it simplifies training.

However, the paper argues that GRPO has a deeper problem: it applies importance weighting at the **token level**, even though the reward is assigned to the **whole response**.

In other words, GRPO tries to correct off-policy mismatch one token at a time using

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

The paper argues that this is not a valid way to do importance correction in practice, because each token is only a single sample from a much larger next-token distribution. That creates high-variance noise in the gradients. Over long responses, that noise accumulates. With clipping on top of that, the result can be severe instability, including model collapse.

The paper’s core takeaway is simple: **the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward**. If the reward is for the entire sequence, then the policy correction should also operate at the sequence level.

## What GSPO changes

GSPO, or **Group Sequence Policy Optimization**, makes that shift explicit.

Instead of using token-level ratios, GSPO defines an importance ratio over the entire response:

$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}.
$$

The paper uses length normalization here to keep the ratio on a more stable scale across responses of different lengths.

GSPO then applies clipping to the whole response rather than to each token. It still uses group-based advantages, where multiple responses to the same query are normalized against each other, but the optimization signal is now aligned with the sequence-level reward.

That alignment is the key idea. Instead of asking, “How should each token be weighted off-policy?” GSPO asks, “How much should this whole response count?”

The paper argues that this is both more theoretically grounded and much more stable in practice.

## Why this matters for long reasoning and coding traces

Sequence-level training is especially relevant for models that generate long responses. The longer the response, the more room there is for token-level importance noise to accumulate. That is exactly the kind of setting where reasoning and coding models operate.

The paper reports that GSPO can keep improving as training compute increases, as the query set changes, and as generation length grows. It also reports better training efficiency and better benchmark performance than GRPO in the experiments described, including evaluations on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces.

That is important because it suggests RL scaling is not just about more data or more updates. It is also about having a policy optimization rule that remains well-behaved as the model starts producing longer and more complex answers.

## GSPO and Mixture-of-Experts training

One of the most interesting claims in the paper is that GSPO solves a stability problem in **Mixture-of-Experts** RL training.

MoE models are sparse: different experts may be activated for different tokens or responses. The paper notes that with GRPO, expert activation can change significantly after a gradient update, which makes token-level ratios even less reliable. In their example, around 10% of activated experts can differ between the old and new policy for the same rollout sample in the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model.

To stabilize GRPO, the authors previously used a workaround called **Routing Replay**, which caches and reuses expert routing during optimization. That helps, but it adds overhead and constrains the model.

GSPO avoids the problem by focusing on sequence likelihood rather than individual token likelihoods. Because the sequence likelihood is less sensitive to those routing fluctuations, GSPO can train MoE models stably without Routing Replay. That is a practical win, not just a theoretical one.

## What this means for RL infrastructure

GSPO may also simplify how RL systems are built.

In the paper’s discussion, the authors note that training and inference engines often differ in numerical precision, which means teams usually recompute likelihoods with the training engine before optimization. But GSPO operates on sequence-level likelihoods, which should be more tolerant of such discrepancies. That opens the door to using likelihoods returned directly by the inference engine in some settings, reducing recomputation overhead.

That may sound like an implementation detail, but it matters a lot at scale. Once RL systems get large enough, small infrastructure simplifications can have major effects on throughput and complexity.

## Why GSPO matters for Qwen3 and what comes next

The paper says these properties contributed to the latest improvements in Qwen3 models. More importantly, GSPO points to a broader lesson: **the next generation of reasoning and coding models will likely depend on RL algorithms that are both stable and scalable**.

That does not mean RL replaces pretraining. But it does mean that post-training is becoming a serious scaling axis. As models get larger, responses get longer, and architectures become more sparse, the training algorithm has to be designed for those conditions from the start.

GSPO’s main contribution is that it treats the whole response as the object being optimized, which matches how rewards are actually assigned. That small conceptual change seems to buy a lot: better stability, better efficiency, easier MoE training, and less infrastructure complexity.

If the paper is right, GSPO is not just a better version of GRPO. It is a sign of where RL for LLMs is going next: toward sequence-level objectives that are robust enough to support much larger, more capable reasoning systems.
