\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models

# Why post-training with RL matters for modern language models

Reinforcement learning has become a pivotal paradigm for scaling language models. In the framing of this paper, RL is not a minor training tweak but a central mechanism for pushing model capability beyond what is achieved by the initial model alone. The reason is that language models can be treated as policies, meaning they produce responses in a way that can be optimized using reward signals. Once this optimization is applied at scale, the model can improve at tasks that require more than shallow pattern matching.

The paper emphasizes that large-scale RL enables language models to tackle sophisticated problems by undertaking deeper and longer reasoning processes. This is important because many of the most valuable language-model applications are not single-step predictions, but responses that require sustained coherence across many tokens. RL gives the training process a way to reward entire responses, rather than only local next-token behavior, so the model can be pushed toward outputs that satisfy a verifier over the full query-response pair.

A useful way to think about the paper’s setup is that a query $x$ is given to the model, the model produces a response $y$, and a verifier assigns a reward $r(x,y)$ in $[0,1]$. RL then becomes the mechanism for adjusting the policy $\pi_\theta$ so that responses with higher reward become more likely. In this sense, RL is the part of the training pipeline that connects language generation to explicit task success.

# RL-driven gains in math, coding, and long-form reasoning

The paper specifically points to competition-level mathematics and programming as examples of tasks that become more tractable through large-scale RL. These are not simple one-step classification problems; they demand extended reasoning, intermediate deductions, and the ability to keep track of structure over a long response. The paper’s claim is that RL helps models learn to carry out these longer reasoning trajectories more effectively.

This emphasis on reasoning length matters because the model is not merely producing an answer token by token in isolation. For a difficult math problem or a programming task, the response often needs to unfold through many steps before arriving at a correct conclusion. RL is presented as a way to improve these multi-step behaviors by rewarding the quality of the full sequence, not just individual next-token choices.

The paper later evaluates this idea using AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces. These benchmarks serve as concrete examples of the kinds of reasoning-heavy tasks the paper associates with RL scaling. The broader point is that RL is tied to improvements in domains where deeper reasoning and longer responses are essential.

# The scaling perspective: compute, model size, and long rollouts

The paper’s motivation is closely tied to scaling. As model size grows, as sparsity increases in Mixture-of-Experts models, and as response length increases, RL training requires a larger rollout batch size in order to keep hardware utilization high. This is a practical scaling issue: if you want to train a very large model efficiently, you need to generate enough data per iteration to make the computation worthwhile.

At the same time, large rollout batches are usually split into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates to improve sample efficiency. This creates an off-policy learning setting, because the responses were sampled from an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ rather than the current policy $\pi_\theta$ that is being optimized. Once data becomes off-policy, the training algorithm must decide how to account for the mismatch between the behavior policy that generated the data and the current policy that is being updated.

This is why clipping appears in PPO-style methods and in GRPO. Clipping is meant to stop overly off-policy samples from dominating the gradient. In other words, the algorithm tries to protect training from updates that are too far from the policy that produced the data. The paper treats this as a necessary practical safeguard, but also argues that clipping alone does not solve the deeper instability problem in GRPO.

The empirical results reinforce the scaling story. The paper reports that GSPO can continue improving as training compute increases, the query set is updated, and generation length is extended. That is an important signal: the method is designed not merely to work at a fixed scale, but to keep benefiting as more training resources are devoted to it.

# Training stability as the main bottleneck

The paper argues that stability is the first requirement for scaling RL successfully. Before one can hope to get the benefits of more compute or larger models, the training dynamics must remain robust. If training becomes unstable, then extra compute does not help; it simply accelerates failure. For that reason, stability is presented as the main bottleneck in large-scale RL.

The paper contrasts two major algorithms in this respect. PPO uses a value model, but that model is expensive and difficult to scale, especially as responses become longer and tasks become more complex. GRPO avoids the value model by using group-relative advantages, which removes one source of burden. However, the paper argues that GRPO introduces a more serious problem: its objective is ill-posed because of how it uses importance sampling weights.

This is the key conceptual issue. In the paper’s view, GRPO does not simply have a tuning problem or a small numerical instability. Rather, its design misuses importance weights in a way that makes high-variance noise accumulate during training. That noise becomes more severe as responses get longer, and the clipping mechanism can amplify the problem instead of resolving it. So the training pipeline becomes increasingly fragile exactly when the model and tasks are becoming more ambitious.

# Catastrophic model collapse in existing RL algorithms

The paper uses strong language to describe what can happen under GRPO: catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. The collapse is not treated as a temporary dip in performance that can be recovered by more training. Instead, once it happens, the model may not recover even if training is restarted from an earlier checkpoint. The paper says that resuming training is unavailing even when one carefully changes clipping ranges, extends generation length, or switches RL queries.

The explanation given is that GRPO applies an importance weight at each token position, but this weight is based on a single token sample from the next-token distribution. Importance sampling, in principle, is meant to correct for differences between a behavior distribution and a target distribution by reweighting samples. The paper emphasizes that this correction depends on averaging over many samples. Applying the weight token by token in the GRPO setting does not perform the intended correction and instead injects noisy gradient estimates.

Because the response is long, that noise can accumulate across many tokens. The paper argues that this accumulation is one reason the instability becomes so severe in large models and long-response tasks. In practice, the result is that the training trajectory can abruptly fail and then remain unrecoverable. This is why the paper treats stability not as a secondary nuisance but as a fundamental obstacle to scaling RL for language models.

# GSPO as the paper's response to GRPO instability

GSPO, or Group Sequence Policy Optimization, is proposed as the paper’s answer to the instability of GRPO. The central idea is simple but important: the importance ratio should be defined at the sequence level, not the token level. Instead of comparing individual token probabilities under $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, GSPO compares the full sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ to $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)$.

This choice is not presented as a cosmetic change. The paper argues that sequence-level importance weighting has a clear theoretical meaning: it measures how far the sampled response $y$ deviates from the old policy’s distribution. Just as importantly, it matches the fact that reward is assigned to the entire response. The paper’s guiding principle is that the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. If the reward is sequence-level, then the correction and optimization should also be sequence-level.

GSPO therefore performs sequence-level clipping, rewarding, and optimization. It also uses group-based advantage estimation, where the reward of each sampled response is normalized relative to the group of responses generated for the same query. This keeps the training signal aligned with the whole response rather than forcing token-by-token weights that the paper views as unstable. In the paper’s analysis, GSPO avoids the unequal token weighting that makes GRPO unpredictable over time.

# What the paper claims GSPO improves

The paper makes three main claims about GSPO: stability, efficiency, and performance. First, GSPO is said to train stably where GRPO can collapse. Second, it is reported to achieve better training efficiency, meaning that it makes better use of the compute and sampled queries available during training. Third, it improves benchmark performance, including on the math and coding evaluations reported in the experiments.

A particularly important claim is that GSPO stabilizes RL training for large Mixture-of-Experts models. The paper explains that MoE models introduce expert-activation volatility: after gradient updates, the set of activated experts for the same response can change substantially. Under GRPO, this makes token-level importance ratios fluctuate badly and can break convergence. Previous workarounds required a Routing Replay strategy, which caches and reuses routing modes to keep the activated experts consistent across updates. GSPO is presented as eliminating the need for this workaround.

This matters because removing Routing Replay simplifies both training and infrastructure. The paper notes that replaying routing modes adds memory and communication overhead and can reduce the effective capacity of the MoE model. GSPO avoids these costs by relying on sequence-level likelihoods, which are less sensitive to token-level routing volatility. In the paper’s view, this is not only a stability improvement but also an infrastructure simplification.

The paper also argues that GSPO is more forgiving of precision discrepancies between training engines and inference engines. Since GSPO uses sequence-level likelihoods rather than token-level likelihoods, it may be possible to use likelihoods returned by the inference engine directly for optimization instead of recomputing them with the training engine. That potential simplification is part of the paper’s broader claim that GSPO can streamline RL systems.

Taken together, the paper’s message is that GSPO provides a more stable foundation for scaling RL in large language models. It is positioned as a theoretically grounded response to the instability of GRPO, and as an algorithm that supports continued gains in compute, response length, and model scale. The paper concludes that these properties helped contribute to the improved results in the latest Qwen3 models.

Core Foundations: Policies, Rewards, and Off-Policy Optimization

# Autoregressive language models as policies

The paper treats an autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$ as a policy $\pi_\theta$. This is a useful way to think about language generation because the model does not choose a whole response all at once. Instead, it produces one token at a time, and each new token depends on the query and on all previously generated tokens. If $x$ is the query and $y$ is the response, then the policy assigns a likelihood to the full response by multiplying the conditional likelihoods of its tokens:
\[
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t}).
\]
Here $|y|$ is the number of tokens in the response, and $y_{<t}$ means the tokens before position $t$.

This factorization is the mathematical expression of autoregressive generation. It says that a response is a sequence of linked decisions, where each decision is made in the context of what has already been generated. In the paper’s notation, this is the basic object being optimized during RL training: not a static prediction, but a policy over complete sequences. That perspective is essential for understanding why later parts of the paper distinguish carefully between token-level quantities and sequence-level quantities.

# Queries, responses, datasets, and verifier-based rewards

The paper uses $x$ to denote a query and $\mathcal{D}$ to denote the set of queries used for training. A response $y$ is generated for a query $x$, and the pair $(x,y)$ is then evaluated by a verifier $r$. The verifier produces a scalar reward
\[
r(x,y)\in [0,1].
\]
This reward is attached to the query-response pair as a whole, not to individual tokens. That detail matters, because it means the feedback signal the model receives is about the quality of the entire response.

A key point in the paper is that the reward is sequence-level. The verifier does not say that one token is good and another is bad; it scores the completed response. This is why the paper later argues that the optimization objective should also operate at the response level. When multiple responses are sampled for the same query, their rewards can be compared against one another. In the paper’s group-based setting, this comparison is turned into an advantage signal by normalizing the rewards within the group. For a group of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ to the same query, the paper defines
\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}.
\]
This turns raw verifier scores into a relative measure of how well each response performed compared with the other responses to the same query.

# Token likelihood versus sequence likelihood

Because the model is autoregressive, it is natural to talk about token likelihoods. The likelihood of each token is the conditional probability of that token given the query and the previous tokens, such as $\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})$. This is the local quantity used at a particular step of generation. It tells you how probable one token is in one context.

The sequence likelihood is the product of all those token likelihoods, $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$. In other words, it measures how probable the entire response is under the policy. The paper’s central observation is that this quantity has a much clearer meaning when the object being optimized is the whole response. If a response is the unit that receives the reward, then the sequence likelihood is the natural object to compare between the old policy and the current policy. The paper later turns this idea into a sequence-level importance ratio. At the intuition level, the sequence likelihood asks a single question: how much does the model, as a whole, prefer this complete response?

This distinction between token likelihood and sequence likelihood becomes more important for long responses. Since the sequence likelihood is a product over many token probabilities, changes at many token positions accumulate across the whole response. That accumulation is one reason the paper is careful about which level of likelihood is used in the optimization objective. A local tokenwise quantity may be easy to write down, but it is not always the right match for a reward that is assigned only after the entire response has been generated.

# What it means to optimize a response-level reward

To optimize a response-level reward means that the learning signal is attached to the entire response, and the model is encouraged to increase the probability of responses that receive better verifier scores. The paper emphasizes a simple guiding principle: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of the reward. Since the verifier judges the full query-response pair, the optimization procedure should also treat the full response as the object being rewarded or discouraged.

This is exactly what the group-based setup is trying to accomplish. When multiple responses are sampled for the same query, the paper forms a relative score for each response by comparing it with the others in the group. That relative score becomes the advantage used in optimization. In GRPO, for example, all tokens in a response share the same group-based advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. The idea is that if a response is better than the other responses sampled for the same query, then the entire response should be reinforced; if it is worse, the entire response should be downweighted.

This is a very different picture from token-by-token reward assignment. Instead of pretending that each token has an independent reward, the paper treats the response as a single decision object composed of many token decisions. The reward is still ultimately about the whole response, and the optimization signal should respect that structure. This matching between reward granularity and optimization granularity is one of the paper’s main conceptual anchors.

# On-policy and off-policy learning in language-model RL

In RL, on-policy learning means that the data being used for optimization come from the same policy that is currently being updated. Off-policy learning means that the data were generated by an older policy, while the model being optimized has already changed. The paper uses the notation $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for the policy that generated the rollout data, and $\pi_\theta$ for the policy being optimized. Once $\theta$ changes, the samples collected under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ become off-policy relative to the current model.

This distinction is not just a formal detail. The paper explains that when a large rollout batch is generated and then reused across several gradient updates, the policy can drift away from the policy that produced the data. As the update proceeds, the same response becomes less representative of the current model. The farther the current policy moves from the old one, the more severe the off-policy mismatch becomes. That is why the paper pays so much attention to how old-policy samples are weighted and how aggressively they are allowed to influence the update.

The paper also notes that the mismatch is particularly troublesome for long responses. A response is not a single action but a long chain of token choices, so any discrepancy between the old and current policies can accumulate across many positions. This is one reason the paper argues that stable RL training for language models requires a careful treatment of off-policy data rather than a naive reuse of rollout samples.

# Why rollout batches are split into mini-batches during training

The paper explains that as models become larger, sparser, and capable of longer responses, RL training requires large rollout batches to make efficient use of hardware. A large batch keeps the training system busy and helps produce enough samples for learning. But once those samples have been collected, they are usually not consumed all at once. Instead, the batch is partitioned into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates.

This partitioning improves sample efficiency, because the same rollout data can be reused for several updates. But it also creates the off-policy situation described above. The first mini-batch is processed using one version of the policy, and later mini-batches are processed after the model has already been updated. So even though the rollouts were generated together, the optimization sees them through gradually changing parameters. The paper presents this as a fundamental practical feature of large-scale language-model RL, not as an unusual corner case.

This is why clipping and importance weighting matter. Without some mechanism to control how much old data can influence the current update, the mini-batch training procedure would make the learning signal increasingly stale. In the paper’s framing, mini-batching is necessary for efficiency, but it also makes the off-policy problem unavoidable.

# The basic principle of importance sampling

The paper invokes importance sampling as the mathematical tool for correcting a mismatch between a behavior distribution and a target distribution. If $f$ is a function of a random variable $z$, then the basic principle is
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)]
=
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_\text{beh}}\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}f(z)\right].
\]
Here $\pi_\text{tar}$ is the target distribution whose expectation we want, and $\pi_\text{beh}$ is the behavior distribution from which the samples were actually drawn. The ratio $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ tells us how to reweight each sample so that, on average, the behavior samples reproduce the target expectation.

The intuition is simple: a sample that is more likely under the target than under the behavior distribution should count more, while a sample that is less likely under the target should count less. The weight does not change the sample itself; it changes how much the sample contributes to the estimate. In the language-model setting of the paper, this principle is used to compare quantities under the current policy with samples collected under the old policy. The same idea appears whether the ratio is defined for tokens or for whole responses.

# When importance reweighting is valid and why sample averaging matters

The paper emphasizes that importance reweighting is not just about computing a ratio. It also depends on averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution. In the paper’s wording, the correction works effectively when there are multiple samples, with $N \gg 1$, from the behavior distribution. The reason is that the weighted average is what approximates the target expectation. A single weighted sample can still be extremely noisy, but many weighted samples can collectively compensate for the distribution mismatch.

This point matters directly for the paper’s critique of token-level importance weights. GRPO applies the importance weight at each token position, but each such weight is based on a single sampled token from the next-token distribution. According to the paper, that is not the setting in which importance sampling is meant to operate effectively. The weight is then asked to correct a mismatch using a very small amount of information, and the result is high-variance noise in the gradient estimate. The paper argues that this noise accumulates over long sequences and becomes a serious source of instability.

The broader lesson is that importance reweighting should be used at the same level as the quantity being estimated. If the reward is attached to a full response, then the correction should also be applied at the response level. That is why the paper’s sequence-level approach is so important: it keeps the statistical correction aligned with the object being optimized, rather than distributing the correction across tokenwise fragments that do not represent the reward signal on their own.

# Clipping as a safeguard against overly off-policy updates

The paper describes clipping as a safeguard that keeps the policy update within a proximal region of the old policy. In PPO and GRPO, the importance ratio is clipped to an interval such as $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$, and the objective uses the smaller of the unclipped and clipped terms. The purpose is to prevent samples that are too far from the current policy from dominating the gradient update. In effect, clipping says that if a sample has become too off-policy, its influence should be limited.

This safeguard is especially important in the setting of repeated mini-batch updates from the same rollout batch. As the policy drifts, some old samples may no longer be reliable indicators of what the current model should do. Clipping keeps those samples from causing overly aggressive updates. The paper also argues that clipping is not merely a numerical trick; it is part of the mechanism that allows off-policy data to be used safely during RL training.

At the same time, the paper’s main claim is that clipping should match the level of the reward and the likelihood ratio. If the reward is sequence-level, then clipping should be sequence-level as well. That is why the paper moves away from tokenwise clipping and toward response-level clipping. In the paper’s view, this alignment between reward granularity, importance weighting, and clipping granularity is what makes the optimization stable and meaningful for language-model RL.

PPO and GRPO: The Starting Point

# Review of the PPO objective

The paper begins from Proximal Policy Optimization, or PPO, as a standard clipped policy-gradient method for training language models with reinforcement learning. In the paper’s notation, an autoregressive language model with parameters $\theta$ is treated as a policy $\pi_\theta$. A query is denoted by $x$, and a generated response by $y$. The overall likelihood of a response under the policy is written as $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})$, where $|y|$ is the number of tokens in the response. A query-response pair $(x,y)$ can be evaluated by a verifier, producing a reward $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$.

PPO is introduced as a way to update the policy using samples generated from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The key idea is that the update should stay within a proximal region of the old policy, rather than changing too abruptly. The paper expresses this through a clipped objective:
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\min\left(w_t(\theta)\widehat{A}_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_t\right)\right].
\]
The objective averages over tokens in the sampled response, and the clipping mechanism limits how much a token’s contribution can grow or shrink. In the paper’s presentation, the KL regularization term is omitted for brevity, because it is not the focus of the discussion.

This formulation captures the central PPO intuition used throughout the paper: training is driven by samples produced by an earlier policy, but the optimizer is discouraged from trusting samples that would require too large a shift in probability under the current policy. The clipping term is therefore a safeguard against overly aggressive updates.

# Token-level importance ratios in PPO

A defining feature of PPO in the paper is that the importance ratio is computed at the token level. For the token $y_t$, the ratio is
\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}.
\]
This ratio compares how likely the current policy thinks a token is, relative to the old policy that generated the sample. Because PPO uses samples from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ while optimizing $\pi_\theta$, this ratio serves as the correction factor that connects the sampled data to the updated model.

The paper emphasizes that PPO applies this ratio at every token position. The objective then takes the minimum of the unclipped term and the clipped term, so that if the ratio moves too far from $1$, the contribution is constrained. The clipping range is controlled by $\varepsilon$. In effect, PPO tries to preserve the usefulness of the old sample while preventing a single token from causing an excessively large gradient step.

In the language used by the paper, PPO is therefore token-centric: each token contributes through its own importance ratio and its own advantage estimate. This makes PPO a strong baseline for policy optimization, but it also creates a dependence on accurate token-level advantage estimates, which becomes crucial in the discussion that follows.

# Advantage estimation with a learned value model

PPO in the paper uses an advantage estimate $\widehat{A}_t$ for each token, and that advantage is estimated by another value model. This is important because the advantage provides the learning signal that tells the policy whether a token is better or worse than expected. In the objective, the advantage multiplies the importance ratio, so the sign and magnitude of the advantage directly control how the policy is updated.

The paper does not expand the value-model training procedure here, but it makes clear why the value model is central: the effectiveness of PPO depends on the reliability of the value estimate. If the value model is inaccurate, then the advantage signal is also unreliable, and the policy update may be guided in the wrong direction. In other words, PPO’s policy optimization quality is tied to the quality of a separate learned component.

This reliance on the value model is not a small implementation detail in the paper’s view. It is part of the reason why PPO is difficult to scale in the setting of large language models, especially when responses become longer and tasks become more complex. The advantage estimate must remain reliable across these harder settings, which the paper treats as a major practical obstacle.

# Why value models are costly and difficult to scale for LLMs

The paper highlights the value model as a major source of cost in PPO. In practice, the value model is usually similar in size to the policy model, so it adds substantial memory and computational burden. This matters especially in large-scale language model training, where resource demands are already high.

Beyond cost, the paper points out a second problem: the algorithm’s effectiveness hinges on the reliability of the value estimate. That means one must not only afford the value model, but also ensure that it remains trustworthy. The paper argues that this becomes harder as responses grow longer and tasks become more complex. So the challenge is both practical and algorithmic: the value model is expensive, and scaling it robustly is difficult.

This is the backdrop against which the paper later motivates a value-model-free alternative. PPO is not presented as flawed in the abstract; rather, it is presented as a strong baseline whose dependence on a learned value model becomes increasingly burdensome in the large-language-model regime.

# The motivation for Group Relative Policy Optimization

Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO, is introduced in the paper as a way to bypass the value model entirely. The motivation is straightforward: if the model can compare multiple responses to the same query directly, then it may not need a separate value function to estimate token-level advantage. GRPO is designed to exploit this idea.

The paper describes GRPO as operating on a group of responses generated for the same query. Instead of estimating an advantage with a learned value model, GRPO uses the relative quality of each response within the group. This is the key conceptual shift from PPO. Rather than asking an external value model what a token or response should be worth, GRPO asks how one sampled response compares to the other sampled responses for the same query.

This group-based perspective is what makes GRPO attractive in the paper’s discussion. It removes the most expensive extra component from PPO, and it replaces it with a reward-normalization procedure computed directly from the sampled responses themselves.

# Group-based sampling over multiple responses per query

In GRPO, the paper samples $G$ responses for each query $x$. These responses are written as $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$ and are drawn from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)$. The use of multiple responses per query is central to the method, because GRPO needs a group in order to define relative advantage.

The intuition is that the model is not judged in isolation on a single response. Instead, each response is compared against other responses to the same query. That makes the learning signal relative rather than absolute. If one response is better than the others in its group, it should receive a positive advantage; if it is worse, it should receive a negative one.

This sampling structure also helps explain why GRPO can avoid the value model. The group itself supplies the baseline for comparison. The method does not need a separately learned estimate of expected return, because it can compare the rewards observed within the group and compute a normalized score directly from them.

# Normalized group rewards as response-level advantages

GRPO defines its response-level advantage by normalizing each response’s reward relative to the other rewards in the same group. The paper’s formula is
\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{G}\right)}{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{G}\right)}.
\]
This is a standardized score: the reward of response $y_i$ is centered by subtracting the group mean, and then scaled by the group standard deviation.

The meaning of this normalization is easy to interpret. If a response’s reward is above the average reward in the group, then its normalized advantage is positive. If it is below average, the normalized advantage is negative. The standard deviation rescales the differences so that the advantages are measured in a common unit across groups. In the paper’s implementation, all tokens in the same response share the same advantage value, namely $\widehat{A}_i$.

This is one of the most important structural differences from PPO. PPO assigns a separate advantage estimate to each token, whereas GRPO assigns one response-level advantage to every token in the response. The reward is still delivered to the entire query-response pair, so this response-level treatment matches the granularity of the reward more closely than token-specific value estimation would.

# The GRPO objective and its token-level clipping

The paper writes the GRPO objective as
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\min\left(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t},\ \mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_{i,t}\right)\right],
\]
where the token-level importance ratio is
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
In GRPO, the token advantages are all the same within a response: $\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i$.

The structure of the objective is therefore a hybrid of two ideas. On one hand, the advantage signal is response-level and comes from the group reward normalization. On the other hand, the optimization is still carried out token by token using token-level importance ratios and token-level clipping. This means that GRPO retains the clipped surrogate style of PPO while replacing the value model with a group-based advantage estimate.

The paper presents this as the defining mathematical form of GRPO. Its appeal is that it keeps the familiar clipped-update structure while removing the need for a value network.

# Strengths of GRPO: avoiding a value model

From the paper’s perspective, the primary strength of GRPO is that it avoids the value model altogether. This is a significant simplification, because it eliminates the memory and computation cost associated with a separate value network. It also removes the need to train and maintain a reliable value estimator.

Another advantage is conceptual simplicity. GRPO uses the relative quality of sampled responses to define advantage, which fits naturally with the idea of evaluating multiple responses to the same query. This makes it especially appealing in large-language-model reinforcement learning, where generating multiple responses is already part of the training process.

At the same time, the paper treats GRPO as a baseline rather than a final solution. Its value-model-free design is clearly useful, but the paper later argues that the token-level importance ratios in GRPO create stability problems when training very large models. For the purposes of understanding the paper, however, the important point is that GRPO is a practical step beyond PPO because it removes the value model bottleneck.

# The paper’s setup for comparing GSPO against GRPO

The paper’s empirical comparison between the new GSPO method and GRPO is built on a specific training setup. The authors experiment with a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base. They report both training reward curves and model performance curves on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces. The AIME'24 metric is average Pass@1 over 32 samplings, LiveCodeBench uses the 202410-202502 split and average Pass@1 over 8 samplings, and CodeForces is measured by Elo Rating.

The RL training procedure partitions each batch of rollout data into four mini-batches for gradient updates. For GSPO, the left and right clipping ranges in its objective are set to $3\mathrm{e}{-4}$ and $4\mathrm{e}{-4}$, respectively. For GRPO, the corresponding clipping ranges are set to $0.2$ and $0.27$, which the paper says were carefully tuned to ensure a fair comparison.

The paper also notes that GRPO requires the Routing Replay training strategy for normal convergence of MoE RL, while GSPO does not. This setup detail is important because it shows that the comparison is not only about reward curves or benchmark scores, but also about whether the method can train stably in the first place. The paper’s broader claim is that GSPO improves upon GRPO in stability, efficiency, and performance under this carefully controlled comparison.

Why GRPO Becomes Unstable

# The Mismatch Between Reward Granularity and Optimization Granularity

The paper’s central criticism of GRPO starts from a simple but decisive observation: the reward is defined for an entire response, while GRPO optimizes the policy through token-level importance ratios. In the paper’s notation, a query $x$ receives a response $y$, and a verifier assigns a sequence-level reward $r(x,y) \in [0,1]$. That reward is attached to the whole query-response pair, not to individual tokens. GRPO, however, still breaks the response into token positions and applies a separate importance ratio to each token during optimization.

This mismatch matters because the algorithm is trying to correct for off-policy data at a finer granularity than the signal it is trying to learn from. The paper argues that this is not just a small implementation detail. It is the root of the instability problem. When the objective is built from token-level corrections but the reward exists only at the response level, the optimization signal is no longer naturally aligned with what was actually evaluated.

# Why GRPO Uses a Single Response-Level Advantage for Every Token

GRPO avoids a value model by using a group of responses to the same query and turning their relative rewards into an advantage. If a query $x$ produces $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$, then GRPO defines the advantage of response $y_i$ as

$$
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}.
$$

The key point is that this is a response-level advantage. Every token in the response $y_i$ inherits the same value $\widehat{A}_i$. In the paper’s notation, $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$ for all token positions $t$.

This design is attractive because it removes the need for a separate value model, which would otherwise add considerable memory and compute cost. But it also means that token-level learning is driven by a single scalar reward signal that was computed for the whole response. The only thing that differentiates one token from another in the gradient is the token-level importance ratio. That makes the correctness of those ratios absolutely central to the stability of the method.

# The Paper’s Claim: Token-Level Importance Ratios Are Misapplied

The paper argues that GRPO misapplies the basic idea of importance sampling. In importance sampling, one wants to estimate an expectation under a target distribution by reweighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution. The paper states the principle as

$$
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}}\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z)\right].
$$

The important thing here is that the ratio is a ratio of distributions, and the paper emphasizes that this correction becomes meaningful when one averages over many samples, with $N \gg 1$, from the behavior distribution. The ratio is supposed to correct a distributional mismatch, not simply rescale a single noisy sample.

GRPO instead uses the token-level ratio

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}.
$$

According to the paper, this is problematic because each token is only one sampled realization from the next-token distribution at that position. That single sample is not enough for the importance weight to play its intended distribution-correction role. Rather than producing a principled correction, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient estimate.

# Importance Sampling Over Distributions Versus Single Sampled Tokens

The paper’s sequence-level viewpoint makes the flaw clearer. For a full response $y$, the likelihood under a policy $\pi_\theta$ is

$$
\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
$$

This is the natural object to compare when the reward is attached to the entire response. A sequence-level ratio compares how probable the whole response is under the current policy versus the old policy, which makes direct sense as a measure of distribution shift. The paper explicitly says that the sequence-level importance weight has a clear theoretical meaning because it reflects how far the sampled response deviates from the current policy.

By contrast, a token-level ratio compares only one sampled token at a particular position. The paper’s critique is not that token probabilities are useless in general, but that using them as importance weights for response-level reinforcement learning is ill-posed. The ratio is being asked to do a job it was not designed to do. That is why the paper argues that the sequence, not the token, should be the unit of correction and optimization.

# How Token-Level Noise Accumulates Over Long Responses

The instability in GRPO becomes worse as responses get longer because the gradient contains many token-level terms, each carrying its own noisy importance ratio. The paper’s gradient expression for GRPO shows this accumulation explicitly:

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i
\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
$$

The paper’s argument is that these token-wise weights are not negligible. They can vary in ways that matter, and their effects do not cancel cleanly. Instead, as the response length grows, the noisy deviations accumulate across more and more token positions. The result is training noise that becomes progressively more severe with longer generations.

This is why the paper emphasizes that the problem is not merely statistical in the ordinary sense. It is structural. A method that applies an unreliable importance ratio at every token in a long sequence is exposed to repeated noise injection at exactly the scale where the reward signal is most coarse.

# How Clipping Can Amplify Rather Than Fix Instability

Clipping is meant to make policy updates safer by excluding samples that are too far off-policy. In PPO and GRPO, the clipped objective limits the effect of large importance ratios so that training remains within a proximal region of the old policy. But the paper argues that clipping cannot repair a fundamentally misapplied importance weight.

The reason is that clipping acts on the same token-level ratios that are already producing high-variance noise. If the underlying ratio does not correctly represent a useful distribution correction, then clipping only truncates the noise rather than turning it into a reliable signal. The paper goes further and states that the noise introduced by the token-level weights is exacerbated by the clipping mechanism, eventually contributing to model collapse.

The paper also reports that once collapse occurs, simple remedies are ineffective. Reverting to an earlier checkpoint, tuning clipping ranges, extending the generation length, or changing the RL queries does not reliably restore training. This is an important part of the diagnosis: clipping is not just insufficient, but in the paper’s empirical observations it does not prevent the failure mode that token-level importance ratios create.

# Why Instability Becomes Worse with Larger Models and Longer Generations

The paper argues that the instability is especially acute when model size, sparsity, and response length increase. Larger models and longer responses require large rollout batch sizes to keep hardware utilization high during RL, and those large batches are commonly partitioned into mini-batches for gradient updates. That means training is already happening in an off-policy setting, because the samples come from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ while the parameters being optimized are $\theta$.

This problem becomes even more pronounced in Mixture-of-Experts models. The paper reports expert-activation volatility: after one or more gradient updates, the experts activated for the same response can change significantly under the new policy. For the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, the paper observes that roughly $10\%$ of the experts activated under $\pi_\theta$ differ from those under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for the same rollout sample after each update. In deeper MoE models, this effect becomes even more prominent.

That volatility makes the token-level importance ratios fluctuate drastically, further invalidating them. In the paper’s discussion, this is one reason why GRPO training of MoE models required an extra stabilization method called Routing Replay. Even then, the need for such a workaround is treated as evidence that the original token-level design is fragile.

# Empirical Symptoms: Collapse, Irreversibility, and Failed Recovery Attempts

The paper describes GRPO instability in very strong terms. The failure is not just a slowdown or a modest loss of performance. It can lead to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. Once collapse happens, the training run is often unrecoverable.

The paper emphasizes that recovery attempts do not reliably work. Even if one returns to a previous checkpoint and carefully retunes hyperparameters such as clipping ranges, the collapsed training dynamics do not necessarily recover. The same is true if one changes the generation length or switches the RL queries. This irreversibility is one of the most persuasive empirical signs that the instability is not a minor tuning issue but a deeper algorithmic flaw.

The paper’s experiments are presented against this background. GRPO can fail in exactly the regimes where large-scale RL is supposed to help most: larger models, longer reasoning trajectories, and more difficult tasks. That makes the instability especially damaging, because it blocks the very scaling behavior RL is supposed to enable.

# The Design Principle: The Optimization Unit Should Match the Reward Unit

The paper’s diagnosis culminates in a simple design principle: the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. If the reward is granted to the entire response, then the correction and optimization should also be carried out at the response level. That is why the paper treats sequence-level importance weights as the correct object for RL training in this setting.

This principle explains the motivation for GSPO, which replaces token-level importance ratios with a sequence-level ratio based on the likelihood of the full response. The paper presents this not as a cosmetic change, but as a way to restore alignment between reward, importance weighting, and optimization. In the paper’s view, that alignment is what GRPO lacks, and that lack is what makes GRPO unstable.

So the core lesson is not merely that GRPO uses the wrong clipping range or that it needs a better tuning recipe. The deeper issue is that it optimizes tokens while evaluating responses. Once the reward unit and the optimization unit diverge, the gradient becomes noisy, the noise accumulates with sequence length, and the training process can collapse.

The GSPO Algorithm: Sequence-Level Policy Optimization

# Sequence-Level Importance Ratios as the Core Idea

Group Sequence Policy Optimization, or GSPO, is built around a simple but important change in perspective: when the reward belongs to an entire generated response, the policy correction should also be done at the level of the entire response. The paper argues that this is the central flaw in GRPO, where the importance ratio is applied token by token even though the reward is attached to the whole sequence. GSPO replaces that token-level correction with a sequence-level correction, so the unit of optimization matches the unit of reward.

This matters because the training data in RL for language models is off-policy. Responses are generated by an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, but the model being updated is the current policy $\pi_\theta$. Some correction is needed to account for this mismatch. GSPO keeps that idea, but it asks a different question from GRPO: instead of asking how each token changed, it asks how the likelihood of the whole response changed. That is the sequence-level importance ratio, and it is the core design choice that makes GSPO coherent at the response level.

# The GSPO Objective and Group-Normalized Advantages

GSPO optimizes a clipped response-level objective over a group of responses to the same query. If $x$ is a query and $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ is a group of $G$ responses sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)$, then GSPO uses

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min \left( s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\,
\mathrm{clip}\left(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon\right)\widehat{A}_i \right)
\right].
\]

This objective has the same overall structure as the clipped policy objectives used in prior work, but everything important is now defined at the response level. Each response $y_i$ is assigned one importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ and one advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. The final objective averages these clipped terms across the group.

The advantage used by GSPO is the same group-based normalization introduced in GRPO. The reward for each query-response pair is computed by a verifier as $r(x,y_i)\in[0,1]$, and then the rewards inside the response group are normalized by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation:

\[
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}
{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}.
\]

This normalization makes the response’s contribution relative to the other responses generated for the same query. In other words, GSPO does not ask whether a response is good in some absolute sense alone; it asks how good it is compared to the other responses in the same group. That group-relative score then becomes the advantage used in optimization. When the response is later differentiated, all tokens in that response inherit this same response-level advantage.

# Defining the Sequence Ratio from Policy Likelihoods

The sequence-level importance ratio in GSPO is based on the likelihood of the entire response under the current policy and the old policy. Since the policy is autoregressive, the likelihood of a response $y_i$ is

\[
\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}),
\]

where $|y_i|$ is the number of tokens in the response. GSPO defines the importance ratio as

\[
s_i(\theta) =
\left(
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}
\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\left(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
\right).
\]

This ratio summarizes how much the current policy differs from the old policy on the sampled response as a whole. The paper emphasizes that this has a clear theoretical meaning in the context of language generation: the ratio measures how far the sampled response under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ has moved relative to the current policy $\pi_\theta$. Because the reward is also sequence-level, this ratio is the natural quantity to use in clipping and optimization.

The key distinction from GRPO is that GSPO does not assign a separate correction to each token. Instead, a single sequence-level correction controls the entire response. That difference is not merely cosmetic. It changes which samples are treated as reliable for gradient estimation, and it changes how the policy update is weighted across the tokens inside one response.

# Why Length Normalization Is Needed

The exponent $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ is a deliberate length normalization. The paper states two reasons for it. First, it reduces variance. Second, it keeps $s_i(\theta)$ in a unified numerical range. Without this normalization, a few token-level likelihood changes could cause the sequence ratio to fluctuate dramatically, and responses of different lengths would not be comparable under the same clipping range.

This is especially important because the raw sequence likelihood is a product across tokens. As the response becomes longer, the unnormalized likelihood ratio can become extremely sensitive to small changes at individual tokens. By averaging the log-ratios across the tokens and then exponentiating, GSPO turns that product into a quantity that is much easier to compare across response lengths. The paper’s point is not that length normalization changes the objective into something unrelated, but that it makes the sequence-level ratio numerically workable and more stable during training.

This also explains why the same clipping range cannot be reused blindly across responses of very different lengths. A long response and a short response should not be expected to produce importance ratios on the same raw scale unless the ratio is normalized by length. GSPO uses this normalization precisely so that a single clipping rule can be applied more consistently.

# Geometric-Mean Interpretation of the Normalized Sequence Ratio

The normalized sequence ratio in GSPO has a direct geometric interpretation. Since

\[
s_i(\theta) =
\exp\left(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
\right),
\]

it is the exponential of the average log token ratio. Equivalently, it is the geometric mean of the token-level likelihood ratios along the response.

This is useful intuition because it tells you what information GSPO keeps from the token-level likelihoods. It does not collapse the response into a single token or let one token dominate the update on its own. Instead, it summarizes the whole response by combining all token-level changes multiplicatively and then normalizing by length. That makes the ratio a compact response-level indicator of how the current policy differs from the old one on the sampled sequence.

The paper’s motivation for this construction is that the response-level ratio aligns with sequence-level reward and is therefore a more meaningful quantity for policy optimization than the token-level ratio used in GRPO. GSPO is not trying to estimate a token-wise correction for a reward that was never defined token by token. It is trying to represent the overall change in likelihood of the entire sampled response.

# Sequence-Level Clipping and Excluding Off-Policy Responses

GSPO keeps the clipped objective form, but clipping is applied to the entire response ratio $s_i(\theta)$ rather than to each token ratio separately. The objective uses

\[
\min\left(
s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\,
\mathrm{clip}\left(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon\right)\widehat{A}_i
\right),
\]

so the response is treated as one unit when deciding whether it is too far from the old policy. If the ratio falls outside the clipping region, the clipped value is used instead, which limits the effect of overly off-policy responses on the gradient.

This is exactly the alignment the paper argues for. The reward is assigned to the whole response, the ratio is computed for the whole response, and the clipping rule is also applied to the whole response. In that sense, GSPO excludes overly off-policy samples at the response level rather than trying to exclude individual tokens within an otherwise sampled response. The paper contrasts this directly with GRPO, where token-level importance ratios can inject high-variance noise into the update.

The gradient analysis in the paper reinforces this picture. When clipping is omitted for simplicity, the gradient of GSPO is weighted by the same sequence-level ratio for all tokens in a response:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\left(
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}
\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\widehat{A}_i
\cdot
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

The key point is that GSPO avoids token-specific importance weights inside a response. Every token in the response contributes through the same response-level multiplier. That is what makes the update uniform at the response level and avoids the unequal token weighting that the paper identifies as a source of instability in GRPO.

# Why GSPO Clipping Ranges Differ from GRPO's

The paper notes that the clipping ranges used in GSPO and in earlier token-level algorithms such as GRPO differ by orders of magnitude. This is not because one method is simply more conservative than the other in a direct, comparable sense. It is because the importance ratios themselves are defined differently, so their numerical scales are different.

GRPO clips token ratios of the form

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) =
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})},
\]

whereas GSPO clips the length-normalized sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$. Since these ratios are not on the same scale, the clipping thresholds are not directly comparable. The paper’s experimental setting reflects this difference: GSPO uses very small numeric clipping ranges, while GRPO uses much larger ones. The important point is that the clipping ranges have to be interpreted relative to the ratio definition they are designed for.

So when one sees that GSPO’s clipping numbers are much smaller than GRPO’s, that should not be read as a weakness or an inconsistency. It simply reflects the fact that GSPO’s ratio is a normalized response-level quantity, while GRPO’s ratio is a token-level quantity. Because the underlying quantities differ, the same numeric range would not mean the same thing.

# Conceptual Alignment Between Sequence Reward and Sequence Optimization

The deepest idea in GSPO is the alignment between what is rewarded and what is optimized. The paper explicitly states that the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward. Since the verifier assigns a reward to the whole query-response pair $(x,y)$, the policy update should also operate on the whole response $y$ rather than on individual tokens.

GSPO implements this alignment directly. The advantage is computed from response-level rewards within a group, the importance ratio is computed from the likelihood of the full response, and the clipping rule filters out responses that are too far from the old policy. Nothing in the update tries to split the reward into a tokenwise signal that was never observed. This is why GSPO is presented as a more principled use of importance sampling in language-model RL.

In the paper’s framing, this alignment is not just elegant; it is also practical. It is tied to the improved training stability, better sample efficiency, and stronger performance reported for GSPO. But even before looking at experiments, the algorithm’s internal logic is clear: reward the response as a whole, compare the response as a whole, and optimize the response as a whole. That is the central design principle of GSPO.

Gradient-Level Comparison Between GSPO and GRPO

# Deriving the GSPO gradient without clipping

To understand why GSPO behaves differently from GRPO, it is useful to begin with the GSPO objective in its unclipped form. The paper defines GSPO at the sequence level as
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i
\right],
\]
where the advantage is computed from the group of responses to the same query,
\[
\widehat{A}_i
=
\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}
{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)},
\]
and the importance ratio is based on sequence likelihood,
\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\left(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\right).
\]
When clipping is omitted, differentiating this objective is straightforward because the expectation is taken over samples drawn from the fixed old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$.

The key step is that the derivative of $s_i(\theta)$ can be written through the derivative of its logarithm. The paper derives
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i \cdot \nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)
\right],
\]
and then expands $\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)$ as the average of token-level log-likelihood gradients:
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\widehat{A}_i
\cdot
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]
This expression is the starting point for the paper's interpretation of GSPO: the entire response contributes a single scalar sequence ratio, and that one scalar multiplies the aggregate gradient of all tokens in the response.

# How the sequence ratio scales all tokens in a response equally

The most important feature of the GSPO gradient is that every token in a given response receives the same multiplicative scale factor, namely the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$. In the final gradient expression, the token-wise gradients appear only inside the average
\[
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
\]
and this whole average is multiplied by the same scalar $s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i$. So although the response contains many tokens, GSPO does not assign each token its own off-policy correction weight. Instead, the correction is computed once for the full response and then applied uniformly to all token gradients belonging to that response.

This is exactly what the paper means when it says that GSPO performs sequence-level clipping, rewarding, and optimization. The sequence likelihood ratio reflects how far the whole response $y_i$ under the current policy deviates from the old policy, and because the reward is also defined for the whole query-response pair $(x,y_i)$, the gradient update is aligned with the same unit. The paper also notes that length normalization is used in
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}},
\]
so that the importance ratio stays within a unified numerical range and is less sensitive to the length of the response. This matters because, without normalization, likelihood changes from a few tokens could produce large fluctuations in the response-level ratio, and responses of different lengths would not be comparable in a stable way.

# Deriving the GRPO gradient without clipping

GRPO begins from a different starting point. Instead of assigning a sequence-level importance ratio to the full response, it defines a token-level ratio
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]
while using the same group-based advantage for all tokens in a response,
\[
\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i.
\]
Ignoring clipping for the moment, the paper writes the GRPO objective as
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t}
\right].
\]
Since $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ is the same across the tokens in a response, the only token-specific part is the importance ratio.

Differentiating this objective yields
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\widehat{A}_i
\cdot
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]
This is the critical contrast with GSPO. In GRPO, each token gradient is multiplied by its own ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, so the tokens in the same response are not scaled uniformly. Even though the response as a whole has a single advantage $\widehat{A}_i$, the per-token correction is different at each position.

# How GRPO assigns different weights to different tokens

The paper's gradient comparison makes it clear that GRPO does not treat a response as a single optimization unit. Instead, it treats every token as if it needed its own off-policy correction factor. That means the gradient contribution from one token can be amplified while another token in the same response can be reduced, even though both tokens belong to the same rewarded sequence and share the same group advantage $\widehat{A}_i$.

This distinction is not just a matter of notation. In the paper's words, the fundamental difference between GSPO and GRPO is how they weight the gradients of the log likelihoods of tokens. GSPO uses one sequence-level weight for the whole response, while GRPO uses a separate token-level weight at each position. Those token weights are not negligible. The paper emphasizes that they can vary within the ranges associated with clipping, and their unequal contributions accumulate as training progresses. So, even before considering clipping, GRPO already builds in a source of within-response imbalance that GSPO avoids.

The intuitive consequence is that GRPO can end up emphasizing some tokens in a response much more than others, not because the reward says they matter more, but because their current token-level likelihood ratios happen to differ. This is precisely the kind of mismatch the paper argues against: when the reward is sequence-level, the optimization should not fracture that reward into separately weighted token updates.

# The role of token-wise likelihood ratios in gradient noise

The paper identifies the token-wise importance ratio as the core source of instability in GRPO. Its argument is that importance sampling is meant to correct a distributional mismatch by reweighting samples from a behavior distribution. But this logic relies on averaging over many samples from that distribution so that the weighting can actually play a corrective role. GRPO does not do that at the level of token likelihoods. Instead, it applies
\[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\]
to a single sampled token from each next-token distribution. The paper argues that this makes the weight a poor distribution-correction mechanism and turns it into a source of high-variance noise.

That noise is especially problematic because the token-level ratios appear inside a sum over all tokens in the response. As the response becomes longer, the gradient accumulates more of these noisy token-specific factors. The paper states that the noise progressively accumulates with increased response length and is further amplified by clipping. In other words, the more tokens there are, the more opportunities there are for the mismatch between token-level weighting and sequence-level reward to distort the update.

This is why the paper views token-wise likelihood ratios as fundamentally ill-suited for the problem it is trying to solve. They do not merely add detail; they inject variability into the gradient at the wrong granularity. The result is not a cleaner approximation to the objective, but a noisier and less reliable update direction.

# Why unequal token weighting can accumulate harmful effects

Once token-level weights are introduced, the differences among tokens are not isolated. They compound across the entire response and across many training steps. The paper stresses that these unequal weights can accumulate and lead to unpredictable consequences as training proceeds. Because every response contributes many token terms, a small imbalance at each position can become a larger distortion once aggregated over long sequences and repeated updates.

This accumulation is the reason the paper connects GRPO's gradient structure to catastrophic model collapse. The instability does not appear as a single isolated error. Rather, the training noise builds up over time until the model collapses, and the paper describes this collapse as often irreversible. It also reports that once collapse occurs, resuming training is unavailing, even if one reverts to a previous checkpoint, tunes the clipping range, extends generation length, or changes the RL queries.

From the gradient perspective, the problem is clear: if different tokens in the same response are repeatedly scaled by different importance ratios, the updates no longer reflect a coherent response-level judgment. The model is trained by many slightly inconsistent signals, and those inconsistencies are exactly what the paper identifies as the source of instability in large-scale RL training.

# Positive and negative advantage regions under clipping

Clipping is meant to keep updates within a proximal region of the old policy, but the paper shows that it interacts differently with positive and negative advantages. In GRPO, the clipped objective is
\[
\min\left(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t},
\,
\mathrm{clip}\left(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\right)\widehat{A}_{i,t}
\right),
\]
and because $\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i$, the sign of $\widehat{A}_i$ determines how the clipping behaves.

For $\widehat{A}_i>0$, the effective token weights can vary in the range $(0,1+\varepsilon]$. For $\widehat{A}_i<0$, they can vary in the range $[1-\varepsilon,+\infty)$. The paper points out that these unequal weights are not negligible. In practice, this means that the gradient does not simply scale all tokens by one clipped factor. Instead, the behavior of the clipping depends both on the token-level ratio and on the sign of the response advantage, so different tokens within the same response may be clipped in different ways.

GSPO uses the same clipping idea, but at the sequence level. So rather than clipping each token separately according to its own ratio, GSPO clips the response as a whole according to its sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$. That is an important conceptual difference: the sign of the advantage still matters, but the clipping decision is made for the response-level quantity that matches the reward structure.

# Interpreting GSPO as a more coherent response-level credit assignment mechanism

The paper's central principle is simple: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward. Because the verifier gives reward to the entire query-response pair $(x,y_i)$, GSPO treats the entire response as the unit of credit assignment. The group-based advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ summarizes how well a response did relative to other responses to the same query, and the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ scales the update for that whole response.

This makes GSPO more coherent than GRPO at the level of the gradient. In GSPO, all tokens in a response are optimized together, using one response-level importance ratio and one response-level advantage. The gradient therefore says, in effect, "this entire response was good or bad relative to the group, so scale every token in it by the same response-level correction." That is exactly what the paper means by sequence-level rewarding and optimization.

GRPO breaks that coherence by assigning separate importance ratios to each token. Even though the reward is still sequence-level, the correction is not. That mismatch is the reason the paper argues GRPO's objective is ill-posed. GSPO removes that mismatch by making the optimization unit and the reward unit the same.

# What the gradient analysis suggests about training stability

The gradient formulas explain why the paper expects GSPO to train more stably than GRPO. GSPO replaces many token-specific ratio factors with a single sequence-level ratio, so the response contributes a smoother and more unified gradient signal. Since the same scalar multiplies all token log-likelihood gradients in the response, GSPO avoids the within-response imbalance that comes from token-wise importance ratios.

By contrast, GRPO's gradient contains token-specific ratios that can differ substantially from one position to the next. The paper argues that these unequal weights introduce high-variance noise, which becomes worse as sequences get longer and which can be amplified by clipping. That is the mechanism the paper connects to instability and collapse. The gradient analysis therefore does more than compare formulas: it gives a structural explanation for why GRPO can become unstable and why GSPO is more robust.

The paper's empirical findings are consistent with this interpretation. GSPO is reported to train stably, improve continuously with more compute, and perform well on the benchmarks considered. It also resolves stability problems in MoE RL training that previously required extra machinery such as Routing Replay. From the viewpoint of the gradients, this outcome is not surprising: when the optimization signal is aligned with the reward at the sequence level, the update is simpler, less noisy, and more stable.

GSPO-token: A Flexible Token-Level Variant

# Why multi-turn or structured tasks may need token-level advantage control

GSPO-token is introduced as a variant of GSPO for situations where a single advantage value for an entire response is too coarse. The paper explicitly points to multi-turn reinforcement learning as one such setting: in those cases, one may want a finer-grained advantage adjustment than the sequence level. The key idea is not to abandon GSPO’s sequence-level view of importance sampling, but to add flexibility in how the learning signal is distributed across tokens within a response.

This matters because the basic GSPO objective assigns one normalized advantage to the whole response. That is a natural fit when the reward is defined at the sequence level and no additional structure is needed. But if the learning problem calls for token-wise customization, then the model may benefit from letting different tokens carry different advantage values while still using the same sequence-level importance ratio to stabilize the update. GSPO-token is precisely the paper’s answer to that need.

# The GSPO-token objective

GSPO-token keeps the core GSPO structure but replaces the single response-level advantage with token-level advantages. The objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\min \left( s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) 
\right].
\]

Here, $G$ is the group size, meaning the number of responses generated for the same query $x$. The response is still sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, just as in GSPO and GRPO. The difference is that instead of giving every token in a response the same advantage, GSPO-token allows a token-specific advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$.

The important structural point is that GSPO-token does not change the stabilizing idea behind GSPO. It still uses a sequence-level importance ratio, and it still clips that ratio to exclude overly off-policy samples from gradient estimation. What changes is the granularity of the advantage signal. In other words, the paper preserves sequence-level weighting while making the internal advantage assignment more flexible.

# The stop-gradient or detach trick in the ratio definition

To make the token-level objective compatible with sequence-level weighting, GSPO-token defines the token-level ratio as

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta) 
= \mathrm{sg} \left[ s_{i}(\theta) \right] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }.
\]

The operator $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ means “take the numerical value, but stop the gradient.” The paper notes that this corresponds to the \texttt{detach} operation in PyTorch. This construction is a bookkeeping device: it lets the objective be written in token-level form while ensuring that the sequence-level factor $s_i(\theta)$ remains the common weight attached to the response.

The role of the stop-gradient is subtle but important. The denominator of the token likelihood ratio is detached, so it does not contribute gradient. That means the ratio is not being used as an ordinary, fully differentiable fraction in which numerator and denominator both change together. Instead, the denominator is frozen at its numerical value, and only the numerator carries gradient information. This is how GSPO-token can keep the forward value of the ratio unchanged while preserving the desired gradient structure.

# Why the token factor is numerically equal to one

Because the numerator and denominator in the token factor have the same numerical value, the fraction

\[
\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }
\]

is numerically equal to $1$. Therefore, $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$. This is exactly what the paper emphasizes: the token-level expression does not alter the value of the importance ratio in the forward pass.

The reason for writing it this way is not to change the number, but to organize the gradient computation. GSPO-token wants every token in the response to inherit the same sequence-level scaling factor, while still allowing token-specific advantage values $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. The numerical equality to one is what makes this possible without introducing a new, incompatible notion of importance sampling at the token level.

This distinction between numerical value and gradient path is central to understanding the method. The factor equals one as a number, but it is still useful because it preserves the correct dependence on $\pi_\theta$ in the gradient. The paper’s construction ensures that the optimization remains sequence-level in its weighting, even though the objective is written token by token.

# Gradient derivation for GSPO-token

The paper derives the GSPO-token gradient by first omitting clipping for simplicity. Starting from the objective,

\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=
\nabla_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t}
\right].
\]

Using the stop-gradient construction, this becomes

\[
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i (\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\widehat{A}_{i,t} \frac{ \nabla_{\theta} \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }
\right],
\]

which can also be written as

\[
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}  
\left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})  
\right].
\]

The structure of this gradient is the key result. Each token contributes a log-likelihood gradient, but all tokens in the same response are multiplied by the same sequence-level factor $s_i(\theta)$. So GSPO-token does not reintroduce the unstable token-wise importance ratios that motivated GSPO in the first place. Instead, it keeps the stable sequence-level weighting and simply lets the advantage signal vary from token to token.

The paper’s derivation also highlights why GSPO-token is aligned with the basic principle of importance sampling. The response-level likelihood ratio is the meaningful object for correcting off-policy mismatch at the level where the reward is defined. The token-wise gradients then serve as the internal mechanism for distributing the learning signal according to $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$.

# How GSPO-token preserves sequence-level weighting while allowing token-wise advantages

The simplest way to understand GSPO-token is to separate two roles. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ decides how strongly a sampled response should influence learning, based on how far that response under $\pi_\theta$ deviates from the response sampled under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. This is the stabilizing part, and it remains unchanged from GSPO.

The token-level advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$, by contrast, decide how the learning signal is distributed across the tokens inside that response. They do not replace the sequence-level ratio. They sit underneath it. That means the response still gets accepted or clipped as a whole according to the same sequence-level criterion, while the internal gradient contributions can be customized token by token.

This is exactly why GSPO-token is described as flexible. It gives the practitioner a way to adapt the advantage structure for tasks that need more than one scalar per response, without giving up the main stability mechanism of GSPO. The paper emphasizes that GSPO-token and GSPO are numerically identical when the token advantages are all the same, which shows that GSPO-token is a generalization rather than a different algorithmic family.

# Equivalence between GSPO and GSPO-token when all token advantages are equal

When every token in a response receives the same advantage, that is, when

\[
\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i},
\]

GSPO-token collapses to GSPO. The paper states that under this condition, GSPO-token and GSPO are numerically identical in the optimization objective, the clipping condition, and the theoretical gradient. In other words, if token-wise customization is turned off, the two formulations become the same algorithm.

This equivalence is important because it shows that GSPO-token does not change the core learning rule. It only adds an extra degree of freedom. If that freedom is not needed, then there is no functional difference between the two objectives. If it is needed, GSPO-token can express it while still preserving the sequence-level importance ratio that GSPO relies on for stability.

From a conceptual standpoint, this is what makes GSPO-token a clean extension. It does not ask the user to choose between stable sequence-level optimization and token-level control. Instead, it shows that the former can remain intact while the latter is introduced as a more expressive advantage assignment.

# When to prefer GSPO versus GSPO-token in practice

According to the paper, GSPO-token is the right choice when a setting calls for token-wise advantage customization, with multi-turn RL given as the motivating example. In such cases, a single response-level advantage may be too blunt, and the token-level formulation provides the needed flexibility while retaining the sequence-level ratio that stabilizes learning.

GSPO remains the simpler default when token-wise customization is unnecessary. If the task is naturally handled by one normalized advantage per response, then GSPO already provides the sequence-level clipping, rewarding, and optimization that the paper argues are essential for stable large-model RL. GSPO-token is therefore best thought of as a compatible extension: use GSPO when response-level treatment is enough, and use GSPO-token when finer-grained token-level advantages are required.

The paper’s larger message is that the stable part of the method is the sequence-level importance ratio, not the token-wise advantage structure. GSPO-token preserves that stable core while adding a more flexible way to shape the learning signal. When all token advantages are equal, nothing changes. When the task benefits from per-token control, GSPO-token gives that control without sacrificing the sequence-level foundation.

Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol

# The cold-start model based on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base

The paper evaluates GSPO starting from a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base. This matters because the goal of the experiments is not to test GSPO in isolation on a toy setting, but to examine whether it can reliably support large-scale reinforcement learning for a strong language model. By beginning from a relatively capable base model, the evaluation focuses on the stability and efficiency of the reinforcement learning stage itself, which is exactly where the paper argues GRPO becomes fragile.

The phrase “cold-start” indicates that the model is not trained from scratch during the reported RL runs. Instead, it begins from a pretrained foundation and is then optimized further with the RL procedure under study. That setup is important for the paper’s claims about training dynamics: the central question is whether GSPO can continue improving an already competent model without the collapse behavior that the authors associate with GRPO. In other words, the base model gives the reinforcement learning algorithm a realistic and demanding starting point, where instability would be especially costly.

This choice of starting point also aligns with the paper’s emphasis on scaling. The model under study is not a small experimental proxy, but a large model from the Qwen3 family. The authors use this setting to show that their method is relevant for the regime where response lengths are long, compute budgets are large, and training stability becomes a decisive issue.

# Rollout generation and four-way mini-batch partitioning

The training procedure described in the paper generates rollout data and then partitions each batch of rollout data into four mini-batches for gradient updates. This detail is important because it reveals how the optimization is actually carried out in practice. The model first produces responses under an older policy, and then those generated responses are reused across multiple updates rather than discarded immediately. This makes the training more sample-efficient, but it also creates the central off-policy setting that motivates the paper.

Partitioning a rollout batch into mini-batches is a standard practical strategy for improving hardware utilization and making large RL runs tractable. The paper emphasizes that this is not a cosmetic choice: once the same sampled responses are used across several gradient steps, the policy being optimized is no longer exactly the policy that produced the samples. That mismatch is a core reason why importance ratios and clipping matter so much.

The paper’s comparison is therefore not just about an abstract objective function. It is about what happens when the objective is used in a realistic training loop with batched rollouts and repeated updates. The four-way partitioning is part of that realism, and it helps explain why the authors frame the problem in terms of training stability rather than only final benchmark performance. A method that looks acceptable on paper can still fail when its gradients are repeatedly applied to stale samples; the experimental design is meant to expose that behavior.

# Why the training regime is naturally off-policy

The paper argues that the RL regime is naturally off-policy because responses are generated by an old policy, denoted $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, while gradient updates are applied to the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$. This mismatch is not accidental; it is inherent in the way rollout-based RL is run at scale. A large batch of samples is collected first, then reused for optimization. Once optimization begins, the policy parameters change, but the samples do not. As a result, the data being used for learning increasingly reflects an earlier version of the model.

The paper connects this directly to the need for clipping. Clipping is meant to limit the influence of samples that have become too “off-policy” relative to the current model. In the authors’ view, the core issue is not merely that the samples are stale, but that the old and current policies can drift enough during optimization that naïvely trusting token-level ratios becomes unstable. This is why the experimental setting is important: it creates the exact environment in which the difference between token-level and sequence-level importance ratios becomes consequential.

The off-policy nature is especially pronounced when responses are long or when the model is large and sparse, because then even modest parameter updates can alter the probability structure of later tokens or the routing behavior in MoE models. The paper uses this to motivate GSPO’s sequence-level formulation, but the experimental protocol itself already reflects that reality. The data are not collected from a stationary policy; they are repeatedly reused under a changing one.

# Benchmarks: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces

The paper evaluates GSPO on three benchmarks: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces. These benchmarks are chosen to probe the types of capabilities that large-scale RL is meant to strengthen, especially mathematical reasoning and programming. Their inclusion is consistent with the paper’s broader narrative that scaling RL should improve performance on tasks requiring deep reasoning rather than only superficial pattern matching.

AIME'24 is reported using average Pass@1 over 32 samplings. This means the model is evaluated multiple times per question, and the metric reflects the chance that the top sampled answer is correct under repeated sampling. The use of many samplings indicates that the evaluation is not based on a single deterministic completion. Instead, it measures how often the model can surface a correct solution when given repeated opportunities, which is appropriate for reasoning tasks where sampling can expose latent capability.

LiveCodeBench, covering the 202410-202502 range, is reported using average Pass@1 over 8 samplings. Like the AIME evaluation, this metric focuses on whether the model can produce a correct first answer among sampled completions. Because LiveCodeBench targets programming tasks, it offers a complementary view of model improvement. The paper uses it to show that GSPO’s benefits are not limited to one type of reasoning problem.

CodeForces is evaluated using Elo rating. Unlike Pass@1, Elo is not a simple per-problem success rate. It is a relative performance measure that summarizes competitive strength across tasks and opponents in a ranking-like framework. The paper includes this benchmark to show that GSPO’s gains can translate into a more global measure of programming performance. Together, these three benchmarks cover both mathematical and coding ability, giving the evaluation a broader basis than a single task family would provide.

# Evaluation metrics: Pass@1 and Elo rating

The two main metrics in the paper are Pass@1 and Elo rating, and they serve different purposes. Pass@1 is a direct measure of whether a model’s first sampled solution is correct. Because the evaluations use multiple samplings, the reported Pass@1 is averaged over those samples. This metric is especially intuitive for reasoning tasks: if a model can often generate one correct answer among several attempts, then it has likely learned something genuinely useful during RL training.

Elo rating serves a different role. It is used for CodeForces, where relative performance against a pool of competitors or problem-solving strength is more informative than a raw accuracy-style measure. Elo compresses a range of outcomes into a single score that reflects broader competitive skill. In the context of the paper, this helps demonstrate that GSPO is not merely improving isolated sample correctness, but supporting stronger end-to-end coding performance.

The use of both metrics is important because the paper’s claims are about training efficiency and final capability. Pass@1 can reveal how training changes the model’s probability of producing correct responses, while Elo can reflect whether those gains matter in a more competitive programming setting. By reporting both, the evaluation avoids relying on a single perspective.

# Clipping ranges used for GSPO and GRPO

A key part of the experimental protocol is that the clipping ranges are set differently for GSPO and GRPO. For GSPO, the left and right clipping ranges are set to $3\times 10^{-4}$ and $4\times 10^{-4}$, respectively. For GRPO, the corresponding ranges are set to $0.2$ and $0.27$. The paper notes that these values were carefully tuned to ensure a fair comparison.

The fact that the ranges differ by orders of magnitude is not presented as an accident, but as a consequence of the different definitions of importance ratio in the two methods. GSPO uses sequence-level likelihood ratios, so the numerical scale of its ratio is different from the token-level ratios used in GRPO. Therefore, the clipping values must be chosen on the scale appropriate to each algorithm. Comparing them fairly requires tuning each method within its own natural range rather than forcing the same raw numbers onto both.

This detail is central to understanding the paper’s evaluation philosophy. The authors are not claiming superiority by using a weak baseline setting for GRPO. Instead, they state that GRPO’s clipping parameters were tuned carefully, and the difference in performance remains. That framing strengthens the experimental claim that GSPO is not just benefitting from a favorable hyperparameter choice, but from a better-matched objective.

# Fairness considerations in hyperparameter tuning

The paper is explicit that the comparison with GRPO is intended to be fair. The clipping ranges for GRPO were carefully tuned, and the same overall training compute and consumed queries are used in the comparison. This matters because RL training can be very sensitive to hyperparameters, especially clipping. A poorly tuned baseline could collapse simply because the chosen settings are bad, which would not be a meaningful comparison.

The authors also emphasize that GSPO achieves better training accuracy and benchmark performance under the same training compute and consumed queries. That is a strong fairness criterion because it compares methods at equal resource budgets rather than allowing one method more data or more optimization steps. In this way, the evaluation focuses on sample efficiency and optimization quality, not just final score after unlimited training.

Another fairness-related point is that the paper contrasts GSPO with GRPO in the same practical training regime, including rollout generation and repeated mini-batch updates. The result is meant to show that GSPO is robust in the very setting where GRPO struggles, not only in a simplified or idealized setup. The tuning and resource matching together support the paper’s claim that GSPO genuinely improves the RL process.

# The role of query refresh and generation-length extension during training

The paper reports that GSPO can deliver continuous performance improvement through increasing training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending the generation length. These choices are part of the experimental story because they probe whether the algorithm can keep benefiting from additional training rather than saturating or destabilizing.

Regularly updating the query set, or query refresh, means that training does not remain fixed on an unchanging collection of prompts. Instead, the model is exposed to new queries over time. This is important because it tests whether the optimization method can generalize across a shifting set of problems and continue extracting useful learning signals as training proceeds. The paper presents GSPO as capable of handling this regime stably.

Extending generation length is equally significant. Longer generations increase the difficulty of RL because they enlarge the window over which instability can accumulate. The paper explicitly links long responses with the problems of token-level importance ratios and with the need for a better sequence-level method. In the experiments, GSPO is described as continuing to improve when generation length is extended, which supports the authors’ argument that its sequence-level formulation is better suited for long-form reasoning training.

Taken together, query refresh and generation-length extension are not incidental training tricks. They are part of the protocol used to show that GSPO scales in the way a successful large-model RL algorithm should: it remains stable, keeps improving with more compute, and continues to work as the task distribution and response length become more demanding.

Empirical Results: Stability, Efficiency, and Performance

The experiments in this paper are designed to test GSPO under realistic large-model RL conditions. The authors fine-tune a cold-start model initialized from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, split each rollout batch into four mini-batches for gradient updates, and compare GSPO against a carefully tuned GRPO baseline. For GSPO, the left and right clipping ranges are set to $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$, while GRPO uses $0.2$ and $0.27$. The paper tracks both training reward and downstream benchmark performance, so the empirical story is not just about whether optimization runs, but whether it keeps improving and whether that improvement transfers to actual capabilities.

# Reward-curve behavior under GSPO versus GRPO

The first empirical signal the paper emphasizes is the behavior of the training reward curves. In RL for language models, the reward curve is a direct window into whether the optimization process is moving in a controlled direction or becoming erratic. GSPO is reported to proceed stably throughout training, which means the reward signal remains usable across the run rather than breaking down as optimization continues.

This matters because the paper’s central claim is that sequence-level optimization avoids the instability caused by token-level importance ratios in GRPO. The reward curves therefore serve as a practical confirmation of that claim. Instead of seeing unstable progress or abrupt failure, the authors report a training trajectory that remains coherent enough to support continued learning.

# Evidence of stable training throughout long RL runs

A major takeaway from the experiments is that GSPO remains stable over long reinforcement learning runs. The paper explicitly states that GSPO training proceeds stably throughout, and this is a central result rather than a side observation. In the setting studied here, stability is not a convenience; it is a prerequisite for being able to push RL further without triggering collapse.

The importance of this result is clearer when viewed against the background of the paper’s motivation. The authors argue that large language model RL can fail catastrophically when the optimization signal is too noisy or too misaligned with the reward structure. GSPO’s stable behavior throughout training shows that the sequence-level formulation is not only theoretically cleaner, but also robust enough to sustain long runs in practice.

# Continuous gains from increasing training compute

One of the most important scaling findings in the paper is that GSPO continues to improve as training compute increases. The authors report that GSPO can deliver continuous performance improvement through increasing the training compute, which is exactly what one wants from a scalable RL algorithm. Rather than plateauing immediately or becoming unstable as more optimization steps are added, the method keeps turning extra compute into better results.

This is significant because scale only helps if the learning process remains healthy at larger budgets. A method that works on a small run but degrades when compute grows is not truly scalable. By showing that performance continues to improve with more compute, the paper argues that GSPO has the right behavior for large-scale RL training of language models.

# Benefits of regularly updating the query set

The paper also reports that GSPO continues to benefit when the query set is regularly updated. This is one of the training axes along which performance keeps improving, alongside compute and generation length. In the authors’ experiments, refreshing the query set does not interrupt stability; instead, it accompanies the continued upward trend in performance.

This result suggests that GSPO is not tied to a fixed set of prompts in a brittle way. As the training queries change, the algorithm can keep extracting useful learning signal. In the context of the paper, that is another sign that GSPO is suited to large-scale RL setups, where the training distribution may be expanded or refreshed over time.

# Benefits of extending generation length

A third scaling axis highlighted in the experiments is generation length. The paper reports that GSPO shows continuous performance improvement when generation length is extended. This is especially relevant because longer responses are a harder setting for RL training: there are more tokens involved, more opportunities for instability, and a stronger need for a learning rule that stays meaningful over the whole sequence.

GSPO’s sequence-level design is well matched to this setting, and the empirical results reflect that match. Instead of becoming less effective as responses grow longer, the method keeps improving. The paper uses this to argue that GSPO is not just stable in short-horizon optimization, but also capable of supporting the longer reasoning processes that are important in modern language model RL.

# Higher training efficiency under the same compute budget

The paper reports that GSPO achieves superior training efficiency over GRPO under the same training compute and consumed queries. This is one of the clearest empirical wins in the study, because it shows that GSPO is not merely stable; it is also a better use of resources. The authors describe GSPO as achieving better training accuracy under the same budget, which means it extracts more learning progress from each unit of compute.

A particularly interesting observation concerns clipping. GSPO clips entire responses rather than individual tokens, and the paper notes a difference of two orders of magnitude in the fractions of clipped tokens between GSPO and GRPO. Even after adjusting clipping ranges, that disparity in magnitude remains. Yet GSPO still attains higher training efficiency. The paper interprets this counter-intuitive result as evidence that GRPO’s token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient for sample exploitation, while GSPO’s sequence-level approach provides a more reliable learning signal.

# Higher benchmark performance under the same consumed queries

The downstream benchmark curves are the other half of the empirical picture. The paper evaluates the model on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces, reporting average Pass@1 over $32$ samplings for AIME'24, average Pass@1 over $8$ samplings for LiveCodeBench (202410-202502), and Elo rating for CodeForces. Under the same consumed queries, GSPO achieves better benchmark performance than GRPO.

This is important because it shows that the gains are not confined to the training objective. Better reward behavior would be less compelling if it failed to translate into external capability improvements. The reported benchmark results indicate that GSPO’s more efficient training dynamics carry through to real evaluation performance, which is exactly what one expects from a method that aligns optimization at the sequence level with sequence-level reward.

# How the results support GSPO as a scalable RL algorithm

Taken together, the empirical findings support the paper’s broader claim that GSPO is a scalable RL algorithm for large language models. The method is stable throughout long training runs, continues to improve as compute is increased, remains effective when the query set is updated, and still works when generation length is extended. At the same time, it uses training compute more effectively than GRPO and produces stronger benchmark results under the same consumed queries.

The scalability claim is strengthened further by the practical simplifications GSPO brings. In the MoE setting discussed elsewhere in the paper, GSPO eliminates the need for Routing Replay, which GRPO required for normal convergence. The authors also argue that GSPO’s sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision discrepancies between training and inference engines, pointing toward simpler RL infrastructure. The empirical results therefore support not just better learning behavior, but a more practical large-scale training workflow.

# Connection to improvements reported for the latest Qwen3 models

The paper closes the empirical story by connecting GSPO directly to the latest Qwen3 models. The authors state that the merits of GSPO contributed to the remarkable improvements in those models, and that GSPO has been successfully applied to their RL training. In other words, the gains reported in the experiments are presented as having immediate model-development impact rather than remaining a purely academic result.

This connection gives the empirical section its broader significance. GSPO is not only shown to be stable, efficient, and performant in a controlled comparison with GRPO; it is also described as part of the training recipe behind stronger Qwen3 systems. That makes the experiments more than a benchmark exercise: they are evidence that the sequence-level formulation can serve as a practical foundation for scaling RL in frontier language models.

Clipping Behavior and What It Reveals

# Sequence-level clipping versus token-level clipping

The paper’s key change from GRPO to GSPO is not merely a different clipping threshold, but a different unit of clipping. In GRPO, clipping is applied at the token level through the token importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}$. Each token in a response is treated as its own optimization site, and each token can be independently clipped if its ratio moves outside the interval $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$. In GSPO, by contrast, the importance ratio is defined on the entire response as $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$, and clipping is performed on that response-level quantity.

This difference matters because the paper ties the optimization unit to the reward unit. The verifier reward $r(x,y)$ is assigned to the whole query-response pair, so GSPO’s response-level clipping matches the way the reward is defined. In that sense, GSPO does not try to make a token-by-token correction for an inherently sequence-level evaluation signal. Instead, it asks a simpler question: is this whole response sufficiently close to the old policy to be trusted for gradient estimation? If not, the entire response is clipped.

The paper emphasizes that this is not just a stylistic difference. It is the consequence of using a sequence-level importance ratio that has a clear interpretation, namely how far a sampled response has moved from the old policy. Because this ratio is computed from the full sequence likelihood, the clipping mechanism naturally operates at the same level as the reward and the policy update.

# Observed differences in clipping fractions across the two algorithms

The most striking empirical observation in the paper is that GSPO clips far more tokens than GRPO. In the reported experiments, the fraction of clipped tokens differs by roughly two orders of magnitude between the two methods. Importantly, the paper notes that changing the clipping ranges does not remove this large gap. So the difference is not just a consequence of one algorithm using a tighter threshold than the other. It reflects the structure of the objectives themselves.

This is surprising at first glance, because one might expect a method that clips more to be discarding more training signal and therefore to learn less efficiently. The paper shows the opposite: GSPO, despite clipping many more tokens, still achieves better training efficiency and better benchmark performance than GRPO under the same training compute and consumed queries.

The fact that the clipping-fraction gap remains even after adjusting the clipping ranges is part of the point. It suggests that what is being counted as “clipped” is not merely a threshold artifact. Rather, GSPO and GRPO are responding differently to the same off-policy data because their importance ratios are defined differently. A token-level ratio and a sequence-level ratio do not produce the same clipping statistics, even when both are tuned carefully.

# Why GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens indirectly through response rejection

GSPO clips whole responses, so a single sequence-level decision can affect every token in that response. If the response-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ lies outside the clipping region, then the response’s contribution is clipped as a unit. When clipping statistics are measured in terms of tokens, this means that an entire response can show up as a large block of clipped tokens. This is the sense in which GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens indirectly.

The paper frames this behavior as a feature rather than a flaw. GSPO is designed to exclude overly off-policy samples from gradient estimation at the response level. Because the reward is attached to the entire response, once the response-level likelihood has drifted too far, the whole sequence is treated as less trustworthy for learning. This is consistent with the paper’s central principle that the optimization unit should match the reward unit.

There is also an important numerical aspect. The paper notes that the clipping ranges in GSPO and prior algorithms such as GRPO typically differ in order of magnitude because the importance ratios themselves are defined differently. Since the sequence-level ratio averages log-likelihood changes over the whole response, it naturally lives on a different scale from a token-level ratio. So GSPO’s apparently more aggressive clipping cannot be compared to GRPO’s clipping by looking only at raw threshold values.

# The counter-intuitive link between more clipping and better efficiency

One of the paper’s most interesting claims is that clipping more can actually improve training efficiency. In the reported experiments, GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens, which means fewer tokens are available for gradient estimation. Yet GSPO still produces better training accuracy and better benchmark performance than GRPO under the same compute budget.

The paper interprets this counter-intuitive result as evidence that not all training signal is equally useful. If a large fraction of the gradient contributions are coming from samples that are too far off-policy, then keeping them in the optimization step may add more noise than value. GSPO’s stronger clipping behavior seems to remove those unreliable contributions more effectively, leaving a learning signal that is more trustworthy even if it is smaller in raw quantity.

This is why the paper argues that higher clipping is not inherently bad. What matters is whether clipping is filtering out harmful gradient estimates or unnecessarily throwing away useful ones. GSPO’s result suggests that, at least for long-response language model RL in this setting, response-level clipping does a better job of selecting the parts of the rollout batch that are worth learning from.

# What this suggests about noise in GRPO's token-level gradients

The paper uses the clipping statistics to reinforce its broader critique of GRPO. In GRPO, the token-level importance ratio is computed from a single token sample at each position. The paper argues that this does not fulfill the intended role of importance sampling, which is to correct for a mismatch between a target distribution and a behavior distribution using averaging over multiple samples. Because the GRPO weight is applied at each token position rather than at the sequence level, it introduces high-variance noise into the gradients.

The empirical clipping gap is consistent with that diagnosis. If GRPO’s token-level estimates were truly providing clean correction, then one would expect the clipping behavior to translate into effective learning. Instead, the paper finds that GRPO’s token-level gradients are noisy and inefficient, especially as response length grows. The clipping mechanism does not fix that noise; the paper says it can actually exacerbate instability.

This is the key conceptual reading of the clipping results. GSPO’s willingness to clip many more tokens is not a sign of excessive conservatism. It is evidence that the algorithm is filtering out off-policy behavior at the right level, while GRPO’s smaller clipped fraction does not imply cleaner training. The paper’s position is that GRPO can retain tokens whose individual weights are numerically acceptable but whose cumulative effect still injects instability into the optimization process.

# Why fewer but cleaner gradients can outperform more but noisier gradients

The paper’s results show that sample count alone is not a good measure of training quality. GSPO may use fewer tokens for gradient estimation because more of them are clipped, but the resulting gradients are more effective. That is why the method can outperform GRPO in both training efficiency and final performance.

The reason is that a gradient estimate is only valuable if it points in a useful direction. In the paper’s view, token-level GRPO gradients are polluted by weight fluctuations that accumulate over long sequences. A token may contribute to the update simply because its ratio happens to fall within the clipping band, not because it is a particularly reliable learning signal. By contrast, GSPO’s sequence-level clipping and sequence-level importance ratio make the update depend on a more coherent measure of how the whole response compares to the old policy.

The paper’s experimental conclusion is therefore not that clipping is good in general, but that cleaner clipping can beat noisier inclusion. Even though GSPO excludes more of the rollout tokens from gradient estimation, the remaining signal is more aligned with the reward and more stable across training. In the paper’s terminology, it is a more reliable and effective learning signal.

# How clipping statistics can be used diagnostically in RL training

The paper’s clipping analysis does more than explain GSPO’s performance. It also shows that clipping statistics can serve as a diagnostic for the quality of the RL objective. If a method clips a large fraction of tokens and still learns well, that suggests the clipped samples were not the source of useful gradient information. If a method clips less but performs worse, that suggests the retained gradients may be noisy or misaligned with the reward.

This is especially important because the paper observes that the difference in clipped-token fractions between GSPO and GRPO remains large even after tuning the clipping ranges. That persistence tells us the discrepancy is structural. It reflects how the algorithms define importance ratios, how they distribute reward across tokens or sequences, and how they handle off-policy data. In that sense, clipping statistics are not just bookkeeping; they reveal whether the learning signal is being measured at the right level.

The paper also uses these statistics to support a broader diagnosis of GRPO. Since token-level importance ratios can fail to correct distribution mismatch and can accumulate noise over long responses, a pattern of clipping behavior that looks modest may still hide unstable optimization. GSPO’s sequence-level clipping makes the problem visible in a different way: it sharply filters entire responses, and the resulting statistics help show that the method is learning from a more coherent subset of the data. For this reason, clipping behavior is not merely a side detail of the algorithm. In the paper, it is one of the clearest windows into whether the RL update is stable, efficient, and properly aligned with the reward.

Mixture-of-Experts Training and the End of Routing Replay

# What makes MoE models different from dense models

The paper treats Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, models as a special case of large language model training because of their sparse activation nature. In the paper’s setting, this sparsity matters most during reinforcement learning, where the model is not just being evaluated once, but repeatedly updated from rollout data. For a given response, only certain experts are activated, so the computation used to assign probability to that response depends on which experts are currently active. This is already different from the dense-model case the paper contrasts with, where such routing-dependent instability does not appear in the same way.

That difference becomes important because RL training in the paper is off-policy: responses are generated by an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and then used to update a current policy $\pi_\theta$. In dense models, this off-policy gap is already something clipping is meant to manage. In MoE models, the paper argues, the gap is even harder to handle because the policy update can also change which experts are activated. So the training problem is not only about comparing two policies; it is also about comparing two different activated networks for the same response.

# Expert-activation volatility across policy updates

The paper introduces the term expert-activation volatility to describe a specific instability in MoE reinforcement learning. After one or more gradient updates, the experts activated for the same response can change significantly. This means that the model’s internal path for the same query-response pair is not fixed across updates. The policy has not only shifted in its output probabilities; it may also be using a different subset of experts to produce those probabilities.

The paper emphasizes that this volatility becomes visible even after a single RL update. In other words, the routing pattern is not something that only drifts slowly over the course of training. Instead, it can change in a way that is large enough to matter immediately when computing the next optimization step. That is why the authors treat expert-activation volatility as a core cause of instability, rather than as a minor implementation detail.

# Why changing expert routes destabilizes token-level importance ratios

GRPO, the baseline discussed in the paper, uses token-level importance ratios of the form $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$. In principle, such a ratio is supposed to correct for the fact that samples came from the old policy rather than the current one. The paper’s criticism is that, in MoE models, this token-level ratio is being asked to do more than it can reliably do. If the activated experts change between $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$, then the numerator and denominator are no longer comparable in the way importance sampling intends.

This is why the paper says the token-level importance weights in GRPO are misapplied and effectively invalidated in MoE settings. Rather than acting as a clean correction for distribution mismatch, they become a source of high-variance noise. Because the ratio is computed for every token, that noise can accumulate over long responses. The paper also notes that clipping can amplify the problem rather than solve it, which is one reason the instability can eventually lead to catastrophic model collapse.

# The observed scale of routing changes in the Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base setting

The paper does not present expert-activation volatility as a theoretical possibility only. It gives a concrete empirical example using the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model. After each RL gradient update, and for the same rollout sample, roughly $10\%$ of the activated experts under the new policy $\pi_\theta$ differ from those under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. That is a substantial routing shift for the same response.

The significance of this number is not just that some experts change, but that they change repeatedly as training proceeds. Since the same response can be routed differently after every update, the mismatch between old and current policy is continually being introduced into the gradient estimate. The paper uses this observation to argue that token-level importance ratios are being computed in a setting where their underlying assumptions are already breaking down in practice.

# How this problem worsens with deeper MoE architectures

The paper explicitly notes that expert-activation volatility becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models. That matters because deeper architectures have more layers in which routing can differ, so the instability has more opportunity to accumulate. The result is that token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can fluctuate even more drastically, which further invalidates them as a stable training signal.

This is one reason the paper frames the problem as especially serious for large-scale RL. As models grow larger and deeper, the same response may be evaluated through increasingly different activated pathways after each update. In that setting, the token-level objective in GRPO becomes more fragile, and the instability is no longer easy to dismiss as a small numerical artifact. It becomes a structural training problem.

# Routing Replay: caching and replaying old expert routes

Before GSPO, the paper says the team used Routing Replay as a workaround. The idea is simple in concept even though it is operationally involved: cache the experts activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and then replay those routing modes when evaluating the current policy $\pi_\theta$ for the purpose of computing the importance ratios. In other words, the old and current policies are forced to share the same activated network when the ratio is measured.

This replay makes the comparison much more controlled. If both policies use the same activated experts for a given token, then the token-level probability ratio is no longer confounded by changes in the route itself. The paper describes this as restoring stability to the token-level importance ratios and ensuring that the same activated network is optimized consistently across gradient updates. In the authors’ previous MoE training setup, this was the mechanism that made GRPO converge normally.

# Why Routing Replay helps GRPO converge

Routing Replay helps because it patches the specific place where GRPO becomes unstable on MoE models. GRPO’s optimization objective is built around token-level advantages and token-level ratios. Once the activated experts become inconsistent across old and current policies, those ratios become noisy and unreliable. By replaying the old routing modes, the ratio is measured against a fixed computational path, so the gradient signal is less distorted.

The paper is clear that this was not a cosmetic improvement. Figure-level evidence is used to support the claim that Routing Replay is an essential technique for the normal convergence of GRPO training on MoE models. In the paper’s framing, the workaround does not merely improve performance slightly; it is what keeps the training process from becoming unstable when token-level optimization is used on sparse models.

# The memory, communication, and capacity costs of Routing Replay

Although Routing Replay stabilizes training, the paper argues that it comes with real costs. Reusing routing modes requires extra memory and communication overhead, because the activated experts from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ must be cached and then reused during optimization. That extra bookkeeping makes the training system more complex.

The paper also says that Routing Replay can limit the actual capacity of the MoE model. The reason is that reusing routing modes imposes an artificial constraint on how the model can behave during training. Instead of allowing the model to use its full sparse-expert flexibility, the optimization process is forced to remain tied to previously cached routes. So while the workaround makes GRPO workable, it does so by adding complexity and by reducing how naturally the model can express its capacity.

# Why GSPO is robust to routing volatility at the sequence-likelihood level

GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay by changing the unit of optimization. Instead of defining importance ratios token by token, GSPO defines the ratio at the level of the whole response:
$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
$
The paper’s key claim is that this sequence-level ratio has a clear theoretical meaning: it measures how far the entire response under the old policy deviates from the current policy, and it does so in a way that matches the sequence-level reward.

This matters for MoE training because GSPO is not trying to make a token-level correction that is sensitive to each local routing choice. The paper notes that the MoE model still maintains its language modeling capability, so the sequence likelihood does not fluctuate as drastically as the token-level path might suggest. By working at the sequence level, GSPO becomes robust to the routing volatility that breaks token-level importance ratios. The length normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ also helps reduce variance and keeps the ratio in a unified numerical range.

# How GSPO enables stable MoE RL without special routing constraints

With sequence-level optimization, GSPO can compute importance ratios conventionally and optimize stably even when expert routes vary. The paper’s conclusion is that GSPO is fully capable of normal convergence on MoE models without Routing Replay. This is not because routing volatility disappears, but because GSPO no longer depends on token-level ratios that are invalidated by that volatility.

The broader consequence is that GSPO simplifies the training system while also making it more robust. The model no longer needs special routing constraints to make RL work, so it can use its full capacity without artificial limitations. In the paper’s view, this is the deeper reason GSPO is a better fit for large-scale MoE reinforcement learning: it aligns the optimization objective with the sequence-level reward and avoids the instability introduced by token-level off-policy correction. That is why the authors describe GSPO as resolving the expert-activation volatility issue fundamentally, rather than merely working around it.

Practical Implications for RL Systems and Infrastructure

# Training-Engine versus Inference-Engine Likelihood Computation

One of the paper’s most important systems-level observations is that RL training for language models often splits into two distinct stages: a rollout stage, where responses are generated, and an optimization stage, where those responses are used to update the policy. In the paper’s setup, this separation matters because the response samples are generated from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, while the policy being optimized is $\pi_\theta$. That means the likelihoods attached to sampled responses are not just bookkeeping details; they are part of the optimization signal itself.

The paper states that, in practice, because of precision discrepancies between training engines such as Megatron and inference engines such as SGLang and vLLM, the likelihoods of sampled responses under the old policy are typically recomputed with the training engine. This is a direct consequence of the fact that the likelihood values are being used inside the optimization objective. If the system that generated the sample and the system that evaluates it do not agree closely enough numerically, the importance ratios that drive learning can become inconsistent. GSPO changes this dynamic because it shifts the optimization unit from tokens to whole responses, making the likelihood computation pipeline less fragile.

# Precision Discrepancies in Megatron, SGLang, and vLLM

The paper highlights a practical mismatch between training and inference infrastructure: the same sampled response may be assigned slightly different likelihoods depending on which engine computes it. This is not presented as a theoretical issue but as an engineering reality that arises when large RL systems are built across different software stacks. The paper explicitly names Megatron as an example of a training engine and SGLang and vLLM as examples of inference engines.

This discrepancy matters because RL optimization uses likelihood ratios as a correction mechanism. In a token-level method, even a modest numerical difference at each token position can affect the ratio used in the gradient. When those differences occur repeatedly across long responses, they can accumulate. The paper’s central claim is that this accumulation is one of the reasons GRPO becomes unstable in large-scale settings, especially when response length grows. GSPO is designed to be less sensitive to that kind of cross-engine mismatch by working at the sequence level instead of the token level.

# Why Token-Level Objectives Often Require Exact Recomputation Under the Training Engine

The paper’s critique of token-level objectives is not only mathematical but also operational. In GRPO, the optimization signal is built from token-wise importance ratios,
$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$.
Because these ratios are defined at the granularity of individual tokens, they depend on a very fine numerical agreement between the likelihoods produced by different parts of the system. The paper argues that this is already problematic in ordinary large-language-model training, and it becomes even more difficult for long responses and more complex tasks.

In practical terms, this is why token-level objectives often push systems toward recomputing likelihoods under the training engine. The ratio has to reflect the same policy and the same numerical conventions as the optimization step, otherwise it stops serving as a reliable correction for off-policy data. The paper goes further in the MoE setting: token-level ratios become even less trustworthy when expert activation changes across updates. To manage that, the authors previously used Routing Replay, which caches the activated experts under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and replays those routing modes in $\pi_\theta$. The need for such a workaround shows how demanding token-level optimization can be for the infrastructure stack.

# Why Sequence-Level Likelihoods May Be More Tolerant to Precision Mismatch

GSPO replaces the token-level ratio with a sequence-level importance ratio,
$s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$.
The paper’s key systems intuition is that this quantity has a much clearer meaning for generation: it measures how far the full response has moved from the behavior policy. Because the reward is assigned to the entire sequence, the sequence-level ratio matches the unit of reward much more naturally than a token-level ratio does.

This change also matters for numerical robustness. The paper explicitly says that sequence-level likelihoods are, intuitively, more tolerant of precision discrepancies. That tolerance comes from the fact that the optimization signal is compressed into one response-level quantity rather than being exposed at every token position. The length normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ further helps by reducing variance and keeping the ratio in a unified numerical range. In other words, GSPO is not just a different objective; it is a way of making the optimization signal less brittle across heterogeneous systems.

# Potential to Use Inference-Engine Likelihoods Directly in Optimization

A major practical consequence of GSPO is that it makes direct use of inference-engine likelihoods plausible. The paper states that, because GSPO uses sequence-level rather than token-level likelihoods, it may be possible to use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine directly for optimization, avoiding recomputation with the training engine. This is a meaningful simplification because the rollout system already computes a response-level likelihood during generation.

The important point is not merely that one computation can be skipped. It is that the boundary between generation and optimization becomes cleaner. If the inference engine can supply a sequence-level likelihood that is sufficiently reliable for GSPO, then the training stack no longer has to reproduce that score just to satisfy the optimizer. The paper emphasizes that this possibility is especially valuable in systems where generation and optimization are already separated, because it reduces the amount of coordination required between the two sides.

# Benefits for Partial Rollout Pipelines

The paper explicitly calls out partial rollout as one of the settings that can benefit from GSPO’s likelihood design. While it does not redefine partial rollout in detail, the systems implication is clear: when a rollout pipeline does not keep generation and optimization tightly coupled in a single engine, it is useful for the optimization stage to accept the likelihoods produced by the rollout stage without further recomputation.

GSPO is well matched to that kind of workflow because its objective consumes a sequence-level score for each response. That means the pipeline can move a completed response and its associated likelihood from the rollout system into the optimizer as a single unit. The paper’s point is that this makes the infrastructure easier to work with in situations where the rollout process is naturally staged or only partly generated before optimization decisions are made.

# Benefits for Multi-Turn RL Pipelines

Multi-turn RL is another setting the paper identifies as especially likely to benefit from GSPO. In such a pipeline, the model participates in repeated interaction, so likelihood information may need to move across multiple turns of generation and optimization. The paper does not present this as a separate algorithmic rule; rather, it points out that the practical convenience of using inference-engine likelihoods directly becomes more valuable when the interaction structure is multi-turn.

This connects naturally to GSPO-token, the paper’s token-level variant, which allows token-wise advantage customization while preserving the sequence-level importance ratio. That variant suggests that GSPO is flexible enough to support settings where different parts of a response may need different advantage assignments. Even so, the central infrastructure benefit remains the same: by anchoring the ratio at the sequence level, GSPO reduces the need to re-evaluate token probabilities under the training engine at every turn of the interaction.

# Benefits for Training-Inference Disaggregated Frameworks

The paper also argues that GSPO is well suited to training-inference disaggregated frameworks, where rollout generation and training are intentionally separated. In such architectures, the natural question is how much of the rollout computation must be repeated on the training side. Under token-level objectives, the answer is often “a lot,” because the optimization signal depends on precise token-wise likelihoods. Under GSPO, the paper suggests that much less recomputation may be necessary.

This is important because the sequence-level objective reduces the dependence on exact token-by-token agreement across systems. If the inference engine can provide the needed sequence likelihood directly, then the data exchanged between the two sides of the system becomes simpler: the rollout side produces the response and its sequence score, and the training side consumes that information for optimization. The paper presents this as a practical route to making disaggregated RL stacks more efficient and less cumbersome.

# How GSPO May Simplify RL Infrastructure End to End

Taken together, the paper’s systems claims point to a broader conclusion: GSPO can simplify RL infrastructure from the likelihood computation layer all the way to MoE stabilization. At the likelihood layer, it reduces the need for token-level recomputation under the training engine. At the optimization layer, it works with response-level scores that are less sensitive to precision mismatch. At the MoE layer, it removes the need for Routing Replay, which the paper says was previously essential for the normal convergence of GRPO on sparse models.

The resulting simplification is not just conceptual. The paper notes that Routing Replay adds memory and communication overhead and can limit the actual capacity of the MoE model. GSPO avoids that workaround by relying on sequence likelihoods, which are less affected by expert-activation volatility. In the authors’ view, this combination of properties makes GSPO a cleaner foundation for large-scale RL training, and they state that these benefits contributed to the improvements in the latest Qwen3 models. From an infrastructure perspective, the key lesson is that choosing the right unit of optimization can remove entire classes of special-case engineering.

Synthesis, Limitations, and Future Directions

# The paper's central lesson about matching reward level and optimization level

The most important conceptual lesson in the paper is that the level at which a reward is defined should match the level at which optimization is performed. In the setting studied here, the verifier assigns reward to an entire query-response pair $(x, y)$, not to individual tokens in the response. That means the natural object being judged is the full sequence. The paper argues that when an algorithm optimizes token-by-token while the reward is sequence-level, it introduces a mismatch between what is being scored and what is being updated. GSPO is designed to remove that mismatch by making the optimization objective sequence-level as well.

This lesson is presented not as a minor implementation detail, but as the core reason GRPO becomes unstable at scale. GRPO attaches a response-level advantage to each token while still weighting each token with its own token-level importance ratio. The paper argues that this combination is fundamentally ill-posed when responses are long and models are large. As training proceeds, the small discrepancies at the token level accumulate across long sequences, and the clipping mechanism can amplify rather than tame the resulting noise. GSPO is the paper’s answer to that problem: if the reward is for the whole response, then the importance correction and clipping should also be for the whole response.

# Why sequence-level importance correction is more principled in this setting

The paper motivates sequence-level importance correction using the basic principle of importance sampling. Importance sampling is meant to estimate an expectation under a target distribution by reweighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution. In the paper’s notation, a response $y$ sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ should be corrected by the ratio $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)$. This is the ratio that directly compares how likely the entire sampled response is under the current policy versus the behavior policy. Because the response is the sampled object, the paper argues that this is the principled unit of correction.

The contrast with GRPO is sharp. GRPO uses token-level ratios of the form $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$, but each token is only one sample from a next-token distribution. The paper’s key critique is that this does not fulfill the corrective role that importance sampling is supposed to play. Instead of properly aligning distributions, it introduces high-variance noise into the gradient estimate. This matters even more as response length grows, because the noise compounds over many tokens. GSPO’s sequence-level ratio is therefore more principled not only because it matches the reward level, but also because it avoids treating single-token fluctuations as if they were sufficient to correct a full-sequence mismatch.

The paper also emphasizes that sequence-level correction is especially meaningful when combined with sequence-level clipping. Clipping is meant to exclude overly off-policy samples from gradient estimation. If the object being updated is the whole response, then clipping the whole response is the consistent choice. GSPO also uses length normalization in the sequence ratio, namely the exponent $1/|y_i|$, to reduce variance and keep ratios in a common numerical range across responses of different lengths. Without this normalization, a few token-level likelihood changes could create extreme sequence ratios, and longer or shorter responses would not be comparable under the same clipping rule.

# Summary of GSPO's advantages over GRPO

The paper presents GSPO as a direct improvement over GRPO in stability, efficiency, and performance. Empirically, GSPO trains stably across the paper’s experiments, while GRPO is described as vulnerable to severe instability and even catastrophic model collapse. GSPO also achieves better training efficiency, meaning that it obtains stronger performance improvements under the same training compute and the same consumed queries. On the benchmark side, the paper reports stronger curves on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces.

A major practical advantage is that GSPO appears to continue improving as training compute increases, the query set is refreshed, and generation length is extended. That is important because the paper’s broader motivation is to support scaled RL training for large language models, especially on difficult tasks that require deeper and longer reasoning. Another notable advantage is that GSPO stabilizes RL training for large Mixture-of-Experts models. In the paper’s discussion of MoE systems, GRPO needed an additional Routing Replay strategy to converge normally, but GSPO removed that need. That is not a small convenience: it means the algorithm itself is more robust to the dynamic expert-routing behavior that makes MoE training difficult.

The gradient analysis in the paper helps explain why these advantages arise. GRPO assigns different token-level weights within a response, and those weights can vary across tokens and across training steps in ways that are not negligible. GSPO instead weights all tokens in a response equally through the same sequence-level ratio. This removes one instability source: token-level gradient weights no longer fluctuate independently within the same response. The paper’s experiments on clipping fractions reinforce this picture. Even though GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens, it still trains more efficiently, which the paper interprets as evidence that GRPO’s token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and less effective for sample exploitation.

# How GSPO changes the design space for LLM RL algorithms

GSPO changes how one thinks about RL infrastructure for language models. The paper’s argument is not simply that a different objective performs better, but that the entire design space becomes simpler when the optimization is organized around sequences rather than tokens. In the paper’s discussion of infrastructure, this shows up in the observation that sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision discrepancies between training engines and inference engines. Because of that, GSPO can potentially use likelihoods returned by the inference engine directly, rather than requiring recomputation with the training engine. The paper highlights this as especially useful in partial rollout, multi-turn RL, and training-inference disaggregated frameworks.

The same simplification appears in the MoE setting. GRPO’s token-level importance ratios are sensitive to expert-activation volatility, because after a gradient update the activated experts for the same response can change substantially. The paper reports that in a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, roughly $10\%$ of the experts activated under the new policy differed from those under the old policy after each update for the same rollout sample. That kind of change undermines the validity of token-level importance ratios. GSPO sidesteps the problem by using the sequence likelihood, which the paper argues is much less sensitive to such routing fluctuations. As a result, GSPO expands the design space for RL algorithms by making stable training possible without elaborate workarounds like Routing Replay.

# Potential limitations and unanswered theoretical questions

Although the paper provides strong empirical evidence for GSPO, it does not present a complete theoretical account of every aspect of the algorithm. The main stability claims are grounded in experiments and gradient intuition, but the paper does not offer a full convergence theory or a formal characterization of exactly when sequence-level clipping is sufficient to prevent collapse. The paper’s central claim is that the sequence-level formulation is better aligned with importance sampling and with sequence-level reward, and the experimental results support that claim, but many details of the stability mechanism remain open in a broader theoretical sense.

Another limitation is that the algorithm’s practical behavior still depends on design choices such as the clipping range and the sequence-length normalization. The paper notes that the clipping ranges for GSPO and prior algorithms typically differ by orders of magnitude because the importance ratios themselves are defined differently. That means one cannot simply transplant a clipping setting from GRPO to GSPO. The paper shows careful tuning in its experiments, but it does not provide a universal rule for choosing clipping thresholds across different tasks, response lengths, or model scales. Likewise, length normalization is motivated and empirically useful, but the paper does not claim that the specific normalization used in GSPO is uniquely optimal.

A further open issue is the tradeoff between simplicity and granularity. GSPO deliberately works at the sequence level, which is a strength when the reward is sequence-level. But the paper also introduces GSPO-token to support finer-grained advantage customization in settings like multi-turn RL. This suggests a limitation of the plain sequence-level objective: some tasks may benefit from token-wise adjustment even if the correction itself is sequence-level. In other words, GSPO solves a major mismatch, but it does not eliminate the need to think carefully about how advantage information should be distributed within a response.

# Open questions on variance, bias, and clipping design

The paper makes a strong case that token-level importance ratios in GRPO create high-variance noise, while sequence-level ratios in GSPO are more stable. What remains open is a more detailed understanding of the variance behavior under different conditions. The paper emphasizes that length normalization reduces variance and keeps the ratio in a unified numerical range, but it does not fully map out how variance scales with response length, model size, or the structure of the generated text. Because the sequence likelihood is a product over tokens, even the normalized ratio can still be sensitive to many small changes, and the paper leaves open how this sensitivity should be best managed in general.

Clipping is another place where bias and variance considerations matter, even though the paper focuses mainly on empirical stability. The whole purpose of clipping is to exclude overly off-policy samples, but doing so inevitably changes which samples contribute to the gradient. The paper shows that GSPO’s clipping works well in practice and that the fraction of clipped tokens differs dramatically from GRPO, yet it does not provide a formal study of how clipping shapes the bias-variance tradeoff. That makes clipping design a clear area for future work. The key question is not just how much to clip, but how to choose the clipping rule so that it remains compatible with the sequence-level interpretation of the objective.

The paper also implies a second open question: how should one think about the scale of clipping when the definition of the importance ratio itself changes? Because GSPO’s ratio is based on sequence likelihood rather than token likelihood, the numeric scale of the ratio changes substantially. The paper observes that the corresponding clipping ranges are therefore very different from those used in GRPO. This means that future research may need to develop more principled guidance for clipping design that takes the unit of optimization into account.

# Possible extensions to richer reward structures and multi-turn interaction

The paper explicitly points to multi-turn RL as a setting where more fine-grained advantage adjustment may be useful. For that purpose, it introduces GSPO-token, a token-level objective variant that preserves the sequence-level importance ratio while allowing token-wise advantage customization. Importantly, the paper shows that when all token advantages are set equal, GSPO-token is numerically identical to GSPO in the optimization objective, clipping condition, and theoretical gradient. This is a useful design property: the framework can support richer reward structures without abandoning the sequence-level correction principle that makes GSPO stable.

This suggests a natural path for future work inside the paper’s framework. If a task provides rewards or preferences at a finer granularity than the whole response, one can ask how to assign token-level or turn-level advantages while still maintaining the stability gains of sequence-level weighting. The paper does not fully develop such extensions, but it clearly identifies the need for them in scenarios like multi-turn RL. The challenge is to preserve the core GSPO insight, namely that the importance ratio should remain sequence-based even when the advantage signal becomes more detailed.

Another possible direction is to generalize the group-based advantage computation itself. In the paper, the advantage is built from normalized rewards across multiple responses to the same query. That construction is central to both GRPO and GSPO because it removes the need for a value model. Future work could explore how different reward structures interact with this group normalization while still preserving the stability benefits of GSPO. The paper does not work out these possibilities, but it makes clear that richer interaction structures will likely require matching refinements in the advantage signal rather than a return to token-level importance correction.

# Broader implications for scaling RL in large language models

The broader message of the paper is that scaling RL for language models is not only a matter of more compute or bigger models. It also requires algorithmic choices that respect the structure of generation and reward. GSPO is presented as evidence that sequence-level optimization can support stable large-scale RL, especially when responses are long, models are sparse, and training is off-policy across mini-batches. In that sense, the paper argues that sequence-level policy optimization can become a foundational building block for the next stage of language-model training.

The practical significance is reinforced by the paper’s discussion of Qwen3. GSPO is described as having contributed to the latest Qwen3 models, and the paper frames this as evidence that stable RL algorithms can directly enable performance gains in frontier systems. The architectural simplifications are also important: GSPO can reduce dependence on auxiliary stabilization methods like Routing Replay and may simplify the interface between training and inference systems. That matters for future RL infrastructure because it suggests that some of the complexity in current pipelines is not inevitable, but rather a consequence of using an optimization objective that is not well matched to the reward structure.

Taken together, the paper’s conclusion is that large-scale RL for language models should be built on a principled relationship between reward, importance correction, and optimization level. GSPO is the paper’s concrete demonstration of that idea. It does not settle every theoretical question, but it shows that when the optimization unit matches the reward unit, stable and scalable training becomes far more plausible. For future research, that is the main lesson to carry forward.
