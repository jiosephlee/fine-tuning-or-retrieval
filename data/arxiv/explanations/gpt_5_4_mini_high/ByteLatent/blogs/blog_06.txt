\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

# Can We Turn Today's LLMs Into Tokenizer-Free Models?

LLMs are usually described in terms of their layers, parameter count, and training data. But there is another design choice hiding in plain sight: the tokenizer. Before the model sees anything, raw text is compressed into a fixed vocabulary of subword units. That makes training efficient, but it also bakes in a particular view of language.

The Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, asks a useful question: what if that constraint was optional? And more specifically, what if we could take a pretrained tokenizer-based model like Llama 3.1 and “byte-ify” it instead of retraining everything from scratch?

## Why tokenizers are a hidden architectural choice

Tokenizers are often treated like plumbing, but they shape the model more than we usually admit. They decide how text is split, how much sequence length the model has to process, and how the output vocabulary is organized. The paper argues that this can create a few problems: sensitivity to input noise, weak handling of spelling and orthography, awkward behavior on multilingual data, and a bias toward the particular compression scheme chosen by the tokenizer.

That matters because many of the cases where LLMs feel brittle are exactly the cases where raw characters or bytes would help. If you misspell a word, mix scripts, or perturb the text slightly, the tokenizer can turn a small change into a very different token sequence. A byte-level model does not have that problem, because bytes are always there.

The tradeoff, historically, has been compute. Modeling bytes means longer sequences, and longer sequences used to make transformers too expensive at scale.

## BLT's answer: dynamic byte patches

BLT does not simply replace tokens with bytes and hope for the best. Instead, it introduces a patch-based architecture. The model reads raw bytes, groups them into patches, processes those patches with a large global transformer, and then decodes back to bytes.

The key idea is that patches are not fixed vocabulary items. They are dynamically formed groups of bytes. BLT can make patches longer when the next-byte prediction is easy, and shorter when the data is more complex. The paper uses entropy estimates from a small byte-level language model to decide where patch boundaries should go. In other words, it tries to spend more compute where the information density is high and less where the next byte is predictable.

This is important because it changes the usual tokenization tradeoff. Standard tokenizers use the same compute budget for every token, even though some spans of text are much harder than others. BLT instead allocates computation dynamically through patch size.

## The interesting part: starting from Llama 3.1

The most forward-looking experiment in the paper is not training BLT from scratch. It is initializing BLT from a pretrained Llama 3.1 model.

The setup is straightforward in concept. The large global transformer inside BLT is initialized with Llama 3.1 weights. Then the byte-level local encoder and decoder are trained to map raw bytes into patches and back again. The global transformer is still updated, but with a learning rate that is one-tenth of the rate used for the local encoder and decoder.

That makes the experiment feel like a practical migration path rather than a purely new model family. The pretrained LLM does not get thrown away. Instead, its central reasoning stack is reused, while BLT learns a new byte-level interface around it.

The authors describe this as a way of turning a tokenizer-based model into a tokenizer-free one. That is a big idea, because retraining a foundation model from scratch is expensive, and tokenizers are usually frozen long before deployment. If this transfer works well, then changing representation does not necessarily mean starting over.

## What the paper found

The results are promising, but they are not magic.

When BLT is initialized from Llama 3.1, it outperforms both the plain Llama 3 baseline and the from-scratch BLT baseline under the same flop budget. It also beats the BLT-Entropy model on MMLU, even though that baseline was trained on more data. The paper interprets this as evidence that reusing a pretrained tokenizer-based backbone can significantly reduce the training flops needed to reach a strong byte-level model.

At the same time, the conversion is not perfect. Compared with the original Llama 3.1 model, the byte-ified BLT still loses some ground, with slight declines on MMLU and HumanEval and larger drops on some other tasks. So the experiment does not show that tokenizer-free models already dominate their tokenized predecessors. It shows that the gap can be narrowed, and that existing pretrained models may transfer into a byte-level form more effectively than one might expect.

That nuance matters. The result is not “tokenizers are obsolete.” It is “we may be able to reuse today’s foundation models even if we want to move away from tokenizers.”

## Why this could reduce retraining costs

The practical appeal is clear. Training a frontier model from scratch is one of the most expensive things in machine learning. If the expensive global transformer can be reused, then the remaining cost is learning the byte-level front and back ends and adapting the system to the new representation.

That could make tokenizer-free adaptation much more realistic. Instead of rebuilding a model every time you want a different input representation, you can potentially preserve the pretrained core and learn how to feed it raw bytes. For domains where spelling, noise, or multilingual text matters, that is especially attractive.

BLT’s broader scaling results make this even more interesting. The paper shows that BLT matches tokenization-based models at scale up to 8B parameters, while also reducing inference flops by as much as 50% when patch sizes are larger. So the byte-level path is not just about robustness. It also creates a compute knob that tokenizers do not give you: patch size can grow alongside model size under a fixed inference budget.

## What this means for the future of foundation models

The most interesting takeaway is that tokenization may stop being a hard architectural boundary. In the BLT view, the model is not tied to a fixed vocabulary. It can operate on raw bytes, form patches dynamically, and still reuse the expensive parts of a pretrained LLM.

That opens up a different model adaptation story. Instead of asking, “Which tokenizer should this new foundation model use?” the question becomes, “How much of the pretrained model can we preserve if we switch to bytes?”

There are still real limitations. The paper notes that its scaling laws were borrowed from Llama 3 rather than derived specifically for BLT, current implementations may still lag tokenizer-based models in wall-clock efficiency, and the entropy model used for patching is trained separately rather than learned end to end. Those are all open problems.

But the direction is compelling. If BLT-like systems continue to improve, future foundation models may be designed so that the tokenizer is no longer a permanent constraint. Instead, it becomes one implementation choice among several, and the model’s core knowledge can move more freely across representations.

That is what makes the Llama 3.1 initialization experiment so interesting. It suggests that the path to tokenizer-free language models may not require abandoning today’s pretrained systems. It may only require teaching them to read the world one byte at a time.
