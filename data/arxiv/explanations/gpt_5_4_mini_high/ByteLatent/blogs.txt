\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

# Why AI Might Stop Using Tokens: The Big Idea Behind Byte Latent Transformer

Large language models are usually trained on tokens, not raw text. That sounds like a small implementation detail, but in practice tokenization shapes almost everything about how a model sees language.

The Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, argues that this hidden preprocessing step has become a real bottleneck. In the paper, the authors show that a byte-level model can now match tokenization-based models at scale, while using compute more efficiently and becoming more robust to noisy or unusual inputs.

## Tokens Are Convenient, but They Are Also a Design Choice

Tokenization breaks text into chunks before the model ever sees it. Those chunks might be whole words, word pieces, punctuation marks, or common subwords. The advantage is obvious: instead of processing every character or byte, the model processes fewer units, which makes training and inference cheaper.

But tokenization is also a heuristic. It is a fixed, hand-designed step that decides how text should be compressed before learning begins. That introduces several problems the paper highlights. Token boundaries can behave differently across domains and languages, the same string can be split in inconsistent ways, and small input changes can produce different token sequences. Token-based models also do not directly see byte-level structure, which means they can miss spelling, orthography, and other fine-grained patterns.

For years, the reason tokenization stayed around was practical. Byte-level models were too expensive because raw text creates longer sequences. More sequence length usually means more computation. BLT’s main contribution is showing that this trade-off is no longer as restrictive as it used to be.

## The Core Idea: Compute Should Depend on Difficulty

BLT starts from a simple observation: not every part of a text sequence is equally hard to predict.

If a model is finishing the middle of a common word, that prediction is often easy. If it is starting a new sentence or making a high-uncertainty choice, the model should spend more compute. Tokenization does not do this. A token gets the same general treatment whether it is easy or hard.

BLT replaces fixed tokens with dynamically sized patches of bytes. A patch is just a group of bytes processed together. The important difference is that patch boundaries are not fixed in advance by a vocabulary. Instead, BLT chooses them based on prediction uncertainty, using the entropy of a small byte-level language model. In simple terms, it groups together bytes where the next-byte prediction is relatively predictable, and it creates new patches where uncertainty rises.

That gives BLT a way to spend more compute where the data is complex, and less where it is easy.

## How BLT Works

BLT is built from three parts.

First, a local encoder reads raw bytes and turns them into patch representations. This part is lightweight and only looks at a limited local context. It also uses hash $n$-gram embeddings, which help the model capture short byte sequences without requiring a fixed vocabulary.

Second, a large global transformer operates on the patch representations. This is where most of the model’s compute lives. Because the global transformer runs once per patch rather than once per byte, it can be invoked less often when the text is easy to predict.

Third, a local decoder turns the patch-level outputs back into bytes.

This design matters because it separates “understanding the local byte stream” from “doing the expensive global reasoning.” In effect, BLT uses raw bytes at the edges, but performs the heavy computation on a more compact latent representation.

## Why Patches Matter More Than Static Tokens

The paper spends a lot of time on the distinction between tokens and patches, and for good reason. Tokens come from a fixed vocabulary chosen before training. Patches do not.

That difference gives BLT more flexibility. With tokenization, increasing average token size usually means changing the vocabulary, which also affects the embedding table and other model components. With BLT, patch size can change dynamically based on the data. This means the model can choose longer patches when the text is predictable and shorter patches when it is not.

The authors also emphasize that BLT’s patching must work incrementally during generation. In other words, it cannot depend on future bytes that have not been generated yet. That makes the approach suitable for autoregressive language modeling, not just offline preprocessing.

## What the Experiments Show

The headline result is straightforward: BLT matches tokenization-based models at scale.

The paper reports flop-controlled scaling studies up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes. Under these controlled comparisons, BLT reaches performance on par with Llama 3. It also shows that, for fixed inference cost, BLT can scale better by increasing both model size and patch size together. In some settings, this leads to up to 50% fewer inference flops.

That is the big systems-level result. But the paper also reports broader behavior changes. BLT is more robust to noisy text inputs, performs better on character-level understanding tasks, and shows stronger results on some low-resource translation settings. These gains are consistent with the idea that raw bytes preserve information that tokenizers may obscure.

The authors also test a variant where BLT is initialized from a pretrained Llama 3.1 model. That setup suggests another practical path forward: converting a tokenizer-based model into a byte-level model rather than training everything from scratch.

## Why This Matters

If BLT’s results hold up more broadly, they suggest that tokenization may stop being a mandatory first step for large language models.

That does not mean tokens are obsolete tomorrow. Token-based models are still highly effective, and tokenization still offers a very convenient compression mechanism. But BLT shows that fixed vocabularies are not the only way to make large-scale language modeling efficient.

The deeper point is architectural. Instead of compressing text once with a static tokenizer, BLT learns when to compress and how much compute to spend. That makes the model more adaptive, more flexible across languages and domains, and less dependent on preprocessing choices made before training begins.

In short, BLT is not just a byte-level model. It is a proposal for a different scaling strategy: one where the model itself decides how to chunk the input, and where raw bytes can compete with tokens at frontier scale.



# From Bytes to Patches: How BLT Decides Where to Spend Compute

Large language models usually start with tokenization: a string is chopped into a fixed vocabulary of tokens, and the model processes one token at a time. That works well, but it also hard-codes a very specific view of text. A token can be a whole word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark, but the model has no direct access to the underlying bytes.

The Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, takes a different route. It works directly on bytes, but it does not treat every byte as an equal unit of computation. Instead, it groups bytes into dynamic chunks called patches. The core idea is simple: easy text should be processed in larger chunks, while hard text should get more attention.

That sounds like a small implementation detail, but it is the key to making byte-level language modeling scale.

## Why bytes need a middle ground

If you process raw bytes naively, you end up with very long sequences. That is expensive, because the main transformer has to do work over every step. Earlier byte-level models tried to deal with this by using smaller or more efficient attention mechanisms, but that mainly helped at small scale. BLT changes the question from “How do we make bytes cheap?” to “Where should we spend compute?”

The answer is patches.

A patch is just a contiguous group of bytes. The important part is that BLT does not use a fixed vocabulary of patches. It learns to segment byte sequences dynamically, so the patching can change with context. The main transformer runs once per patch, so patch size directly affects compute. Larger patches mean fewer transformer steps; smaller patches mean more steps and more capacity spent on that part of the input.

The trick is deciding where the boundaries should go.

## Entropy patching: boundaries where prediction gets hard

BLT’s most interesting patching method is entropy patching. The idea is to use a small byte-level language model to estimate how hard each next-byte prediction is. If the model is very uncertain about the next byte, that is a good sign that the current region deserves its own patch boundary. If the next bytes are easy to predict, BLT can safely keep grouping them together.

In other words, BLT looks for changes in uncertainty.

This makes intuitive sense. Consider a word completion task. In the paper, the authors point out that predicting the first byte after a question like “Who composed the Magic Flute?” is harder than predicting the remaining bytes of “Mozart.” Once the first letter is known, the rest of the word becomes much easier. So BLT can spend more compute around the uncertain part, then compress the predictable continuation into a larger patch.

The same logic applies more broadly. In structured or repetitive text, such as multiple-choice prompts, the next-byte uncertainty can stay low for long stretches, so patches can become larger. In denser regions, such as code or math, the model can break patches more often and allocate more steps where the content is more information-rich.

## How BLT decides where to split

BLT does not use a hand-written rule like “split at every space” or “split every $k$ bytes.” Instead, it trains a small byte-level autoregressive model on the same data distribution as the main model and uses that model’s next-byte entropy as a signal.

The paper describes two boundary rules.

The first is a global threshold: if the entropy at position $t$ is above some threshold, BLT starts a new patch there. The second is a relative rule: it looks for places where entropy rises sharply compared to the previous byte, which approximates the idea that uncertainty is no longer decreasing within the current patch.

That second rule is especially useful because it captures a natural pattern. Within a patch, the bytes often get progressively easier to predict as the local context becomes clearer. When that trend breaks, a new patch boundary makes sense.

In practice, these boundaries are computed during a lightweight preprocessing step while loading data. That keeps patching inexpensive enough to use at scale.

## Why this is better than fixed patching

Before BLT, patching methods were mostly static. You could group every $k$ bytes together, or split on whitespace. Those strategies help reduce sequence length, but they do not adapt to the actual difficulty of the text.

That is the main limitation of fixed patching: it spends the same amount of compute everywhere.

Entropy patching fixes that. It uses the model’s own estimate of uncertainty to decide whether a region should be short or long. Predictable regions can be compressed aggressively. Uncertain regions get more boundaries and therefore more transformer steps.

This matters because the expensive part of BLT is the global latent transformer. The local encoder and decoder are lightweight, but the global model is where most of the flops go. So every time entropy patching makes a patch longer, it effectively saves compute. That saved compute can be used either to reduce inference cost or to grow the model while staying within the same budget.

That is why the paper’s scaling result is phrased the way it is: patches scale better than tokens because patch size is no longer tied to a fixed vocabulary. BLT can make patches longer when the data allows it, and then use the budget it saves to expand model capacity.

## Why not just use tokenization?

Tokenization does compress text, but it does so with a fixed vocabulary chosen before training. That creates a hidden mismatch: a token is not always aligned with what is actually hard or easy to predict. Two sequences with the same token count can have very different information content.

BLT’s patches are different. They are dynamic and context-sensitive. They are also incremental, meaning the same prefix must always be segmented the same way regardless of how the sequence continues. That matters during generation, where the model cannot look ahead to future bytes. This is one reason BLT is not just “tokenization by another name.”

The paper explicitly notes that BPE tokenization is not incremental in this sense, because the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on the continuation. BLT’s patching is designed to avoid that problem.

## The big picture

Entropy patching is the mechanism that lets BLT act like a compute-aware byte model. It is not trying to assign every byte equal importance. It is trying to match compute to difficulty.

That leads to a useful mental model:

- Easy, repetitive, or highly predictable text can be grouped into larger patches.
- Hard, ambiguous, or information-dense text gets shorter patches and more transformer steps.
- The expensive global model is invoked only as often as needed.

This is the central reason BLT can train on raw bytes without giving up the efficiency that token-based models usually get from compression. It also explains why the paper sees strong robustness and long-tail behavior: byte-level modeling keeps the model close to the actual surface form of text, while dynamic patching prevents the sequence length from exploding.

The result is a model that does not just read bytes. It learns where the bytes are easy, where they are hard, and where to spend its compute accordingly.



# Patches Scale Better Than Tokens: The Efficiency Story in Plain English

Large language models usually begin with a tokenizer: a preprocessing step that turns text into a fixed vocabulary of tokens. That works well, but it also creates a rigid compute pattern. Every token gets the same kind of treatment, even though not every part of text is equally hard to predict.

The Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, takes a different route. It reads raw bytes and groups them into dynamic patches. Easy, predictable stretches of text can be packed into longer patches, while harder regions get shorter ones and more model attention. The result is not just a different way to represent text, but a different way to spend compute.

## Why tokens leave efficiency on the table

Tokenization is a compression heuristic, not a learned decision about where the model should focus. In a standard tokenizer-based LLM, the model processes each token more or less uniformly. That means it can waste effort on easy continuations, like the end of a word that is already obvious from context, while giving the same amount of work to genuinely difficult predictions.

BLT is built around the opposite idea: compute should be allocated dynamically. It uses a small byte-level model to estimate how hard the next-byte prediction is. Where the next byte is uncertain, BLT places a patch boundary and invokes more global reasoning. Where the text is predictable, it keeps the patch longer and avoids unnecessary work.

This matters because, at scale, the expensive part of a Transformer is not just attention. The big feed-forward layers dominate compute, and those layers are executed every time the main model runs. So if you can reduce how often the main model is invoked, you can lower total inference cost.

## What a patch buys you

Think of a patch as a group of bytes that BLT handles together. The local encoder and decoder work at the byte level, while the large latent Transformer handles patch representations. This split gives BLT a useful lever: patch size.

Longer patches mean fewer calls to the expensive global Transformer. That is where the efficiency gains come from. In the paper’s experiments, BLT can reach up to roughly 50% fewer inference FLOPs than a tokenization-based baseline while still matching strong model quality at scale.

The key point is that this is not just about making the input shorter. It is about making the expensive part of the network run less often on easy data, so more of the budget goes to the parts of the sequence that actually need it.

## Why larger patches can make the model bigger too

At first glance, larger patches sound like a pure compression trick. But BLT turns them into a scaling strategy.

If larger patches reduce the number of expensive latent Transformer steps, then some of that saved compute can be spent elsewhere. In BLT’s case, the savings can be reallocated to make the latent Transformer larger. So instead of spending the same budget on many small patch steps, you can spend it on fewer patch steps with a more capable core model.

That is the central scaling story in the paper: patches do not just reduce cost. They open up a new tradeoff between patch size and model size.

This is why the paper says BLT can “simultaneously grow both patch and model size” under a fixed inference budget. Bigger patches reduce how often the main Transformer runs, and that freed-up budget can be used to increase the number of parameters in the global model.

## What “fixed inference budget” means

A fixed inference budget is simply the amount of compute you are willing to spend when the model is actually serving requests. In practice, that budget might be constrained by latency, GPU cost, throughput, or all three.

If you are deploying an AI assistant, you do not just care about training cost. You care about how expensive each response is to generate. A model that is slightly better but much more expensive to serve may not be practical. That is why fixed inference-budget comparisons matter: they ask which model gives the best quality for the same serving cost.

BLT is interesting because it expands the design space under that constraint. Instead of only choosing a model size, you also choose how aggressively to patch the input. The paper shows that, at fixed inference cost, larger patch sizes can support larger models and better scaling trends than token-based architectures.

## What the scaling experiments showed

The paper runs a controlled scaling study up to 8B parameters and 4T training bytes. Under compute-controlled comparisons, BLT matches or outperforms tokenization-based baselines like Llama 3. More importantly for deployment, BLT also improves inference efficiency.

In the fixed inference-flop experiments, BLT models outperform tokenizer-based models in both the smaller and larger compute classes the paper studies. The larger-patch variants, especially those with average patch sizes around 6 to 8 bytes, become increasingly attractive as model scale grows. They start from a weaker position at smaller scales, but their scaling trends improve faster.

That is the practical takeaway: a patch-based model can look worse early on and still win later, because the compute it saves can be reinvested into model capacity.

The paper also notes that the FLOP savings are not just theoretical hand-waving. The gains come from reducing how often the global Transformer is invoked, which is the part of the architecture that carries most of the cost.

## The bigger lesson

BLT’s scaling results suggest that tokenization is not the only way to make language models efficient. A model can work directly on bytes, decide dynamically where the text is easy or hard, and use that structure to spend compute more intelligently.

The broader message is simple:

- longer, context-aware patches can reduce inference cost,
- the saved budget can be redirected into a larger core model,
- and at a fixed serving budget, that can produce better scaling than a fixed-vocabulary tokenizer.

BLT is still a research system, and the paper measures FLOPs rather than real wall-clock latency. But as a scaling story, it is compelling: the unit of computation does not have to be a token. If the model can learn better boundaries, it can also learn better ways to spend its budget.



# Why Byte-Level AI Is More Robust to Typos, Weird Spelling, and Low-Resource Languages

Most large language models do not actually read text as raw text. They read tokens: chunks produced by a tokenizer before the model ever sees the input. That works well in practice, but it also introduces a brittle preprocessing step. A typo, a strange spelling, or an unusual script can change how text is segmented, which changes what the model sees.

The Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, takes a different route. It works directly on raw bytes and groups them into dynamic patches on the fly. That sounds like a small implementation detail, but it changes how the model handles the messy parts of language: noisy text, spelling variation, character-level tasks, and low-resource languages.

## Why bytes help when text gets messy

Tokenizer-based models are trained to predict over a fixed vocabulary. That means the model is always operating on a compressed representation of the input, not the actual characters. When the input gets noisy, the tokenizer can hide important details or split text in surprising ways.

BLT avoids that problem by keeping the byte-level signal intact. The model still uses patches to stay efficient, but those patches are built from bytes, not from a fixed vocabulary of subword units. As a result, the model retains direct access to spelling, casing, punctuation, and script-level information.

That matters because many real-world inputs are not cleanly formatted. People misspell words, vary capitalization, repeat characters, drop letters, or mix scripts and languages. Byte-level modeling gives the network a better chance of recovering from that noise.

## BLT is noticeably stronger on noisy text

The paper tests robustness by corrupting standard classification prompts with several character-level noise patterns. These include uppercase conversion, random casing, character deletion, repeated characters, and a fully spaced-out uppercase style called AntSpeak.

On noised HellaSwag, BLT outperforms tokenizer-based models across the board. The paper reports an average gain of 8 points over a model trained on the same data, and BLT also improves over a much larger Llama 3.1 baseline trained on more data.

That result is a good example of the core advantage of byte-level modeling. If the input is slightly damaged, BLT does not have to rely on a tokenizer making a best guess about how to split the text. It sees the corruption directly and can learn to recover from it.

## Character-level understanding is a real advantage, not just a side effect

Some tasks are almost entirely about characters rather than words. BLT does especially well there.

One example is the CUTE benchmark, which focuses on composition, orthographic similarity, and sequence manipulation. The paper reports that BLT-Entropy outperforms both Llama 3 tokenizer-based models by more than 25 points on this benchmark. It is particularly strong on spelling tasks, where it reaches 99.9% accuracy on both tasks.

That is an important signal. Tokenizer-based models often know how words are spelled only indirectly, through the tokens they happen to use. BLT, by contrast, works at the level where spelling actually lives. That makes it better at tasks that require precise character awareness.

The paper also evaluates grapheme-to-phoneme conversion using Phonology Bench. Here too, BLT outperforms the baseline Llama 3 tokenizer-based model in a 5-shot setting. Again, this is a task where direct access to the surface form of the text helps, because the model has to reason about the relationship between written characters and pronunciation.

There is one nuance worth noting. The paper says that word deletion and insertion are the two CUTE tasks where BPE performs better. So byte-level modeling is not a universal win on every character manipulation problem. But the broader pattern is clear: when the task depends on spelling, orthography, or sequence-level character structure, bytes give the model a better foundation.

## Low-resource translation is another place where bytes shine

The paper also tests BLT on translation with FLORES-101, covering six language families and 21 lower-resource languages with different scripts. This is where byte-level modeling shows another practical benefit.

BLT outperforms the Llama 3 tokenizer-based model overall, with a 2-point advantage when translating into English and a 0.5-point advantage when translating from English. On popular language pairs, BLT is comparable to or slightly better than Llama 3. On many lower-resource language pairs, it does better.

This makes sense if you think about what tokenizers are optimized for. A tokenizer built for high-resource text may do reasonably well on common English patterns, but it can be much less graceful on rare words, unusual scripts, or long-tail spellings. Bytes do not have that problem. Every script is representable, and the model does not depend on a vocabulary that may be a poor fit for the language.

In other words, byte-level modeling helps most where the language is least tidy: rare words, inflected forms, mixed scripts, and low-resource settings.

## Why this works in BLT specifically

BLT is not just “a byte model.” It is a byte model that tries to spend compute where the data is hard.

Instead of processing every byte with the same amount of transformer computation, BLT groups bytes into patches. Predictable regions get larger patches, while high-entropy regions get finer-grained computation. That lets the model stay efficient without throwing away character-level detail.

This is part of why the paper can claim both robustness and scale. BLT matches tokenizer-based performance at scale, while also improving inference efficiency and making the model more tolerant to noisy or unusual text.

## The practical takeaway

If your data is clean and well-behaved, tokenization is convenient and often works well. But a lot of real text is not clean. It contains typos, strange capitalization, accented characters, unusual scripts, code-mixed language, and rare forms that tokenizers do not always handle gracefully.

BLT’s results suggest that directly modeling bytes is a strong way to deal with that long tail. It preserves the raw structure of language, which helps on noisy inputs, spelling-sensitive tasks, phonology, and lower-resource translation.

The broader lesson is simple: when you remove the tokenizer, you remove a source of brittleness. And for the messy parts of human language, that can make a real difference.



# Inside the BLT Architecture: The Three-Part Design That Makes Byte-Level LLMs Work

Most language models are built around a simple idea: turn text into tokens, then run a transformer over those tokens. BLT takes a very different route. It starts from raw bytes, groups them into dynamically sized patches, and uses three separate components to do the work.

That split is what makes BLT interesting. It is not just “a transformer on bytes.” It is a byte-level system designed to spend compute selectively, using small local modules where the problem is easy and a larger global model where the sequence needs real reasoning.

## Why bytes alone are not enough

A plain byte-level transformer sounds attractive because it avoids tokenization, but there is a catch: byte sequences are much longer than token sequences. If you run a full transformer on every byte, the cost explodes.

BLT’s answer is to compress bytes into patches before handing them to the expensive part of the model. The key idea is that not every part of the sequence deserves the same amount of compute. Predicting the next few bytes inside a word is usually easier than deciding what comes next at the start of a new word or sentence. BLT tries to reflect that difference in its architecture.

The result is a system with three parts:

1. A local encoder that turns bytes into patch representations.
2. A global latent transformer that reasons over patches.
3. A local decoder that turns patch outputs back into bytes.

## 1) The local encoder: bytes in, patches out

The local encoder is the first stage that sees the raw byte stream. Its job is not to do all the heavy lifting. Instead, it builds compact representations for groups of bytes.

BLT adds a few useful ingredients here. One is a lightweight transformer that works over bytes with a limited local context. Another is a cross-attention block that pools the bytes in each patch into a single patch representation. This is how BLT goes from a long byte sequence to a shorter patch sequence.

There is also a small but important detail: BLT augments byte embeddings with hash-based $n$-gram embeddings for byte sequences of length 3 through 8. That gives the local encoder some awareness of short byte patterns, which helps it represent things like character fragments, spelling structure, and other subword information that tokenized models often hide.

The encoder is deliberately small compared to the global model. That matters because its job is local feature extraction, not long-range reasoning. In BLT, the expensive compute should not be spent repeatedly on every byte.

## 2) The global latent transformer: the main reasoning engine

Once the encoder has produced patch representations, the global latent transformer takes over. This is the big transformer in the system, and it operates on patches rather than bytes.

That design choice is the central efficiency win. If a patch contains several bytes, the global model processes one latent unit instead of many raw positions. Fewer positions means fewer transformer steps, which means fewer flops.

The global transformer uses block-causal attention, so each patch can attend to previous patches in the current document, but not to future ones. In other words, it plays the same autoregressive game as a normal LLM, just at the patch level instead of the token level.

This also changes how compute is allocated. Tokenized models spend roughly the same amount of compute on every token. BLT does not have to. When data is predictable, patches can be longer, so the global transformer runs less often. When the next byte is harder to predict, patches can be shorter, so BLT allocates more compute where the uncertainty is higher.

That is why the paper emphasizes entropy-based patching. BLT uses a small byte-level language model to estimate next-byte entropy and place patch boundaries where the prediction problem changes. The practical effect is simple: easy regions get compressed more aggressively, while complex regions get more modeling capacity.

## 3) The local decoder: patch context back to bytes

After the global transformer has processed the patch sequence, BLT still needs to produce byte-level outputs. That is the job of the local decoder.

The decoder mirrors the encoder in reverse. It takes the patch-level outputs from the global model and expands them back into byte-level representations using cross-attention. Then a lightweight transformer operating on bytes produces the final byte predictions.

This reverse mapping is important. The global model works at the patch level for efficiency, but the model still needs to generate bytes directly. The decoder makes that possible without forcing the main transformer to operate on the full byte sequence.

So the decoder is not an afterthought. It is the mechanism that lets BLT keep byte-level fidelity while still gaining the efficiency of patch-level computation.

## How the three pieces fit together

A useful way to think about BLT is as a pipeline:

Raw bytes are first segmented into patches. The local encoder turns each patch into a compact latent vector. The global transformer reasons over those latent vectors. The local decoder then expands the result back into bytes.

This is a very different design from a standard token-based LLM. In a token model, the same transformer handles everything. In BLT, the work is split by level of abstraction. Bytes handle fine-grained structure. Patches handle global reasoning. The system chooses how much of each to use based on the data.

That is the core architectural idea.

## Why this is more than just an efficiency trick

The obvious benefit is lower compute. Because the global transformer runs once per patch rather than once per byte, longer patches can reduce inference cost substantially. The paper reports that BLT can trade minor metric loss for up to 50% fewer inference flops, and that larger patch sizes create room to scale the global model while keeping the same inference budget.

But the architecture also brings another benefit: robustness. Since BLT works directly with bytes, it has direct access to character-level structure. That helps on noisy inputs, orthographic tasks, phonology, and low-resource machine translation, where tokenization can obscure useful details.

So BLT is not just compressing text more aggressively. It is reorganizing the whole model around the observation that not all parts of a sequence deserve equal compute.

## The main takeaway

BLT’s contribution is architectural, not just tokenization-free modeling. It replaces the usual “one transformer over one sequence of tokens” setup with a three-part system:

- a local encoder that turns bytes into patch representations,
- a large global latent transformer that does the expensive reasoning on patches,
- and a local decoder that reconstructs bytes from patch-level context.

That design gives BLT a flexible compute budget. It can spend more on hard regions and less on easy ones. It can scale model size and patch size together. And it can do all of this while starting from raw bytes instead of a fixed vocabulary.

If you want the short version, BLT is a transformer system built around selective computation. The bytes are still there, but the model only pays full price when it needs to.



# Can We Turn Today's LLMs Into Tokenizer-Free Models?

LLMs are usually described in terms of their layers, parameter count, and training data. But there is another design choice hiding in plain sight: the tokenizer. Before the model sees anything, raw text is compressed into a fixed vocabulary of subword units. That makes training efficient, but it also bakes in a particular view of language.

The Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, asks a useful question: what if that constraint was optional? And more specifically, what if we could take a pretrained tokenizer-based model like Llama 3.1 and “byte-ify” it instead of retraining everything from scratch?

## Why tokenizers are a hidden architectural choice

Tokenizers are often treated like plumbing, but they shape the model more than we usually admit. They decide how text is split, how much sequence length the model has to process, and how the output vocabulary is organized. The paper argues that this can create a few problems: sensitivity to input noise, weak handling of spelling and orthography, awkward behavior on multilingual data, and a bias toward the particular compression scheme chosen by the tokenizer.

That matters because many of the cases where LLMs feel brittle are exactly the cases where raw characters or bytes would help. If you misspell a word, mix scripts, or perturb the text slightly, the tokenizer can turn a small change into a very different token sequence. A byte-level model does not have that problem, because bytes are always there.

The tradeoff, historically, has been compute. Modeling bytes means longer sequences, and longer sequences used to make transformers too expensive at scale.

## BLT's answer: dynamic byte patches

BLT does not simply replace tokens with bytes and hope for the best. Instead, it introduces a patch-based architecture. The model reads raw bytes, groups them into patches, processes those patches with a large global transformer, and then decodes back to bytes.

The key idea is that patches are not fixed vocabulary items. They are dynamically formed groups of bytes. BLT can make patches longer when the next-byte prediction is easy, and shorter when the data is more complex. The paper uses entropy estimates from a small byte-level language model to decide where patch boundaries should go. In other words, it tries to spend more compute where the information density is high and less where the next byte is predictable.

This is important because it changes the usual tokenization tradeoff. Standard tokenizers use the same compute budget for every token, even though some spans of text are much harder than others. BLT instead allocates computation dynamically through patch size.

## The interesting part: starting from Llama 3.1

The most forward-looking experiment in the paper is not training BLT from scratch. It is initializing BLT from a pretrained Llama 3.1 model.

The setup is straightforward in concept. The large global transformer inside BLT is initialized with Llama 3.1 weights. Then the byte-level local encoder and decoder are trained to map raw bytes into patches and back again. The global transformer is still updated, but with a learning rate that is one-tenth of the rate used for the local encoder and decoder.

That makes the experiment feel like a practical migration path rather than a purely new model family. The pretrained LLM does not get thrown away. Instead, its central reasoning stack is reused, while BLT learns a new byte-level interface around it.

The authors describe this as a way of turning a tokenizer-based model into a tokenizer-free one. That is a big idea, because retraining a foundation model from scratch is expensive, and tokenizers are usually frozen long before deployment. If this transfer works well, then changing representation does not necessarily mean starting over.

## What the paper found

The results are promising, but they are not magic.

When BLT is initialized from Llama 3.1, it outperforms both the plain Llama 3 baseline and the from-scratch BLT baseline under the same flop budget. It also beats the BLT-Entropy model on MMLU, even though that baseline was trained on more data. The paper interprets this as evidence that reusing a pretrained tokenizer-based backbone can significantly reduce the training flops needed to reach a strong byte-level model.

At the same time, the conversion is not perfect. Compared with the original Llama 3.1 model, the byte-ified BLT still loses some ground, with slight declines on MMLU and HumanEval and larger drops on some other tasks. So the experiment does not show that tokenizer-free models already dominate their tokenized predecessors. It shows that the gap can be narrowed, and that existing pretrained models may transfer into a byte-level form more effectively than one might expect.

That nuance matters. The result is not “tokenizers are obsolete.” It is “we may be able to reuse today’s foundation models even if we want to move away from tokenizers.”

## Why this could reduce retraining costs

The practical appeal is clear. Training a frontier model from scratch is one of the most expensive things in machine learning. If the expensive global transformer can be reused, then the remaining cost is learning the byte-level front and back ends and adapting the system to the new representation.

That could make tokenizer-free adaptation much more realistic. Instead of rebuilding a model every time you want a different input representation, you can potentially preserve the pretrained core and learn how to feed it raw bytes. For domains where spelling, noise, or multilingual text matters, that is especially attractive.

BLT’s broader scaling results make this even more interesting. The paper shows that BLT matches tokenization-based models at scale up to 8B parameters, while also reducing inference flops by as much as 50% when patch sizes are larger. So the byte-level path is not just about robustness. It also creates a compute knob that tokenizers do not give you: patch size can grow alongside model size under a fixed inference budget.

## What this means for the future of foundation models

The most interesting takeaway is that tokenization may stop being a hard architectural boundary. In the BLT view, the model is not tied to a fixed vocabulary. It can operate on raw bytes, form patches dynamically, and still reuse the expensive parts of a pretrained LLM.

That opens up a different model adaptation story. Instead of asking, “Which tokenizer should this new foundation model use?” the question becomes, “How much of the pretrained model can we preserve if we switch to bytes?”

There are still real limitations. The paper notes that its scaling laws were borrowed from Llama 3 rather than derived specifically for BLT, current implementations may still lag tokenizer-based models in wall-clock efficiency, and the entropy model used for patching is trained separately rather than learned end to end. Those are all open problems.

But the direction is compelling. If BLT-like systems continue to improve, future foundation models may be designed so that the tokenizer is no longer a permanent constraint. Instead, it becomes one implementation choice among several, and the model’s core knowledge can move more freely across representations.

That is what makes the Llama 3.1 initialization experiment so interesting. It suggests that the path to tokenizer-free language models may not require abandoning today’s pretrained systems. It may only require teaching them to read the world one byte at a time.
