\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Why Move Beyond Tokenization?

# Tokenization as the Non-End-to-End Part of Modern LLMs

Modern large language models are often described as end-to-end systems, but the paper argues that one important exception remains: tokenization. Tokenization is a hand-designed preprocessing step that groups bytes into a static vocabulary of tokens before the model ever sees the data. In other words, while the transformer itself is trained end-to-end, the representation it receives is not learned from data in the same way. The authors view this as a significant mismatch with the rest of the modern LLM pipeline.

This matters because the model never directly sees the raw byte sequence once tokenization has happened. Instead, it operates on a compressed representation chosen by a fixed set of rules and a fixed vocabulary. The paper’s central claim is that this static preprocessing step imposes a bias on how text is represented, and that this bias becomes increasingly problematic as models are scaled up and used across diverse domains, languages, and input conditions. BLT is proposed as a way to remove this non-learned bottleneck while still keeping training and inference efficient.

# How Fixed Vocabularies Bias Compression and Representation

The paper emphasizes that tokenization is not just a convenience; it is a compression scheme with consequences. A fixed vocabulary decides which byte sequences should be treated as single units and which should be split into smaller pieces. That choice affects both how much computation the model spends on a piece of text and what information is made explicit versus hidden. With a tokenizer, the model sees a token stream, not the original bytes, so the underlying byte-level structure is no longer directly available.

The authors argue that this creates a mismatch between compression and prediction difficulty. Tokenizers are built from heuristics that compress text in a static way, but the complexity of the next prediction is not static. A token can be easy to predict in one context and difficult in another, and two byte sequences of similar length may deserve very different amounts of compute. The paper’s intuition is that tokenization allocates compute according to a fixed representation scheme, while the true need for compute depends on the uncertainty of the prediction itself.

This is why the paper frames fixed-vocabulary tokenization as a source of bias. It decides in advance which pieces of text should be grouped together, even though the “right” grouping depends on the context. The result is that the model’s view of the input is shaped by a compression policy that may not line up with the structure the model actually needs to solve the task.

# Failures of Tokenization in Practice

The paper highlights four broad shortcomings of tokenization-based language models: domain sensitivity, input noise sensitivity, weak orthographic knowledge, and multilingual inequity. These are all consequences, in different ways, of hiding the raw byte structure behind a fixed vocabulary.

Domain and modality sensitivity arise because the tokenizer’s vocabulary is built from a particular distribution of text. When the input domain changes, the tokenization may become less natural or less efficient. The paper points out that tokenization can bias how strings are compressed, and this bias can make models less stable across different domains and modalities. In the same spirit, tokenization can be brittle under input noise: if characters are altered, repeated, dropped, or cased differently, the tokenization itself may change substantially, which can disrupt the model’s behavior.

The paper also stresses a lack of orthographic knowledge. Since a tokenizer may represent an entire subword unit as a single token, the model may know that the token exists but not be able to directly reason about the letters inside it. This becomes visible in tasks that require manipulation of spelling or character sequences. The authors cite benchmarks such as CUTE to show that token-based models can struggle to exploit spelling information even when they effectively “know” the word as a token.

Finally, multilingual inequity arises because a fixed vocabulary tends to serve some scripts and languages better than others. If a language is broken into many more tokens on average, then the model spends more steps and more compute representing the same content. The paper treats this as a structural disadvantage of fixed-vocabulary systems, since the representation is not equally natural for all languages.

# Why Raw-Byte Modeling Is Attractive in Principle

Against these limitations, the appeal of raw-byte modeling is straightforward: bytes are universal, and they avoid the need for a hand-designed vocabulary altogether. A byte-level model can, in principle, represent any text without depending on a particular tokenizer, which makes it more directly grounded in the actual input. This is especially attractive for modeling spelling, punctuation, mixed scripts, noise, and other character-level phenomena that tokenization can blur.

The paper argues that byte-level modeling should also improve robustness because it keeps the underlying structure visible to the model. If the input changes slightly at the character level, the representation changes in a correspondingly local way rather than being remapped through a tokenizer. This direct access to the raw sequence is what makes byte-level methods promising for tasks that require exact character understanding, such as orthographic manipulation, grapheme-to-phoneme conversion, and low-resource translation where tokenization may not align well with the data.

In principle, then, bytes solve several problems at once: they remove vocabulary design as a bottleneck, make the model’s input more faithful to the raw text, and better expose subword structure. The challenge, as the paper explains, is not whether bytes are useful, but whether they can be used efficiently enough to compete with tokenization-based models at scale.

# Why Byte-Level Transformers Have Been Hard to Scale

The main obstacle to byte-level language modeling is sequence length. If text is represented directly as bytes, the model has many more positions to process than it would with subword tokens. Prior work tried to address this with more efficient self-attention or even attention-free architectures, but the paper argues that these approaches mainly help at smaller scales. Once models become large, the dominant cost is not only attention; it is also the large feed-forward network layers that run on every byte.

This is the crucial scaling problem. A transformer that processes every byte one by one pays the full cost of its main network at every position, so the computational burden grows quickly. The paper points out that this is why tokenization became so important historically: it reduced sequence length and therefore the number of model steps. But tokenization does this using a static vocabulary, which brings back the representation issues discussed earlier.

The paper’s key observation is that to make byte-level modeling practical at large scale, one must reduce the number of times the expensive part of the model runs. Efficient attention alone is not enough. The system needs a way to spend more compute on difficult regions of text and less compute on easy regions, so that raw-byte modeling does not simply mean “more bytes, more cost.” This is the motivation for patching.

# Patches: Dynamic Compute Allocation for Bytes

BLT’s central idea is to group bytes into dynamically sized patches and make patches the main unit of computation. Instead of processing every byte with the full transformer stack, BLT first encodes bytes into patch representations, runs a large global transformer over those patches, and then decodes back to bytes. The paper emphasizes that patches are not tokens: they do not come from a fixed vocabulary, and they are not predetermined subword units. They are learned groupings of arbitrary byte sequences.

This design lets BLT allocate compute where it is needed. The paper’s intuition is that some parts of a string are relatively easy to predict and do not need much computation. For example, the paper notes that the ending of a word is often easier to predict than the first word of a new sentence. A fixed tokenization scheme would give both regions the same treatment, but BLT can instead use smaller or larger patches depending on uncertainty. When the next-byte prediction is hard, the model can create shorter patches and invoke the global transformer more often; when the text is predictable, it can use longer patches and save computation.

The paper explores three patching ideas: fixed-size strided patches, space-based patches, and entropy-based patches. The most important one is entropy patching, which uses next-byte entropy from a small byte-level language model to identify where patch boundaries should go. High-entropy locations indicate uncertainty, so they are good candidates for patch breaks. This makes patching contextual rather than rule-based. In the paper’s framing, patching is a way to approximate uniform information density: more compute where the data is complex, less compute where it is simple.

# High-Level Summary of BLT's Contributions

The paper’s main contribution is the Byte Latent Transformer, a tokenizer-free architecture that learns directly from raw bytes while remaining efficient enough to scale. BLT combines a large global latent transformer with two smaller local models: one to encode bytes into patches and one to decode patches back into bytes. The global transformer consumes the bulk of the compute, so deciding how often it runs becomes the mechanism for controlling cost. This is the architectural step that lets the model trade patch length against compute.

A second major contribution is the paper’s scaling study. The authors present what they describe as the first flop-controlled scaling study of byte-level models up to 8B parameters and 4T training bytes. This is important because it tests whether a byte-level model can be trained and scaled under the same kind of careful compute accounting used for tokenizer-based models. The answer, according to the paper, is yes: BLT can be trained end-to-end from bytes at scale.

A third contribution is that BLT changes the scaling tradeoff itself. Because patches are not tied to a fixed vocabulary, the model can increase patch size and model size simultaneously while keeping inference cost fixed. That is a new scaling dimension unavailable to standard tokenization-based models, where changing token size usually means redesigning or enlarging the vocabulary. The paper argues that this opens a better path for scaling under fixed inference budgets.

# Main Headline Results: Parity, Savings, and Robustness

The strongest headline result is that BLT matches tokenization-based performance at scale while improving efficiency. The authors report that BLT matches the training flop-controlled performance of Llama 3 up to 8B scale, and that it can use up to $50\%$ fewer flops at inference. In other words, BLT does not merely recover token-level performance from raw bytes; it can do so with substantially lower inference cost when patching is used well.

The paper also reports that BLT’s robustness advantages are real and measurable. On noisy inputs, BLT is more robust than tokenizer-based models. It also shows better character-level understanding on tasks involving spelling, phonology, and low-resource machine translation. On the CUTE benchmark, BLT-Entropy is reported to outperform both Llama 3 tokenizer-based models by more than 25 points, and on noisy HellaSwag the model has an average advantage of 8 points over a model trained on the same data. These results support the paper’s claim that raw-byte modeling preserves important subword and character information that tokenization can obscure.

Taken together, the paper’s message is that tokenization is not the only way to make language models practical. By replacing fixed-vocabulary tokens with dynamically sized byte patches, BLT shows that raw-byte modeling can be both scalable and efficient. The result is a model that keeps the benefits of direct byte-level access while recovering the performance and efficiency that made tokenization so dominant in the first place.

Foundations: Bytes, Tokens, Patches, and Compute

# Bytes as a universal text representation

The most basic unit in the paper is the byte. BLT learns directly from raw byte data rather than from a tokenizer-built vocabulary, so the model starts from the lowest-level textual representation that the paper considers. This matters because bytes are universal: any text can be represented as a sequence of byte values, and BLT does not need to decide in advance which words, fragments, or subwords should count as units. In the paper’s notation, byte-level inputs are written as $b_i$ or $x_i$, and the byte vocabulary has size 256. That simple fact is important for understanding why the architecture can work across domains and languages without a fixed vocabulary.

The paper’s motivation for bytes is not only universality but also fidelity. Tokenization compresses text using heuristics that can introduce biases in how the text is represented. The introduction explicitly notes several shortcomings of tokenization: sensitivity to domain and modality, sensitivity to input noise, lack of orthographic knowledge, and multilingual inequity. By contrast, bytes preserve the underlying sequence exactly as it appears, including spelling, punctuation, digits, and other character-level details. This is why the paper connects byte modeling to improved robustness and to character-level understanding tasks later in the experiments.

# Subword tokenization and Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)

Tokenization is the standard alternative to raw bytes in modern language models. In the paper, tokenization is described as a heuristic preprocessing step that groups bytes into a static set of tokens. BPE is the canonical example: it builds a finite vocabulary of byte groups before training, and the model then operates on those tokens rather than on bytes themselves. The key distinction is that tokens are fixed vocabulary items, while bytes are the raw primitive sequence. In the paper’s terminology, tokenized models do not have direct access to the underlying byte features.

This design creates a trade-off. Larger vocabularies produce larger tokens on average, which reduces the number of sequence steps the model must process. At the same time, a larger vocabulary increases the size of the embedding table and the final output projection layer. The paper uses Llama 3 as an example: compared to Llama 2, Llama 3 increases the average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes, but it does so at the cost of a 4x larger embedding table. That is the tokenization trade-off in the paper’s framing: fewer steps versus more parameter and memory overhead.

Another important point is that tokenization allocates the same amount of compute to every token, even though not every prediction is equally difficult. The paper argues that this is a mismatch between representation and prediction complexity. A token boundary may be convenient for compression, but that does not mean the token is the right place to spend compute. This mismatch is one of the main reasons BLT replaces fixed token units with dynamically chosen patches.

# What a patch is and how it differs from a token

A patch is a group of bytes, but unlike a token it does not come from a fixed vocabulary. The paper defines a patching function $f_p$ that segments a byte sequence $\mathbf{x}=\{x_i\}_{i=1}^n$ into a shorter sequence of patches $\mathbf{p}=\{p_j\}_{j=1}^m$ with $m<n$. Conceptually, the patching function marks where one patch ends and the next begins; the paper describes this as mapping each byte to $\{0,1\}$, where 1 indicates the start of a new patch. This makes patches a structural way to group bytes rather than a symbolic vocabulary to replace them.

The reason patches matter is that, in BLT, they are the primary units of computation. The paper emphasizes that the cost of processing data is largely determined by how many steps the main Transformer must execute, and for BLT that means how many patches are needed. Average patch size is therefore a direct lever for compute: larger patches mean fewer patch-level steps, while smaller patches mean more detailed computation. Unlike tokenization, patches are not tied to a prebuilt dictionary, so they can vary with context and with the amount of information in the data.

The paper contrasts several patching strategies to make this idea concrete. A static scheme can group bytes into fixed-size blocks, whitespace-based patching can start new patches around space-like bytes, and entropy-based patching can choose boundaries from next-byte uncertainty. These are all ways of deciding where to spend compute, but they differ in how adaptive they are. The central idea is that patch boundaries should reflect the structure of the data, not just a fixed compression rule.

# Autoregressive language modeling over different sequence units

Autoregressive modeling means predicting the next element of a sequence from the previous ones. The paper uses this idea across multiple levels. Token-based models predict the next token; byte-level models predict the next byte; and BLT combines both levels by using local byte models and a global patch model. The global model maps latent patch representations $p_j$ to output patch representations $o_j$, while the local decoder turns those patch representations back into raw bytes $y_i$. So even though the architecture is patch-based at the center, it still ultimately generates bytes autoregressively.

This multi-level design is important because the prediction problem is not equally hard everywhere in the sequence. The paper’s example is intuitive: predicting the ending of a word is often easier than predicting the first word of a new sentence. Likewise, after a space, the first byte of a word may be much more uncertain than the remaining bytes of that word. BLT’s patching strategy uses this observation directly. It tries to place patch boundaries where uncertainty is high, so that the model can devote more compute to hard decisions and less compute to easy continuations.

This is also why the paper talks about entropy-based patching. The idea is not just to compress bytes, but to group them in a way that tracks prediction difficulty. In other words, autoregressive modeling is still the core task, but the paper changes the unit over which the model spends its most expensive compute.

# Why sequence length matters for efficiency

Sequence length is central because every extra step through a transformer costs compute. Byte-level modeling makes sequences longer than token-level modeling, since bytes are smaller units than subword tokens. The paper’s response is not to abandon bytes, but to reduce the number of expensive steps by grouping bytes into patches. Since the main Transformer in BLT operates on patches, not on individual bytes, the effective sequence length for the expensive part of the model becomes the number of patches.

The paper is explicit that average patch size is the main factor controlling the compute cost of processing data. If the same text is represented using larger patches, the global model runs fewer times. That lowers training and inference cost, and it also creates room to increase the model size of the expensive global Transformer. The paper’s scaling experiments use this trade-off directly: longer patches save compute, and the saved compute can be used elsewhere.

The paper also points out that fixed patching schemes can waste compute or misallocate it. A fixed stride can spend the same amount of effort on easy and hard parts of the sequence, even when the data complexity is very different. That is why dynamic patching is so important in the BLT story: sequence length is not just a property of the data, but a controllable design choice that affects how much work the model does.

# Attention cost versus feed-forward cost in large Transformers

Earlier byte-level work often focused on reducing attention cost, because long byte sequences make attention expensive. The paper argues that this is not enough at scale. Its key point is that, for large transformers, the feed-forward layers become the dominant cost because they run on every byte. So even if attention is made cheaper, the model can still be very expensive if it must process very long byte sequences through large feed-forward blocks.

This is why BLT organizes computation into three transformer blocks: two lightweight local models and one large global latent Transformer. The local encoder and local decoder handle the byte-level bookkeeping, while the global model consumes most of the flops and performs the main sequence modeling over patches. The important design choice is that the global model is only invoked when the patching scheme says it should be, so the architecture can allocate expensive computation where the data are most uncertain.

The paper’s perspective is therefore different from simply “making attention faster.” Instead, it asks a more fundamental question: how often should the expensive transformer be run at all? Patching is the mechanism that answers that question. By choosing patch boundaries dynamically, BLT controls the number of expensive transformer steps rather than merely optimizing one component of those steps.

# Training FLOPs and inference FLOPs as comparison tools

The paper uses FLOPs, or floating point operations, as the main way to compare compute across models. This is a model-agnostic measure of cost: instead of comparing wall-clock time or hardware-specific implementation details, the paper counts how many operations the model needs. For transformer models, it follows the Chinchilla-style accounting of feed-forward layers, projections in self-attention, attention computation, and output projection. One notable detail is that the embedding lookup is treated as a zero-flop operation because it is implemented as an efficient lookup rather than a dense matrix multiplication.

The paper also assumes that the backward pass costs twice as much as the forward pass. That makes training FLOPs a natural way to compare pretraining budgets. Inference FLOPs matter separately, because a model used in deployment should be cheap to run after training is finished. The paper repeatedly uses both notions: training-flop-controlled scaling to compare model quality under equal pretraining cost, and inference-flop-controlled scaling to compare the cost of using the model later.

For BLT, FLOPs per byte are computed from several components: the local encoder, the global latent Transformer, the local decoder, and the cross-attention blocks that connect them. This is important because the architecture has multiple compute stages, and each contributes differently depending on patch size. Using FLOPs as the comparison tool makes it possible to ask whether a model is better because it is more accurate or simply because it spent more compute.

# Bits-per-byte as a tokenizer-independent metric

Perplexity is tied to a tokenizer, because it measures uncertainty per token. That makes it awkward when comparing byte-based models with token-based models. The paper therefore uses bits-per-byte, or BPB, as a tokenizer-independent alternative. BPB is defined as
$$\mathrm{BPB}(x)=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}},$$
where $\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})$ is the total cross-entropy loss on the data and $n_{\text{bytes}}$ is the number of bytes. In plain terms, it normalizes the model’s uncertainty by the amount of raw byte data being predicted.

This normalization is exactly what makes BPB useful in the paper. Because bytes are the underlying common representation, BPB lets the authors compare tokenized models and byte-based models on the same scale. It also aligns naturally with BLT’s training and scaling analyses, since the model is built around raw bytes rather than token units. A lower BPB means the model is assigning higher probability to the observed byte sequence, so it is the natural byte-level analogue of a better language model fit.

# Why fair comparisons require controlling for context length and compute

A central challenge in comparing patch-based and token-based models is that changing patch size changes the amount of computation per example. Larger patches shorten the effective sequence length of the expensive part of the model, so a model could appear better simply because it saw more bytes for the same number of transformer steps. The paper treats this as a confound and explicitly controls for it. In expectation, it keeps the number of bytes per batch constant and reduces the sequence length of models with larger patches so that no model gets an unfair advantage from larger context.

The paper also pays attention to batching details. Because dynamic patching produces different ratios of bytes to patches, BLT packs batches of patches to avoid padding the expensive latent Transformer. It also pads and sometimes truncates byte sequences to avoid memory spikes from unusually large patches. These implementation details are not cosmetic; they are part of the fairness story, because they keep compute comparable across patching schemes.

The same logic applies to entropy-based patching thresholds. The paper chooses thresholds so that the model reaches a target average patch size on the pretraining mix, rather than letting the patching rule drift into a different compute regime. For some experiments, it even resets the entropy context with new lines and uses the approximate monotonicity constraint to reduce entropy drift. The bigger lesson is that fair comparisons in this setting require controlling both the context length and the compute budget. Only then can improvements be attributed to the representation and architecture, rather than to an accidental advantage in how much data the model processed.

From Bytes to Patches: The Patching Problem

# Formalizing a Patching Function

BLT begins from the idea that raw bytes should not all receive the same amount of computation. Instead of committing to a fixed tokenizer, it groups bytes into patches, and those patches become the units that the larger model processes. In the paper, a patching function $f_p$ takes a byte sequence $\pmb{x}=\{x_i \mid i=1,\ldots,n\}$ and segments it into a shorter sequence of patches $\pmb{p}=\{p_j \mid j=1,\ldots,m\}$, where $m<n$. The segmentation can be represented as a binary decision at each byte position: each $x_i$ is mapped to either $0$ or $1$, with $1$ indicating the start of a new patch.

This definition is important because it makes clear that patching is not just a preprocessing detail. It is the mechanism that decides how a byte sequence is compressed into a sequence of computation units. Once the bytes are split into patches, the main transformer in BLT runs once per patch rather than once per byte. In that sense, patching is the bridge between raw text and the model’s expensive global computation.

# Mapping Byte Sequences to Patch Boundaries

To segment a byte sequence, a patching function must decide where one patch ends and the next begins. In BLT, that decision is expressed through patch boundaries. Every boundary is a place where the model starts a new patch, and the collection of those boundaries determines how the original byte stream is grouped.

The paper emphasizes that these groupings are meant to be contextual. A good patching rule should not merely split text mechanically; it should form patches that reflect how information is distributed in the sequence. That is why the paper treats patching as a learnable or at least data-driven problem rather than a fixed, universal rule. The ideal patch boundaries are those that produce regions of relatively uniform information density, so that the model can spend more compute where prediction is difficult and less where prediction is easy.

# Average Patch Size as the Main Driver of Compute Cost

The central computational fact in the paper is that the number of patches, not the number of bytes alone, determines how often the main transformer is invoked. For a fixed byte sequence, smaller patches produce more patch boundaries, and therefore more transformer steps. Larger patches produce fewer steps and therefore lower compute cost. This is why the average patch size is the key quantity for both training and inference.

This relationship makes patching the model’s main lever for allocating compute. If a sequence is predictable, the paper argues that it is wasteful to spend a full transformer step on every small fragment. If a sequence is dense with information, then finer-grained patches may be worthwhile because they allow the model to devote more capacity where the next-byte prediction is harder. BLT’s patching problem is therefore a problem of matching compute to complexity.

# Strided Patching Every $k$ Bytes

The simplest patching strategy in the paper is strided patching, where the model groups bytes into fixed-size patches of exactly $k$ bytes. This is straightforward to implement and easy to reason about. Because the patch size is fixed, the average number of patches is easy to control, and so the flop cost is easy to manage. The paper notes that this kind of patching was used in earlier work such as MegaByte.

The main advantage of strided patching is therefore its simplicity. It gives a clear knob for changing the effective patch size, and it does so without needing a learned boundary detector or any additional decision-making at runtime. But this simplicity is also its weakness. A fixed stride does not know whether the next bytes are part of whitespace, the end of a word, code, or a dense mathematical expression. It treats all regions alike, even though the paper stresses that different regions can differ greatly in prediction difficulty.

That mismatch leads to two practical problems. First, compute can be wasted on bytes that are easy to predict, such as low-information regions like whitespace in code. Second, compute can be underallocated to sequences with high information density, such as mathematical text. In addition, fixed strides can split the same word differently depending on where the stride happens to fall, which makes the chunking of similar byte sequences inconsistent and non-contextual.

# Benefits and Drawbacks of Static Patching

Static patching, of which strided patching is the clearest example, has a real engineering advantage: it is predictable. Since every patch has a prescribed size, the model designer can control the average patch size directly and can estimate how much compute will be spent on a sequence. This makes static patching a natural baseline and a useful way to reduce the cost of byte-level modeling compared with processing each byte independently.

But the paper’s point is that predictability is not the same as good allocation. Static patching assigns the same amount of global computation to every patch, even when the complexity of the text changes sharply from one region to another. That means static patching is efficient in a narrow sense, but it is not adaptive. It can improve over byte-by-byte processing, yet still fail to line up with where language actually becomes difficult.

This is why the paper treats static patching as an important but limited starting point. It is easy to implement, and it gives a direct way to set cost, but it does not solve the deeper problem of deciding where the model should think more carefully. BLT’s broader goal is to make that decision depend on context rather than on a fixed grid.

# Space Patching and Linguistic Boundary Heuristics

The paper next considers space patching, a simple heuristic that starts a new patch after any space-like byte. In the paper, space-like bytes are defined as bytes that are not a Latin character, a digit, or a UTF-8 continuation byte, with the additional requirement that each patch contain at least one non-space-like byte. This rule uses natural linguistic boundaries, especially in languages where words are separated by spaces.

Space patching has an intuitive appeal because it aligns computation with words. The paper points out that a latent transformer step is then effectively allocated to each word, which ensures that words are patched consistently across sequences. It also makes sense from the standpoint of prediction difficulty: the first byte after a space is often much harder to predict than later bytes in the same word. The paper gives the example of the answer to “Who composed the Magic Flute?” Once the first letter $M$ is known, completing “Mozart” becomes much easier than choosing the first letter in the first place.

This heuristic therefore does better than fixed strides at aligning compute with linguistic structure. It is still simple, but it is no longer blind to one important kind of boundary. In that sense, space patching is a meaningful improvement over purely position-based patching.

# Why Rule-Based Boundaries Help but Remain Limited

Even though space-based boundaries are more linguistically sensible than fixed strides, the paper is clear that rule-based patching remains limited. Space patching works well when the language and domain follow the assumptions built into the rule, but it cannot gracefully handle all languages and domains. It is still tied to a particular surface cue, and that cue does not fully capture where prediction is easy or hard.

A further limitation is that rule-based boundaries cannot vary the patch size in a truly contextual way. Every time the rule fires, it creates a boundary, regardless of whether the text at that point is unusually predictable or unusually uncertain. This means that rule-based patching improves alignment with language structure, but it does not solve the more general problem of adapting compute to information density. The paper’s larger argument is that a good patching function should respond to the difficulty of the next-byte prediction itself, not only to a surface heuristic.

# Dynamic Entropy Patching: A Data-Driven Alternative

To move beyond handcrafted boundaries, the paper introduces entropy patching, which uses next-byte uncertainty to place patch boundaries. The key idea is to train a small byte-level autoregressive language model on the same data distribution and use that model to estimate how uncertain the next byte is at each position. The paper writes the entropy under the byte distribution $p_e$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ as
$$
H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i=v \mid \pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i=v \mid \pmb{x}_{<i}).
$$
Patch boundaries are then identified at positions where the uncertainty is high.

The paper studies two ways to turn these entropy estimates into boundaries. One uses a global threshold,
$$
H(x_t) > \theta_g,
$$
and the other looks for a relative increase,
$$
H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r.
$$
The second method can also be understood as finding places where the entropy stops decreasing monotonically within a patch. In both cases, the goal is the same: to form patches around regions of relatively uniform information density.

This approach is different in spirit from rule-based patching. Instead of assuming that spaces or fixed positions tell us where the hard decisions are, BLT estimates uncertainty directly and places boundaries where the model expects the next byte to change in difficulty. The paper computes these boundaries in a lightweight preprocessing step during dataloading, which keeps the method practical while making the segmentation more responsive to the actual training data.

# The Distinction Between Patches and Fixed-Vocabulary Tokens

A crucial part of the paper’s argument is that patches are not tokens. Tokens come from a finite vocabulary chosen before training, while patches are dynamically formed sequences of bytes with no fixed vocabulary. This distinction matters because tokens are created by a tokenizer, which compresses text according to a heuristic scheme. The model then operates on the resulting token sequence, but it no longer has direct access to the underlying byte-level features.

The paper also stresses that tokenization-based models allocate the same amount of compute to every token. That creates a trade-off: increasing the vocabulary can make tokens longer on average and reduce the number of model steps, but it also increases the size of the output projection and embedding tables. The paper points to Llama 3 as an example, where the average token size increases compared with Llama 2, but at the cost of a much larger embedding table. This fixed-vocabulary setup leaves little room for truly dynamic control over step count and compute.

Patches, by contrast, are not restricted by a vocabulary size fixed in advance. Any group of bytes can be mapped to a latent patch representation by the learned encoder and decoder modules. That flexibility is what lets BLT decouple the notion of a computation unit from the notion of a token. In the paper’s framing, this is why patching can allocate compute more efficiently than tokenization.

# Incremental Patching and Why It Matters During Generation

For patching to work in an autoregressive model, the boundary decision must be available when only the prefix has been generated. The reason is straightforward: whether the current bytes form a new patch determines whether the expensive latent transformer should be invoked. During generation, the model cannot rely on future bytes that have not yet appeared. Therefore a valid patching scheme must satisfy the incremental property
$$
f_p(\pmb{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\pmb{x})_{<i}.
$$

This condition says that if you patch a prefix on its own, you should get the same boundaries you would get if that prefix were part of the full sequence. That is exactly what BLT needs in order to generate byte by byte without ambiguity. If the patching rule depended on the continuation, then the model could not know how to segment the current prefix consistently as it generates new bytes. So incremental patching is not just a formal nicety; it is a requirement for causal generation.

# Why Standard BPE Is Not an Incremental Patching Scheme

The paper uses this incremental criterion to draw a sharp line between patches and standard BPE tokenization. BPE is built from a finite vocabulary of byte groups, but the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on the continuation. That means the tokenization of a prefix is not guaranteed to be stable when more bytes are appended, so BPE does not satisfy the incremental condition above.

This is one reason the paper treats BPE and patching as fundamentally different. BPE decides segmentation with a global view of the sequence, whereas BLT needs a segmentation rule that works online during generation. The paper notes that one can add a special delimiter token to make BPE incremental, but that comes at the cost of increasing the byte-sequence length. That extra length undermines the very efficiency gains that tokenization is supposed to provide.

The broader lesson is that patching is not just “tokenization at a different granularity.” It is a causal, dynamically formed segmentation of bytes that is designed to support adaptive compute allocation. That is why the patching problem sits at the center of BLT: choosing boundaries well is what allows the model to spend more effort where the next byte is uncertain and less where the sequence is easy to continue.

Entropy Patching: Learning Where Compute Should Go

Entropy patching is BLT’s data-driven way of deciding where a new patch should begin. Because each patch determines when the expensive global transformer is invoked, patch boundaries are effectively a mechanism for allocating compute. The guiding idea is straightforward: if the next byte is hard to predict, the model should spend more computation there; if the next byte is already highly predictable, several bytes can be grouped together and processed with less frequent global computation. In this way, patches become contextualized groupings of bytes with relatively uniform information density.

# Why entropy is a useful proxy for local prediction difficulty

Entropy patching starts from a simple intuition about language modeling: not every byte in a sequence is equally difficult to predict. Some bytes appear in highly constrained contexts, while others open up many possible continuations. The paper uses entropy as the signal for this uncertainty. High entropy means the model is unsure about the next byte, which suggests that the local region is information-dense and deserves a fresh patch boundary. Low entropy means the continuation is relatively obvious, so it is efficient to keep grouping bytes into a longer patch.

This matches the broader design goal of BLT. The architecture is built to avoid spending the same amount of compute on every byte. A large transformer step is not always necessary, especially for predictable parts of a sequence such as the ends of words. By contrast, positions where the model must choose among many plausible continuations, such as the beginning of a word or a harder transition in the text, are better candidates for a new patch. The paper’s example of predicting the first byte of “Mozart” after “Who composed the Magic Flute?” illustrates the point: once the first letter is known, the remaining bytes are much easier to predict.

# Training a small byte-level language model to estimate entropies

To decide where these high-uncertainty regions are, BLT uses a separate small byte-level auto-regressive language model, called the entropy model. This model is trained on the same training distribution as the full BLT model, so its uncertainty estimates are aligned with the kind of data the main model will see. It is intentionally lightweight compared with the full architecture, because its role is not to solve the language modeling task itself but to provide patching signals.

In the default setup described in the paper, the entropy model has $100$M parameters, $14$ layers, a hidden dimensionality of $512$, and sliding-window attention over $512$ bytes. The paper also studies different entropy model sizes, receptive fields, and architectures in ablations. An important practical note is that when the receptive field is small enough, the trained entropy model can be encoded in an efficient lookup table. This reinforces the idea that the entropy model is a preprocessing tool for compute allocation, not the main expensive component of BLT.

# Computing next-byte entropy from the entropy model

For each byte position $i$, the entropy model produces a distribution over possible next bytes conditioned on the previous bytes. The paper defines the next-byte entropy score using the model distribution $p_e$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ as

$$
H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})
$$

where $\mathbf{x}_{<i}$ is the prefix before position $i$. The key point is that this score is computed from the model’s belief about the next byte, given the local context. The score therefore changes as the prefix changes, which is exactly what makes it useful for dynamic patching.

You can think of this score as a local summary of how constrained the next byte is. When the distribution over bytes is sharp, the entropy score is low and the next byte is easy to predict. When the distribution is spread across many possible bytes, the score is high and the region is difficult. BLT uses these scores to decide where the patch boundaries should go, rather than relying on a fixed rule like whitespace or a fixed stride.

# Global threshold patching using a fixed entropy cutoff

The simplest entropy-based patching rule in the paper is global threshold patching. Here, a byte starts a new patch whenever its entropy score exceeds a fixed threshold $\theta_g$:

$$
H(x_t) > \theta_g
$$

This creates a very direct connection between uncertainty and computation. Bytes whose entropy exceeds the threshold are treated as sufficiently hard to justify a new patch, while bytes below the threshold remain inside the current patch. In effect, the threshold acts as a dial that controls how aggressively the model splits the input into patches.

This rule is attractive because it is easy to interpret. A lower threshold causes more boundaries and therefore shorter patches, which increases the number of latent transformer steps. A higher threshold produces fewer boundaries and longer patches, which reduces compute. Since patch size is the main factor controlling how many times the global transformer runs, the threshold becomes a practical way to trade off between efficiency and modeling granularity.

# Approximate monotonicity patching using entropy increases

The paper also proposes a second entropy-based rule called approximate monotonicity patching. Instead of using an absolute entropy cutoff, this rule looks at how entropy changes from one byte to the next. A new patch begins when the entropy rises enough relative to the previous byte:

$$
H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r
$$

This criterion is useful because it captures local changes in predictability rather than just absolute uncertainty. The paper describes it as identifying points that break approximate monotonically decreasing entropy within the patch. The intuition is that within a patch, predictability often gradually increases as the local continuation becomes more determined; a sudden rise in entropy suggests a transition to a new region and therefore a good place for a boundary.

This relative rule can be helpful because it does not depend only on whether a byte is globally “hard” or “easy.” Instead, it reacts to local shifts in the uncertainty profile. In the paper’s experiments, the global-threshold and approximate-monotonicity rules are both used as entropy-based patching methods, and they are compared as alternative ways to separate low-entropy and high-entropy bytes.

# Interpreting boundaries as breaks in decreasing predictability

The most intuitive way to understand entropy patching is as segmentation by predictability. Inside a patch, BLT is trying to group together bytes whose uncertainty is broadly similar. When predictability is decreasing smoothly, it makes sense to keep the current patch going. When predictability changes sharply, a new patch is warranted because the model is entering a different kind of local problem.

This interpretation fits the paper’s broader claim that patches should reflect information density. The first bytes of a word, for example, often carry more uncertainty than the later bytes. Likewise, the beginning of a new sentence or a new answer can be much harder to predict than the tail end of a familiar pattern. Entropy patching uses these shifts to determine where global computation should be concentrated. The result is not just compression for its own sake, but compression that is guided by the model’s own sense of difficulty.

# Choosing thresholds to target a desired average patch size

Because patch size determines how often the global latent transformer is called, the entropy threshold is not arbitrary. The paper chooses thresholds so that the resulting patching scheme achieves a desired average patch size on the pretraining data mixture. This makes patch size a controlled design parameter rather than an accidental consequence of the entropy model.

This matters for two reasons. First, it lets BLT match a compute budget by tuning how many bytes are grouped into each patch. Second, it allows fair comparisons between different patching schemes and against tokenizer-based models. Larger patches reduce the number of global transformer steps and therefore lower inference flops, but they also change the modeling problem by making each patch less fine-grained. The threshold therefore determines not only efficiency, but also the balance between local and global modeling capacity.

# Preprocessing patch boundaries during data loading

In BLT, patch boundaries are identified during a lightweight preprocessing step executed during dataloading. This means that the model does not need to learn a separate classifier to decide boundaries on the fly. Instead, the entropy model is used to produce entropy scores, and those scores are converted into patch boundaries before the main model processes the data.

The paper explicitly contrasts this with methods that train a classifier to predict entropy-based patch boundaries. BLT’s approach is simpler: compute entropies, apply the threshold rule, and obtain the patch segmentation. Once the patches are determined, the main model can operate on them as its primary units of computation. This makes patching a clear preprocessing stage rather than an additional learned component inside the architecture.

# Entropy drift and the effect of model context

A subtle issue with entropy patching is that entropy estimates depend on the context given to the entropy model. Because the entropy model only sees a finite amount of preceding text, changing the amount of available context can change the estimated entropy even when the local content is the same. The paper refers to this effect as entropy drift.

The paper observes this drift empirically in structured content such as multiple-choice tasks, which are often highly repetitive. In those settings, entropy patching can yield progressively larger patches because repeated content becomes lower entropy in the entropy model’s context. That means patch size can vary not only because the text itself changes, but also because the history seen by the entropy model changes. This is an important practical point: patch boundaries are sensitive to how entropy is measured, not just to the text being segmented.

# Resetting entropy context and using newline boundaries in large-scale runs

To address entropy drift in the large-scale BLT-Entropy run, the paper makes two related adjustments. First, it resets the entropy context with new lines. Second, it uses the approximate monotonicity constraint, which the authors say suffers less from entropy drift caused by changes in context length. These changes are aimed at making the entropy estimates more stable in repetitive or structured data.

Importantly, this adjustment only changes how entropies are computed. The overall procedure for selecting the entropy threshold remains the same. In the large-scale run with patch size $4.5$, this design choice helps keep patching behavior more controlled when the data contains long repetitive stretches. The result is a patching scheme that remains tied to local uncertainty, but is less affected by longer-range context artifacts.

Entropy patching is the core mechanism that lets BLT decide where compute should go. By turning next-byte uncertainty into patch boundaries, the model spends more of its capacity on difficult regions and less on easy ones. That is what makes the architecture dynamic: it does not treat every byte the same, but instead allocates computation according to the local structure of the data.

BLT at a High Level: A Hierarchical Byte-Level Language Model

# The Three-Part BLT Architecture: Encoder, Global Model, Decoder

BLT is built from three cooperating components: a local byte-level encoder $\mathcal{E}$, a large global autoregressive Transformer $\mathcal{G}$, and a local byte-level decoder $\mathcal{D}$. The central idea is that raw bytes are first turned into compact patch representations, those patch representations are processed by the large global model, and then the resulting patch outputs are expanded back into raw bytes. In other words, BLT does not replace bytes with tokens. Instead, it inserts a learned middle level of representation between bytes and the large Transformer.

This makes BLT a hierarchical model. The hierarchy is not merely conceptual; it is built directly into the computation graph. The local encoder and local decoder are both much smaller than the global model, with $l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$ and $l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$. The local models are responsible for handling byte-level detail, while the global model is responsible for the heavier sequence modeling over patches. That division of labor is the key to understanding the architecture. The model does not try to do everything at the byte level, and it does not discard byte-level information at the input. It keeps bytes at the interface and compresses them only where the computation is most expensive.

# Bytes as the Input and Output Interface of the Model

BLT works directly on raw bytes. On the input side, a byte sequence is represented as $b_i$, and each byte is embedded using a matrix with shape $\mathbb{R}^{256 \times h_{\mathcal{E}}}$, since there are 256 possible byte values. On the output side, the model predicts raw bytes again, denoted in the paper as $y_i$. So BLT is byte-in, byte-out. It does not require a fixed vocabulary of tokens to translate the text into model inputs or outputs.

This choice matters because it keeps the model close to the actual written signal. The architecture never loses access to the underlying byte sequence, which means that the model can reason over spelling, punctuation, and other character-level details directly. In the paper’s framing, tokenization is a heuristic preprocessing step that groups bytes into a static set of tokens, while BLT avoids that preprocessing step entirely. The local encoder and decoder are what make this possible: they let the model remain byte-native while still allowing the middle of the system to operate on compressed representations.

# Patch Representations as the Main Units of Global Computation

The middle of BLT does not operate on bytes. It operates on patches. A patch is a dynamically formed group of bytes, and the sequence of patches is shorter than the original byte sequence. If the original byte sequence has length $n$, patching maps it to a patch sequence of length $m < n$. The important point is that the global Transformer does not see individual bytes as its main units. It sees patch representations, which are latent summaries produced by the local encoder.

These patch representations are the main units of computation in BLT. The paper emphasizes that the computational cost of the model is primarily determined by the number of steps executed by the main Transformer, and in BLT that means the number of patches. A longer patch means fewer global Transformer steps. A shorter patch means more steps. So patch size is not just a descriptive property of the data; it is a control knob for how much computation the global model spends.

This is why patches are not just a different name for tokens. They are latent computational units. A patch representation is not drawn from a fixed vocabulary, and it is not a predefined symbol. It is a learned summary of a contiguous byte span, built to carry enough information for the global model to reason over. The local encoder constructs these representations, the global model transforms them, and the local decoder turns them back into bytes.

# Why the Global Latent Transformer Receives Most of the Compute Budget

The global latent Transformer $\mathcal{G}$ is the expensive part of BLT, and that is by design. It is the large autoregressive model in the middle of the system, and it uses a block-causal attention mask so that each patch can attend to previous patches and the current patch, but not future ones. Because it processes patch representations rather than bytes, it is the place where the model can spend substantial capacity without paying that cost on every byte.

This choice reflects an important observation in the paper: at scale, the main cost of a Transformer is not just attention, but also the large feed-forward layers that run at every step. If those steps are taken once per byte, cost becomes very high. If they are taken once per patch, cost drops. BLT therefore concentrates most of its parameter budget and flops in the global model while keeping the byte-level modules lightweight. The global model is the one that benefits most from being large, because it is the component that performs the main long-range reasoning over the compressed sequence.

The intuition is that not every byte deserves the same amount of global processing. Predicting the end of a common word is often easier than deciding what new word should begin a sentence. The paper uses this kind of example to motivate the architecture: the model should not pay the same global cost for every local prediction. Instead, it should reserve the heavy machinery for the places where the data are complex and uncertain.

# How Local Models Allow Byte Awareness Without Paying Full Global Cost on Every Byte

The local encoder and local decoder are what let BLT stay byte-aware while still being efficient. The local encoder reads byte sequences and turns them into patch representations. It uses a lightweight Transformer stack with a local block-causal attention mask, so each byte attends only to a fixed window of preceding bytes, and that window cannot cross document boundaries. This gives the encoder access to local byte context without forcing the expensive global model to process every byte directly.

The encoder also enriches byte representations with hash $n$-gram embeddings. The paper constructs byte grams from sizes 3 through 8, hashes them into embedding tables, and adds those embeddings to the byte embedding before normalization. The purpose is to give the local model richer awareness of the surrounding byte pattern. In other words, the local encoder is not a mere downsampler. It is a byte-level module that learns expressive representations of local structure before pooling them into patches.

The local decoder performs the complementary task. It takes the global patch outputs and reconstructs byte-level representations, then predicts raw bytes autoregressively. The decoder uses cross-attention in the opposite direction: bytes are the queries, and patch representations are the keys and values. This means the global model can remain compressed and efficient, while the decoder restores the fine-grained byte sequence. The result is a system where byte-level detail is preserved at the edges of the architecture, but the expensive part of the model is not forced to run at byte resolution throughout.

# Autoregressive Prediction in a Hierarchical Model

BLT remains autoregressive, but it is autoregressive at two levels. At the patch level, the global Transformer predicts patch outputs from previous patch inputs. At the byte level, the local decoder predicts each byte from previous bytes and the patch information supplied by the global model. So generation is not a single flat process. It is a nested one.

This nested structure is important because it keeps the model compatible with incremental generation. During generation, BLT must decide whether the current position is a patch boundary, since that decision determines whether more compute from the global Transformer should be invoked. The paper states this formally as an incremental patching property: a valid patching scheme must satisfy
$$f_p(\pmb{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\pmb{x})_{<i}.$$
This means that the patching of a prefix must not depend on future bytes. The architecture needs this property because generation proceeds left to right, and the model cannot wait for future bytes before deciding how to allocate computation to the current prefix.

This is where BLT differs from tokenization in an important way. The paper points out that BPE is not an incremental patching scheme, because the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on the continuation. BLT, by contrast, is built so that patch boundaries can be determined incrementally. That makes the hierarchical autoregressive process workable at inference time. The model can generate, decide on patch boundaries, and then invoke the global Transformer only when needed.

# How BLT Dynamically Allocates More Compute to Harder Parts of the Sequence

The central mechanism for dynamic compute allocation is entropy patching. BLT uses a small byte-level language model to estimate the next-byte entropy at each position, and it places patch boundaries where the entropy indicates that the prediction is difficult. The paper gives two criteria: a global entropy threshold,
$$H(x_t) > \theta_g,$$
and an approximate monotonicity criterion,
$$H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r.$$
Both methods are ways of finding places where the next byte is unusually uncertain.

The intuition is straightforward. If the next byte is hard to predict, the model should spend more computation there. If the next few bytes are easy to predict, they can be grouped into a longer patch and processed with fewer global steps. This makes patches a form of adaptive compression. The model is not compressing all regions equally. It is compressing predictable regions more aggressively and uncertain regions less aggressively.

The paper’s examples make this idea concrete. The first byte of a word after a sentence boundary can be much harder to predict than the remaining bytes of the word. Likewise, structured content such as multiple choice questions can produce longer patches in repetitive regions, because the entropy model sees low uncertainty there. BLT therefore allocates more global compute where information density is high and less where it is low. That is the mechanism behind the claim that patches scale better than tokens.

# Why Operating Without a Fixed Patch Vocabulary Matters

A fixed vocabulary is one of the defining features of tokenization-based language models. Tokens are byte groups drawn from a finite set determined before training. BLT does something different. It uses patches, which are dynamically formed byte groups without a fixed vocabulary. This difference is not cosmetic. It changes how compute is controlled and how information is represented.

In tokenized models, increasing vocabulary size tends to make tokens larger on average, which reduces the number of steps the model takes, but also increases the size of the output projection layer. The paper stresses that this tradeoff leaves little room for major changes in token size and inference cost. BLT breaks that tradeoff. Because patches are not drawn from a fixed vocabulary, the architecture can vary average patch size without paying the same vocabulary cost that tokenizers impose.

Just as importantly, BLT preserves direct access to byte features. With tokens, the model only sees the token identity, not the underlying bytes. With patches, the local encoder reads the bytes directly and creates patch representations from them. That means the same byte sequence can be grouped differently depending on context, and the grouping can change to reflect prediction difficulty rather than a predetermined compression scheme. This flexibility is what makes BLT a hierarchical byte-level model rather than just a token model with a different segmentation rule. The patches are learned latent units of computation, not a new static vocabulary.

The Local Encoder: Turning Byte Sequences into Patch Representations

# Byte Embedding Lookup for the 256-Byte Vocabulary

The local encoder in BLT begins from the simplest possible raw input representation: individual bytes. Because bytes take values in a fixed range of $256$ possibilities, the model starts with an embedding lookup matrix of shape $\mathbb{R}^{256 \times h_{\mathcal{E}}}$. Each byte $b_i$ is mapped to a learned vector $x_i$ in this embedding space. This is the first step in converting raw byte sequences into something a transformer can process.

The choice to embed bytes directly is important because BLT is designed to operate without a fixed vocabulary of tokens. Instead of assuming that the input has already been segmented into words or subwords, the model begins with the universal byte alphabet. This means every possible text string can be represented in the same basic format, regardless of language, orthography, or domain. The encoder therefore does not depend on a tokenizer to decide what counts as a unit of input.

At this stage, however, the embedding of a single byte is only a local and very narrow view of the sequence. A single byte can tell the model which character fragment is present, but it cannot directly express how that byte fits into a larger pattern. The local encoder therefore does not stop at these raw byte embeddings. Instead, it augments them with information from surrounding bytes and then uses attention mechanisms to organize the result into patch representations.

# Why Single-Byte Embeddings Are Not Enough

A raw byte embedding captures identity, but not enough context. Many linguistic and structural patterns in text are not determined by one byte alone, but by how that byte combines with nearby bytes. For example, a byte can be part of a word, part of a prefix, part of a character sequence in another script, or part of a symbol in code or math. A single-byte embedding cannot distinguish these possibilities by itself.

BLT addresses this by explicitly modeling not just the current byte $b_i$, but also byte n-grams ending at position $i$. The paper’s motivation is that robust and expressive representations require the encoder to incorporate information about the preceding bytes. This is especially important because the local encoder is responsible for building patch representations from raw bytes at low cost. If it only knew the isolated identity of each byte, the patch representations would be much less informative.

The paper’s approach is to treat byte-level information and n-gram context as complementary. The raw byte embedding provides the base identity of the input, while the additional n-gram features supply a compressed summary of the immediate context. Together, they create a richer starting point for the encoder’s transformer layers and cross-attention pooling.

# Hash n-Gram Embeddings Over Byte n-Grams of Length 3 Through 8

BLT augments each byte with hash embeddings of the byte n-grams ending at that position. For each byte position $i$, the model constructs byte grams
$$
g_{i,n} = \{b_{i-n+1}, \ldots, b_i\}
$$
for $n$ ranging from $3$ to $8$. These are overlapping windows of the preceding bytes, so the model sees multiple scales of recent context at once. The smallest n-grams capture short local patterns, while the larger ones capture broader fragments.

The paper uses n-gram lengths from $3$ to $8$ because these sizes are long enough to encode useful subword structure without becoming too expensive. This gives the encoder a way to represent the current byte as part of multiple local contexts. The model is not trying to memorize entire words at this stage; rather, it is building features that reflect how bytes tend to appear together.

Each n-gram length gets its own embedding table. For each $n \in \{3,4,5,6,7,8\}$, the n-gram is mapped through a hash function into a fixed-size embedding table $E_n^{hash}$. This means that the model does not need to store a separate learned embedding for every possible n-gram, which would be impractical. Instead, the hash function compresses the space of n-grams into a manageable table size.

The result is that the encoder can represent many different byte patterns using a bounded amount of memory. This is the core idea behind the hash n-gram features: they provide contextual signal without requiring a fixed vocabulary of subwords.

# RollPolyHash and Fixed-Size Hash Tables

The paper defines the hash embedding lookup as
$$
e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3,\ldots,8} E_n^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))
$$
with
$$
\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \% |E_n^{hash}|.
$$
The role of RollPolyHash is to convert the byte n-gram into an index in a fixed-size embedding table. The modulo operation ensures that the hash output always lies within the table size. The key idea here is not that the hash uniquely identifies every n-gram, but that it provides a fast and memory-bounded way to map many possible byte sequences into a learned feature space.

This design is useful because there are far too many possible byte n-grams to assign each one its own embedding directly. By using a hash table, BLT gains a compact approximation of n-gram identity. Different n-grams may collide in the same table entry, but the paper treats this as part of the tradeoff that makes the method practical at scale.

The paper also notes that the embeddings are normalized by the number of n-gram sizes plus one. That normalization keeps the scale of the augmented input under control as the model adds together the raw byte embedding and several hash-based features. Without this step, the input magnitude could grow simply because more features are being summed. Normalization ensures that the encoder receives a stable input representation regardless of how many n-gram terms contribute.

# Combining Raw Byte Embeddings with Hashed n-Gram Features

The encoder input for byte $i$ is formed by adding the raw byte embedding to the hash n-gram embeddings. In the paper’s notation, the augmented embedding is
$$
e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3,\ldots,8} E_n^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n})).
$$
This is a simple but powerful combination. The raw byte embedding preserves the identity of the current byte, while the hash n-gram features summarize the local context in which that byte appears.

This combination reflects a general design principle in the paper: the encoder should not force the model to choose between isolated byte identity and contextual structure. Instead, it should expose both at once. The raw embedding tells the model what the input is, while the n-gram features tell it something about what kind of sequence it is part of. For example, the same byte can occur in many different environments, and the n-gram features help distinguish those environments.

The paper reports that these hash n-gram embeddings are important for performance. In the ablations, hash embeddings help across domains, especially on Wikipedia and GitHub. The authors also find diminishing returns after about $300K$ hashes, indicating that the feature helps substantially, but only up to a point. In the encoder, this means the n-gram features are not a minor detail; they are one of the mechanisms that make the byte-level representation competitive with token-based models.

# Local Block-Causal Self-Attention Over a Fixed Byte Window

After constructing the augmented byte embeddings, the local encoder processes them with a transformer stack. The encoder is lightweight, with $l_{\mathcal E} << l_{\mathcal G}$ layers, because its purpose is not to do all the reasoning itself. Its purpose is to transform bytes into expressive patch representations efficiently.

The local encoder uses a local block-causal attention mask. Each byte attends only to a fixed window of $w_{\mathcal E}$ preceding bytes. This means the encoder can model local dependencies without paying the cost of full-sequence attention. The window is fixed, so the computational cost stays controlled even when the input is long.

The paper emphasizes that this local transformer can cross dynamic patch boundaries but cannot cross document boundaries. That detail matters because patch boundaries are not the same as semantic boundaries, and the encoder is allowed to build context across them when necessary. But it still respects document separation, so it does not mix unrelated text from different examples.

Intuitively, this local self-attention stage gives each byte a context-aware representation before the model decides how to pool bytes into patches. It is a way of refining the raw byte and n-gram features into a richer local state. Those refined states are then used by the encoder’s cross-attention block to produce patch-level representations.

# Pooling Byte Information to Initialize Patch Queries

A central step in the encoder is the conversion from byte-level states to patch-level queries. BLT does not simply average bytes into patches in a fixed way. Instead, the encoder first initializes each patch query by pooling the byte representations corresponding to that patch. The paper states that the query vector for each patch $p_j$ is initialized by pooling the byte representations in the patch and then applying a linear projection.

This is important because the patch representation should summarize the bytes inside the patch before it attends back to them. The pooling operation provides a first approximation of what the patch contains. It is a compact way to create a patch-level state from a variable number of byte representations. Since patches can be dynamically sized, the pooling step has to be able to handle different patch lengths naturally.

The paper notes that in the experiments, max-pooling is used to initialize the queries for the first encoder cross-attention layer. That means the encoder starts with the strongest byte-level signal within the patch as a summary. The exact pooling choice is part of the implementation, but conceptually the role is the same: to produce an initial patch query that is informed by the bytes it represents.

This step helps bridge the byte and patch levels. The local encoder is not just a byte model and not yet the global patch model. It is the module that constructs a patch representation from bytes in a way that preserves useful local content.

# Multi-Head Encoder Cross-Attention from Patches to Bytes

After pooling, BLT applies a cross-attention block inside the encoder. This block is modeled closely after the input cross-attention module of Perceiver, but adapted so that the latent representations correspond to patch representations rather than a fixed latent array. The encoder cross-attention uses multiple heads and a residual connection, with pre-LayerNorm on queries, keys, and values.

The purpose of this module is to let each patch representation gather information from the bytes inside that patch. In the paper’s formulation, the query for patch $j$ is derived from the pooled patch state, while the keys and values are projections of the byte representations. Formally, the queries come from the patch representation, and the keys and values come from the byte-level hidden states from the previous layer.

This is a crucial difference from ordinary self-attention. Instead of every byte attending to every other byte, the model lets a patch-level query summarize the bytes that belong to that patch. The result is a lower-dimensional representation that can be passed to the global transformer. In this sense, encoder cross-attention is a pooling mechanism, but one that is learned and content-dependent rather than fixed.

Because the attention is multi-headed, the model can collect different kinds of information from the same patch at once. The paper also notes that when patch representations are larger than the local model dimension, the cross-attention keeps the representations in multiple heads of dimension $h_{\mathcal E}$ and later concatenates them into dimension $h_{\mathcal G}$. This design matches the local and global modules while preserving a tractable computation pattern.

# Restricting Each Patch Query to Attend Only to Bytes Within That Patch

One of the most distinctive features of the encoder is that each patch query only attends to the bytes in its own patch. This is enforced by a patch-specific masking strategy. The patch representation is therefore built from the exact bytes that belong to that patch, rather than from the whole sequence.

This restriction is what makes the cross-attention act like a learned pooling operator instead of a global attention mechanism. It keeps computation localized and ensures that the patch representation is a summary of the intended byte group. Since patches are the units that the global transformer will operate on, each patch must faithfully represent its own content.

The paper’s masking choice also aligns with the larger BLT philosophy: compute should be allocated where it is needed. A patch that covers a simple and predictable region can be represented efficiently, while a more complex patch can still be summarized using the same mechanism, but with more meaningful local context. By restricting attention within the patch, the encoder maintains this localized structure and avoids wasting computation on unrelated bytes.

The absence of positional embeddings in this cross-attention module is also worth noting. The paper explicitly says that no positional embeddings are used there. The patch-local byte order is already present in the byte representations and the surrounding transformer layers, so the cross-attention block can focus on gathering content rather than encoding position again.

# Dimension Matching Between Local Encoder States and Global Patch States

The local encoder and the global transformer operate at different hidden dimensions. The local encoder uses states in dimension $h_{\mathcal E}$, while the global patch representations live in dimension $h_{\mathcal G}$. This means the encoder has to convert byte-level information into a form suitable for the global model.

The paper handles this by using the patch cross-attention block to produce patch representations that are later concatenated into the larger global dimension. In practice, the patch representations are maintained as multiple heads of dimension $h_{\mathcal E}$ during cross-attention and then concatenated into the $h_{\mathcal G}$ space. This makes the representation compatible with the global transformer without forcing the local encoder to be as expensive as the global model.

This dimension separation is part of BLT’s efficiency story. The local encoder is kept lightweight, so it can do the byte-level work cheaply. The global transformer is reserved for the patch-level computation that is more expensive but also more powerful. By keeping these responsibilities separate, BLT can spend most of its compute where patch-level reasoning is actually needed.

The decoder uses a mirrored version of this idea, but within the encoder alone the main point is that the byte-level representations are condensed into a patch-level format that can be passed to the global model. That condensation is what allows the overall architecture to scale more efficiently than a byte-only transformer would.

# Why the Encoder Design Improves Robustness and Representation Quality

The paper argues that the local encoder improves both robustness and representation quality because it works directly from bytes while still capturing structured local context. Direct byte modeling preserves information that tokenizers can obscure or discard. Since the encoder sees the raw byte sequence, it is sensitive to orthographic details, subword structure, and character-level variations that token-based systems may miss.

The hash n-gram features strengthen this effect by giving the encoder direct access to short byte patterns. This helps the model recognize local structure in words, code, and other byte sequences without relying on a fixed vocabulary. The paper’s robustness results later show that BLT performs well on noisy text, character-level tasks, and low-resource machine translation. Those gains are consistent with the encoder design, because the encoder preserves and organizes byte-level details rather than collapsing them into a static token inventory.

The local attention window also contributes to representation quality. It lets the encoder contextualize each byte with nearby bytes before patch pooling occurs. That means the patch summary is not just a bag of bytes; it is a learned summary of a locally contextualized sequence. The patch cross-attention then refines this summary into a representation that the global transformer can process efficiently.

In short, the local encoder improves BLT by doing three things at once. It preserves raw byte identity, augments that identity with hashed n-gram context, and then compresses the result into patch representations using local attention and patch-restricted cross-attention. This combination is what allows BLT to build expressive latent units from bytes at low cost, which is the central purpose of the encoder.

The Global Transformer and Local Decoder

# The latent global Transformer as an autoregressive model over patches

At the center of BLT is the latent global Transformer, denoted by $\mathcal{G}$. Unlike a standard language model that runs directly over bytes or tokens, this model operates over patch representations. Its input is a sequence of latent patch vectors $p_j$, and its output is a sequence of patch-level representations $o_j$. The index $j$ refers to patches, not bytes, so the model is reasoning over a compressed sequence whose length depends on how the bytes were grouped by the patching function.

This global model is still autoregressive. That means each patch-level output is computed only from the patches that come before it in the document, together with the current patch position under the block-causal mask. In the paper’s design, $\mathcal{G}$ is the main place where BLT spends compute, so the choice to move from bytes to patches is not just a data-format change. It is the mechanism that makes it possible to allocate the expensive Transformer layers at a coarser and more efficient level of abstraction.

# Block-causal attention within documents

The global Transformer uses a block-causal attention mask. The paper describes this as restricting attention to be up to and including the current patch within the current document. In other words, the model can attend backward through earlier patch representations, but it cannot look ahead to future patches. This is what preserves the autoregressive nature of the model while still allowing patch-based computation.

The “within the current document” part is important because BLT is trained on packed data, where multiple documents may appear in the same batch. The mask ensures that attention does not leak information across document boundaries. So the block-causal pattern enforces two things at once: temporal ordering within a document, and isolation between documents. This lets the global Transformer behave like a proper sequence model while still benefiting from the shorter patch sequence.

# Why patch-level modeling makes long-context computation cheaper

The main reason BLT can be more efficient than byte-level or token-level baselines is that the global Transformer runs once per patch, not once per byte. The paper emphasizes that the number of patches needed to encode a sequence is the main driver of cost. When patches are longer on average, fewer global Transformer steps are needed, and that reduces the flops spent in the expensive part of the architecture.

This matters especially at scale because the global Transformer consumes most of the compute during both pre-training and inference. BLT’s design lets easy regions of text be grouped into longer patches, so the model does not spend the same amount of compute on every byte. Instead, compute can be concentrated where the next-byte prediction problem is harder. In the paper’s framing, this is why patch-based modeling can make long-context computation cheaper: the model is still processing the same underlying data, but it does so through fewer expensive global updates.

# Output patch representations from the global model

The output of the latent global Transformer is a sequence of latent patch representations $o_j$. These are not byte predictions yet. They are internal states that summarize the patch-level context after the model has processed the sequence autoregressively. In the architecture, these outputs act as the bridge between patch-level reasoning and byte-level generation.

It is helpful to think of $o_j$ as the global model’s patch-wise conclusion about what comes next. The paper does not treat them as fixed tokens or as final outputs of the system. Instead, they are latent representations that are handed to the decoder, which then turns them into byte predictions. This division of labor is one of BLT’s main ideas: the global model handles long-range structure over patches, while the decoder converts that information back into raw bytes.

# The local decoder as a byte-level reconstruction and prediction module

BLT’s local decoder, denoted by $\mathcal{D}$, is a lightweight Transformer-based model with $l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers. Its role is to turn global patch information back into byte-level predictions. The paper describes it as decoding a sequence of global patch representations $o_j$ into raw bytes $y_i$, and as predicting bytes as a function of previously decoded bytes. This makes it the byte-level counterpart to the patch-level global model.

The decoder is intentionally small compared with the global Transformer. That choice matches the division of labor in the architecture: the expensive long-range reasoning happens in $\mathcal{G}$, while $\mathcal{D}$ handles the more local job of turning latent information into actual byte outputs. The decoder therefore has to combine two sources of information: the byte-level hidden states created by the local encoder, and the patch-level summaries produced by the global model.

# Decoder cross-attention with bytes as queries and patches as keys and values

In the decoder cross-attention block, BLT reverses the roles used in the encoder. The byte representations are now the queries, and the patch representations are the keys and values. The initial byte representations are taken from the final encoder layer, so the decoder starts with $D_0 = h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$. Then the decoder cross-attention uses the final patch states from the global model, passed through the linear transformation and split operation $\mathcal{D}_C(o_j)$, as the source of keys and values.

The paper writes the decoder update as
\[
B_l = D_{l-1} + W_o\left(\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right),
\]
where
\[
Q_i = W_q(d_{l-1,i}), \quad K_i = W_k(\mathcal{D}_C(o_j)), \quad V_i = W_v(\mathcal{D}_C(o_j)).
\]
This means that each byte position asks the global patch states for information, and then incorporates that information through attention. As in the encoder, BLT uses multiple heads, pre-LayerNorm, no positional embeddings in this cross-attention module, and a residual connection around the block. The intuition is that the decoder is not guessing bytes in isolation; it is consulting the patch-level summary of the sequence before producing each byte-level state.

# Alternating decoder cross-attention and local transformer layers

The local decoder is not just a single cross-attention step followed by an output layer. The paper says it applies a series of alternating cross-attention and Transformer layers. The cross-attention layer comes first, because the decoder needs to turn the patch-level information into byte representations before it can do local byte-level processing. After that, the local decoder Transformer layer operates on the resulting byte sequence.

This alternation is important because it lets the decoder repeatedly combine global and local information. Cross-attention injects the patch-level latent context into the byte stream, and the local Transformer then refines that byte stream using byte-to-byte dependencies. The result is a decoder that can stay aligned with the byte sequence while still benefiting from the higher-level patch representations produced by the global model.

# Predicting bytes with a 256-way output layer

Because BLT works directly with bytes, the decoder predicts from a fixed vocabulary of $256$ byte values. In the FLOPs discussion, this appears as $V = 256$ for the local decoder. This is different from token-based language models, where the output layer depends on a potentially large subword vocabulary. Here, the decoder’s final projection is simply a byte classifier.

This output layer is the final step in turning the decoder’s internal byte representations into an actual next-byte distribution. The model is not choosing among subword units or patches at this stage. It is choosing among the raw byte values themselves. That is what makes the system tokenizer-free at the output level: the global model works over patches, but the decoder ultimately predicts the next byte directly.

# How the decoder uses encoder byte states and global patch states together

A useful way to understand the decoder is to see it as joining fine-grained and coarse-grained information. The local encoder produces byte states, and the final encoder layer output $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$ is used to initialize the decoder. At the same time, the global Transformer produces patch states $o_j$, which carry the broader contextual information learned at the patch level. The decoder combines these two streams through cross-attention.

This is why BLT does not simply discard the encoder once patches have been formed. The encoder’s byte states preserve local structure, while the global patch states summarize higher-level dependencies across the sequence. The decoder can then use both: it starts from the encoder’s byte-level hidden representation, consults the global patch states, and refines the result through alternating layers. In the paper’s design, that cooperation is what lets BLT move from bytes to patches and then back to bytes without losing the information that each level of representation provides.

# Inference-time boundary decisions and incremental generation

At inference time, BLT must decide whether the current step in the byte sequence is a patch boundary, because that decision determines whether the expensive latent Transformer should be invoked. The paper emphasizes that this decision has to be made without looking at future bytes. That is why the patching function must be incremental. Formally, BLT requires
\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}.
\]
This means that if you only know a prefix, the patching of that prefix must already agree with the patching you would get if you later saw the full sequence.

This property is what makes incremental generation possible. The model can process a prefix, decide where the current patch ends, and continue generating without having to revise earlier patch boundaries after seeing more text. The paper contrasts this with BPE tokenization, which is not incremental because the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on what follows. BLT’s patching scheme avoids that problem, so boundary decisions can be made online during generation while remaining consistent with the prefix already produced.

Training BLT Fairly: Data, Hyperparameters, and Measurement

The paper’s experimental design is built around a simple idea: if BLT is to be compared fairly with token-based language models, then the comparison must control the data source, the amount of raw byte context, the effective batch size, the optimization recipe, and even the metric used to report quality. The sections below explain how the authors make those controls explicit so that the resulting comparisons reflect modeling choices rather than bookkeeping differences.

# Pretraining Datasets: the Llama 2 Data Mix and BLT-1T

All of the model scales studied in the paper are trained on one of two datasets. The first is the Llama 2 dataset, which contains $2$T tokens collected from publicly available sources and then cleaned and filtered to improve quality. The second is BLT-1T, a new dataset containing $1$T tokens gathered from public sources and also including a subset of the pretraining data released by Datacomp-LM. The paper emphasizes that neither dataset includes data gathered from Meta products or services.

These two datasets serve different experimental purposes. The Llama 2 mix is used for scaling-law experiments, where the goal is to study architectural choices under the training-token regime recommended by Llama 3. BLT-1T is used for the full pretraining run that supports downstream comparisons with Llama 3. Keeping the roles separate matters because it prevents the paper from mixing architecture selection with final evaluation.

# Tokenizer-Based Baselines and the Llama 3 $128$K Vocabulary

When the paper compares BLT to a token-based baseline, it uses the Llama 3 tokenizer with a $128$K vocabulary. This choice is deliberate: in the authors’ experiments, that tokenizer produced stronger baseline performance than the Llama 2 tokenizer. In other words, the paper does not compare BLT against a weak baseline and call the result a success; it chooses the stronger available tokenizer baseline.

The distinction between tokens and patches is important here. The paper uses “tokens” to mean byte groups drawn from a finite vocabulary fixed before training. By contrast, patches in BLT are dynamically grouped byte sequences and do not come from a fixed vocabulary. That difference matters for fairness because tokenizer choice itself changes how raw text is segmented, compressed, and counted during training and evaluation.

# The Entropy Model Used to Define Dynamic Patches

BLT’s dynamic patching depends on a small byte-level language model that estimates how uncertain the next byte is. The intuition is straightforward: if the next byte is easy to predict, the paper can group more bytes into a larger patch; if the next byte is hard to predict, the model should spend compute sooner by creating a boundary. In this way, patching is not just a segmentation trick but a mechanism for allocating compute according to local data complexity.

In the paper’s default setup, this entropy model is itself a byte-level autoregressive Transformer trained on the same training distribution as the full BLT model. Unless otherwise noted, it has $100$M parameters, $14$ layers, hidden dimensionality $512$, and sliding-window attention over $512$ bytes. The paper computes next-byte entropies as

\[
H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}),
\]

where $p_e$ is the entropy model distribution over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. Patch boundaries are then chosen by thresholding these entropy values, either with a global threshold or by comparing each entropy to the previous one:

\[
\text{Global Constraint} \qquad H(x_t) > \theta_g
\]

\[
\text{Approx. Monotonic Constraint} \qquad H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r.
\]

The paper notes that patch boundaries are identified during a lightweight preprocessing step executed during dataloading, rather than by training a separate boundary predictor in the main model. For the large-scale BLT-Entropy run with patch size $4.5$, the authors reset the entropy context with new lines and use the approximate monotonicity constraint because it suffers less from entropy drift as context length changes. They also note that when the entropy model’s receptive field is small enough, it can even be encoded efficiently as a lookup table.

# Matching Expected Byte Context Across Patch Sizes

A central fairness issue in BLT is that patch size changes the number of expensive Transformer steps the model takes. If a model is allowed to use larger patches while also seeing more raw bytes, then its gains might come from extra context rather than from a better architecture. The paper therefore controls the comparison by matching expected byte context rather than merely matching the number of patches.

The key rule is that the number of bytes in each batch is kept constant in expectation. When the patch size gets larger, the sequence length in bytes is reduced so that the model does not receive extra raw context for free. On the Llama 2 data, the paper uses an $8$k byte context, while on BLT-1T it increases the context to $16$k bytes on average but still keeps the batch size at $16$M bytes on average. This is the right unit of control because BLT’s compute is spent on bytes, not on token counts borrowed from some unrelated tokenizer.

# Packing Patches, Padding Bytes, and Truncation

Dynamic patching means that two sequences with the same number of bytes can produce different numbers of patches. That variability is useful for compute allocation, but it creates a practical problem during training: the expensive latent Transformer would waste work if it had to process many padded patch positions. To avoid this, the paper packs batches of patches so that every batch has the same number of patches.

The authors also use padding and truncation to keep memory usage predictable. During training, they pad and possibly truncate byte sequences to $12$k and $24$k bytes for the Llama 2 and BLT-1T datasets, respectively. The purpose is to avoid memory spikes caused by sequences that happen to produce unusually large patches. These choices are not about improving the model directly; they are about ensuring that the comparison remains stable and that dynamic patching does not make training artificially brittle.

# Shared Transformer Choices Inherited from Llama 3

Apart from the byte-to-patch machinery, BLT largely follows the Transformer design used in Llama 3. The feed-forward layers use SwiGLU, rotary positional embeddings are used in self-attention with $\theta = 500000$, and RMSNorm is used for normalization. The point of inheriting these components is to keep the core Transformer recipe familiar, so that changes in results can be attributed more confidently to BLT’s patching strategy rather than to unrelated architectural inventions.

The paper also chooses attention implementations to match the structure of each module. Flash Attention is used for self-attention layers with fixed masks such as block-causal or fixed-window block-causal attention, and the fixed-width attention mask uses a window size of $512$. Because BLT’s cross-attention layers use dynamic patch-dependent masks, the authors use Flex Attention to obtain fused implementations and speed up training. In short, the implementation strategy is chosen to fit the masking pattern of each part of the model.

# BLT-Specific Optimization and Hyperparameters

The paper studies BLT at several scales: $400$M, $1$B, $2$B, $4$B, and $8$B parameters. Within this range, it fixes a number of BLT-specific hyperparameters so that the scaling experiments are not confounded by constant retuning. One such choice is the initialization of the first encoder cross-attention queries, which uses max-pooling. Another is the use of hash $n$-gram embeddings with $500{,}000$ hashes, a single hash function, and $n$-gram sizes from $3$ to $8$.

For optimization, the paper uses AdamW with $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.95$, and $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$. The learning rate is $4 \times 10^{-4}$ for all models. The authors report that this choice came from a search between $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$ at the $400$M and $1$B scales, where the same learning rate was found to be optimal. Training also uses a linear warm-up of $2000$ steps, followed by cosine decay of the learning rate to $0$, weight decay of $0.1$, and global gradient clipping at $1.0$. For scaling-law experiments on Llama 2 data, the training batch sizes follow the Llama 3 recipe or its equivalent in bytes.

# How BLT FLOPs Are Counted

The paper evaluates efficiency using FLOPs rather than wall-clock time. Its FLOPs accounting largely follows the equations used in Chinchilla-style Transformer analysis, including the feed-forward layers, the $qkvo$ projections in self-attention, and the attention and output projection costs. One important simplification is that the input embedding layer is treated as an efficient lookup, so it contributes $0$ FLOPs. As in prior work, the backward pass is counted as having twice the FLOPs of the forward pass.

For BLT, the per-byte FLOP estimate combines the local encoder, the global latent Transformer, the local decoder, and both cross-attention blocks:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{FL}_{\textsc{BLT}} &= \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m=n_{ctx}/n_p, V=0) / n_p \\
&+ \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0) \\
&+ \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256) \\
&+ \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=n_p, r=n_p/k) \times k/n_p \\
&+ \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=k, r=k/n_p).
\end{aligned}
\]

Here $n_{ctx}$ is the byte sequence length, $n_p$ is the patch size, $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$ are the byte-level context lengths for the local encoder and decoder, $V$ is the output vocabulary size used only in the local decoder, $r$ is the query-to-key/value ratio, and $k$ is the ratio of patch dimension to byte dimension, with $k=2$ in the paper’s figure. The main intuition is that the global Transformer cost is divided by patch size because it runs once per patch, not once per byte. That is why patch size is such a central experimental control.

# Why BPB Replaces Perplexity

Perplexity is a natural metric when every model uses the same tokenizer, because it measures uncertainty per token. But BLT and token-based models do not share the same tokenization, so token-level perplexity is not a fair common unit. A model that segments text into different units is not being asked the same prediction problem, even if it is trained on the same underlying data.

To make the comparison tokenizer-independent, the paper reports Bits-Per-Byte, or BPB:

\[
\mathrm{BPB}(x) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})}{\ln(2) \cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}.
\]

This quantity normalizes the summed cross-entropy by the number of bytes in the input and by the conversion factor $\ln(2)$, so the result is expressed in bits per byte. In practical terms, BPB lets the paper compare a byte-level model and a token-level model on the same raw-text unit, rather than on tokenizer-specific pieces that may not correspond to the same amount of information.

# Why These Controls Make the Comparison Fair

The paper’s experimental setup is careful because BLT changes the unit of computation. If the authors did not control byte context, batch size in bytes, patch packing, and FLOP accounting, then a larger patch size could look better simply because it gives the model more raw context or reduces the number of counted steps. Likewise, if the paper used tokenizer-specific perplexity, then the score would depend on the segmentation scheme rather than on the model’s ability to predict the data.

By fixing the datasets, using a strong tokenizer baseline, calibrating an entropy model on the same training distribution, matching expected byte context, and measuring quality with BPB and efficiency with FLOPs, the paper makes the comparison interpretable. The reader can then understand the reported results as a test of BLT’s central idea: dynamically grouping bytes into patches so that compute is spent where the data is hardest.

Compute-Optimal Scaling: Matching Token-Based LLMs at Scale

# What Compute-Optimal Training Means in Scaling-Law Studies

A compute-optimal scaling study asks a simple but important question: if you have a fixed amount of training compute, how should you divide it between model size and training data so that the resulting model performs as well as possible? In this paper, that idea matters because the authors are not just comparing whether a byte-level model can train at all; they are comparing whether it can train efficiently enough to live on the same kind of scaling curve as a strong token-based model.

To make that comparison precise, the paper follows the compute-optimal training ratios used for modern language models and uses them as the reference point for both baseline token-based models and BLT. The metric used for cross-comparison is bits-per-byte, or BPB, rather than perplexity, because perplexity depends on a fixed tokenizer. BPB is tokenizer-independent and is defined as $\mathrm{BPB}(\pmb{x})=\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x}) / (\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}})$. That makes it the right quantity for asking whether a byte-level architecture is actually competitive with a tokenizer-based one.

The intuition behind compute-optimal training is that a model can fail to look good for reasons that have little to do with architecture. If it is too large for the amount of data it sees, or too small to make use of the data, then it is not being trained at a fair point on the curve. The paper therefore evaluates BLT under matched compute budgets, so that improvements can be attributed to the architecture and patching strategy rather than to an unfair advantage in training scale.

# Parameter-Matched Comparisons from $1\text{B}$ to $8\text{B}$ Parameters

The paper’s main scaling study compares BLT and BPE-based models at matched parameter scales from $1\text{B}$ to $8\text{B}$. The comparisons are parameter-matched in the sense that each BLT model is paired with a BPE Transformer of the same scale and architectural size for the global component, so that the two models are trying to solve the same problem with the same overall capacity. This is important because it isolates the effect of working with bytes and patches from the effect of simply using a bigger model.

These experiments are run on the Llama 2 dataset, and the BPE baseline uses the optimal parameter-to-data ratio determined by Llama 3. In other words, the paper does not compare BLT to a weakly tuned tokenizer model; it compares BLT to a strong, compute-optimal baseline. The result is striking: BLT models either match or outperform their BPE counterparts as the models scale up, and this trend persists across the range of sizes studied. The authors emphasize that this is the first byte-level Transformer architecture to achieve matching scaling trends with BPE-based models in a compute-optimal regime.

The paper also uses an ablated approximation to SpaceByte, built by taking BLT-style space patching without n-gram embeddings or cross-attention. That approximation improves over MegaByte, but it still falls well short of the strong token-based baseline. This makes the main point of the scaling study very clear: the ability to operate on bytes is not enough by itself. The architecture and the patching strategy both matter.

# Training on Up to $4\text{T}$ Bytes Under FLOP Control

A central claim of the paper is that BLT can be trained end-to-end at very large scale without a fixed vocabulary. The authors present a flop-controlled scaling study of byte-level models up to $8\text{B}$ parameters and $4\text{T}$ training bytes. This is a major part of the result, because earlier byte-level work was often limited to much smaller models where the attention cost was the main bottleneck. Here, the paper addresses the more difficult regime where feed-forward computation dominates and scale itself becomes the real test.

To keep the comparison fair, the paper takes care to control the amount of training compute and the amount of data seen. For models using entropy-based patching, the authors choose thresholds that produce a target average patch size on the pretraining mix. They then keep the number of bytes in each batch constant in expectation, so that larger patch sizes do not secretly give the model more context or more data. On the Llama 2 data, they use an $8\text{k}$ byte context, while on BLT-1T they increase the context to an average of $16\text{k}$ bytes while maintaining the same average batch size of $16\text{M}$ bytes. Because dynamic patching changes the ratio of bytes to patches, they also pack batches of patches to avoid padding in the more expensive latent transformer.

These choices matter because the study is trying to answer a narrow question: does patching let BLT spend the same compute more effectively than a token-based model? If the answer were confounded by longer contexts, larger batches, or more bytes per step, then the conclusion would be weak. The paper’s experimental design is meant to rule that out.

# Evidence That BLT Matches or Exceeds BPE Scaling Trends

The main scaling result is that BLT matches the training FLOP-controlled performance of Llama 3 while using up to $50\%$ fewer flops at inference. That is the core reason the paper argues that patches scale better than tokens: BLT can spend its compute more selectively, rather than assigning the same amount of work to every token-like unit.

This becomes especially clear when the authors examine patch size. Tokenizers such as Llama 2 and Llama 3 produce average token sizes of about $3.7$ and $4.4$ bytes, respectively, while BLT can operate with average patch sizes of $6$ or even $8$ bytes. Since the latent transformer is run once per patch, larger patches reduce the number of expensive global steps. In that sense, inference flops are inversely related to average patch size. The paper notes that an $8$-byte patch size can lead to nearly $50\%$ inference flop savings.

The important scaling observation is that the larger-patch BLT models do not just save compute; they also scale well. In the paper’s compute-optimal comparisons, a BLT model with patch size $8$ bytes starts out worse than the BPE Llama 2 baseline at $1\text{B}$ parameters, but ends up better by the $7\text{B}$ scale. That is a strong sign that larger patches become more useful as model size increases, rather than being a short-term trick that only works at small scale.

# Why Static Patching Falls Short

The most basic way to build patches is to use a fixed stride, such as grouping every $k$ bytes into a patch. This is easy to implement and it gives a straightforward way to control cost, because the average patch size directly determines how often the global transformer is invoked. However, the paper argues that this simplicity comes with a serious drawback: compute is not allocated according to difficulty.

The intuition is easy to see. Some byte regions are highly predictable, such as many whitespace or formatting patterns, while others are much more information-dense, such as math or complex code. A fixed patching scheme gives both regions the same treatment. That means it may waste a transformer step where the prediction is easy, or fail to spend enough compute where the next-byte uncertainty is high. It also produces inconsistent segmentation, since the same word can be split differently depending on where it appears.

This is why the paper treats static patching as a baseline rather than a final solution. It gives a clean control mechanism for flop budgeting, but it does not solve the fundamental problem of deciding where computation is actually needed.

# What Space Patching Improves, and Where It Still Underperforms

Space patching is a simple improvement over fixed-stride patching. Instead of splitting bytes at a fixed interval, it starts new patches after space-like bytes, so that words are handled more consistently and a latent transformer step is naturally allocated to each word. The intuition is that the first bytes of a word are often harder to predict than the rest, so it makes sense to place more compute at those boundaries.

The paper gives a concrete example: predicting the first byte of the answer to “Who composed the Magic Flute?” is much harder than predicting the later bytes once the answer has already begun. Space patching can give that hard first decision its own patch boundary, which is better aligned with prediction difficulty than fixed-stride patching. In the paper’s ablations, space patching improves over static patching and is a close competitor to entropy-based patching in scaling curves.

But space patching still falls short of the strongest token baselines. It cannot gracefully handle all languages and domains, and it cannot vary patch size. In the BLT-1T downstream evaluation, BLT-Space underperforms the Llama 3 tokenizer on all but one task, even though it achieves a meaningful reduction in inference flops through its larger average patch size of $6$ bytes. The paper also notes that, with the same training budget, the larger-patch space model covers $30\%$ more data than the other two models, which may move it further away from the compute-optimal point. So space patching helps, but it does not fully close the gap.

# How Entropy Patching Closes the Remaining Gap

Entropy patching is the paper’s data-driven answer to the limits of space-based rules. Instead of using whitespace as a proxy for difficulty, BLT trains a small byte-level language model and measures next-byte entropy. High entropy means the next byte is uncertain; low entropy means it is predictable. The patching rule then places boundaries where uncertainty is high, either by comparing entropy to a global threshold or by looking for increases relative to the previous entropy.

This matters because entropy gives the model a way to group bytes by information density rather than by a hand-designed linguistic heuristic. The resulting patches are contextualized: they tend to be more uniform in how much uncertainty they contain, which is exactly the kind of structure you want if the goal is to allocate compute dynamically. In the large-scale BLT-Entropy run, the authors also reset the entropy context with new lines and use an approximate monotonicity constraint to reduce entropy drift.

The paper’s results show that entropy patching is the strategy that most cleanly closes the remaining gap to tokenizer-based baselines. All of the non-static patching schemes outperform static patching, and entropy patching preserves those gains even on downstream benchmarks. On the BLT-1T evaluation suite, BLT-Entropy outperforms the Llama 3 model on $4$ out of $7$ tasks while being trained on the same number of bytes. The paper attributes this to two factors working together: better use of training compute through dynamic patching, and direct modeling of byte-level information.

# The Effect of Stronger Tokenizers Such as Llama 3’s $128\text{K}$ Vocabulary

The paper is careful not to compare BLT only against weak tokenizer baselines. It uses the Llama 3 tokenizer with a $128\text{K}$ vocabulary because that tokenizer gives a stronger baseline than Llama 2’s tokenizer in their experiments. In fact, the paper observes that token-based models trained with the Llama 3 tokenizer outperform those trained with the Llama 2 tokenizer on the same data.

This matters because it raises the bar. A stronger tokenizer generally produces larger average tokens and fewer sequence steps, but it does so with a tradeoff: the embedding table becomes larger, and the model still inherits a fixed-vocabulary view of the input. The paper notes that Llama 3 increases average token size from $3.7$ to $4.4$ bytes, but at the cost of a $4\times$ larger embedding table compared to Llama 2. So the tokenizer baseline is stronger, but it is also more expensive and still constrained by the static vocabulary.

BLT’s point is that it can compete with that stronger baseline without relying on a fixed vocabulary at all. Instead of picking a tokenizer size and accepting its tradeoffs, BLT chooses patch sizes dynamically and lets the compute adapt to the input. That is part of why the scaling result is so notable.

# Patch Sizes Larger Than Token Sizes and Their Impact on Scaling

One of the paper’s most interesting findings is that BLT can use patches that are larger than the average tokens produced by BPE tokenizers. That sounds counterintuitive at first, because larger units might seem like they should make the model less precise. But in BLT, larger patches are not simply a crude compression trick. They are a way to reduce the number of expensive latent-transformer steps while preserving the information in the raw bytes through the local encoder and decoder.

The paper’s scaling experiments show that average patch sizes of $6$ and $8$ bytes can still produce strong results. Since the global transformer is the most expensive component, larger patches save compute where it matters most. The saved compute can then be reallocated to model capacity elsewhere, rather than spent on repeatedly processing easy byte regions. This is why the paper says that patches scale better than tokens: the patch size can be increased independently of a fixed vocabulary, and that gives the architecture another axis along which to trade compute for performance.

The paper also emphasizes that these larger patch sizes become more feasible as the model gets bigger. The byte-level encoder and decoder grow much more slowly than the latent transformer, so their share of the total FLOPs shrinks at larger scales. The authors note that when total parameters grow $20\times$ from $400\text{M}$ to $8\text{B}$, the local model parameters only roughly double. That makes larger patches more attractive, because the cost they change is increasingly dominated by the global model.

# Why Larger Patch Sizes Become More Competitive at Larger Model Scales

The fixed-inference scaling experiments make this point especially clearly. Starting from $400\text{M}$ and $3.6\text{B}$ parameter models, the authors compare token-based models and BLT-Entropy models with average patch sizes of $6$ and $8$ bytes, all under matched inference-flop classes. The result is that BLT has better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures in both cases. Token-based models are better at very small training budgets, but BLT overtakes them not far beyond the compute-optimal regime.

The crossover point itself moves in BLT’s favor as the inference-flop class gets larger. The paper reports that this crossover shifts from about $3\times$ to about $2.5\times$ the compute-optimal budget when moving to the larger flop-class models. The larger-patch $8$-byte model also shows a steeper scaling trend in the larger flop class and overtakes sooner. This is the strongest evidence that larger patches are not just an efficiency hack; they become more competitive as the model and data scale up.

The broader lesson is that BLT opens a new scaling dimension that tokenizer-based models do not have. A token-based model can change its vocabulary, but that is a fixed design choice made before training. BLT can instead choose patch sizes dynamically and use the saved compute to grow the latent transformer. That is why the paper’s central claim is not merely that byte-level modeling works, but that patches scale better than tokens when compute is the thing being held fixed.

Patches Scale Better Than Tokens

# Why fixed-vocabulary token models cannot freely trade sequence length for model size

The paper’s central claim begins with a limitation of tokenization-based language models: they process a fixed vocabulary of tokens, and that vocabulary is chosen before training. This matters because tokenization determines how raw bytes are grouped into units, but it is only a heuristic compression scheme. It can shorten sequences, yet it does so in a way that is not guaranteed to align with the actual difficulty of the prediction problem. In the paper’s framing, token-based models allocate the same amount of compute to every token, even though some predictions are easy and others are hard.

That rigidity creates a narrow tradeoff. If the tokenizer uses larger average tokens, the model takes fewer steps through the sequence, but the price is a larger output projection and embedding table. The paper highlights this with Llama 3, which increases average token size from $3.7$ to $4.4$ bytes compared with Llama 2, but at the cost of making the embedding table $4\times$ larger. So token-based models do not get a free reduction in sequence length; they pay for it elsewhere in the architecture. This is why the paper argues that fixed-vocabulary tokenizers leave little room for significant variation in token size and inference cost.

The deeper problem is that token boundaries are not necessarily aligned with where the model needs more or less reasoning effort. The paper gives a simple intuition: finishing the end of a familiar word is usually much easier than choosing the first word of a new sentence. Yet a token-based model still spends the same kind of step on both situations. The result is a mismatch between the model’s compute schedule and the structure of the data.

# How BLT reallocates saved compute from longer patches into a larger latent Transformer

BLT replaces fixed tokens with dynamically sized patches, which are groups of bytes formed on the fly. Because patches have no fixed vocabulary, BLT is not forced to commit to a single granularity of text representation in advance. Instead, it can group bytes into long patches when the next-byte prediction is easy and use shorter patches when the content is more uncertain. The paper’s key idea is that compute should be allocated where the data are complex, rather than uniformly across all positions.

This dynamic design changes the scaling picture. The paper emphasizes that the global latent Transformer consumes the bulk of the FLOPs during both pre-training and inference. If the model can represent predictable regions with longer patches, then it invokes this expensive global model fewer times. Those saved FLOPs are not just lost; they can be reallocated to increase the size of the latent Transformer itself. In other words, longer patches do not merely make the model cheaper. They create budget that can be spent on a larger, more capable global model.

This is why the paper treats patch size as a genuine scaling axis. Token-based models mostly scale by making the model bigger while keeping the tokenization fixed. BLT, by contrast, can scale both the patch size and the model size at the same time while keeping the inference budget fixed. That possibility is the core reason the authors say patches scale better than tokens.

# Beyond-compute-optimal training on the BLT-1T dataset

To test BLT at scale, the paper trains an $8\mathrm{B}$-parameter model beyond the compute-optimal ratio on the BLT-$1\mathrm{T}$ dataset. BLT-$1\mathrm{T}$ is a larger, higher-quality dataset built from public sources and a subset of the data released by Datacomp-LM. The paper uses this run to examine whether BLT can remain competitive when it is not limited to the exact compute-optimal setting used for architectural comparison.

The broader experimental design matters here. The authors do not only ask whether BLT can match a token-based model at one carefully tuned operating point. They also ask whether it can still behave well when trained beyond that point, which is important because practical large-scale models are often trained with more data than the strict compute-optimal recipe would suggest. The paper’s claim is that this larger run lets them compare BLT and token-based models on downstream tasks under realistic large-model conditions.

A key result from this stage is that BLT-Entropy can outperform the Llama 3 tokenizer-based model on several downstream tasks while using the same number of training bytes. That is important because it suggests BLT’s gains are not just an artifact of a smaller or easier training regime. Instead, the architecture remains competitive when scaled to a serious pre-training run.

# The downstream benchmark suite

To measure what the model has actually learned, the paper evaluates BLT on a standard mix of classification and generation tasks. The classification benchmarks are ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, PIQA, and MMLU. The generation benchmarks are MBPP and HumanEval. Together, these tasks probe common-sense reasoning, world knowledge, and code generation.

The paper uses slightly different evaluation protocols depending on the task type. For the classification benchmarks, it uses prompt scoring: the model assigns likelihoods to the choice characters, and the final score is average accuracy. ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge are evaluated in $0$-shot settings, HellaSwag and PIQA are also evaluated $0$-shot, and MMLU is evaluated $5$-shot. For the code tasks, the paper reports pass@$1$, with MBPP in a $3$-shot setting and HumanEval in a $0$-shot setting. This mix matters because it shows the model is being tested both for general language understanding and for more structured generation.

The reason these tasks are useful for the paper’s argument is that they do not just measure next-byte prediction in the abstract. They test whether the token-free representation strategy carries over into meaningful downstream behavior. If patches really do scale better than tokens, that advantage should appear not only in language-modeling metrics but also in these more application-facing benchmarks.

# Comparing BLT-Entropy, BLT-Space, and BPE Llama 3 under matched training FLOPs

One of the most important controlled comparisons in the paper involves three models trained on BLT-$1\mathrm{T}$ under equivalent FLOP budgets: a BPE Llama 3 baseline, BLT-Space, and BLT-Entropy. The Llama 3 tokenizer is used as the token-based baseline because it performs better than the Llama 2 tokenizer in the authors’ experiments. BLT-Space uses space-like bytes as patch boundaries, while BLT-Entropy uses entropy-based patching with an approximately monotonicity constraint and a reset of the entropy context using new lines.

This comparison is informative because it separates architectural gains from simply spending more compute. All three models are trained with the same budget, so any difference comes from how they organize computation, not from how much computation they receive. Under this setup, BLT-Entropy outperforms the Llama 3 model on $4$ out of $7$ tasks. That is the strongest downstream evidence in the paper that dynamic byte patching can match or exceed a modern token-based system when both are trained fairly.

BLT-Space tells a slightly different story. It underperforms the Llama 3 tokenizer on all but one task, but it achieves a significant reduction in inference FLOPs because it uses larger patches, with an average patch size of about $6$ bytes. By comparison, the BPE and entropy-patching models have average patch sizes of about $4.5$ bytes on the training data mixture. The tradeoff is subtle: the larger patch size lets BLT-Space cover $30\%$ more data with the same training budget, but that may push it farther away from the compute-optimal point. So BLT-Space shows the efficiency benefit of larger patches, even when the quality tradeoff is not yet favorable.

# The inference-time entropy threshold tradeoff

The paper also shows that BLT’s patching policy can be adjusted at inference time. For BLT-Entropy, the authors change the entropy threshold from $0.6$ to $0.1$ during inference, and they report that this improves task performance at the cost of more inference steps. This is a direct example of the paper’s broader thesis: patching is not just a preprocessing detail, but a knob that controls how much computation the model spends.

The value of this adjustment is that it makes the efficiency-performance tradeoff explicit. A lower threshold leads to more patch boundaries, which means more opportunities for the latent Transformer to operate. That can improve accuracy, but it also raises inference cost. The paper uses this knob to show that BLT can be tuned according to deployment needs, rather than being locked into a single compute profile.

This matters because it illustrates a kind of flexibility that token-based models do not have in the same way. With tokens, the vocabulary is fixed and the granularity is largely set before training. With BLT, the entropy threshold can be adjusted to move the model along a continuum between faster and more detailed computation.

# Fixed-inference-FLOP model classes and equivalent comparisons

The paper’s second major scaling experiment asks a different question: if inference cost must stay fixed, can BLT still improve as training compute grows? To answer this, the authors create fixed-inference-FLOP model classes. They start from models of $400\mathrm{M}$ and $3.6\mathrm{B}$ parameters using the Llama 2 tokenizer, then find FLOP-equivalent models with the Llama 3 tokenizer and BLT-Entropy models whose average patch sizes are $6$ and $8$ bytes on the training data mixture.

These are carefully matched comparisons. The paper makes sure BLT is not helped by silently seeing longer contexts or more bytes than the token-based systems. Instead, the number of bytes processed is kept constant in expectation, and sequence lengths are adjusted accordingly. In the authors’ training setup, dynamic patching also changes the ratio of bytes to patches, so they pack batches of patches to avoid padding the more expensive latent Transformer. This makes the comparison about architectural scaling, not implementation artifacts.

The point of this setup is to see whether a larger model that takes fewer, more meaningful steps can outperform a smaller model that takes more steps. The answer is yes: BLT shows better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures in both inference FLOP classes. Token-based models tend to be stronger at small training budgets, but BLT overtakes them not far beyond the compute-optimal regime.

# Patch sizes of $6$ and $8$ under constant inference budgets

The paper’s fixed-inference study makes BLT’s scaling advantage especially concrete. In these experiments, BLT can grow both model size and patch size while keeping the inference FLOP budget fixed. This is possible because longer patches reduce how often the latent Transformer is invoked, and those saved FLOPs can be spent on enlarging the latent model instead of changing the overall budget.

The authors examine BLT-Entropy models with average patch sizes of $6$ and $8$ bytes. For the patch-size-$8$ models, they use $3$ encoder layers instead of $1$. Across the training FLOP budgets they test, the BLT models show better scaling trends than the tokenization-based alternatives. The larger patch-size-$8$ model has the steeper scaling trend in the larger FLOP class and overtakes the others sooner.

This is the sense in which patches scale better than tokens. A token-based model mostly has one way to improve under a fixed inference budget: make the tokenization and model tradeoff work a little better within the same basic structure. BLT has a second axis of growth. It can increase patch size and model size together, so the total model family can move into a region of better performance without violating the inference constraint.

# Crossover behavior as training compute increases

The scaling plots in the paper show a crossover behavior that is important for interpreting the results. At small training budgets, BPE models often perform better. This is not surprising, because tokenization-based systems benefit from a familiar and efficient training regime early on. But as training compute increases, BLT catches up and then surpasses the token-based models.

The crossover point moves closer to the compute-optimal regime when the inference FLOP class is larger. In the paper, it shifts from about $3\times$ to about $2.5\times$ the compute-optimal training budget. That means the point at which BLT starts to dominate arrives earlier, in compute terms, when the system is designed for a larger inference budget. The paper reads this as evidence that investing more in pre-training can be worthwhile if the model will later be deployed under a fixed inference budget.

This also helps explain why the authors view BLT not as a one-off architectural trick, but as a new scaling direction. The model does not merely win at one size. It changes where the performance crossover happens, and it does so in a way that improves as the deployment budget becomes more substantial.

# Why local-model cost shrinks relative to global-model cost at larger scales

A subtle but very important observation in the paper is that the byte-level local models do not grow nearly as fast as the global latent Transformer. Over a $20\times$ increase in total parameters, from $400\mathrm{M}$ to $8\mathrm{B}$, the local model parameters only roughly double. That means the local encoder and decoder become a smaller fraction of the total cost as the model grows.

This matters because the main compute savings from larger patches come from the latent Transformer. The local byte-level modules still need to exist, but they are comparatively light. As the model scales, the cost structure becomes more favorable: the part of the model that is invoked less often dominates more of the total budget, while the byte-level components remain relatively small. This helps explain why larger patch sizes look increasingly attractive at larger scales.

The paper gives one concrete example of this effect. BLT-Entropy with patch size $8$ increases from $1.6\times$ to $1.7\times$ the size of the Llama 2 model when moving to the larger model scale. The important point is not the exact ratio itself, but the trend behind it: the local model overhead does not explode, so scaling up the global model is still compatible with a large patch size. In that sense, the byte-level machinery becomes a fixed support system for a growing latent Transformer rather than a bottleneck.

# The headline efficiency result

The paper’s main efficiency claim is that BLT can match the training FLOP-controlled performance of Llama 3 while using up to about $50\%$ fewer FLOPs at inference. That is the practical payoff of the whole architecture. The model is not only competitive in quality; it can also reduce the cost of running the model after training.

Seen together, the experiments explain why. Token-based systems pay a fixed compute price per token, even when the next-byte prediction is easy. BLT instead uses patches to allocate more compute where the data are uncertain and less where they are predictable. Because the global latent Transformer is the expensive component, reducing how often it runs creates room to grow the model itself. That is why the paper can simultaneously increase patch size and model size while keeping the same inference budget.

The broader lesson is that patches introduce a scaling dimension that tokens do not have. Tokenization compresses input before the model sees it, but BLT lets the model decide how much compression is appropriate from context to context. At scale, that flexibility is what allows BLT to achieve better scaling trends, better inference efficiency, and competitive downstream performance under matched compute.

Robustness, Character Knowledge, and the Long Tail of Language

# Why byte-level access should improve robustness to surface-form perturbations

The central robustness claim in the paper is simple: if a model sees raw bytes directly, it preserves the exact form of the text instead of first compressing it through a fixed vocabulary. That matters because tokenization is a heuristic preprocessing step. It groups bytes into tokens according to rules chosen before training, and those rules can hide or distort details such as spelling, casing, punctuation, and other character-level cues. The paper argues that this can make token-based language models sensitive to input noise and weak on orthographic knowledge.

BLT is designed to avoid that loss of information. Because it operates on bytes, it keeps direct access to the fine-grained structure of the input. This gives the model a chance to recognize that a perturbed string is still the same underlying text, even when the surface form has changed. The paper frames this as one of the main advantages of byte modeling: robustness is not only about handling corrupted inputs, but also about preserving the information needed to interpret them correctly in the first place.

# Noisy-input evaluation and the design of character-level corruption tests

To test robustness, the paper creates noised versions of benchmark classification tasks and compares tokenizer-based models with BLT. The design is meant to isolate the effect of input corruption. Each corruption scheme is applied to the prompt, the completion, or both, treated as separate evaluation conditions, and the results are averaged. This setup is important because it checks whether a model can still solve the task when the exact character sequence has been altered.

The evaluation is especially revealing for byte models because the perturbations do not change the higher-level task, but they do change the surface form in ways that can disrupt tokenization. A model that reasons over bytes should be better equipped to ignore or adapt to those changes. In contrast, a tokenizer-based model may see a perturbed string as a different sequence of tokens, which can interfere with the behavior learned during training.

# AntSpeak, Drop, RandomCase, Repeat, and UpperCase noising schemes

The paper uses five character-level noising schemes. AntSpeak converts the entire text into uppercase, space-separated characters. Drop randomly removes $10\%$ of the characters. RandomCase randomly converts $50\%$ of the characters to uppercase and $50\%$ to lowercase. Repeat repeats $20\%$ of the characters, up to four times. UpperCase transforms all characters in the text to uppercase.

These perturbations target different kinds of surface variation. Some change casing, some change length, and some disrupt the usual spacing or repetition pattern of the text. What they have in common is that they make the input noisier while keeping it human-recognizable. That makes them a good test of whether a model has learned the underlying content rather than memorizing a fragile tokenization pattern.

# Robustness results on noisy HellaSwag

On noised HellaSwag, the paper reports that BLT outperforms tokenizer-based models across the board. The average advantage is about 8 points over the model trained on the same data, and BLT even improves over the Llama 3.1 model trained on a much larger dataset. The paper treats this as strong evidence that byte-level access helps models remain reliable when the input has been perturbed.

The result is notable because HellaSwag is not a character task in the narrow sense. It is a commonsense completion benchmark, so the model still needs to understand context and choose a plausible continuation. BLT’s advantage suggests that robustness to noise is not separate from language understanding; rather, preserving byte-level detail can make the core prediction problem easier to solve when the text has been corrupted.

# Grapheme-to-phoneme evaluation and phonological knowledge

The paper also tests whether BLT can use character-level information for pronunciation-related reasoning. It does this through grapheme-to-phoneme, or G2P, evaluation on Phonology Bench in a 5-shot setting. Here the model must map graphemes, meaning the written characters of a word, into phonemes, meaning its pronunciation. BLT outperforms the baseline Llama 3 1T tokenizer-based model on this task.

This is an important kind of character knowledge because it goes beyond simply recognizing that characters are present. The model has to connect spelling with sound. The paper’s result shows that direct byte access can support this kind of structured mapping, which token-based models may handle less naturally because they work through a compressed token vocabulary rather than the raw character sequence itself.

# The CUTE benchmark for orthographic reasoning and character manipulation

CUTE is one of the paper’s strongest demonstrations of character-level understanding. The benchmark includes tasks grouped into three broad categories: understanding composition, understanding orthographic similarity, and manipulating sequences. The paper emphasizes that this is a difficult setting for tokenizer-based models because they may know the spellings of their tokens, but still struggle to use that information to manipulate text.

BLT-Entropy performs especially well here. It outperforms both BPE Llama 3 models by more than 25 points and reaches 99.9\% on both spelling tasks. The paper highlights that these gains are large even though BLT was trained on 16 times less data than Llama 3.1. That makes the result especially compelling: character-level access appears to give BLT a capability that is hard for token-based models to recover just from scale.

# Why BLT excels on spelling and sequence-manipulation tasks

The reason BLT does so well on spelling tasks follows directly from its input representation. Because the model sees bytes, spelling is not hidden inside a token ID. The sequence of characters remains available to the model, so it can learn to inspect, compose, and transform text at a very fine level of detail. That is exactly the kind of structure needed for spelling-oriented tasks on CUTE.

The paper’s examples show that BLT can manipulate character sequences in ways that tokenizer-based models struggle with. This is consistent with the idea that byte-level modeling gives the network direct access to subword structure. Instead of depending on a vocabulary that may merge or fragment words in idiosyncratic ways, the model reasons from the underlying bytes upward. In the paper’s experiments, that seems to be a major advantage for tasks where the precise arrangement of characters matters.

# Cases where token-based models still perform better, such as some word-level manipulations

The paper is careful not to claim that BLT wins on every manipulation task. On CUTE, word deletion and word insertion are the two tasks where BPE performs better. This is a useful limitation to keep in mind, because it shows that character-level access is not a universal shortcut to all forms of language manipulation.

The paper suggests that the gap is not very wide and that building from characters to words may be easier than building the reverse direction. It also notes that BPE models might benefit from additional prompt engineering. So while BLT has a clear advantage on spelling and many forms of character manipulation, some tasks that are more naturally word-centric still remain challenging.

# Low-resource machine translation on FLORES-101

The paper next looks at machine translation on FLORES-101, evaluating translation into and out of six popular language families and 21 lower-resource languages with various scripts. Performance is measured using SentencePiece BLEU. In this setting, BLT outperforms the Llama 3 tokenizer-based model by 2 points on translation into English and by 0.5 points on translation from English.

The pattern is especially interesting because BLT is not merely matching the tokenizer-based model on high-resource language pairs. It is also doing better in many lower-resource settings, while performing comparably or slightly better on popular language pairs. The paper uses this to argue that byte-level modeling is particularly helpful when language data are scarce, diverse in script, or otherwise outside the main concentration of the training distribution.

# Long-tail generalization across scripts, rare forms, and multilingual settings

A recurring theme in the paper is the “long tail” of language: uncommon spellings, noisy text, rare character sequences, and languages or scripts that may not be well served by a fixed vocabulary. The character-level and translation results are all presented as evidence that BLT handles these difficult cases better than token-based models. Because bytes can represent any text directly, BLT does not depend on whether a tokenizer has already decided that a particular form is common enough to deserve its own token.

This matters for multilingual generalization as well. The translation results suggest that direct byte modeling helps when the model must cope with diverse scripts and lower-resource language families. Instead of relying on a token inventory that may be uneven across languages, BLT builds its representations from the raw byte stream. The paper’s claim is that this makes the model more adaptable to long-tail inputs, which are exactly the kinds of cases where tokenization can become brittle.

# How these findings support the claim that byte modeling improves robustness and subword understanding

Taken together, the paper’s robustness and character-level evaluations support a coherent conclusion. BLT is better than tokenizer-based models at handling noisy text, spelling-related reasoning, pronunciation mapping, and low-resource multilingual translation. These are all settings where the exact character sequence matters. The common thread is that byte-level access preserves information that tokenization may obscure, and BLT is able to turn that extra information into measurable gains.

At the same time, the paper presents a balanced picture. The results do not say that bytes solve every problem better than tokens. Some word-level manipulation tasks still favor BPE. But the broader pattern is clear: direct byte modeling improves robustness to input corruption and gives the model a stronger handle on subword structure and the long tail of language. That is the main lesson of this chapter, and it is one of the paper’s most important empirical contributions.

Byte-Fying Pretrained Token Models

# Motivation for Reusing Existing Pretrained Token-Based LLMs

The paper presents byte-ifying as a practical transition path rather than a wholesale rejection of existing tokenizer-based large language models. The basic idea is straightforward: if a tokenizer-based model has already learned a great deal about language, then it may be worthwhile to reuse that knowledge when building a tokenizer-free model. Instead of starting from random weights and asking a byte-level model to learn everything from scratch, BLT can begin from a pretrained token model and adapt that model into a byte-based system.

This motivation is important because BLT is not just a different input representation; it is a different way of allocating computation. The paper argues that fixed-vocabulary tokenization can be limiting, but it also recognizes that pretrained token models already contain useful learned structure. The byte-ifying experiment is therefore a bridge between two worlds: it tests whether the core sequence-modeling ability learned under tokenization can survive the move to raw bytes and dynamic patches, while also taking advantage of BLT’s efficiency advantages.

# Initializing BLT's Global Transformer from Llama 3.1 Weights

BLT is built around a large global autoregressive Transformer that operates on patch representations. In the byte-ifying setup, the paper initializes this global Transformer with the weights of a pretrained Llama 3.1 model. This means that the central high-capacity component of BLT does not begin as a blank slate. Instead, it starts from a model that has already been trained at scale in a token-based regime.

This choice reflects the architectural role of the global Transformer in BLT. The global model consumes the bulk of the flops and is the part that carries the main sequence-level modeling burden. By transferring weights into this component, the paper is effectively reusing the strongest part of the pretrained token model inside the BLT framework. The byte-specific pieces then learn how to present byte data to that core and how to recover bytes from its outputs.

# Training Local Encoder and Decoder Modules Around a Pretrained Latent Core

Once the global Transformer has been initialized from Llama 3.1, the surrounding BLT modules must learn to work with it. The local encoder maps raw bytes into patch representations, and the local decoder maps patch representations back into bytes. In the architecture described earlier in the paper, these local modules are lightweight compared with the global Transformer, and they are the pieces that make BLT tokenizer-free.

In the byte-ifying workflow, these local modules become especially important because they create the interface between raw byte sequences and the pretrained latent core. The paper’s architecture already includes mechanisms for encoding bytes into patches and decoding patches back into bytes, so the transfer experiment does not need to change BLT’s basic design. Instead, it asks whether the local encoder and decoder can be trained around an already knowledgeable global model. The result is a model that combines a pretrained sequence core with byte-level input and output handling.

# Using a Smaller Learning Rate for the Transferred Global Model

A key detail of the byte-ifying procedure is that the transferred global Transformer is updated with a learning rate that is one-tenth of the learning rate used for the local encoder and local decoder. The paper presents this as part of the training setup for the BLT model initialized from Llama 3.1. The global model is still trained, but it is adapted more cautiously than the newly introduced local byte-processing components.

This makes intuitive sense in the context of transfer. The global Transformer has already learned a great deal in the token-based setting, so the goal is not to overwrite that knowledge too aggressively. The smaller learning rate gives the pretrained backbone a more conservative update path, while the local encoder and decoder can move more quickly to learn the byte-patch interface. In other words, the optimization strategy reflects the division of labor inside BLT: the global core supplies prior knowledge, and the local modules learn the mechanics of byte-level communication.

# Comparison with BLT Trained from Scratch Under Matched Compute

The paper compares the byte-ified model against BLT and Llama 3 baselines trained with the same flop budget. In that matched-compute setting, BLT initialized from Llama 3.1 significantly outperforms both the standard Llama 3 baseline and the BLT baseline trained under the same computational budget. This is one of the clearest signs that transferring a pretrained token model into BLT is worthwhile.

The comparison matters because it shows that the gains are not simply due to spending more compute. The model is still operating under a controlled budget, yet the pretrained initialization gives it a stronger starting point than training BLT from scratch. The paper also notes that this setup can substantially reduce training flops, making it an attractive route when the goal is to obtain a competitive tokenizer-free model without paying the full cost of end-to-end pretraining from raw bytes.

# Performance Gains and Remaining Gaps Across Benchmark Tasks

The paper reports that the BLT model initialized from Llama 3.1 still achieves superior performance on MMLU compared with a BLT-Entropy model trained on a significantly larger dataset. That is an important result because it suggests that transferring pretrained token-based weights can improve data efficiency: the model can do more with less training data, at least on some benchmarks. The paper also frames this as evidence that the method can be effective for reducing training flops.

At the same time, the comparison with the original LLaMA 3.1 model trained on 15T tokens shows that the conversion is not perfect. The byte-ified model experiences a slight performance decline on MMLU and HumanEval, and a more substantial drop on other tasks. The paper uses this gap to argue that more work is needed to fully exploit the pretrained model after conversion. In particular, it points to optimizing data mixtures and other hyperparameters as an open direction for improving the transfer outcome.

# Interpreting the Method as Converting a Token Model into a Tokenizer-Free Model

One of the paper’s most direct claims is that this setup can be viewed as transforming a tokenizer-based model into a tokenizer-free one. In that interpretation, the pretrained LLaMA 3.1 model is not merely used as a warm start; it is effectively being converted into a BLT model. The token-based backbone becomes the latent core of a byte-level system, while the byte-side encoder and decoder replace the tokenizer-dependent front end.

This perspective is useful because it clarifies what has changed and what has been preserved. What changes is the way the model receives and emits data: BLT operates on raw bytes and patches rather than a fixed token vocabulary. What is preserved is the central Transformer knowledge learned during large-scale token-based pretraining. The paper’s results suggest that some of the linguistic and benchmark competence learned under tokenization transfers into the new byte-based setting, even though the representation and computation scheme are different.

# What the Results Suggest About Sample Efficiency and Future Hybrid Training Strategies

The transfer experiment suggests that byte-level training does not have to be a cold start. Because the pretrained global Transformer gives BLT a strong initialization, the model can reach better performance than scratch-trained baselines under the same compute budget. The comparison against a BLT model trained on more data further suggests that the byte-ified approach can be more sample efficient in some settings, since it leverages prior work done during token-based pretraining rather than relearning everything from the beginning.

The paper’s broader message is that this kind of hybrid strategy may be a promising future direction. The limitations section notes that the authors have already seen indications of success from initializing and freezing the global Transformer in early experiments, and they argue that future work could better preserve the benefits of byte-ifying while pushing performance beyond that of tokenizer-based models. Taken together, the results point toward a practical training strategy in which pretrained token models and byte-level architectures are not competitors, but components of a single transfer pipeline.

Ablations, Related Work, and Open Problems

# Entropy model size and context-length ablations

A central design choice in BLT is that patch boundaries are not fixed in advance. Instead, they are guided by a separate byte-level entropy model that estimates where the next-byte prediction is difficult. The ablation study asks a simple but important question: how strong does this entropy model need to be in order for dynamic patching to work well? The paper varies both the size of this model and its context window, training byte-level entropy transformers from $1$M to $100$M parameters and using context lengths from $64$ to $512$. The result is clear: better entropy models produce better scaling behavior, and longer contexts also help. At the same time, the gains do not continue forever. The paper reports diminishing returns once the entropy model grows beyond about $50$M parameters.

This matters because the entropy model is not the main language model; it is a lightweight component whose job is to identify patch boundaries well enough for the larger model to spend compute where it is most useful. The paper’s ablations show that this boundary-estimation problem is easier to solve with more capacity and more context, but only up to a point. Once the entropy model is good enough to distinguish low-entropy regions from high-entropy ones, additional size buys little. That is one of the recurring themes of the paper: several BLT components are useful, but many of them exhibit saturation, which suggests that the architecture is not held together by any single massive submodule.

The paper also notes a practical detail in the large-scale entropy-based run. For BLT-Entropy at patch size $4.5$, the entropy context is reset with new lines and the approximate monotonicity constraint is used instead of the global threshold, because it is less sensitive to entropy drift caused by changing context length. This is an example of a design decision that is specific to entropy patching rather than to BLT as a whole. It shows that the entropy model is not just a theoretical device; its behavior depends on how the surrounding text is presented, and the paper actively tunes this part of the system to make patching more stable.

# Comparing strided, space, BPE-inspired, and entropy-based patching

The paper compares four different ways of grouping bytes. Strided patching groups a fixed number of bytes together, space patching starts a new patch after space-like bytes, BPE-inspired patching uses the Llama 3 tokenizer as a patching baseline, and entropy-based patching uses the entropy model described above. These schemes are not just different implementation choices. They embody different beliefs about what a patch should represent and where compute should be spent.

Strided patching is the simplest. It is easy to control, because the patch size is fixed, so the number of Transformer steps can be adjusted mechanically. But the paper emphasizes two disadvantages. First, it does not allocate compute where the data is actually hard. A fixed patch size can waste a step on an easy region, such as predictable whitespace or repetitive code, while under-allocating compute in information-dense regions such as math. Second, it can segment similar byte sequences inconsistently, because the same word may be split differently depending only on where the stride happens to land.

Space patching is a more informed heuristic. By creating a new patch after space-like bytes, it aligns patches with word boundaries and ensures that the model spends a Transformer step on each word. The paper presents this as a meaningful improvement over pure striding, because the first byte after a space is often the hardest part of a word to predict. The example of completing “Mozart” after “M” makes the intuition concrete: once the first character is known, the rest of the word is much easier. Still, the paper argues that space patching is not enough on its own. It cannot vary patch size, and it cannot handle all languages and domains gracefully.

BPE-inspired patching is different in kind. The paper uses the Llama 3 tokenizer as a baseline, but it draws a sharp distinction between tokens and patches. Tokens come from a finite vocabulary fixed before training, whereas patches are dynamic byte groups with no fixed vocabulary. This difference is crucial because tokenization hides the underlying bytes from the model. BLT’s ablations show that tokenization-based patching remains competitive as a baseline, but it also reinforces the paper’s main claim: fixed-vocabulary tokenization constrains how compute can be allocated.

Entropy-based patching is the paper’s preferred dynamic approach. It uses the estimated uncertainty of the next byte to decide where a patch should start, either by applying a global entropy threshold or by looking for points where entropy rises relative to the previous byte. The paper’s scaling ablations show that dynamic patching outperforms static striding, and that space patching is a close competitor to entropy-based patching. On downstream benchmarks, the gains from entropy-based patching carry through even when compared against the simpler space heuristic. The lesson is not that the entropy model is magical; rather, it is a practical way to make patching contextual, so that compute follows data complexity instead of a rigid rule.

# Cross-attention ablations in the encoder and decoder

BLT uses cross-attention in both the local encoder and the local decoder to move between byte-level representations and patch-level representations. The ablation study asks where cross-attention matters most. The answer is asymmetric: decoder cross-attention is the most effective component, while encoder cross-attention gives only a modest benefit.

This asymmetry is informative because the two modules serve different purposes. The encoder must compress bytes into patches, while the decoder must reconstruct bytes from patch representations. The paper finds that adding cross-attention in the decoder helps more consistently than adding it in the encoder. In the encoder, cross-attention helps mainly when it is paired with a good way of initializing the query representation, and even then the improvement is slight. In the decoder, by contrast, cross-attention is broadly useful.

The effect is especially noticeable on Common-Crawl and for larger patch sizes. That is another clue about the role of cross-attention: it becomes more valuable when the model is dealing with larger or more heterogeneous byte groups, because the patch representation has to summarize more content before the global model sees it. In other words, cross-attention is most useful when the patch boundary hides more information that must be recovered or summarized carefully. The paper therefore treats cross-attention not as a decorative architectural choice, but as one of the mechanisms that makes patch-based scaling practical.

# Query initialization strategies for encoder cross-attention

The encoder cross-attention block needs an initial query representation for each patch. The paper tests three strategies. One is to use the same learned embedding for every global state. Another is to use a hash embedding of the bytes in the patch. The third is to initialize the query by pooling the encoder hidden representation of the bytes in that patch at the relevant layer. This is a useful ablation because it isolates a seemingly small choice that can nevertheless affect whether the encoder cross-attention block has a meaningful starting point.

The paper’s conclusion is that pooling is the best initialization strategy. Using a shared learned embedding is too generic, because it gives every patch the same starting point. Hash embeddings are more specific, because they encode information from the bytes themselves, but the paper finds that pooling hidden representations works better still. The reason, as supported by the results, is not that pooling is a universally superior method in the abstract, but that it gives the query access to the current encoder state of the patch, which is already contextualized by the preceding byte-level processing.

This result also helps explain why encoder cross-attention only produces a slight gain overall. If the query is initialized poorly, the patch summary may not improve much when it attends to its own bytes. With pooling, the encoder cross-attention block becomes a more coherent summarization step. Even so, the paper’s ablations suggest that encoder cross-attention is secondary compared to the decoder side, so the exact query initialization is important but not decisive on its own.

# n-gram hash embedding vocabulary size and diminishing returns

Another byte-level enhancement in BLT is the use of hash $n$-gram embeddings. For each byte position, the model forms byte $n$-grams for $n$ from $3$ to $8$, hashes them into a fixed embedding table, and adds the resulting representation to the byte embedding. This gives the model access not just to the individual byte, but to short local contexts around it. The paper uses this mechanism to inject local orthographic structure into the encoder.

The ablation study varies the hash-table size, testing configurations with $0$, $100$K, $200$K, and $400$K entries, and also reports the main model setting of $500$K hashes. The pattern is consistent across domains: hash embeddings help everywhere, and they are particularly helpful on Wikipedia and GitHub. The gains are larger in the lower-capacity settings and become smaller as the hash table grows. At $8$B scale, the difference between $500$K and $300$K hashes is only about $0.001$ BPB after $15$K steps, which is a strong sign of diminishing returns.

This is one of the clearest examples in the paper of a useful but not infinitely scalable design choice. The hash embeddings do matter. The paper explicitly says that they are vital to bringing BLT’s performance up to tokenization-based models. But they are also not the main source of BLT’s scaling advantage. Once the model has enough local character information to recognize short patterns, further enlarging the hash table produces only modest improvements. The paper also notes that the gains from hash embeddings and cross-attention are largely complementary, because they help on different datasets. That makes the embeddings a practical component of the system, but not the sole reason BLT works.

# Local encoder and decoder depth tradeoffs

The local encoder and decoder are intentionally lightweight compared to the global latent Transformer. The ablations over local depth test how much capacity is actually needed in these byte-level modules. The paper’s conclusion is that BLT can work well with a very small encoder, even just one layer, as long as it is paired with hash $n$-gram embeddings. At the same time, the decoder benefits from being heavier.

This pattern makes sense in the context of BLT’s division of labor. The local encoder’s job is to prepare patch representations efficiently. Once hash embeddings supply a strong local signal, the encoder does not need many layers to do its work. The decoder, however, has to turn patch-level outputs back into byte-level predictions, which is a more delicate reconstruction problem. The paper’s ablations therefore suggest that the representational burden can be shifted away from the encoder and toward the decoder and the global model.

The most important lesson is that local capacity is not where BLT’s scaling story lives. The architecture is built so that the expensive part is the global Transformer, while the byte-level modules remain small. That is why changes in local depth are helpful but do not dominate the compute picture. The design is consistent with the paper’s broader claim that BLT is about allocating compute where it is most valuable, rather than spending equal effort on every byte.

# Why different BLT components appear to scale at different rates

The paper’s scaling results show that not all BLT components scale in the same way. This is not surprising once the architecture is separated into local byte modules and the global latent Transformer. The global model consumes most of the flops, while the local encoder and decoder are relatively small. As model size increases, the share of total compute taken up by the byte-level modules grows more slowly than the global portion. The paper explicitly notes that when total parameters grow $20\times$ from $400$M to $8$B, the local parameters only roughly double.

This asymmetry helps explain why larger patch sizes become more attractive at larger scales. Patch size mainly affects how often the latent Transformer is invoked. If patches are longer, the global model is called less often, which saves compute. Those saved flops can then be reassigned to making the latent Transformer larger. Because the local modules do not scale as quickly, their relative cost becomes less important as the whole model grows. The paper argues that this is why larger patch sizes can start behind BPE-based models at small scale but eventually catch up and even outperform them at larger scale.

The scaling study also suggests a second lesson: BLT is not simply trying to mimic the behavior of tokenizers. It is creating a new tradeoff space. Tokenization-based models are constrained by a fixed vocabulary, so changes in average token length are tied to changes in the output layer and embedding table. BLT, by contrast, can increase patch size without a fixed vocabulary penalty. The result is a new scaling dimension in which patch size and model size can be increased together under a fixed inference budget. That is one of the paper’s central empirical findings, and it depends on the different scaling rates of the architecture’s parts.

# Historical background: character RNNs, character Transformers, ByT5, MambaByte, MegaByte, SpaceByte, and related patching methods

BLT sits inside a long line of character- and byte-level modeling work. The paper traces this history back to early character language models, especially character RNNs. Those early models were attractive because they could model out-of-vocabulary words organically instead of relying on back-off mechanisms. The paper cites work showing character-based CNN and LSTM systems that used input-side character processing to build stronger word representations, as well as byte-level LSTM systems that performed well on machine comprehension and on morphologically rich languages. This early literature established the basic intuition that smaller-than-word units can improve flexibility and multilingual coverage.

The transformer era changed the discussion but did not remove the difficulty. Character Transformers were developed to combine attention with subword or character inputs, but the paper notes that they still lagged behind word-level models for a long time. GPT-2 observed that byte-level language models were not competitive with word-level models on large-scale data. Later work showed that transformers could be made to work at the byte level, and some models did outperform earlier character systems, but often at the cost of much higher compute. The paper specifically discusses ByT5, which achieved robustness and competitiveness with token-based models on smaller data budgets, but required expensive attention over every byte. It also discusses CANINE, which used a deep character-level encoder and downsampling, and MambaByte, which leveraged the Mamba architecture and reported strong flop-controlled performance at the $350$M scale on bits-per-byte.

The most relevant predecessor family for BLT is patching-based byte modeling. The paper mentions hierarchical patching approaches, dynamic patching with learned boundary predictors, entropy-based patching methods, and compression-based schemes such as arithmetic coding with equal-info windows. Among these, MegaByte is the closest direct ancestor: it is a decoder-only model that uses fixed static patches and local decoding to move from bytes to patches and back. SpaceByte extends this idea by patching on spaces and adding a local encoder. The paper’s own ablations use these systems as baselines and show that static patching alone is not enough to close the gap with state-of-the-art tokenization-based models at scale. BLT’s contribution is to make patching dynamic and learnable enough to turn these earlier ideas into a scalable architecture.

# Implementation limits: theoretical FLOPs versus wall-clock efficiency

The paper is careful to distinguish theoretical flop counts from actual runtime. It defines and compares models in flop-controlled settings, but it also acknowledges that flop parity is not the same as wall-clock parity. To estimate compute, the paper follows the standard Transformer flop accounting used in Chinchilla-style analyses, treating the embedding lookup as a $0$-flop operation and counting the backward pass as roughly twice the forward pass. For BLT, it adds together the costs of the local encoder, the global latent Transformer, the local decoder, and both cross-attention blocks.

That accounting is useful for comparing architectures, but the paper also notes that existing transformer libraries are optimized for tokenization-based models. BLT’s dynamic patch-dependent masks are less conventional, so the implementation uses tools such as FlashAttention for fixed masks and FlexAttention for dynamic masks. Even with those optimizations, the authors caution that their models may not yet match tokenizer-based systems in wall-clock efficiency. This is an important limitation because the paper’s headline results are about flop efficiency, not a guarantee of speed in every practical codebase.

The implementation section also shows that some engineering choices are made specifically to keep the comparison fair and the training stable. For example, batches are packed to avoid padding in the latent Transformer, and sequences are padded or truncated to control memory spikes from unusually large patches. These details are not the conceptual heart of BLT, but they matter in practice. They make clear that the architecture’s benefits are measured under carefully controlled compute assumptions, and that further systems work may be needed before those benefits are fully reflected in runtime.

# Open questions about BLT-specific scaling laws

One of the paper’s explicit limitations is that it uses Llama 3’s scaling laws to choose the number of training steps for architectural comparisons. That is a reasonable starting point, but it may not be the optimal choice for BLT itself. The paper notes that these scaling laws were calculated for BPE-level Transformers, which means they might give suboptimal data-to-parameter ratios for a byte-latent model. This leaves an open question: what do the scaling laws look like when the model itself is patch-based?

This issue is not just a small tuning problem. BLT changes the effective unit of computation, so the relationship between data size, parameter count, patch size, and compute budget may differ from token-based models. The paper’s results suggest that larger patch sizes and larger model sizes can be jointly beneficial under a fixed inference budget, but it does not claim to have derived the optimal BLT-specific tradeoff. In that sense, the present work establishes feasibility and promise rather than a final theory of scaling.

The paper also acknowledges that many experiments were conducted at scales up to $1$B parameters when making architectural decisions, even though the final models go up to $8$B. That means some hyperparameter choices may shift at larger scales. The open problem is not just to fit more bytes or more parameters, but to understand how the optimal architecture itself changes as scale increases. The paper leaves open the possibility that even better scaling trends could emerge once BLT-specific scaling laws are derived directly.

# Future directions: end-to-end learned patching, better byte-fication, and larger-scale training

The paper closes with several future directions that follow directly from its limitations. The first is end-to-end learned patching. In BLT, the entropy model is trained separately and used to define patch boundaries as a preprocessing step. The authors point out that learning patching jointly with the rest of the model would be an interesting next step. Such a method could potentially adapt patch boundaries to the main objective instead of relying on a separately trained uncertainty model.

A second direction is better “byte-fication” of pretrained tokenizer-based models. The paper presents initial experiments in which BLT is initialized from a pretrained Llama 3.1 model by copying the global Transformer parameters and then updating the global model more slowly than the local encoder and decoder. This strategy improves convergence and can outperform both a from-scratch BLT baseline and a tokenizer-based baseline trained with the same compute. It suggests that pretrained token-based models may be useful starting points for byte-level systems, but the paper also finds that the transfer is not yet perfect. Some tasks drop more than others, and the authors note that better data mixtures and hyperparameter choices may be needed.

The third direction is simply more scale. The paper repeatedly hints that larger-scale training could change the picture again, both for optimal patch size and for the balance among BLT components. Since the current results already show that larger patches can improve with scale, the authors view more data and larger models as a plausible path toward even better performance. In short, the future work is not about replacing BLT’s core idea, but about making that idea more jointly trainable, more transferable, and more effective at still larger scales.

# Final synthesis: what BLT changes about language-model scaling and representation

BLT’s main conceptual change is that it makes patch size a first-class scaling dimension. Traditional tokenization-based models compress input using a fixed vocabulary and then spend essentially the same compute on every token. BLT replaces that rigid structure with dynamic patches whose sizes depend on local uncertainty. This means the model does not have to treat all parts of the input as equally expensive. Easy regions can be grouped into larger patches, while hard regions can receive more fine-grained computation.

The ablations show that this idea works because several smaller design choices line up with it. The entropy model must be good enough to identify useful boundaries, but it does not need to be enormous. Cross-attention is especially useful on the decoder side and only modestly helpful in the encoder. Hash $n$-gram embeddings improve local representation, but with diminishing returns. The local encoder can be extremely shallow if the rest of the architecture is strong. Together, these findings suggest that the essential part of BLT is not any single module, but the combination of dynamic patching with a two-stage byte-to-patch architecture.

The broader implication is that BLT changes how one should think about scaling language models. It shows that raw bytes are not necessarily a dead end at large scale, and that the inefficiency of byte sequences can be mitigated by learning where to spend compute. It also shows that byte-level modeling can improve robustness and long-tail behavior without giving up competitive scaling performance. The paper does not claim to have solved all engineering issues, but it does establish a new framework: instead of fixing the representation with a tokenizer and scaling the model around it, one can scale the representation itself by learning how to patch bytes into the right units of computation.
