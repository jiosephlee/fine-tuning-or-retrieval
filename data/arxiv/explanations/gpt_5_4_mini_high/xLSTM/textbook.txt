\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

From Classical LSTMs to xLSTM: Why Revisit Recurrence?

# A brief history of sequence models in deep learning

The paper places xLSTM in a long historical line of sequence models whose central challenge is the same: how can a model carry information forward through time without losing it? In the 1990s, the Long Short-Term Memory, or LSTM, was introduced with two core ideas, the constant error carousel and gating, to overcome the vanishing gradient problem in recurrent neural networks. That innovation made LSTMs much more effective at learning long-range dependencies than earlier recurrent models, and for a long time they were among the most successful sequence models in deep learning.

The paper emphasizes that this success was broad, not narrow. LSTMs were used for text generation, handwriting generation, sequence-to-sequence translation, evaluating computer programs, image captioning, source code generation, rainfall-runoff modeling, and hydrological forecasting. They were also central in reinforcement learning systems such as AlphaStar and OpenAI Five. In language modeling, the paper states that LSTMs were the first large language models. This is an important historical point: before Transformers became dominant, LSTMs were the main architecture that scaled sequence learning into the regime of large, useful models.

What makes this history relevant to xLSTM is that the LSTM was not replaced because it stopped working altogether. Rather, it was overtaken by a different architecture that scaled better. xLSTM is motivated by the idea that the recurrent family still has valuable properties, and that its weaknesses might be addressed directly instead of abandoning recurrence entirely.

# Why LSTMs were crucial for early large-scale language modeling

At the center of the original LSTM is a simple recurrence:

\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \quad h_t = o_t \psi(c_t).
\]

The paper uses this equation to explain the constant error carousel. The cell state $c_t$ is updated additively: part of the previous state is kept through the forget gate $f_t$, and new content is written through the input gate $i_t$ and cell input $z_t$. This additive structure is the key to why LSTMs could preserve information much more effectively than ordinary recurrent neural networks. The hidden state $h_t$ is then produced by applying an output gate $o_t$ to a normalized or squashed version of the cell state.

The intuition is that the memory cell acts as a protected storage unit. The input gate decides what enters memory, the forget gate decides what remains from the past, and the output gate decides what part of the memory becomes visible as the hidden state. The paper writes out the gate equations explicitly in terms of input weights, recurrent weights, and bias terms. All gates are sigmoid in the original LSTM, and the cell input and hidden activation functions are typically $\tanh$. In later LSTM formulations, multiple scalar memory cells were grouped into vectors, allowing recurrent weight matrices to mix the outputs of different cells.

This design mattered for language modeling because it gave the model a way to accumulate information over many tokens while still being trainable. The paper argues that LSTMs excel at learning abstractions: they can extract semantic information and store it in memory cells. Their usefulness was visible in practice through phenomena such as number neurons, syntax neurons, linguistic neurons, and sentiment neurons. In other words, LSTMs were not just memorizing tokens; they were learning compressed, useful sequence state.

# The rise of Transformers and parallel self-attention

The paper then describes the major shift in sequence modeling: the advent of Transformers in 2017. The key innovation was parallelizable self-attention. Compared with recurrent computation, self-attention made it much easier to scale models and training because the interactions across positions could be computed in parallel rather than step by step.

This shift mattered because, although LSTMs were powerful, they still had structural limitations. Their recurrent nature meant that information moved through time sequentially. As Transformers became larger and more effective, they outpaced LSTMs in language modeling and marked the start of a new era. The paper presents xLSTM as an attempt to revisit recurrence in light of this history: not to deny the success of Transformers, but to ask what happens if one keeps the recurrent core and modernizes it.

That question is especially meaningful because the paper does not treat recurrence as obsolete. Instead, it treats recurrence as a design space with untapped potential. The issue is not whether recurrence can model sequences at all, but whether it can be reworked to avoid the limitations that prevented LSTMs from scaling further.

# What LSTMs still do well: compression, state tracking, and recurrence

The paper is explicit that LSTMs still have strengths, even though they also have important limitations. One strength is compression. The classical LSTM stores information in a compact cell state, and this compressed memory can capture useful sequence information over time. The paper later points out that xLSTM inherits this compressive character and that such memory is well suited for industrial applications and edge implementations.

Another strength is state tracking. The paper argues that LSTMs are especially suited for problems where the model must keep track of evolving state across a sequence. This includes formal language tasks, code evaluation, and tracking entities in long narratives. These are settings where the model cannot simply react locally to the current token. It must maintain a persistent internal record of what has happened before.

A third strength is recurrence itself. Recurrence naturally makes the model a sequence processor rather than a bag-of-tokens processor. The LSTM does this while also learning abstractions. The paper’s point is not that classical LSTMs are weak in all respects, but that their recurrence is valuable enough to preserve if the known problems can be addressed. xLSTM is built around that idea.

# The paper’s motivating question about scaling LSTMs to billions of parameters

The central question of the paper is straightforward: how far can we get in language modeling if we scale LSTMs to billions of parameters, use the latest ideas from modern large language models, and at the same time fix the known limitations of LSTMs? The paper identifies three main limitations.

The first limitation is the inability to revise storage decisions. The paper illustrates this with nearest neighbor search. In that task, the model must scan a sequence and remember the most similar vector seen so far. A classical LSTM struggles when a better match appears later, because its gating mechanism is not well suited to revising what has already been stored. This is one of the main motivations for introducing exponential gating in xLSTM.

The second limitation is limited storage capacity. The original LSTM compresses information into scalar cell states, which limits how much structure can be stored. The paper demonstrates this with rare token prediction. On Wikitext-103, LSTM performs worse on rare tokens because rare events require richer memory than scalar storage can provide. This motivates the matrix memory in mLSTM.

The third limitation is lack of parallelizability due to memory mixing, meaning hidden-hidden connections between successive time steps. These recurrent interactions make computation sequential. This is where Transformers gained an advantage, since their core mechanism could be parallelized much more effectively. The paper’s goal is to keep recurrence while eliminating, or at least reducing, the bottlenecks that made the original LSTM hard to scale.

# The two core xLSTM ideas: exponential gating and new memory structures

xLSTM modifies the classical LSTM in two main ways. The first is exponential gating with normalization and stabilization. The second is a change in memory structure. Together, these changes produce two new variants: sLSTM and mLSTM.

The idea behind exponential gating is that the input and forget gates can use exponential activations rather than only sigmoid activations. This gives the model a more flexible way to control how strongly information is written and retained. But exponentials can also become numerically unstable, so the paper introduces stabilization. In the sLSTM, this is done using an additional state $m_t$ that tracks gate magnitudes and allows the gates to be rewritten in stabilized form without changing the output of the network or the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters.

The second idea is to change the memory itself. In sLSTM, the memory remains scalar, but the gating and memory mixing are redesigned. In mLSTM, the memory becomes a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, which greatly increases storage capacity. The paper treats these as two complementary directions: one improves the way scalar memory is controlled, and the other enlarges memory so that richer associations can be stored.

# Preview of sLSTM, mLSTM, xLSTM blocks, and experimental results

The sLSTM can be written as

\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \quad n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t, \quad h_t = o_t \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
\]

The additional state $n_t$ is a normalizer state. Intuitively, it keeps track of the total gate strength that has gone into the memory so that the hidden state can be normalized. The paper says this normalizer sums the product of the input gate and all future forget gates. Stabilization is handled by the state $m_t$, which allows the use of exponential gates without overflow. In the paper’s formulation, sLSTM can still use memory mixing through recurrent connections from the hidden state to the cell inputs and gates, and it can also be organized into multiple heads, with mixing within each head but not across heads.

The mLSTM expands memory from a scalar to a matrix and stores key-value pairs using a covariance-style update rule:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top.
\]

Here the forget gate acts like a decay rate and the input gate acts like a learning rate. The model also maintains a normalizer state and retrieves memory using a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$. The hidden state is computed through matrix retrieval rather than scalar recurrence. The paper emphasizes that this architecture is fully parallelizable because it does not use memory mixing. In that sense, mLSTM is closer to a matrix-based associative memory. The covariance update is motivated by classical work on associative memories and by the goal of maximizing separability of retrieved vectors.

xLSTM blocks then place these recurrent modules inside residual block architectures. The paper uses the idea that a model should summarize the past in a high-dimensional space so that different histories or contexts become more separable. This is related to Cover’s theorem, which the paper invokes to justify why higher-dimensional nonlinear embeddings can be easier to separate linearly. For sLSTM, the paper uses a post up-projection block, similar in spirit to Transformers. For mLSTM, it uses a pre up-projection block, similar in spirit to State Space Models. The full xLSTM architecture is formed by residually stacking these blocks with pre-LayerNorm backbones, as in modern large language models.

The paper also introduces a notation for mixed architectures: xLSTM[$a:b$] means a ratio of $a/b$ mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. For example, xLSTM[7:1] means seven mLSTM blocks for every one sLSTM block. In the experiments, this helps distinguish models that rely mostly on matrix memory from those that use more scalar-memory recurrence.

The experiments are then used to test whether these ideas actually matter. On synthetic tasks, xLSTM’s exponential gating and memory mixing help it solve state-tracking problems in formal languages, while its matrix memory improves performance on associative recall tasks. On the Long Range Arena, xLSTM shows consistently strong performance on long-context tasks. On 15B tokens from SlimPajama, xLSTM outperforms the compared methods in validation perplexity and shows favorable scaling behavior. On 300B tokens, xLSTM maintains low perplexity when extrapolating to longer contexts, achieves the best validation perplexities for the tested model sizes in the strongest configurations, and performs best on the vast majority of downstream tasks. On PALOMA, xLSTM also performs strongly across 571 text domains, and the paper reports that xLSTM can achieve substantially higher decoding throughput because its memory is constant with respect to sequence length.

Taken together, the paper’s preview is clear: xLSTM is not presented as a nostalgic return to old recurrent models, but as a modernized recurrent architecture that keeps the useful parts of LSTM while directly targeting the limitations that prevented it from competing with Transformers at scale.

LSTM Foundations: Constant Error Carousel and Gating

# Why vanilla recurrent neural networks suffer from vanishing gradients

A recurrent neural network processes a sequence one step at a time, carrying a hidden state forward from the past into the present. That design is natural for language and other sequences, because later elements often depend on earlier ones. The difficulty is that information has to survive many time steps before it can still matter. In practice, the paper frames this as the vanishing gradient problem: when training signals have to propagate through a long chain of recurrent steps, it becomes hard for the model to learn long-range dependencies reliably.

The original LSTM was introduced specifically to overcome this problem. Its central idea was not to make recurrence disappear, but to give recurrence a dedicated memory path that can preserve information more cleanly than a plain recurrent update. Instead of forcing everything to pass through the same transformation at every step, LSTM separates storage from exposure and lets gates decide what to keep, what to write, and what to reveal. That simple structural change is what made LSTMs a major step beyond vanilla recurrent networks.

# The constant error carousel as the core LSTM idea

The paper identifies two core LSTM ideas: the constant error carousel and gating. The constant error carousel is the additive update of the cell state. In the simplest form, the memory is updated as

\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t .
\]

This equation is the heart of the model. The previous memory $c_{t-1}$ is not discarded and replaced all at once. Instead, it is carried forward, scaled by the forget gate $f_t$, and combined with new information $i_t z_t$. That additive path is the reason the cell state serves as a stable memory channel.

The intuition is that the cell state can keep moving forward through time without being repeatedly overwritten. The gates control the amount of retention and the amount of new writing, so the model can preserve useful history when needed and revise it when the current input demands it. The paper calls this direct, gated additive pathway the constant error carousel because it provides a route through which information, and therefore learning signal, can circulate over long spans.

# Cell state, hidden state, and additive memory updates

The paper treats the memory cell as the central processing and storage unit. The cell state $c_t$ is where the long-term information is stored, while the hidden state $h_t$ is what is exposed to the rest of the network. These two quantities are related, but they play different roles. The memory can accumulate information internally, and the hidden state can decide how much of that internal information should be visible at the current step.

That distinction is made explicit by the output equation

\[
h_t = o_t \, \psi(c_t) .
\]

Here the cell state is first normalized or squashed by $\psi$, and only then is it filtered by the output gate $o_t$. The paper emphasizes that $\psi$ is needed because the cell state would otherwise be unbounded. So the cell state is the storage medium, while the hidden state is the controlled output of that storage. This separation is one of the reasons LSTMs are easier to use for long sequences than simpler recurrent architectures.

The additive memory update itself is also important conceptually. The current state is not built by replacing the old state with a fresh nonlinear transform. Instead, old information and new information are combined in a way that allows the network to decide how much of each should survive. That is what makes the LSTM memory feel more like a controlled accumulation process than a simple feedforward transformation repeated over time.

# Input, forget, and output gates

The original LSTM memory cell contains three gates: input, forget, and output. Each gate is a learned control signal that depends on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$, together with a bias term. The paper writes the gate preactivations as affine functions of these quantities, and then applies a sigmoid nonlinearity. The forget gate was introduced later, but in the formulation used here all three gates are part of the memory cell.

The input gate $i_t$ determines how much new information enters the cell state. The forget gate $f_t$ determines how much of the old memory survives into the next step. The output gate $o_t$ determines how much of the cell state is exposed as the hidden state. This division of labor is central to the LSTM design. Rather than forcing a single update rule to do everything, the model learns separate decisions for writing, preserving, and reading memory.

Because the gates are data-dependent, the model can behave differently in different contexts. For example, when a piece of information should be kept for a long time, the forget gate can stay open enough to preserve it. When the current input contains something important, the input gate can open to let new content into the cell. And when the model should expose only part of its internal memory to the rest of the network, the output gate can regulate that exposure. The paper’s view is that this gated control is exactly what makes LSTMs robust sequence models.

# The roles of sigmoid and tanh activations

The gates in the LSTM use the sigmoid function,

\[
\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-x)} .
\]

The paper uses sigmoid for all gates because it naturally produces values that act like soft switches. A gate value near $0$ closes a pathway, while a value near $1$ opens it. This makes the gate outputs easy to interpret as fractions of information that should be allowed through. In other words, the gates are not hard binary decisions, but continuous control variables.

The other nonlinearities are typically $\tanh$, written in the paper as $\varphi$ for the cell input activation and $\psi$ for the hidden-state activation. The cell input $z_t$ is passed through $\varphi$ after the affine transformation, which shapes the content that can be written into memory. The hidden-state activation $\psi$ is applied to the cell state before the output gate uses it, and the paper explicitly notes that this squashes or normalizes the state so that it does not grow without bound. Together, these nonlinearities make the memory update both expressive and controlled.

This choice of activations is not incidental. The sigmoid gates provide bounded control, while the $\tanh$-style transforms provide a nonlinear representation for the content itself. The result is a memory mechanism that can both store information and keep it numerically well behaved.

# Full scalar LSTM update equations

For a single scalar memory cell, the paper gives the complete LSTM update as

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \\
h_t &= o_t \tilde{h}_t, \qquad \tilde{h}_t = \psi(c_t), \\
z_t &= \varphi(\tilde{z}_t), \qquad \tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z h_{t-1} + b_z, \\
i_t &= \sigma(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad \tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i h_{t-1} + b_i, \\
f_t &= \sigma(\tilde{f}_t), \qquad \tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f h_{t-1} + b_f, \\
o_t &= \sigma(\tilde{o}_t), \qquad \tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_o h_{t-1} + b_o .
\end{aligned}
\]

This set of equations makes the architecture very explicit. The current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ are transformed separately for the cell input and for each gate. Each of these pathways has its own weights and bias: $\mathbf{w}_z$, $\mathbf{w}_i$, $\mathbf{w}_f$, $\mathbf{w}_o$, the recurrent weights $r_z$, $r_i$, $r_f$, $r_o$, and the biases $b_z$, $b_i$, $b_f$, $b_o$.

The model first computes a candidate cell input $z_t$ and gate values $i_t$, $f_t$, and $o_t$. Then it updates the memory state $c_t$, and finally it turns that memory into the hidden state $h_t$. The key point is that the memory update is additive and gated, not a pure overwrite. That is the mechanism that lets the cell carry information across many steps while still being able to adapt to new data.

# From a single memory cell to vector-valued multi-cell LSTMs

The paper notes that later formulations combine multiple scalar memory cells into a vector, written as $\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$. This changes the memory from a single scalar storage variable into a collection of interacting memory components. Once the memory becomes vector-valued, recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ can be used instead of scalar recurrent weights.

This extension matters because it allows the outputs of different memory cells to be mixed with one another. In the scalar version, one cell stores one stream of memory. In the vector-valued version, multiple cells can be organized together, and the recurrent matrices can combine their outputs. The paper presents this as a natural generalization of the original LSTM idea. It preserves the same basic logic of gated storage, but increases representational capacity by allowing several memory cells to operate as a coordinated system.

The paper also states that all components of the memory cell are crucial, which is consistent with the way the vector formulation is built. The architecture is not just about increasing the number of units. It is about preserving the gating and memory-update logic while scaling the memory structure to more expressive settings.

# Hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections and memory mixing

A defining feature of the original LSTM is that the current computation depends on the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$. The paper’s equations show this directly: the previous hidden state contributes to the cell input and to all three gates. These hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections are what make the model genuinely sequential. Each step can condition its update on what the model has already processed.

When there is only one scalar memory cell, this dependence is straightforward. When multiple cells are combined into a vector, the recurrent connections can become matrix-valued and can mix the outputs of the different cells. The paper refers to this as memory mixing. In that setting, the hidden state is not just a passive readout of memory. It becomes a channel through which different memory components influence one another across time.

This mixing is part of what makes the original LSTM so expressive. It allows the network to build richer internal summaries of the past than a strictly independent memory bank would permit. At the same time, it keeps the core LSTM logic intact: memory is written, forgotten, and read through gates, and the hidden state carries the consequences of those decisions forward into the next step.

# Why classical LSTMs remained effective across many domains

The paper argues that LSTMs have stood the test of time because they are very good at learning abstractions. In the paper’s terms, they can extract semantic information and store it in their memory cells. That makes them useful whenever a task requires a model to carry structured information across time rather than merely react to the latest input. The paper cites evidence in the form of number and syntax neurons, linguistic neurons, and sentiment neurons, all of which suggest that LSTMs can organize information in meaningful ways inside their memory.

Their practical record is broad. The paper lists successful applications in text generation, handwriting generation, sequence-to-sequence translation, evaluating computer programs, image captioning, source code generation, rainfall-runoff modeling, hydrological models for flooding warnings, and reinforcement learning systems such as AlphaStar, OpenAI Five, and a magnetic controller for nuclear fusion. Before the Transformer era, LSTMs were also the first Large Language Models, and they prevailed over text generation until 2017.

That history helps explain their continued relevance. LSTMs are not just an old architecture that was replaced and forgotten. They are a mature sequence model with a well-understood memory mechanism, explicit gating, and a strong track record in both scientific and industrial settings. The paper’s conclusion is that these ideas were powerful enough to shape early language modeling and remain important enough to motivate new extensions.

The Three Main Limitations of Classical LSTMs

# Inability to revise storage decisions once information is written

The classical LSTM was built around a simple but powerful memory update rule, $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, together with the hidden-state readout $h_t = o_t \psi(c_t)$. This structure gives the model a persistent memory path, often described as the constant error carousel, because information can be carried forward through time without being repeatedly overwritten. That persistence is one of the reasons LSTMs were so effective in early sequence modeling.

But the same additive design also creates a practical weakness at modern scale: once something has been written into memory, the model has limited ability to revise that decision later. The paper emphasizes this as a major limitation of standard LSTMs. In a long sequence, new evidence may arrive that suggests an earlier stored item was not the best choice after all. A classical LSTM can attenuate or reinforce what is already stored, but its update mechanism is not especially well suited to decisively replacing an old memory with a better one.

# The Nearest Neighbor Search example as a diagnostic task

To make this limitation concrete, the paper uses a Nearest Neighbor Search task. In this task, the model is given a reference vector and must scan through a sequence to find the most similar vector, then return the value attached to that best match at the end of the sequence. The task is sequential by nature, because the model has to compare each new candidate against what it has seen before and keep track of the best one so far.

This diagnostic task is useful because it exposes whether a model can update its memory when a later item is more relevant than an earlier one. The paper reports that a standard LSTM struggles here: it has difficulty revising a stored value when a more similar vector appears later in the sequence. In other words, the model can remember, but it cannot easily change its mind. xLSTM is introduced partly to fix exactly this behavior through exponential gating.

# Why sigmoid gating can make memory updates too conservative

In the original LSTM, the gates are sigmoid gates. The input gate, forget gate, and output gate are all produced by $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+\exp(-x))$, so they are naturally bounded between $0$ and $1$. That bounded range is useful because it keeps the memory mechanism controlled and stable, but it also makes updates inherently cautious. The gates can suppress or pass along information, but they do not strongly amplify a new storage decision.

From the paper’s point of view, this conservatism is part of the reason classical LSTMs struggle to revise memory. If the model needs to strongly favor a newer and better piece of information, a purely sigmoid-gated update can be too gentle. xLSTM addresses this by introducing exponential gating. In the sLSTM and mLSTM variants, gates can be written in exponential form, and the paper adds normalization and stabilization so that these larger values do not cause numerical overflow. The key idea is to give the network a more flexible way to update memory without losing stability.

# Limited storage capacity of scalar memory cells

A second limitation of the classical LSTM is that its memory cell is scalar. Even when later formulations collect multiple scalar cells into a vector, each unit still stores only a scalar quantity. The paper highlights this as a form of limited storage capacity: information must be compressed into scalar cell states, which constrains how much can be retained and how richly it can be represented.

This limitation matters because sequence data often contains many distinct dependencies that need to be preserved at the same time. A scalar memory cell can carry a summary, but the paper argues that this summary may be too compressed for modern language modeling. That is why xLSTM introduces a matrix memory in mLSTM. By moving from a scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$ to a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, the model can store and retrieve information with much greater capacity than a single scalar state allows.

# Rare token prediction as evidence of insufficient memory capacity

The paper gives a second diagnostic for this storage problem using rare token prediction on Wikitext-103. The data are partitioned by token frequency, and the model’s perplexity is measured across these partitions. The result is that classical LSTM performs worse on rare tokens. The paper interprets this as evidence that the model’s storage capacity is not sufficient for the full range of token types that appear in language.

This is a particularly informative test because rare tokens are exactly the kind of items that can be lost when memory is too compressed. Frequent patterns may be easier to handle, but less common tokens place a greater burden on the model’s ability to preserve specific details. The paper’s solution is the matrix-memory design of mLSTM, which is intended to increase what can be stored and later recovered. In that sense, the rare-token experiment is not just a benchmark result; it is direct evidence that scalar memory is too restrictive for modern language modeling.

# The cost of memory mixing for parallel execution

The third major limitation is that classical LSTMs mix memory across time through hidden-hidden recurrent connections. The paper calls this memory mixing. In the original formulation, and also in later versions with multiple scalar memory cells collected into a vector, recurrent weight matrices can mix the outputs of those cells from one time step to the next. That interaction between the current update and the previous hidden state makes the recurrence richer, but it also creates a serial dependency.

Once memory mixing is present, the model cannot update all time steps independently. Each step depends on the hidden state that came before it, so the sequence has to be processed in order. This is the core reason the paper identifies lack of parallelizability as a limitation. The computation is not just recurrent in a conceptual sense; it is operationally sequential.

# Sequential dependence versus hardware efficiency

The practical consequence of this sequential dependence is that it does not match the strengths of modern hardware. Contemporary accelerators are especially effective when work can be arranged as parallel matrix operations. A model that must step through a sequence one token at a time cannot exploit that parallelism in the same way. The paper therefore treats memory mixing not only as an architectural choice, but also as a performance bottleneck.

This is why the paper separates the two xLSTM variants. The sLSTM keeps memory mixing, which helps with state tracking, but it is not fully parallelizable. The mLSTM, by contrast, abandons memory mixing so that the recurrence can be reformulated in parallel. That tradeoff is central to the paper’s argument: a modern LSTM must balance expressive recurrent memory with the need for efficient computation on GPUs.

# Design goals for an extended LSTM suitable for modern language modeling

Taken together, the three limitations point to a clear design problem. If LSTMs are to be scaled to modern language-model sizes, they need to do three things at once: revise storage decisions more flexibly, store more information than a compressed scalar cell can hold, and execute efficiently enough to remain practical at scale. The paper’s answer is xLSTM, which is built specifically to address these requirements.

The design has two complementary parts. The sLSTM introduces exponential gating, stabilization, and memory mixing to improve how the model revises and tracks state. The mLSTM replaces scalar memory with a matrix memory and uses a covariance-style update rule so that storage capacity is much larger, while also remaining fully parallelizable. Both variants are then placed inside residual blocks and stacked into xLSTM architectures. In this way, the paper does not discard the LSTM idea; it extends it so that it can meet the demands of modern language modeling.

A Common xLSTM Ingredient: Exponential Gating, Normalization, and Stabilization

The first major change introduced by xLSTM is not a new memory cell by itself, but a new way of controlling how memory is written, forgotten, and safely kept numerically stable. In the original LSTM, the gates are sigmoid-based and therefore bounded between $0$ and $1$. xLSTM keeps the additive recurrent memory update that made LSTMs effective, but it replaces the usual bounded update gating with positive exponential gating and then adds normalization and stabilization so that the model remains trainable at scale. This shared ingredient appears in both sLSTM and mLSTM, even though the two variants differ in how their memory is structured.

The paper’s motivation is straightforward: bounded gates are useful, but they also make the model conservative about revising storage decisions. xLSTM is designed to be more flexible about how strongly it writes and how strongly it keeps information, while still preserving the recurrent structure that makes memory useful in the first place. The rest of this chapter explains how the paper accomplishes that, and why the normalization and stabilization steps are not optional extras but essential parts of the design.

# Why replace sigmoid-style update gating with exponential gating

The original LSTM update rule is built around an additive memory recurrence,
$$
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,
\qquad
h_t = o_t \psi(c_t),
$$
where the forget gate $f_t$ and input gate $i_t$ regulate how much of the previous memory is retained and how much of the new candidate content $z_t$ is written. In the classic formulation, these gates are sigmoid outputs, so they are always between $0$ and $1$. That bounded range is part of what makes the LSTM stable, but it is also part of what limits the model’s ability to revise a previous storage decision when new evidence becomes more convincing.

The paper emphasizes this issue through tasks such as nearest neighbor search and state tracking. In these settings, a model may need to keep scanning a sequence and replace an earlier stored item with a later one if the later item is more relevant. A gate that can only express values in $[0,1]$ is essentially a bounded soft switch. It can attenuate or preserve, but it cannot represent a more flexible notion of write strength that is naturally larger than one. xLSTM’s answer is to let the gate become a positive scale factor rather than a bounded probability-like quantity. That is the role of exponential gating.

The key idea is not to abandon the additive recurrence, but to give the recurrence richer multiplicative control. If a later input deserves a much stronger write than an earlier one, the gate should be able to express that directly. If a previous memory should carry more weight than a new candidate, the gate should also be able to reflect that. Exponential gating makes this possible because it produces strictly positive values without imposing the upper bound that sigmoid gates impose.

# Input-dependent and learnable gates in the LSTM tradition

xLSTM does not invent gating from scratch. It keeps the LSTM tradition that gates are learned functions of the current input and, in the scalar-memory variant, the previous hidden state. In the original LSTM, the gate preactivations are affine functions of $\mathbf{x}_t$, $h_{t-1}$, and a bias term. The paper keeps this basic pattern:
$$
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i h_{t-1} + b_i,
\qquad
\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f h_{t-1} + b_f,
\qquad
\tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_o h_{t-1} + b_o.
$$
The gate itself is then obtained by applying a nonlinearity to the preactivation.

This matters because the gates are not fixed hand-designed schedules. They are learned control signals, and they can adapt to the current token, the current context, and the current hidden summary. That is what allows an LSTM to decide, step by step, when to write, when to keep, and when to reveal. xLSTM keeps this logic intact. The change is that the input and forget gates are no longer constrained to act like bounded selectors. They become learned positive strengths.

The paper preserves the output gate as a sigmoid gate. So the architectural picture is still recognizably LSTM-like: the model learns how much to store, how much to forget, and how much of the memory to expose. The difference is that storage and forgetting are now controlled by exponential quantities, while exposure remains a bounded gate. This separation is important because it lets xLSTM expand the expressive range of memory control without discarding the familiar recurrent structure.

# Input gates and forget gates with exponential activations

In sLSTM and mLSTM, the input gate is exponential:
$$
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t).
$$
The forget gate can be either sigmoid or exponential in the paper’s formulation:
$$
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)
\quad \text{or} \quad
f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t).
$$
The point is that the gate values are no longer restricted to $[0,1]$ by default. They are positive scaling factors. The paper interprets the forget gate as a decay rate and the input gate as a learning rate. That interpretation is natural once the gates are treated as positive multiplicative strengths rather than probabilities.

This change gives the model much more flexibility in how it updates memory. A large positive input gate can make a new write strongly influence the memory state. A strong forget path can preserve or carry forward previously accumulated information with significant weight. Because both gates are positive, they can participate cleanly in additive memory updates while still giving the model a richer range of temporal behaviors.

At the same time, the paper is very clear that exponential gating cannot be used naively. Exponentials can become large very quickly, which makes overflows a serious problem. So the choice of exponential gating is coupled immediately to the next two ingredients: a normalizer state that keeps track of effective write strength, and a stabilizer state that keeps the gate computations numerically safe.

# The normalizer state and its role in tracking effective write strength

For sLSTM, the paper introduces a second recurrent state alongside the cell state:
$$
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t.
$$
This is the normalizer state. The hidden state is then formed not by applying $\tanh$ to the cell state, but by dividing the cell state by the normalizer state:
$$
h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t,
\qquad
\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
$$
The purpose of $n_t$ is to keep track of how much effective write mass has accumulated in memory. It is not storing content itself. Instead, it stores the strength of the update process that produced the content.

This becomes clearer if one unrolls the recurrence. Every contribution written into memory at time $j$ is multiplied by the input gate $i_j$ and then carried forward by all future forget gates. The same is true for the normalizer, except that the content $z_j$ is omitted. So the cell state is a weighted accumulation of content, while the normalizer is a weighted accumulation of the same gate strengths. Their ratio is therefore a normalized memory summary, not just a raw sum. In the paper’s language, the normalizer state keeps record of the strength of the gates.

The same idea extends to mLSTM, where the normalizer is vector-valued rather than scalar. There, the hidden state uses a denominator based on the dot product between the query and the normalizer state, with a lower bound to keep the division stable:
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\left\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\right\}}.
$$
The specific form differs, but the principle is the same. The model does not expose a raw unnormalized memory. It exposes memory relative to the strength with which it was built.

# How normalized hidden states differ from tanh-squashed cell states

In the original LSTM, the hidden state is obtained by squashing the cell state through a function $\psi$, typically $\tanh$:
$$
h_t = o_t \psi(c_t).
$$
This means the cell state itself may grow internally, but the exposed hidden state is forced through a saturating nonlinearity. That is a very different operation from the normalization used in xLSTM. In sLSTM, the hidden state is
$$
h_t = o_t \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
$$
Here, the output is not a generic bounded transform of the cell state. It is a ratio between content and accumulated gate strength.

This distinction matters conceptually. A tanh squashing function compresses magnitude into a fixed range, which can hide differences between memory states once the magnitude becomes large. By contrast, dividing by $n_t$ does not merely compress the cell state; it adjusts the cell state according to how much writing and forgetting has occurred. The hidden state therefore remains tied to the actual history of memory updates. It is a normalized representation of memory, not just a bounded one.

The output gate still plays its familiar role. It decides how much of this normalized memory is exposed as the hidden state. So xLSTM keeps the LSTM separation between storage and exposure, but replaces the old notion of “normalize the cell with $\tanh$” by a more explicit notion of “track the accumulated gate mass and divide by it.” That is one of the central conceptual differences introduced by the new gating scheme.

# The stabilizer state $m_t$ and the max-based log-domain trick

Once gates become exponential, numerical stability becomes a central concern. The paper addresses this by introducing an additional stabilizer state $m_t$. For sLSTM, it is defined as
$$
m_t = \max\left(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t)\right).
$$
This state tracks the relevant scale of the current forget path and the current input gate in log space. Instead of allowing the exponentials to grow without control, the model keeps a running reference point that reflects the largest relevant log-magnitude at each step.

The corresponding stabilized gates are
$$
i'_t = \exp\left(\log(i_t) - m_t\right) = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t),
$$
and
$$
f'_t = \exp\left(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t\right).
$$
These expressions rescale the gate values relative to the current stabilizer state. The key intuition is that the model is not changing the meaning of the gates; it is changing the numerical frame in which they are represented. By subtracting the current dominant scale in log space, the implementation avoids evaluating very large exponentials directly.

This is why the stabilizer is described as a max-based log-domain trick. It is a running scale tracker. It compares the scale of the new input gate with the scale of the forget path that has already been accumulated, and it chooses the larger one as the new reference point. That reference point is then used to re-express the gates in a numerically manageable form.

# Stabilized input and forget gates

The stabilized gates are the values that are actually used in the stabilized forward pass. Because $m_t$ is chosen as the maximum of the relevant log-scales, the resulting exponentials are normalized relative to that maximum. In practical terms, the stabilized gates stay in a safe numerical range, rather than exploding as the raw exponentials might. This is crucial because the gates are used repeatedly across a long recurrence, and a small numerical issue at one step can propagate through the entire sequence.

The paper applies the same stabilization logic to both sLSTM and mLSTM. In sLSTM, the recurrent state $m_t$ makes the exponential gate values safe while preserving the meaning of the cell and normalizer updates. In mLSTM, the same stabilization is used even though the memory itself is matrix-valued. So stabilization is a shared ingredient, not a variant-specific detail.

It is also important that stabilization is not done by clipping or approximation. The paper’s formulas define a mathematically exact rescaling. That means the stabilized gates are not “close to” the original ones in some heuristic sense; they are a different parameterization of the same computation. This exactness is what makes the next property possible: the output of the network, and even the gradients, are preserved.

# Why stabilization preserves outputs and gradients

The paper states that replacing $f_t$ with $f'_t$ and $i_t$ with $i'_t$ in the forward pass does not change the output of the whole network. It also states that the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters are unchanged. This is a strong result, and it is one of the reasons the stabilization scheme is so useful. It means that the method is not merely a numerical patch; it is an exact reformulation of the same model.

Intuitively, the reason this works is that the stabilizer absorbs a common scale factor into the recurrent computation. The memory update, the normalizer update, and the output normalization are all organized so that only the relative strengths of the gates matter. Once those relative strengths are preserved, the external behavior of the model is preserved as well. The appendix proves this formally, but the conceptual message is simple: the stabilized gates compute the same function, only in a safer numerical representation.

Because the function is unchanged, backpropagation sees the same model. So the gradients with respect to the learnable parameters are also unchanged. That is particularly important in large-scale training, where one wants numerical stability without altering the optimization problem. xLSTM’s stabilization is designed to provide exactly that.

# Numerical overflow, underflow, and practical training stability

The paper explicitly points out that exponential activation functions can produce large values and therefore cause overflows. That is the main numerical danger introduced by exponential gating. The stabilizer state $m_t$ is the mechanism that addresses this danger, and the normalizer state ensures that the hidden representation remains scale-aware rather than accumulating raw unbounded sums. Together, these components keep the recurrent computation numerically well behaved.

The practical lesson is that xLSTM does not rely on exponential gating in isolation. It couples exponential gating with normalization and exact stabilization so that the gates can be expressive without becoming unstable. This is what makes the shared ingredient usable in both sLSTM and mLSTM, and it is what allows the paper to scale the design to large language modeling experiments. The model gains a richer way to represent write strength and memory retention, while the stabilization machinery keeps the computation trainable in practice.

In that sense, the first xLSTM ingredient is best understood as a package: exponential gates give the model more expressive control over memory, the normalizer state turns that control into a meaningful normalized hidden representation, and the stabilizer state makes the whole construction numerically safe. The paper’s central claim is that these three pieces together preserve the spirit of the LSTM while removing some of its most important limitations.

sLSTM: Revisable Scalar Memory with New Memory Mixing

# The sLSTM Forward Pass Step by Step

The scalar sLSTM keeps the central LSTM idea of a recurrent memory cell, but it changes the gate dynamics and the way the cell state is turned into the hidden state. In the paper’s notation, a single time step begins with the current input vector $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$. These are linearly projected to form the cell input and the three gates, and then the memory is updated. The key point is that the memory update is still additive, as in the original LSTM, but the new gate parameterization and the extra normalizer state make the memory revisable.

The forward pass is
\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \qquad
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t, \qquad
h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t, \qquad
\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
\]
The cell input and gates are computed from affine transforms of $\mathbf{x}_t$ and $h_{t-1}$:
\[
z_t = \varphi(\tilde{z}_t), \qquad
\tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z h_{t-1} + b_z,
\]
\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i h_{t-1} + b_i,
\]
\[
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\ \text{or}\ \exp(\tilde{f}_t), \qquad
\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f h_{t-1} + b_f,
\]
\[
o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t), \qquad
\tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_o h_{t-1} + b_o.
\]
This is the entire update in compact form: compute gates and candidate content, update the scalar memory, normalize it with the normalizer state, and finally expose part of it through the output gate.

The sequencing matters. The current hidden state $h_{t-1}$ influences the current candidate content and all three gates, so the model does not simply store the input stream; it also conditions its next decision on what it has already summarized. In the paper’s framing, this is the mechanism that lets the model revise earlier storage decisions rather than only accumulate them.

# Cell State Update with Exponential Input Gating

The cell state update remains additive, just as in the original LSTM:
\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t.
\]
What changes in sLSTM is the form of the gates. The input gate is exponential,
\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t),
\]
instead of being restricted to a sigmoid. This matters because the input gate is no longer limited to values between $0$ and $1$. In the paper’s design, the model can therefore write a new piece of information with variable strength, rather than only deciding whether to pass it through or suppress it.

This is one of the main ways sLSTM departs from the vanilla LSTM. In a standard LSTM, the input gate mostly acts as a soft on/off switch. In sLSTM, the exponential input gate is part of a broader change in the gate algebra: the model can make stronger write decisions when the current evidence is more relevant. The paper uses this as one of the mechanisms that helps the model revise storage decisions in sequence problems.

The candidate content $z_t$ itself is still produced by a nonlinear transform of the current input and previous hidden state,
\[
z_t = \varphi(\mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z h_{t-1} + b_z),
\]
so the model still follows the basic LSTM pattern of separating “what content to write” from “how strongly to write it.” The novelty lies in letting the write strength be exponential rather than sigmoid-bounded.

# Normalizer State and Hidden State Computation as $c_t$ Divided by $n_t$

The normalizer state is the second key addition to sLSTM:
\[
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t.
\]
The paper describes it as a state that “sums up the product of the input gate times all future forget gates.” That description becomes clear if one expands the recursion. The current normalizer contains the current input gate, plus older input gates discounted by the forget gates that came after them. In other words, $n_t$ keeps track not only of what has been written, but also of how much that content has been preserved over time.

The hidden state is then formed by normalizing the cell state:
\[
\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}, \qquad h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t.
\]
This ratio is the central normalization idea in sLSTM. Instead of directly squashing $c_t$ with a fixed nonlinearity, as in the original LSTM, the model divides by a learned, time-dependent normalizer. The result is that the exposed memory content is scaled relative to the total strength of the gating history. The paper’s intuition is that the network should not only remember content, but also remember how strongly that content has been selected.

A useful way to read this is that $c_t$ stores the accumulated evidence, while $n_t$ stores the accumulated confidence with which that evidence was admitted into memory. The quotient $c_t/n_t$ therefore acts like a normalized summary of the past. This is what allows sLSTM to preserve the additive memory update while changing the behavior of the memory readout.

# Output Gating in sLSTM

The output gate in sLSTM keeps the familiar role from the original LSTM:
\[
o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t), \qquad h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t.
\]
It controls how much of the normalized memory is exposed as the hidden state. Because the hidden state is also fed into the recurrent projections at the next time step, the output gate influences not only what the model emits at time $t$, but also what information becomes available for future gating and content computation.

This preserves an important LSTM intuition. The memory cell is not forced to reveal everything it stores. Instead, the cell computes an internal normalized summary, and the output gate decides how much of that summary should become visible. In sLSTM, this mechanism works on top of the new ratio $c_t/n_t$, so the output gate is controlling a normalized memory representation rather than a directly squashed cell state.

The paper keeps the output gate sigmoid-valued, unlike the input gate and optionally the forget gate. That choice matters because the output gate is not the part of the architecture that is responsible for revising storage decisions; its role is to regulate exposure. The revision mechanism comes from the combination of exponential gating and normalization in the state update itself.

# Optional Sigmoid or Exponential Forget Gating

The forget gate in sLSTM is allowed to be either sigmoid or exponential:
\[
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\ \text{or}\ \exp(\tilde{f}_t).
\]
This flexibility is part of the paper’s “exponential gating” modification. The forget gate still serves the same conceptual role as in the original LSTM: it determines how much of the previous memory is retained. But with the exponential option, the gate is no longer restricted to a bounded range.

That flexibility comes with a numerical challenge. Exponential values can grow very large, so the paper introduces a stabilization state $m_t$:
\[
m_t = \max\bigl(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t)\bigr),
\]
\[
i_t' = \exp(\log(i_t) - m_t) = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t),
\]
\[
f_t' = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t).
\]
The paper states that replacing $f_t$ with $f_t'$ and $i_t$ with $i_t'$ in the forward pass does not change the output of the whole network and does not change the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters. So the stabilized form is not a different model; it is a numerically safer representation of the same computation.

This stabilization is important because it lets the model use exponential gates without losing control of scale. In the paper’s design, the extra state $m_t$ is what makes the new gating scheme practical rather than merely expressive in theory.

# How sLSTM Differs from Vanilla LSTM in Behavior and Intuition

The original LSTM uses the additive cell update
\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \qquad h_t = o_t \psi(c_t),
\]
with sigmoid gates and a squashing or normalization function $\psi$ applied to the cell state before output. sLSTM keeps the additive memory update, but changes two things at once: the gates can be exponential, and the cell state is normalized by the separate normalizer state $n_t$ before being exposed.

That difference changes the model’s behavior in an important way. In a vanilla LSTM, the cell state is passed through a fixed nonlinearity, so the output is controlled mainly by the magnitude of $c_t$ itself. In sLSTM, the output depends on the relationship between the accumulated cell content and the accumulated gate strength. This makes the memory more explicitly revisable, because the model is not just storing content; it is also tracking how strongly and how consistently that content has been accepted into memory.

The paper uses the nearest-neighbor-search example to illustrate the limitation of standard LSTM behavior. When a better match appears later in the sequence, a vanilla LSTM struggles to revise what it has already stored. sLSTM is designed to address that specific issue through exponential gating and normalization. So the intuition is not that sLSTM abandons LSTM memory, but that it makes the memory easier to revise when new evidence is stronger than older evidence.

# Why Exponential Gating Helps Revise Earlier Storage Decisions

The most direct intuition for exponential gating is that it gives the model a larger dynamic range for writing and forgetting. A sigmoid gate can only attenuate or pass through within a bounded interval, whereas an exponential gate can scale memory contributions much more flexibly. That matters in problems where later observations should replace earlier ones rather than merely blend with them.

The paper’s nearest-neighbor-search example captures this idea well. A sequence is scanned for the most similar vector to a reference vector, and the associated value must be retrieved at the end. The challenge is not only storing an item, but also revising that stored item when a later, more relevant vector appears. With exponential gating, a later input can have enough influence to dominate earlier content, while the normalizer state keeps the representation from becoming arbitrarily scaled. In this sense, the network can revise its storage decision instead of merely refining it.

This is why the paper presents exponential gating as a remedy for one of the classic limitations of LSTMs. The model still uses a recurrent memory cell, but the memory is now governed by a gate system that can express stronger selection pressure. That makes the update process more responsive to new evidence, which is exactly what revisable memory is supposed to do.

# Multiple Memory Cells in sLSTM

Although the basic equations are written for a scalar cell, the paper notes that sLSTM can also have multiple memory cells, just like later formulations of the original LSTM. In that case, the scalar states are combined into a vector, and the recurrent connection becomes matrix-valued. The result is that the outputs of different memory cells can interact through recurrent weight matrices.

In the paper’s notation, this means recurrent matrices $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{z}}$, $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{i}}$, $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{f}}$, and $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{o}}$ can connect the hidden-state vector to the cell input and the gates. This is the mechanism the paper calls memory mixing. Instead of each cell evolving in isolation, the cells can influence one another through the previous hidden state.

This matters because it gives the scalar memory structure more expressive power than a set of independent scalar registers. The memory is still built from scalar cells, but those cells are no longer separate. Their interaction is what lets sLSTM behave as a richer recurrent system while keeping the scalar nature of the basic cell state.

# Heads in sLSTM and Within-Head versus Across-Head Mixing

The paper extends the multiple-cell design by allowing sLSTM to have multiple heads. The important detail is that memory mixing happens within each head, but not across heads. So heads partition the memory into groups, and each group can mix internally while remaining separate from the others.

This is presented as a new way of memory mixing. In the paper’s terminology, the introduction of heads together with exponential gating is not just a bookkeeping choice; it changes the structure of how memory can be organized. Within a head, the recurrent matrices can mix the cells, so each head can maintain its own internal recurrent state. Across heads, however, there is no direct memory mixing.

The distinction is useful because it clarifies the role of heads in sLSTM. They are not merely copies of the same memory cell. They are separate memory subspaces, each with its own internal dynamics. That gives the architecture a structured way to represent different histories or contexts while still preserving the recurrent nature of the model.

# Why sLSTM Is Expressive on State-Tracking and Formal Language Tasks

The paper uses formal language tasks to test whether exponential gating with memory mixing actually helps with state tracking. This matters because a sequence model often has to do more than remember tokens; it has to keep track of an evolving latent state that determines what comes next. The paper argues that models without memory mixing, such as Transformers and State Space Models, cannot solve some regular grammars like parity, while xLSTM’s recurrent state-tracking mechanism is designed for exactly these kinds of problems.

The formal language experiments are constructed to assess this ability directly. The paper extends the tasks from the cited work so that models can be trained on multiple sequence lengths and then evaluated for extrapolation. Accuracy is measured on the tokens that matter for the task, and the score is scaled from $0$ to $1$. In this setting, the purpose of the model is not just to predict the next symbol locally, but to maintain the correct abstract state of the sequence over time.

That is the core reason sLSTM is expressive on these tasks. Exponential gating helps the model revise stored decisions, and memory mixing lets the model combine information across cells through recurrent matrices. Together, these features support the kind of long-range latent-state computation that formal language recognition requires. In the paper’s framing, this is what makes sLSTM a strong candidate for sequence problems where correct prediction depends on keeping track of the state of the process, not just the most recent input.

mLSTM: Matrix Memory and Associative Retrieval

# Why Move from Scalar Memory to Matrix Memory

The original LSTM idea stores the past in a scalar memory cell $c_t$, updated by an additive rule and controlled by gates. That design is powerful, but the paper argues that it also creates a bottleneck: a scalar cell compresses information too aggressively. When a model needs to remember many distinct associations over a long sequence, a single scalar state is a limited container.

The mLSTM variant addresses this by replacing the scalar memory cell with a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This is the central structural change. Instead of storing the past as one number, mLSTM stores it as a matrix that can represent richer relationships between what was seen before and what may be needed later. In the paper’s framing, this is how xLSTM increases storage capacity: it moves from compressing the past into scalar cells to storing key-value associations in a higher-dimensional memory.

The motivation is not just larger size for its own sake. The paper’s key point is that the matrix memory makes associative retrieval more expressive. A scalar memory can remember that something happened, but a matrix memory can remember how one vector should be associated with another. That is the conceptual leap behind mLSTM.

# Key, Value, and Query Vectors in mLSTM

To turn memory into an associative store, mLSTM uses the Transformer-style terminology of keys, values, and queries. From the input vector $\mathbf{x}_t$, the model computes three vectors:

\[
\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q,
\qquad
\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k,
\qquad
\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v.
\]

Here, the query $\mathbf{q}_t$ is what the model will use to probe memory, the key $\mathbf{k}_t$ is what gets written as an address, and the value $\mathbf{v}_t$ is the content to be recovered later. The paper uses this terminology because the retrieval problem is exactly the one associated with key-value memory: store an association now, and recover the corresponding value later when a matching query appears.

This is important intuitively. A key does not by itself contain the answer; rather, it tells the system where the answer is stored. A value is the content that should be returned. The query is the current request. If the query matches a stored key, the stored value should be brought back. mLSTM is built around this idea directly.

# The Matrix Cell State $\mathbf{C}_t$ and Outer-Product Storage

The core mLSTM update is

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top.
\]

This equation is the matrix-memory analog of the original LSTM cell update. The forget gate $f_t$ scales the old memory, while the input gate $i_t$ controls how strongly the new association is written. The new information enters as an outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$.

The outer product is the key idea. It stores the value and the key together in a matrix form: every component of $\mathbf{v}_t$ is paired with every component of $\mathbf{k}_t$. That means the memory is not merely collecting a list of past values. It is encoding a structured association between what should be remembered and how it should be addressed later.

A useful way to think about the update is that the matrix memory is being written like a table of associations. The forget gate decides how much of the old table remains, and the input gate decides how much weight the new association gets. Because the write operation is multiplicative in key and value, the stored memory can later be accessed by a matching query through matrix multiplication.

# The Covariance Update Rule for Key-Value Association

The paper identifies the update

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top
\]

as a covariance update rule, and this is the conceptual basis for the matrix memory in mLSTM. In the LSTM formulation, the forget and input gates turn this into a gated version of that update, but the central memory-writing mechanism remains the same: add a value-key outer product to the memory matrix.

The paper notes that this covariance-style update is optimal for maximal separability of retrieved binary vectors, which is equivalent to maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio. The intuition is that when memory is used for association, stored patterns should remain distinguishable from one another. A good update rule makes it easier to retrieve the right value when a query arrives later. In this sense, the matrix memory is not just bigger; it is organized to preserve retrievability.

The paper also assumes layer normalization before projecting inputs to keys and values, so those vectors have zero mean. Under that assumption, the covariance update rule fits naturally with the statistics of the stored representations. This is why the paper frames the matrix memory as a covariance-based associative store rather than as an arbitrary matrix accumulator.

# Connections to Bidirectional Associative Memories

The retrieval problem mLSTM solves is the same one studied in Bidirectional Associative Memories, or BAMs. In that setting, a pair of vectors is stored together, and later a cue or query is used to recover the associated partner. The mLSTM memory behaves in exactly that spirit: it stores key-value pairs at time $t$ and later uses a query at time $t+\tau$ to recover the value.

This connection is more than historical. It clarifies what the matrix memory is for. The matrix is not merely a compressed summary of the past; it is an associative map from keys to values. When the query matches the stored key structure, the corresponding value is recovered through the matrix-memory dynamics. That makes mLSTM especially suitable for tasks in which the model must remember and later retrieve specific associations.

The paper’s presentation of mLSTM emphasizes this BAM-like behavior because it helps explain why a matrix memory is useful. A scalar memory can represent state, but a matrix memory can represent associations between state components. That is the essential difference.

# Connections to Fast Weights, Linear Attention, and Retention-Style Models

The paper explicitly connects the covariance update rule in mLSTM to Fast Weight Programmers. In that view, the memory matrix is a fast-changing state that is updated online as new information arrives. The paper notes that later variants of fast weights were equipped with a constant decay rate multiplied to the previous memory and a constant learning rate multiplied to the new outer product. In mLSTM, these roles are played by the forget gate and the input gate.

This is why the paper says the forget gate corresponds to a decay rate and the input gate corresponds to a learning rate. The output gate then scales the retrieved vector. So, although mLSTM is framed as an LSTM extension, it also sits in a broader family of associative-memory models that use matrix or linear-state updates.

The paper also places mLSTM near several modern models that use related ideas: RWKV-5, RWKV-6, Retention, Linear Transformer, and HGRN2. The common theme is that information is accumulated through a structured update rule rather than through full attention over all past tokens. mLSTM shares this associative-memory spirit, but it does so with the LSTM gating language and with a matrix-valued cell state.

# The Normalizer Vector State $\mathbf{n}_t$

Alongside the matrix memory, mLSTM maintains a normalizer vector state $\mathbf{n}_t$:

\[
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t.
\]

This state is the normalization counterpart to $\mathbf{C}_t$. The paper describes it as a weighted sum of key vectors, where each key is weighted by the input gate and all future forget gates. Its purpose is to keep track of the strength of the stored associations so that the retrieval output can be normalized properly.

Intuitively, $\mathbf{n}_t$ records how much “support” the memory has accumulated for the current query space. If $\mathbf{C}_t$ stores the actual key-value associations, then $\mathbf{n}_t$ tracks the scale of the key contributions that went into that memory. This is crucial because the retrieval step needs to know not just what was stored, but how strongly it was stored.

Without a normalizer, matrix retrieval can produce outputs with unstable magnitudes. The normalizer gives the model a way to compensate for that by scaling the retrieved vector according to the accumulated key strength.

# Retrieval by $\mathbf{C}_t$ Multiplied with $\mathbf{q}_t$

Once the memory has been written, retrieval happens by multiplying the matrix state with the query:

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max \{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \}}.
\]

This is the central read operation in mLSTM. The matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ combines all previous key-value associations, and the query $\mathbf{q}_t$ probes that matrix to produce a retrieved vector. The hidden state is then

\[
\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t,
\]

so the output gate scales the retrieved content elementwise.

The intuition is straightforward: the query asks, “Which stored association is relevant right now?” The matrix memory answers by projecting the query through all stored associations at once. Because the memory was built from outer products of values and keys, the multiplication with $\mathbf{q}_t$ produces a value-like output when the query aligns with the corresponding key.

The output gate then decides how much of that retrieved content should be exposed as the hidden state. So the read path in mLSTM has two stages: memory lookup through the matrix, and output control through gating.

# The Denominator $\max$ of Absolute $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ and $1$ for Stable Normalization

The denominator in the retrieval formula is

\[
\max \{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \}.
\]

The paper emphasizes that the dot product $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ can be close to zero, which would make the retrieval unstable if used directly as a divisor. Taking the absolute value and lower bounding it by $1$ protects the computation from blow-ups.

This normalization is not an arbitrary trick; it is part of making the matrix memory numerically usable. The denominator reflects the strength of the normalizer state relative to the query. If that strength is small, dividing by it would amplify noise. By enforcing a minimum value of $1$, the model avoids extreme scaling while preserving the intended normalization behavior.

So the denominator plays the same role that a safe normalization constant would play in any associative-memory system: it keeps the retrieval magnitude controlled even when the raw dot product is weak.

# Input, Forget, and Output Gates in mLSTM

mLSTM keeps the LSTM gating idea, but the paper changes the gate activations to support the matrix-memory design. The input gate is exponential,

\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad \tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_i,
\]

and the forget gate can be either sigmoid or exponential,

\[
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t) \quad \text{or} \quad \exp(\tilde{f}_t), \qquad \tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_f.
\]

The output gate remains sigmoid-valued,

\[
\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t), \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t = \mathbf{W}_o \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_o.
\]

These gates are the control knobs of the memory system. The input gate determines how strongly new key-value information is written. The forget gate determines how much of the previous memory persists. The output gate determines how much of the retrieved memory is passed on as the hidden state.

Because exponential gates can become very large, the paper stabilizes them with an additional state $m_t$. The stabilized gates are

\[
m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t)),
\]

\[
i_t' = \exp(\log(i_t) - m_t) = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t),
\]

\[
f_t' = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t).
\]

The paper states that replacing $f_t$ by $f_t'$ and $i_t$ by $i_t'$ does not change the network output or the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters. This is important because it means the stabilization is mathematically faithful to the intended computation while making the model numerically safer.

# Why Matrix Memory Increases Storage Capacity

The paper’s main argument for mLSTM is that a matrix cell state has greater storage capacity than a scalar one. A scalar memory cell can only compress the past into one degree of freedom per cell, while a matrix memory can encode relations among many dimensions at once. That extra structure matters when the model must remember multiple distinct associations and later retrieve one of them based on a query.

The outer-product storage rule gives the memory a form of addressability. Instead of merely remembering that something happened, mLSTM remembers how a key should lead to a value. This is a richer storage format because it allows the model to select among multiple stored associations using the query. That is why the paper views mLSTM as better suited for associative recall than scalar-memory LSTMs.

The paper’s broader claim is that this richer memory helps overcome one of the major limits of classic LSTMs: limited storage capacity. In mLSTM, the memory is not just larger in size; it is larger in representational power.

# Multiple Cells, Multiple Heads, and Parallelization

The paper notes that mLSTM can have multiple memory cells, just like the original LSTM. It also states that for mLSTM, multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent, because there is no memory mixing. This is an important architectural point.

In a system with memory mixing, hidden-to-hidden connections couple one cell or head to another, so the way memory is partitioned matters. In mLSTM, there is no such coupling. As a result, the distinction between organizing the model into multiple cells or multiple heads does not change the fundamental memory computation. The matrix memories remain separate, and the model does not need hidden-hidden recurrence to exchange information between them.

This absence of memory mixing is also what makes mLSTM parallelizable. The paper states that the recurrence can be reformulated in a parallel version. That is one of the main reasons mLSTM is attractive as a modern recurrent architecture: it keeps the associative-memory flavor of LSTMs while allowing efficient matrix-based computation. The paper further notes that these computations can be done in parallel on GPUs, so the heavier matrix memory does not necessarily translate into prohibitive wall-clock cost.

In short, mLSTM combines a richer memory representation with a computation pattern that avoids the strict sequential bottleneck of classic recurrent memory mixing. That combination is central to why the paper treats mLSTM as a serious modern alternative for sequence modeling.

Parallelism and Complexity: Why mLSTM Scales Differently from sLSTM

# What makes recurrence sequential in classical LSTMs

The core reason a classical LSTM is sequential is that each time step depends on the hidden state from the previous time step. In the paper’s formulation, the memory update is additive,
\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,
\qquad
h_t = o_t \psi(c_t),
\]
but the gates and the cell input are themselves computed from both the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$. That means the model cannot determine the quantities at time $t$ until it has already computed the quantities at time $t-1$. Even though the cell state update is designed to help gradients flow through time, the recurrence still forms a strict chain.

This is what makes classical LSTM processing inherently left-to-right over time. The hidden state is not just an output; it is part of the next step’s computation. So if the model is reading a sequence token by token, the computation of the next token representation is blocked until the current token has been processed. In other words, the LSTM has linear sequence processing in the sense that it revisits each token once, but it does not have time-step parallelism because the next state is causally tied to the previous one.

# Why removing hidden-to-hidden memory mixing enables mLSTM parallelization

The paper identifies hidden-to-hidden memory mixing as the key obstacle to parallel computation. In classical LSTM and in sLSTM, the gates depend on the previous hidden state, which means information must be passed step by step through the recurrent pathway. That hidden-state feedback is what forces sequential execution. If a gate at time $t$ needs $h_{t-1}$, then the computation at $t$ cannot start until $t-1$ is finished.

mLSTM removes that source of dependence. Its query, key, value, and gates are computed directly from the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and bias terms, not from $h_{t-1}$. The memory update is then
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t,
\]
with the hidden state read out from the matrix memory by
\[
\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t,
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}.
\]
Because the current step no longer needs the previous hidden state to form its gates, the recurrence loses the hidden-to-hidden bottleneck that prevents parallelization.

The paper also emphasizes that this is not just a small implementation detail. It is a structural change in the model family. sLSTM keeps memory mixing, including across multiple cells and heads, while mLSTM abandons that mixing to become fully parallelizable. That is the central trade-off: sLSTM preserves richer recurrent interaction, but mLSTM gains a computation pattern that is far friendlier to modern hardware.

# Rewriting the mLSTM recurrence as a parallel computation

The reason mLSTM can be reformulated in parallel is that its state update is built from algebraic operations that combine neatly across time. Each step contributes an outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ to the matrix memory and a key vector $\mathbf{k}_t$ to the normalizer state. Since the gates and projections come directly from $\mathbf{x}_t$, the model does not need to wait for a previous hidden state to produce the next local transformation.

The paper’s point is not that the recurrence disappears, but that the recurrence becomes reorganizable. Instead of a strict hidden-state chain where each step must be evaluated one after another, the mLSTM update can be expressed in a parallel form using standard matrix operations. That is why the authors describe mLSTM as fully parallelizable. The key intuition is that the model stores information in a way that is compatible with GPU-style batched linear algebra rather than relying on sequential hidden-state mixing.

This matters because the sequence dimension is the expensive axis in language modeling. If the model can restructure its recurrence so that many operations can be carried out together, then the hardware can do much more work at once. The paper’s design choice is therefore not only about modeling power, but also about matching the computation to what modern accelerators are good at.

# Linear compute growth and constant memory with respect to sequence length

A central complexity advantage of xLSTM in the paper is that its computation grows linearly with sequence length, while its memory with respect to sequence length stays constant. The reason is simple: the model does not keep a growing list of all past states the way attention-based systems do. Instead, it carries a fixed-size recurrent state forward. Once the state is initialized, each new token updates that state, but the size of the state does not expand as the sequence gets longer.

This is especially important to understand in contrast to Transformer-style processing. In xLSTM, longer sequences cost more compute because there are more time steps, but they do not force the model to store a longer and longer history in memory. That is what the paper means by compressive memory. The past is summarized into a fixed internal state, which makes xLSTM attractive when the context is long but the available memory budget is limited.

The paper explicitly connects this to practical deployment. Because the memory footprint does not grow with sequence length, xLSTM is described as well suited for industrial applications and for edge settings. In those settings, a model that keeps a bounded state is easier to deploy than one whose storage grows with every additional token.

# The cost of $d \times d$ matrix updates and retrievals

The advantage of mLSTM’s matrix memory comes with a cost: the memory is no longer a scalar cell state but a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. That means each update and each retrieval involves matrix-level arithmetic. The update
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top
\]
requires processing a $d \times d$ state, and the readout
\[
\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t
\]
also involves matrix multiplication. So although the memory does not grow with sequence length, each step is computationally heavier than the scalar update of a classical LSTM.

The paper is explicit that this is a trade-off. mLSTM increases storage capacity by using a matrix memory, but that richer memory has a larger arithmetic cost than a scalar cell. Still, the authors point out that these computations can be carried out in parallel on GPUs, so the wall-clock impact is smaller than the raw matrix size might suggest. In other words, the model pays for greater memory capacity with more arithmetic, but the hardware can absorb much of that cost when the implementation is efficient.

# Trade-offs between memory capacity and arithmetic cost

The entire motivation for mLSTM is that scalar memory can be too compressed. Classical LSTMs, with scalar cell states, have to squeeze all useful history into a small number of scalar channels. The paper uses this to motivate the matrix memory, which can store key-value associations in a much richer form. This is the storage side of the trade-off: more expressive memory should handle harder retrieval and tracking problems better.

But richer memory is never free. A matrix state has far more entries than a scalar state, so every update and every retrieval becomes more expensive. The paper’s framing is that xLSTM deliberately trades arithmetic cost for memory capacity. That trade-off is acceptable when the task needs more structure in memory, such as long-context language modeling or associative retrieval, because the model’s ability to preserve and recover information becomes more important than minimizing each individual update.

This is also why the paper distinguishes sLSTM from mLSTM. sLSTM keeps a scalar memory and uses memory mixing, which preserves a familiar recurrent flavor. mLSTM enlarges the memory itself and removes memory mixing, which makes the state more expressive and the computation more parallelizable. The two variants occupy different positions in the same design space.

# Comparison with Transformer attention complexity

The paper contrasts xLSTM with Transformer attention primarily on sequence-length complexity. Transformer attention is quadratic in context length because tokens interact pairwise across the sequence. That pairwise interaction is powerful, but it also becomes increasingly expensive as the sequence grows. xLSTM, by contrast, processes the sequence with linear compute growth and constant memory with respect to sequence length.

This difference is one of the main reasons xLSTM is interesting as a long-context model. Instead of building interactions by comparing every token with every other token, xLSTM compresses the past into recurrent state. The paper argues that this gives xLSTM a better fit for settings where long sequences matter and memory must stay bounded. It is not that xLSTM eliminates computation; it changes where the cost is paid.

The paper’s experiments support this complexity story at a practical level. The authors report that xLSTM maintains low perplexities on longer contexts in their extrapolation experiments, while Transformer-based models show the familiar cost of handling long histories. So the asymptotic advantage is not only theoretical; it also connects to the observed behavior in long-context evaluation.

# Comparison with FlashAttention-style kernels and Mamba-style scans

The paper places mLSTM in the same broad hardware-aware conversation as FlashAttention-style kernels and the scan used in Mamba. These are all attempts to make sequence models faster by organizing computation around efficient GPU operations rather than naive sequential loops. The important point is that mLSTM’s matrix-based recurrence is compatible with this kind of acceleration because it can be reformulated in parallel.

At the same time, the paper is careful to distinguish theory from implementation quality. In the current codebase, the mLSTM kernels are not yet fully optimized, and the authors note that the implementation is about $4$ times slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba. That does not mean the design is inherently slower; it means the present kernels leave room for improvement. The paper explicitly suggests that faster kernels could be obtained in the spirit of FlashAttention.

sLSTM is different again. Because it retains memory mixing, it does not admit the same full parallelization as mLSTM. The authors still report a fast CUDA implementation with GPU memory optimizations down to the register level, and they say it is typically less than two times slower than their parallel mLSTM implementation. So the real-world comparison is shaped by both algorithmic structure and kernel engineering.

# Why xLSTM is attractive for long-context and edge deployment settings

The strongest deployment argument in the paper is the combination of linear compute and constant memory with respect to sequence length. For long-context settings, this means the cost of processing a longer sequence rises in a controlled way without forcing memory to grow with the entire history. That makes xLSTM especially attractive when the input stream is long and unpredictable.

The paper also emphasizes that the memory is compressive. Instead of storing all past tokens explicitly, xLSTM compresses them into a recurrent state. This is valuable not only for long context, but also for deployment environments where memory is limited. The authors explicitly say this makes xLSTM well suited for industrial applications and edge implementations. In those settings, bounded memory usage is often just as important as raw predictive accuracy.

The experimental results reinforce this motivation. In the long-context experiments, xLSTM maintains strong behavior on longer sequences, and in generation throughput tests it benefits from constant memory by supporting larger batch sizes. Those properties are exactly what one would want in a model meant to scale to demanding deployment settings.

# Practical speed differences between sLSTM and mLSTM

In practice, sLSTM and mLSTM differ not only in theory but also in how they run on hardware. sLSTM keeps the sequential dependence created by memory mixing, so it cannot be made fully parallel across time. That makes it less amenable to the most aggressive forms of sequence acceleration. mLSTM, by removing memory mixing, is the variant that can be parallelized more directly.

The paper reports that their fast CUDA implementation makes sLSTM typically less than two times slower than mLSTM. At the same time, the current mLSTM kernels are still about $4$ times slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba, because the kernels are not yet optimized. This is an important lesson: theoretical parallelism does not automatically translate into best-in-class runtime. The quality of the kernel matters.

So the practical picture is nuanced. mLSTM is the more parallel design, but sLSTM can still be engineered efficiently despite its sequential dependency. The difference between them is not just speed, but how their internal structure interacts with hardware. mLSTM is the better fit when parallelization is the priority, while sLSTM preserves memory mixing at the cost of sequential computation.

From Cells to Networks: xLSTM Blocks and Deep Residual Architectures

# Why Modern Language Models Rely on Residual Stacking

The paper does not present sLSTM and mLSTM as isolated recurrent cells that are used one at a time. Instead, it places them inside residual blocks and then stacks those blocks into full architectures. This matters because the paper’s goal is not just to build a better memory cell, but to build a language model that can be scaled to the size of modern large models. Residual stacking is the organizing principle that makes that possible: the model is assembled from repeated building blocks, each of which contributes a refinement to the representation.

This design choice follows the same pattern that made deep convolutional networks and Transformers successful. In the paper’s terms, xLSTM architectures are built by residually stacking xLSTM blocks. That means the unit of design is not just the memory mechanism itself, but the memory mechanism plus the surrounding block structure that makes it fit into a deep network. The result is a model family that can grow in depth while keeping the recurrent memory ideas at its core.

# Pre-LayerNorm Residual Backbones

The paper states that xLSTM uses the most commonly used pre-LayerNorm residual backbones, as in contemporary large language models. In other words, xLSTM does not introduce a completely new deep-network scaffold. Instead, it adopts the standard backbone style that has become familiar in modern language modeling and then places its recurrent blocks inside that framework.

This matters because the backbone determines how the model is organized as a whole. The recurrent module is therefore not the entire network; it is one component inside a larger residual system. The paper’s architecture is built so that the xLSTM blocks can be stacked in the same way other modern language models are stacked. That is what allows the authors to talk about xLSTM not merely as an LSTM variant, but as a language-model architecture.

# Cover’s Theorem and the Value of Nonlinear Summaries in Higher Dimensions

A central architectural idea in the paper is that xLSTM blocks should non-linearly summarize the past in a high-dimensional space so that different histories or contexts become easier to separate. This is where Cover’s theorem comes in. The theorem, as used in the paper, says that non-linearly embedded patterns are more likely to become linearly separable when they are lifted into a higher-dimensional space than when they remain in the original space.

The paper uses this idea as a motivation for block design in language modeling. If a model must predict the next token, it has to distinguish between different past histories that may look similar at first glance but imply different futures. The block should therefore produce a representation in which those histories are better separated. In this sense, the higher-dimensional projection is not just an implementation detail. It is part of the mechanism by which xLSTM tries to make sequence history easier to use for prediction.

# Post Up-Projection Residual Blocks for sLSTM

For sLSTM, the paper uses a residual block with post up-projection. The order here is important. The paper describes this block as one that non-linearly summarizes the past in the original space, then linearly maps into a high-dimensional space, applies a non-linear activation function, and then linearly maps back to the original space. This is the block style the paper associates with Transformers, and it is the one used for sLSTM.

This choice fits the role of sLSTM within xLSTM. The sLSTM is the version with scalar memory, a scalar update, and memory mixing. It keeps the LSTM idea of storing and revising information over time, while the surrounding residual block gives that memory a place inside a modern deep architecture. The paper also notes that sLSTM can use multiple memory cells, and can have multiple heads without memory mixing across heads, but with memory mixing across cells within each head. Together with exponential gating, this establishes a new way of memory mixing.

# Pre Up-Projection Residual Blocks for mLSTM

The mLSTM uses the other block design: pre up-projection. Here the paper’s ordering is reversed. The input is first linearly mapped to a higher-dimensional space, then the model non-linearly summarizes the past in that higher-dimensional space, and then it linearly maps back to the original space. The paper describes this as the block style used for state space models.

The reason given in the paper is straightforward: for mLSTM, the memory capacity becomes larger in the high-dimensional space. That fits the broader idea behind mLSTM itself, which replaces the scalar cell state with a matrix memory and uses a covariance-style update rule. Since the memory is already richer than in the scalar case, the architecture benefits from doing the summarization after the expansion to a larger internal space. The result is a block that matches the matrix memory’s greater storage capacity with a correspondingly larger working space.

# Why the Paper Pairs sLSTM with Post Up-Projection and mLSTM with Pre Up-Projection

The pairing is not arbitrary. The paper deliberately assigns post up-projection to sLSTM and pre up-projection to mLSTM. sLSTM is the variant that preserves memory mixing, so it is embedded in a Transformer-like residual block that first processes information in the original space and then expands it. mLSTM, by contrast, is designed for larger memory capacity and parallelization, so it is placed in the block structure that first expands the representation and then applies the recurrent summary there.

The paper also emphasizes an important asymmetry between the two variants. For mLSTM, multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent because there is no memory mixing. For sLSTM, heads and cells behave differently: memory mixing can occur across cells within each head, but not across heads. This makes the two variants structurally distinct, and the block designs reflect that distinction. In short, the paper pairs the block order with the kind of memory each variant is meant to support.

# Combining Recurrent Memory Modules with Feedforward Transformations

One of the most important architectural ideas in the paper is that recurrent memory is not used by itself. Instead, it is combined with linear projections that move information between the model dimension and a higher-dimensional internal space. The block therefore mixes temporal storage with feedforward transformation. This is how the paper turns a memory cell into a modern deep-learning building block.

This combination matters because it allows xLSTM to behave like a residual module rather than like a standalone recurrent network. The recurrent core keeps track of sequence history, while the surrounding projections and non-linear transformation give the block a way to reshape that history into a form that can be stacked and reused. The paper’s design goal is not simply to remember more, but to remember in a way that supports separation of contexts and next-token prediction.

# Constructing Full xLSTM Architectures by Stacking Blocks

An xLSTM architecture is built by stacking xLSTM blocks residually. This is the point where the paper moves from a single recurrent module to a full language model. The architecture is therefore modular: one chooses the kind of block, places it inside a residual backbone, and then repeats that block many times. The paper explicitly frames xLSTM architectures as the result of residually stacking the new LSTM variants.

This makes the model family flexible. Different networks can contain different mixtures of sLSTM-based and mLSTM-based blocks, while still sharing the same overall backbone style. The paper uses this modularity to assemble models at language-model scale. In other words, the architecture is not a one-off design; it is a template for building larger systems from the same kinds of parts.

# Interpreting xLSTM[$a:b$] Ratios of mLSTM-Based to sLSTM-Based Blocks

To describe how many blocks of each type appear in a model, the paper uses the notation xLSTM[$a:b$], where $a/b$ is the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. This notation is useful because it gives a compact description of the architecture’s composition at scale. It tells you not just how many blocks there are, but how the two memory mechanisms are distributed throughout the network.

The paper gives a concrete example: xLSTM[$7:1$] means that out of eight blocks, seven are mLSTM-based and one is sLSTM-based. If the total block number is $48$, that corresponds to $42$ mLSTM-based blocks and $6$ sLSTM-based blocks. The notation xLSTM[$1:0$] would mean an all-mLSTM model, while xLSTM[$0:1$] would mean an all-sLSTM model. This ratio notation becomes especially important when the authors compare different mixtures in their experiments.

# Design Patterns for Scaling xLSTM to Language-Model Size

The paper’s scaling strategy is to keep the basic architecture fixed in spirit while increasing its size through residual stacking and block composition. The same pre-LayerNorm residual backbone is used as the scaffold, and the same distinction between post up-projection for sLSTM and pre up-projection for mLSTM is maintained across experiments. The model can then be scaled by changing the number of blocks and by adjusting the ratio of mLSTM-based to sLSTM-based blocks.

This modular design is what makes xLSTM suitable for language-model scale. The paper emphasizes that xLSTM networks have linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length, unlike Transformers. That makes the architecture attractive not only for scaling depth and parameter count, but also for handling long contexts. In the paper’s experiments, this same design is used to build models of different sizes, showing how the architecture can be expanded into full language models without changing its basic structure.

xLSTM in the Landscape of Modern Sequence Models

# Linear attention methods and their goal of avoiding quadratic attention cost

A useful starting point for understanding xLSTM is to look at the pressure that shaped many recent sequence models: the cost of standard Transformer self-attention grows quadratically with context length. That makes long sequences expensive to process, especially when the goal is large-scale language modeling. Several families of methods were therefore proposed to preserve the usefulness of attention-like sequence modeling while reducing this cost.

The paper places methods such as the Synthesizer, Linformer, Linear Transformer, Performer, Structured Global Convolution, and the Hyena hierarchy in this category. These approaches do not all do the same thing, but they share a common aim. Some, like the Synthesizer, learn synthetic attention weights without direct token-to-token interactions. Others, like Linformer, use a low-rank representation to approximate attention. Linear Transformer rewrites attention in a form that scales linearly, Performer approximates the softmax with positive orthogonal random features, and SGConv and Hyena replace attention with fast long convolutions. The unifying intuition is that the model should still be able to process long sequences, but without paying the full quadratic cost of comparing every token to every other token.

xLSTM is motivated by the same efficiency problem, but it answers it in a different way. Rather than approximating attention, it returns to recurrence and asks how far an LSTM can be scaled if its memory and gating are redesigned. In the paper’s framing, xLSTM retains the long-sequence efficiency goal that motivated linear attention methods, while pursuing it through recurrent memory cells, exponential gating, and a residual-block architecture.

# State Space Models such as S4, S5, H3, and Mamba

State Space Models became prominent for much the same reason as linear attention methods: they are linear in context length and therefore avoid the quadratic scaling of standard attention. In the paper, the sequence-modeling landscape includes S4, DSS, GSS, S5, BiGS, H3, and Mamba. These models attracted attention because they offer a different route to long-context processing, one based on structured state evolution rather than pairwise token comparisons.

The broad intuition behind this family is that the model carries an internal state forward through the sequence, updating it at each step so that it summarizes the past in a compact form. This makes them attractive when one wants long-context capability without the cost of full attention. In the paper’s terminology, they are one of the main modern alternatives to Transformers, and they have shown promising performance in language modeling.

xLSTM belongs to the same broader conversation, but it does not simply adopt the state-space approach. Instead, it preserves the basic LSTM idea of gated memory while modifying the memory structure itself. The paper’s mLSTM is fully parallelizable and uses a matrix memory, while the sLSTM keeps recurrent memory mixing and uses exponential gating. This is why xLSTM can be read as a recurrent family that is competing directly with state-space sequence models, not as a minor variant of them. The paper’s experiments are designed to test that claim by comparing xLSTM favorably with Mamba, H3, and related models on both synthetic and large-scale language tasks.

# Recent recurrent alternatives such as RWKV, HGRN, and HGRN2

Recurrent neural networks never disappeared from sequence modeling; rather, they were revisited in new forms once it became clear that recurrence naturally scales linearly in context length. The paper identifies RWKV, HGRN, HGRN2, and related models as part of this newer recurrent line. It also mentions Deep Linear Recurrent Units as another example of recurrence regaining relevance for language modeling.

RWKV is especially notable in the paper because it is presented as a well-known recurrent approach to large language modeling with competitive performance relative to Transformers. HGRN and HGRN2 likewise represent attempts to modernize recurrence with gates and architectural choices that fit current language-modeling practice. The common motivation here is not just efficiency, but also the hope that recurrence can retain expressive sequential structure while remaining trainable at scale.

xLSTM sits close to these methods in spirit, but it tries to push the recurrent idea further. The paper’s central question is whether scaling LSTMs to billions of parameters, while using modern training and architecture techniques, can recover strong language-modeling performance. In the experiments, xLSTM is compared directly to RWKV variants and HGRN2, and it performs strongly enough that the authors argue it is competitive with the best current recurrent alternatives. What distinguishes xLSTM is not simply that it is recurrent, but that it revises the LSTM memory design itself.

# Gating as a recurring idea across modern sequence models

Gating is one of the most important ideas inherited from the original LSTM. In the classical LSTM, the input gate controls how much new information is written, the forget gate controls how much of the previous cell state is retained, and the output gate controls how much of the memory is exposed as the hidden state. This mechanism was introduced to address vanishing gradients and to make memory updates additive rather than purely multiplicative.

The paper emphasizes that gating has reappeared in many later sequence models. It explicitly points to HGRN, HGRN2, GLA, GSS, BiGS, MEGA, RWKV, and Mamba as examples where gating plays a central role again. This recurrence is not accidental. Gating gives a model the ability to regulate information flow, which is crucial when sequence data contains both relevant and irrelevant signals and the model must decide, step by step, what to keep.

xLSTM extends this theme by using exponential gating. In the sLSTM and mLSTM variants, input and forget gates can be exponential rather than purely sigmoid. The paper stresses that exponentials can become numerically unstable, so xLSTM introduces a stabilizer state $m_t$ and stabilized gates that preserve the output of the network and the derivatives of the loss. This matters because it shows that xLSTM is not merely replacing one activation by another; it is redesigning gating so that the model can revise storage decisions more flexibly while remaining numerically sound. The paper’s ablation studies also support the importance of gating: making each gate learnable and dependent on the input gives an incremental benefit.

# Covariance-style memory updates across different model families

One of the most distinctive changes in xLSTM is the move from a scalar memory to a matrix memory in mLSTM. Instead of storing only a single value at each step, the model stores associations between a key vector $\mathbf{k}_t$ and a value vector $\mathbf{v}_t$ in a matrix $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. The update rule is

$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$,

which is a covariance-style outer-product update. The paper interprets this as a fast associative memory: the key-value pair is written into the matrix, and later a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$ can retrieve the stored information through matrix multiplication.

This idea is not unique to xLSTM. The paper connects it to Fast Weight Programmers and to newer methods such as RWKV-5, RWKV-6, Retention, Linear Transformer, and HGRN2. The shared intuition is that the model should accumulate a memory of past associations using an update that looks like a weighted outer product. In xLSTM, the forget gate acts like a decay rate, the input gate acts like a learning rate, and the output gate scales the retrieved result. That mapping makes the relation to the broader literature especially clear: these models all use some variant of gated accumulation to build a fast memory over time.

The paper also explains why this can work well. It notes that the covariance update rule is optimal for maximal separability of retrieved binary vectors, which is equivalent to maximizing signal-to-noise ratio. In other words, this type of update is a principled way to store associations that can later be recovered by a query. xLSTM’s contribution is to integrate that idea into an LSTM framework with gates, normalization, and a memory state that can be used inside a modern residual architecture.

# Why Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2 are especially close to xLSTM

Among the models discussed in the paper, Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2 are described as the closest to xLSTM conceptually. The reason is that they already share some of xLSTM’s central ingredients: matrix-like memory accumulation, gating, or both. They also live in the same design space of efficient sequence models that try to replace the quadratic cost of attention with some form of recurrent or recurrent-like state.

At the same time, the paper highlights a crucial difference. These closer models do not allow memory mixing in the way sLSTM does. That absence matters because memory mixing is what lets information from one hidden state influence later memory updates in a richer way than simple accumulation. The authors argue that this is precisely why xLSTM is more expressive in the settings they care about. In the paper’s view, memory mixing is the missing ingredient that helps solve state-tracking problems.

The distinction is especially clear in the mLSTM and sLSTM split. mLSTM is fully parallelizable and uses a matrix memory, while sLSTM keeps sequential memory mixing. Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2 are close because they resemble one side or the other of that design, but xLSTM combines the ideas with explicit LSTM-style memory control and, in the sLSTM case, genuine memory mixing across cells. That combination is what makes xLSTM more than a simple rewrite of existing efficient recurrent models.

# The distinctive role of memory mixing in sLSTM

Memory mixing is the mechanism that makes the sLSTM different from approaches that merely accumulate a running memory. In the original LSTM, the cell state is updated additively, but the update itself depends on the previous hidden state through recurrent weights. The paper reminds us that later formulations even combine multiple scalar memory cells into a vector and use recurrent weight matrices to mix the outputs of those cells. In xLSTM, that general idea is carried forward and adapted to the new architecture.

The paper’s sLSTM can have multiple memory cells and multiple heads. Within each head, memory mixing occurs across cells, but not across heads. This is a new design choice: the introduction of heads together with exponential gating creates a different way of mixing memory than in standard LSTMs. Because the hidden state influences the gates and cell input at the next step, the model can revise what it has stored rather than only deciding whether to keep or forget a value once and for all.

This is also why sLSTM is not parallelizable. The hidden-hidden connections force the computation to proceed sequentially, step by step. The paper treats that as a cost, but also as a feature, because it is the mechanism that helps the model solve tasks requiring state tracking. In the nearest-neighbor search example discussed in the paper, a standard LSTM struggles to revise a stored value when a more similar vector is encountered later, whereas xLSTM can do better because its exponential gating makes revision easier. So memory mixing is not just a structural detail; it is the source of a qualitatively different kind of sequence processing.

# Expressivity arguments about state tracking in RNNs versus Transformers and SSMs

A central claim in the paper is that memory mixing makes LSTMs more expressive than State Space Models and Transformers for certain kinds of sequence reasoning. The paper cites prior work arguing that memory mixing enables state tracking, and that state tracking is required for tasks such as evaluating code or following entities through a long narrative. Put simply, state tracking means that the model must maintain an internal abstract state that updates in response to each new input symbol.

The paper’s formal-language experiments are meant to make this point concrete. It reports that models without memory mixing, including Transformers and State Space Models, cannot solve some regular grammars such as the parity task. This is presented as evidence that simply having long-context efficiency is not enough. A model must also be able to revise and coordinate its state in the right way. That is where recurrent memory mixing matters.

xLSTM is designed to address this expressivity gap. Its exponential gating and modified memory structures are intended to make it easier to update storage decisions and to keep track of evolving state over long sequences. In the paper’s experiments, this pays off on formal languages, associative recall, and longer-context evaluations. The broader lesson is that efficiency and expressivity are separate problems: many modern models have made progress on the first, but xLSTM is trying to improve both at once.

# When compressive memory is an advantage and when it is a limitation

xLSTM’s memory is compressive, and the paper treats that as both a strength and a tradeoff. On the positive side, xLSTM networks have linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length. That means they do not need to store an ever-growing cache of past tokens, which makes them attractive for industrial settings and for edge deployment. The paper also reports favorable generation-time scaling and high throughput, precisely because memory use does not grow with the context length in the same way it does for a Transformer.

At the same time, compression can limit what the model can keep. This is the problem that the paper illustrates with rare-token prediction: the original scalar LSTM compresses information into scalar cell states, and that limited storage capacity hurts performance on rare tokens. mLSTM addresses this by using a matrix memory, which gives much greater storage capacity. The paper’s Multi-Query Associative Recall results are meant to show that this richer memory improves retrieval on more difficult key-value tasks, and that the sLSTM block does not reduce capacity but can actually help leverage it.

There is still a tradeoff. The matrix memory of mLSTM requires $d \times d$ storage and computation, which is more expensive than a scalar state. The paper argues that this cost is acceptable because the update and retrieval can be parallelized with standard matrix operations, so the wall-clock overhead remains manageable. It also notes a possible limitation: because the memory is independent of sequence length, very long contexts could eventually overload it, although the reported experiments did not find this to be a problem up to contexts of $16{,}384$. In this sense, xLSTM shows both sides of compressive memory very clearly. It is powerful because it is compact, but it must be designed carefully so that compactness does not become a bottleneck.

Capability-Focused Experiments: Formal Languages, Associative Recall, and Long Range Arena

# Why synthetic tasks are useful for understanding sequence model behavior

The paper uses synthetic and diagnostic experiments because they let us isolate a single capability at a time. That is much harder to do on standard language modeling benchmarks, where many factors are entangled at once: storing information, revising earlier decisions, keeping track of an internal state, and coping with long contexts. By designing tasks whose demands are known in advance, the authors can ask whether a model succeeds because it has the right mechanism, rather than because it merely fits patterns in a large training set.

This is especially important for xLSTM because the paper proposes two distinct design changes: exponential gating with stabilization and a modified memory structure. The experiments are chosen to test these ideas separately. Formal language tasks probe whether the model can track state and revise what it stores. Associative recall probes how much information the model can store and retrieve. Long Range Arena tests whether the model can process long sequences reliably. Together, these benchmarks form a capability-focused lens on the architecture.

The key advantage of such benchmarks is that they make model behavior interpretable. If a model fails on a formal language task, the failure tells us something about state tracking and memory mixing. If it fails on associative recall, the failure points to limited storage capacity. If it struggles on long sequences, the issue is context handling. In the paper, these tests are not presented as replacements for language modeling, but as diagnostic tools that explain why the architectural changes matter.

# Formal language tasks and the need for state tracking

The paper first studies formal language tasks, which are designed to test whether a model can keep track of a latent state as it reads a sequence. The authors extend the tasks from Deletang et al. so that they support multi-length training and therefore allow length extrapolation. This is important because a model that merely memorizes training lengths may appear competent while failing on longer sequences. By training on multiple lengths and testing beyond them, the experiment checks whether the model has learned the underlying rule rather than a fixed pattern.

The paper frames these tasks in terms of state tracking. Here, the model must maintain an internal representation that summarizes the sequence prefix in a way that remains useful for predicting the relevant output at the end. That summary is not just a bag of past tokens; it must be updated as new symbols arrive. For this reason, formal language tasks are a natural fit for recurrent architectures, especially those that can revise their internal state. The paper uses them to ask whether xLSTM can go beyond the limitations of standard LSTMs and alternative sequence models.

The authors evaluate accuracy only on the tokens relevant to the task, and they scale that accuracy between $0$ and $1$, where $0$ corresponds to random performance and $1$ to perfect performance. This choice makes the evaluation focused and task-specific. It measures whether the model can solve the grammatical problem, not whether it can exploit irrelevant cues elsewhere in the sequence.

# Parity and related tasks as tests of memory mixing

Among the formal language tasks, the paper highlights parity as a revealing example. Parity is a regular grammar task, and it exposes whether a model can carry forward the right internal state over time. The paper uses it to illustrate a central limitation of models without memory mixing: they cannot solve certain regular grammars because they cannot perform the necessary state tracking.

Memory mixing here refers to the hidden-hidden connections that carry information from one time step to the next. In standard LSTM-style recurrence, such mixing allows the current state to depend on the previous hidden state, which in turn can summarize everything seen so far. This is exactly what a parity-like task needs. As the sequence grows, the model must repeatedly update an internal state according to the incoming symbols. If the architecture cannot revise that state properly, it will fail even on conceptually simple rules.

The paper’s main point is that xLSTM addresses this issue through exponential gating combined with memory mixing. On the formal language tasks, that change matters. Models such as Transformers or State Space Models without memory mixing are reported as unable to solve these tasks, including parity, which the authors connect to prior findings that such models are fundamentally less powerful than recurrent models for certain state-tracking problems. In this sense, the parity experiment is not just a benchmark result; it is evidence that the model can preserve and update the right kind of recurrent state.

# Comparing sLSTM, mLSTM, mixed xLSTM, Transformers, SSMs, and RNN baselines

The paper compares several two-block architectures on the formal language tasks: xLSTM with only sLSTM blocks, written as xLSTM[0:1]; xLSTM with only mLSTM blocks, written as xLSTM[1:0]; mixed xLSTM, written as xLSTM[1:1]; and a range of baselines including Llama, Mamba, RWKV, Retention, Hyena, LSTM, and LSTM placed in Transformer blocks. This comparison is designed to separate the contributions of the two xLSTM variants from the behavior of other sequence modeling families.

The experimental setup is especially useful because it keeps the models small and comparable, using two-block architectures. That makes the results easier to interpret. If a model succeeds in this setting, the success is tied more directly to its recurrence and memory mechanism than to sheer scale. In the paper’s framing, this is a way to test whether the new xLSTM components really address the known LSTM limitations.

The results show that the models without memory mixing fail on tasks that require state tracking. That includes Transformers and State Space Models in the paper’s discussion of formal grammars. By contrast, xLSTM variants, especially those with the new gating and recurrent structure, are able to solve the task family much better. The experiment therefore separates models that can merely process sequences from those that can maintain the specific internal state needed for formal language recognition.

# What the formal language results say about expressivity

The formal language results are used to argue that xLSTM is more expressive in the relevant sense than models that lack memory mixing. The paper ties this to the broader claim that memory mixing enables state tracking, and that state tracking is required for solving tasks like parity and related regular grammars. When a model can maintain and revise its internal state correctly, it can represent distinctions that are invisible to architectures without such recurrence.

This matters because the paper is not simply claiming that xLSTM is another fast sequence model. It is making a stronger claim about capability. The formal language experiments indicate that the new gating and memory structure give the model access to behaviors that standard Transformers and State Space Models cannot reproduce in these settings. In that sense, the benchmark is a direct test of expressivity under sequence constraints.

The paper also positions the result within a larger theoretical perspective. It cites earlier findings that recurrent models can be more powerful than Transformers and State Space Models for state tracking tasks. The xLSTM results align with that view, while showing that the specific architectural changes introduced in the paper are enough to make the model competitive on these diagnostic problems. The takeaway is that expressivity here is not abstract; it is tied to whether the architecture can carry the correct latent state through time.

# Multi-Query Associative Recall as a controlled memory-capacity benchmark

The second major diagnostic task is Multi-Query Associative Recall. This benchmark is intended to measure memory capacity in a controlled setting. Each sequence contains randomly chosen key-value pairs drawn from a large vocabulary, and the model must memorize them for later retrieval. Because the keys and values are explicitly paired, the task tests whether the model can store associations, not whether it can infer them from language statistics.

The paper uses this benchmark to isolate the effect of the new matrix memory in mLSTM. Unlike the scalar memory used in the original LSTM, the matrix memory is designed to store richer associations. The retrieval operation uses a query vector to recover the stored value, which makes the task conceptually close to associative memory. In this way, the benchmark directly probes whether the architecture can support a larger and more structured storage mechanism.

Because the task is carefully controlled, performance can be interpreted as a genuine measure of memory capacity. A model that succeeds is not merely good at language modeling; it is capable of preserving multiple associations across a sequence and retrieving them correctly when requested. This is why the paper treats Multi-Query Associative Recall as the right diagnostic for the matrix memory component of xLSTM.

# Increasing the number of key-value pairs and context length to stress memory

To make the benchmark more demanding, the paper increases the number of key-value pairs up to $256$ and extends the context length up to $2048$. This is a deliberate stress test. If a model’s memory is too limited, it will either lose track of older pairs or confuse one association with another as the sequence grows longer and denser.

By increasing both the number of pairs and the context length, the authors make the benchmark probe two related aspects of memory: how much can be stored, and how robustly can it be retained over a longer sequence. This is important because a model might appear to work on small versions of the task but collapse when the number of associations grows. The paper’s expanded setting is meant to prevent such false confidence.

The harder configurations are especially informative because they show whether the model degrades gracefully or fails abruptly. In the paper, the strongest claims about xLSTM come from these more difficult settings, where the differences between architectures become clearer. The hardest task, with $256$ key-value pairs, is particularly revealing in how the different memory mechanisms behave.

# Why Transformers remain a gold standard on associative storage

The paper explicitly notes that Transformers are the gold standard on associative recall. The reason given is that their memory is exponential in the coding dimension. In the context of this benchmark, that gives them an advantage for storing and retrieving associations. The comparison is therefore not merely between xLSTM and a generic baseline, but between xLSTM and a model class with a particularly strong storage mechanism for this kind of task.

This makes the benchmark valuable as a reference point. If xLSTM can approach or outperform other non-Transformer models on associative recall, then its matrix memory is doing meaningful work. But if a Transformer remains best overall, that does not undermine the benchmark; it simply sets a strong upper reference for what associative storage can look like in this setting.

The paper’s framing is careful here. It does not claim that xLSTM is universally superior on all memory tasks. Instead, it asks how much of Transformer-like retrieval behavior can be reproduced with recurrent machinery and a matrix memory. That is why the Transformer baseline matters so much: it establishes the standard against which the new memory design is measured.

# How xLSTM[1:1] and xLSTM[1:0] perform on associative recall

On Multi-Query Associative Recall, xLSTM[1:1] performs best among all non-Transformer models, including in small models. This is an important result because it shows that the mixed architecture is not just helpful for state tracking; it also supports strong associative storage. The paper further notes that the sLSTM block does not diminish memory capacity, but rather leverages it, which becomes especially clear on the hardest setting with $256$ key-value pairs.

This is a subtle but important point. One might expect that adding an sLSTM component, with its memory mixing, could interfere with pure storage capacity. The experiment suggests the opposite: the mixed design can preserve or even improve the usefulness of memory. In other words, the sLSTM block does not simply compete with the matrix memory; it helps the system use it more effectively.

The paper also reports extrapolation analyses showing that xLSTM’s enhanced memory capacities allow it to generalize to contexts longer than those seen during training. That makes the associative recall result more than a memorization benchmark. It suggests that the architecture is not just fitting a fixed training regime, but can support retrieval under longer and more demanding conditions. In this sense, xLSTM[1:1] and xLSTM[1:0] help demonstrate that the two memory designs complement one another.

# Long Range Arena as a benchmark for long-sequence processing

The third diagnostic setting is Long Range Arena. The paper uses it to assess how well xLSTM handles long sequences and large contexts. Unlike the associative recall benchmark, which isolates memory storage, Long Range Arena is broader: it tests whether a model can process long inputs across several tasks and maintain performance as the sequence length grows.

The reason this benchmark matters is that long-context processing is where many sequence models struggle. A model may perform well on short or medium sequences but become unreliable as the context length increases. The paper treats Long Range Arena as a direct test of xLSTM’s ability to remain effective in such conditions. It is therefore a bridge between the controlled synthetic tasks and the more realistic language modeling experiments later in the paper.

The reported result is that xLSTM demonstrates consistent strong performance across the tasks. The paper interprets this as evidence that the architecture is remarkably efficient in handling different aspects of long-context problems. That wording is important: it suggests not just raw accuracy on one task, but robustness across several kinds of long-sequence processing.

# Interpreting the synthetic results as evidence for the two xLSTM design choices

Taken together, the synthetic experiments provide a clear picture of why xLSTM was designed the way it was. The formal language tasks show why exponential gating and memory mixing matter: they allow the model to revise storage decisions and track state over time. The associative recall task shows why the matrix memory matters: it increases storage capacity and supports retrieval of key-value associations. Long Range Arena then suggests that these abilities remain useful across a wider variety of long-context problems.

The experiments therefore separate the two main design ideas and test them in different ways. Exponential gating with memory mixing is validated by tasks that require state tracking and revisable recurrence. The matrix memory is validated by tasks that require storing and retrieving many associations. When both mechanisms are combined, the mixed xLSTM architecture performs especially well among non-Transformer models.

What makes this set of results compelling is that each benchmark addresses a different limitation of the original LSTM. The paper’s broader argument is that xLSTM overcomes the inability to revise storage decisions, the limited storage capacity of scalar memory, and the lack of parallelizability in the original recurrent form. The synthetic experiments do not prove the entire argument on their own, but they provide targeted evidence that the two new design choices do exactly what they were intended to do.

Language Modeling at 15B Tokens: Comparisons and Ablations

# The SlimPajama dataset and autoregressive training setup

The first large-scale language-modeling study in the paper is built around a simple but demanding setting: all models are trained on $15$B tokens drawn from SlimPajama and are optimized in the same autoregressive next-token prediction regime. This matters because autoregressive language modeling forces a model to predict each token from the tokens that came before it, which is exactly the setting in which sequence models are compared in the paper. By holding the data source and training objective fixed, the authors can focus on the question that motivates the work: what happens when LSTM-style models are scaled up and modernized so that they can compete with current large language model families?

SlimPajama serves here as the common training corpus for a wide range of architectures. The comparison is not about whether one model has access to more data than another, but about what each architecture can extract from the same amount of training signal. That makes the results especially informative: if a model performs well in this setting, the improvement is tied to its sequence-processing design rather than to differences in training data. The paper uses this setup both for direct method comparison and for ablations, so the experimental design is deliberately controlled.

Because the task is next-token prediction, the models are evaluated in the same autoregressive manner during training and validation. This is the natural setting for language modeling, where a good model should assign high probability to the true next token given the context seen so far. The study therefore asks not just whether a model can fit the training distribution, but whether its architecture helps it generalize as a language model.

# Why validation perplexity is the main evaluation metric

The central metric in this comparison is validation perplexity. In the paper, models are trained on SlimPajama and then evaluated on held-out validation text, and the comparison is made through perplexity because it directly reflects next-token prediction quality. Lower perplexity means the model is assigning better probabilities to the correct next tokens, so it is the natural summary statistic for an autoregressive language model.

This choice is especially appropriate in a study like this one because the paper is not evaluating a single downstream task with a narrow success criterion. Instead, it is comparing general language-modeling ability across several architectures. Validation perplexity gives a compact view of how well the model has learned the token distribution in the held-out data. It is sensitive to the model’s overall predictive confidence, which makes it useful when comparing architectures that may differ substantially in how they store, update, and retrieve information.

The paper also uses perplexity as a way to study scaling. If a model’s perplexity decreases systematically as size increases, that suggests the architecture is scaling in a stable and predictable way. In the 15B-token study, the validation perplexity results therefore serve two purposes at once: they identify the strongest model among the compared methods, and they provide evidence about whether that model family is likely to remain competitive at larger scale.

# Model classes compared: Transformers, SSMs, RNNs, and linear Transformers

The comparison set spans the major sequence-model families that have been important in modern language modeling. On one side are Transformers, represented by GPT-3 and Llama. On another side are State Space Models, represented by H3 and Mamba. The paper also includes recurrent architectures, represented by RWKV-4, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, HGRN, and HGRN2. Finally, it includes methods that the paper groups with linear Transformers, such as GLA, RetNet, and Hyena.

This grouping is important because the paper is comparing different answers to the same scaling problem. Transformers use attention and have been the dominant architecture for large language models, but they are not naturally linear in sequence length. State Space Models and several recurrent or linear-attention-inspired methods try to preserve long-context modeling while improving efficiency. The paper positions xLSTM as another family in this same broad design space, but one that comes from extending LSTM principles rather than replacing recurrence entirely.

The role of the baselines is therefore not just to provide a leaderboard. They help locate xLSTM within the current landscape of sequence modeling. If xLSTM performs well against Transformers, SSMs, RNNs, and linear Transformer-style models under the same training budget, then the paper’s claim is that LSTM ideas can still matter at large scale, provided that the architecture is modified in the right way.

# The 350M-scale comparison setting

To keep the comparison controlled, the paper matches the models to a GPT-3-size configuration of about $350$M parameters. This corresponds to an embedding dimension of $1024$ and $24$ residual blocks. The same overall model scale is used across the comparison so that architectural differences, rather than raw size differences, drive the results. This is the appropriate design if the goal is to understand the effect of the xLSTM modifications themselves.

The paper notes one subtle but important detail: only GPT-3 uses shared weights for token and output embeddings, so GPT-3 has fewer parameters than the others in practice. That means the comparison is not merely a count of parameter totals; it is a structured evaluation at a common architectural scale. This is the scale at which the paper reports that xLSTM outperforms all existing methods in validation perplexity.

The comparison also uses mixed-precision training, with a note that for RWKV-5, RWKV-6, GLA, and HGRN2 the mixed-precision setup did not rely on PyTorch automatic mixed precision. The paper includes this detail because large-scale training is sensitive to implementation choices, and the authors want to be transparent about how the competing methods were trained. Even so, the main takeaway at this scale is simple: xLSTM is the strongest method in the comparison set on validation perplexity.

# Performance of xLSTM versus GPT-style Transformers, Mamba, RWKV, HGRN2, RetNet, and Hyena

At the heart of the 15B-token study is the result that xLSTM outperforms all of the compared methods in validation perplexity. This includes GPT-style Transformers such as GPT-3 and Llama, SSMs such as H3 and Mamba, recurrent methods such as RWKV and HGRN/HGRN2, and linear Transformer-style methods such as GLA, RetNet, and Hyena. The result is significant because these baselines represent several of the strongest architectural alternatives available for language modeling at this scale.

The performance claim is not just that xLSTM is competitive, but that it leads the field within this experimental setup. Since all models are trained on the same SlimPajama data in the same autoregressive setting, the improvement cannot be attributed to a different corpus or objective. Instead, it reflects the effect of the xLSTM design changes: exponential gating, modified memory structure, and the residual block integration that makes large-scale training feasible.

The comparison also has an interpretive value. Transformers are the strongest and most widely used baseline class in modern language modeling, so outperforming them at this scale signals that xLSTM is not merely a nostalgic return to recurrence. Rather, it is a redesigned recurrent family that can hold its own against the dominant architecture family of the field. The same conclusion holds against SSMs and the other recurrent or linear methods in the comparison.

# Results for xLSTM[$1$:$0$] and xLSTM[$7$:$1$]

The paper evaluates xLSTM in more than one composition. The notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$] refers to the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. Thus xLSTM[$1$:$0$] is the pure mLSTM variant, while xLSTM[$7$:$1$] is a mixture dominated by mLSTM blocks with a smaller number of sLSTM blocks. These variants matter because they help disentangle which part of xLSTM contributes to which behavior.

In the 15B-token comparison, xLSTM as a family leads the validation perplexity results. The paper does not present the variants as interchangeable, however. They serve different roles in the architecture and can behave differently under different evaluation settings. That distinction becomes especially important later in the paper’s larger-scale experiments, but even in the 15B-token study the inclusion of both variants makes it possible to ask whether pure matrix-memory style recurrence or a hybrid of mLSTM and sLSTM offers the better tradeoff.

The broader result from the comparison is that xLSTM is not a single trick. It is a family with multiple design points, and both the pure and hybrid versions are part of the architecture explored in the paper. The reported validation results show that the xLSTM family is strong as a whole, and the ablation study clarifies why.

# Scaling behavior at this training scale

The paper does not stop at a single perplexity table. It also examines scaling behavior at the $15$B-token training scale, in the spirit of earlier scaling-law studies. The purpose is to determine whether the performance advantage of xLSTM is stable as model size changes, or whether it is only a feature of one particular parameter setting.

The reported scaling curves suggest that xLSTM will continue to perform favorably at larger model sizes. In other words, the model’s advantage is not an isolated point on a graph. Instead, the scaling behavior indicates that the architecture is learning in a way that remains beneficial as capacity increases. This is important because the paper’s broader question is about scaling LSTMs to the level of modern large language models, and scaling behavior is the most direct way to test whether that ambition is plausible.

The scaling results also support the interpretation of the validation perplexity table. If a model is already strong at the $350$M-parameter scale and its trend with scale is favorable, then the result is more convincing than a one-off gain. The paper therefore treats the scaling analysis as evidence that xLSTM is not just a tuned medium-sized model, but a candidate architecture for larger language models.

# Ablating the path from vanilla LSTM to xLSTM

The ablation study is organized as a step-by-step transformation from a vanilla LSTM into xLSTM. This is a useful experimental strategy because it isolates the contribution of each architectural decision. Rather than presenting xLSTM as a monolithic design, the paper asks how performance changes when each modification is added in sequence.

The first step is to integrate LSTM layers into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones. The second step is to extend that setup into a post up-projection block. Only after these scaffolding changes are in place does the paper add the two main xLSTM innovations: exponential gating and matrix memory. This progression matters because it shows that the final architecture is built from both modern deep-learning infrastructure and new memory mechanisms.

The ablation results show that the improvements are cumulative. Moving a vanilla LSTM into a residual backbone is already part of making it comparable to contemporary large models. The up-projection block further adapts the architecture to the style of large-scale sequence modeling used in modern systems. But the biggest conceptual changes come from the new gating and memory design, which the paper identifies as the main sources of xLSTM’s gains.

# Adding residual backbones to LSTM layers

The first ablation step is architectural rather than algorithmic: LSTM layers are inserted into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones. This matters because large language models are typically built as stacks of residual blocks, not as isolated recurrent cells. In the paper, xLSTM is not treated as a standalone recurrent module but as a component that must fit into a modern deep residual architecture.

The point of this change is to bring LSTM into the same structural environment used by contemporary large models. A residual backbone makes it possible to stack many blocks while maintaining stable optimization. By itself, this does not yet create xLSTM, but it establishes the pathway from classical recurrent networks to a form that can be scaled like today’s large models.

The ablation indicates that this modernization step is helpful, but it is not the whole story. In other words, simply embedding LSTM in a residual stack is not enough to explain the final performance. The gains continue as the architecture is further refined, which suggests that the later xLSTM-specific choices are doing important additional work.

# Adding up-projection block structure

The next ablation step introduces the up-projection block structure. The paper distinguishes two styles of residual blocks: a post up-projection block, in which the model first summarizes information and then maps it into a higher-dimensional space, and a pre up-projection block, in which the model first maps into a higher-dimensional space and then performs the nonlinear summary there. The paper uses the post version for sLSTM and the pre version for mLSTM.

This distinction is tied to the idea that high-dimensional spaces can make different histories or contexts easier to separate. The paper invokes Cover’s theorem to motivate this intuition: when nonlinear patterns are embedded in a higher-dimensional space, they are more likely to become linearly separable. In language modeling, that matters because the model must distinguish among many possible histories before predicting the next token.

In the ablation path, the introduction of the up-projection block is an important intermediate step. It adapts the LSTM from a classic recurrent cell into a block that better matches the geometry of large language models. But, again, it is not the final explanation for the performance gains. It prepares the architecture for the stronger changes that follow.

# Adding exponential gating and matrix memory

The final and most important ablation step is the addition of exponential gating and matrix memory. According to the paper, these are the two principal modifications that define xLSTM beyond the modernized residual scaffolding. The ablation results attribute the strong performance improvement to both of them.

Exponential gating changes how storage decisions are made. In the xLSTM design, gates can use exponential activations, together with normalization and stabilization. This gives the model a way to revise storage decisions more flexibly, which the paper connects to the ability to handle tasks that require state tracking. The point is not just to turn memory on or off, but to let the gating mechanism support more refined control over what is retained.

Matrix memory, on the other hand, increases storage capacity. In mLSTM, the memory cell is upgraded from a scalar to a matrix, so the model can store and retrieve richer associative information. This directly addresses one of the original LSTM’s limitations: compressing too much information into scalar memory states. The ablation therefore shows that the gains come from two complementary directions. Exponential gating improves how the model decides what to keep, and matrix memory improves how much and what kind of information can be stored.

# Gate-design ablations and the importance of input-dependent learnable gates

The paper also ablates different gating mechanisms themselves. This part of the study is important because gating is one of the central ideas inherited from the original LSTM, and xLSTM extends it rather than discarding it. The ablation asks which form of gating best supports the new architecture.

The key conclusion is that having each gate be learnable and influenced by the input has an incremental positive effect. In other words, the gates should not be static or fixed; they should adapt based on the current input, because the input is what tells the model whether the present token should update memory, preserve memory, or influence output. This is consistent with the paper’s broader argument that gating is not merely a technical detail but a mechanism for controlling memory revision.

The gate ablations therefore reinforce the central thesis of the paper. xLSTM’s gains are not produced by one isolated innovation. They depend on a gate design that can respond to the current input, on a memory system that can store richer representations, and on a residual architecture that makes these ideas work at scale. The ablations show that each of these components contributes, but the strongest effect comes from the full combination.

# What the ablations reveal about the sources of xLSTM’s gains

Taken together, the ablations tell a coherent story about where xLSTM’s performance comes from. The residual backbone and up-projection changes are important because they place LSTM in the architectural form used by modern large models. They help make the model trainable and expressive in a deep residual stack. But the paper’s results make clear that these scaffolding changes are not the main source of the final gain.

The most important improvements come from the xLSTM-specific modifications: exponential gating and matrix memory. Exponential gating strengthens the model’s ability to revise storage decisions, while matrix memory increases storage capacity and enables richer associative retrieval. The gate-design ablations further show that learnable, input-dependent gates provide additional benefits. The paper’s conclusion from these studies is that xLSTM improves because it retains the useful memory-control ideas of LSTM while removing some of the limitations that made classical LSTMs less competitive at large scale.

So the ablation study does more than rank variants. It explains the mechanism behind the improvement. The results suggest that xLSTM’s gains are not simply a consequence of having more parameters or a different residual wrapper. Instead, they arise from a better memory system and a better gating system, both of which are integrated into a large-model backbone suited to modern language modeling.

xLSTM as a Large Language Model: 300B Tokens, Downstream Tasks, and Scaling Laws

# Training xLSTM at $125$M, $350$M, $760$M, and $1.3$B Parameters

The paper’s large-scale language modeling study is designed to answer a simple but ambitious question: if the LSTM family is modernized and scaled up using current large-language-model practices, how far can it go? To test this, the authors train xLSTM models at four sizes: $125$M, $350$M, $760$M, and $1.3$B parameters. The point of using several sizes is not merely to find one good model, but to see whether the same architectural ideas continue to work as capacity increases.

A key detail in the experimental setup is that the models are trained in an autoregressive language modeling regime on $300$B tokens from SlimPajama. This gives the study a scale that is comparable to other modern large-language-model experiments, rather than a small-scale proof of concept. The paper also uses a consistent context length of $2048$ during training, which matters later when the authors test whether the models can generalize to much longer sequences at inference time.

The experiments also distinguish between two xLSTM compositions. The notation xLSTM[$a:b$] means that the architecture uses a ratio of $a/b$ mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. In the large-scale runs, the paper compares xLSTM[$1:0$], which is only mLSTM, and xLSTM[$7:1$], which mixes mostly mLSTM blocks with a smaller number of sLSTM blocks. All experiments use pre up-projection blocks for mLSTM and post up-projection blocks for sLSTM. That design choice is part of what makes the reported results directly comparable across sizes.

# The $300$B-Token SlimPajama Training Regime and Baseline Selection

The training corpus is SlimPajama, and the large-scale setting uses $300$B tokens. This is important because the paper is not evaluating xLSTM in isolation; it is testing whether the model can compete under the same kind of scale that has made Transformers dominant in language modeling. In that sense, the $300$B-token regime is the paper’s most direct stress test.

For the large-scale comparison, the authors choose one representative model from each of the main competing families: Llama for Transformers, Mamba for State Space Models, and RWKV-4 for recurrent neural networks. This choice is deliberate. Rather than comparing xLSTM to every available baseline, the paper focuses on representative systems that stand for the major architectural directions in modern language modeling. That makes the results easier to interpret: if xLSTM does well here, it is not just beating one particular implementation, but the broader family it represents.

The selection of RWKV-4 is also tied to the practical training setup described in the paper. For the $300$B-token experiments, the authors note that reasonable training precision settings for RWKV-5, RWKV-6, and HGRN2 were only found after these experiments had already begun. As a result, RWKV-4 serves as the recurrent baseline in the large-scale study. This detail is useful because it shows that large-language-model benchmarking is not only about architecture, but also about what can be trained reliably at scale.

# Sequence Length Extrapolation from $2048$ to $16384$

One of the most revealing tests in the paper is sequence length extrapolation. The models are trained with context length $2048$, and then the authors evaluate them at test-time context lengths up to $16384$. This is a much harsher setting than ordinary validation, because the model must process sequences far longer than those seen during training.

The result is that xLSTM maintains low perplexities for longer contexts, whereas the other methods degrade more noticeably. In the paper’s framing, this matters because it shows that xLSTM is not only good at fitting the training-length distribution, but also robust when the context becomes much longer. That robustness is especially important for language modeling, where relevant information may appear far back in the sequence.

The paper’s interpretation is consistent with the architecture itself. xLSTM is a recurrent model with constant memory complexity, and its memory is designed to summarize the past rather than depend on a fixed attention window. The authors present this as part of the reason xLSTM can handle longer contexts well: the model’s state is built to carry information forward over time, so extending the sequence does not force it to rely on a longer and longer explicit cache in the way a Transformer does. The experiment does not prove this causally in isolation, but it strongly supports the architectural story the paper tells.

# Validation Perplexity and Downstream Reasoning Tasks

The paper uses validation perplexity as the main next-token prediction metric. Lower perplexity means the model is assigning higher probability to the correct next token, so it is a direct measure of language modeling quality. In the $300$B-token experiments, the authors evaluate all four model sizes on the SlimPajama validation set.

The striking result is that both xLSTM[$1:0$] and xLSTM[$7:1$] are the best models for all model sizes with respect to validation perplexity. This means that, on the held-out validation distribution, the xLSTM family consistently outperforms the Transformer, SSM, and recurrent baselines considered in the paper. The result is important because it holds across $125$M, $350$M, $760$M, and $1.3$B parameters rather than being confined to one favorable scale.

The paper also evaluates the models on downstream tasks that measure common-sense reasoning. Here again, xLSTM is the strongest method in the vast majority of tasks and across all model sizes. The main exception mentioned in the paper is ARC, where Mamba is in some cases the best method. This matters because it shows that xLSTM’s language-modeling gains are not limited to perplexity alone; they extend to tasks that require the model to use learned language representations for broader reasoning-like behavior.

The comparison between xLSTM[$1:0$] and xLSTM[$7:1$] is especially informative. The hybrid model with some sLSTM blocks performs extremely well on validation and downstream benchmarks, but the pure mLSTM variant is not consistently worse and can be better on specific language modeling evaluations later in the paper. This tells the reader that adding a small amount of sLSTM structure is not automatically beneficial in every setting. Instead, the best block mixture depends on which behavior one wants to emphasize.

# PALOMA Across $571$ Text Domains

The PALOMA benchmark is where the paper most clearly tests breadth. Rather than focusing on a single validation set or a small family of tasks, the authors evaluate next-token prediction across $571$ text domains. These domains range from sources like nytimes.com to Reddit communities such as r/depression, so the benchmark spans very different writing styles and topical distributions.

The main metric is again perplexity, but now the question is not just whether xLSTM works on the validation distribution. The question is whether it can maintain strong language modeling performance across many domains that differ in tone, vocabulary, and style. In this setting, xLSTM[$1:0$] performs better than xLSTM[$7:1$]. The pure mLSTM model also outperforms Mamba in $568$ out of $571$ domains, Llama in $486$ out of $571$, and RWKV-4 in $570$ out of $571$.

This is one of the clearest places where the paper shows that the choice of xLSTM composition matters. On validation perplexity and downstream tasks, the mixed architecture xLSTM[$7:1$] is among the strongest models. On PALOMA, however, the pure matrix-memory variant xLSTM[$1:0$] is better. The paper does not claim that one is universally superior; instead, it demonstrates that different mixtures of sLSTM and mLSTM can shift the balance of performance depending on the evaluation setting.

For a student reading the paper, this is an important lesson about architecture evaluation. A model can be broadly strong while still exhibiting systematic differences across benchmarks. The paper’s results suggest that sLSTM blocks may contribute useful inductive bias in some language-modeling and reasoning settings, but that broad-domain next-token prediction especially rewards the pure mLSTM version.

# Power-Law Scaling Laws and Their Offsets

To understand whether xLSTM is merely a good medium-scale model or a serious large-scale contender, the paper fits scaling laws. The idea is to see how performance changes as model size increases and whether the curves follow a predictable power-law pattern. This kind of analysis is important because it tells us whether the observed gains are likely to persist when the model is scaled further.

The paper reports that all models show similar scaling behavior, but with different offsets. In practical terms, that means the curves have similar overall shape, yet xLSTM is shifted in a favorable direction relative to the other methods. The ranking given in the paper is that RWKV-4 performs worst, followed by Llama and then Mamba, while xLSTM is better than Mamba with a margin comparable to the gap between Mamba and Llama.

The meaning of an offset is easy to miss, but it is central to the paper’s argument. If two methods have similar scaling slopes but one has a better offset, then the better method is consistently ahead across the sizes considered. That is exactly the kind of evidence the authors want: xLSTM is not just winning at one parameter count, but doing so in a way that suggests the advantage may remain as models become larger. The paper therefore concludes that larger xLSTM models should continue to compare favorably with Transformers and State Space Models.

# Generation Time, Decoding Throughput, and Constant-Memory Inference

The paper does not only care about accuracy. It also measures generation time and maximal throughput for the $1.3$B-scale models. This is important because a model can be accurate but still impractical if inference is too slow or too memory-intensive. The authors compare xLSTM to similarly sized Mamba, Llama, and RWKV implementations from HuggingFace, with a static key-value cache for Llama.

In the generation-time results, xLSTM and the other recurrent models, Mamba and RWKV-4, scale linearly, while Llama shows quadratic scaling. The experimental setting uses batch size $1$ and prefill $16$, which the paper notes is maximally favorable to the Transformer. Even in that setting, the recurrent-style models maintain the advantage in scaling behavior.

The throughput comparison highlights another advantage of xLSTM: because it has constant memory, it can use much larger batch sizes than Llama. That leads to the highest throughput among the compared systems. In other words, xLSTM is not only competitive in quality, but also benefits from an inference profile that can be favorable for practical deployment. The paper’s broader point is that constant-memory recurrence can be a real advantage when the goal is to generate text efficiently at scale.

# What These Results Say About xLSTM Relative to Current LLM Architectures

Taken together, the large-scale experiments make the paper’s central claim concrete. xLSTM is not presented as a small modification of LSTM for niche sequence tasks. It is shown to be a model family that can be trained at modern language-model scale, evaluated on long-context extrapolation, benchmarked on reasoning-related downstream tasks, tested across hundreds of text domains, and analyzed with scaling laws.

The overall picture is that xLSTM compares favorably with current Transformer and State Space Model architectures. On validation perplexity, the xLSTM variants are best across all model sizes. On downstream tasks, xLSTM is usually best, with only occasional exceptions such as ARC. On PALOMA, the pure mLSTM variant is especially strong, showing that xLSTM’s matrix memory can generalize well across broad-domain language modeling. And the scaling-law results suggest that these advantages are not isolated accidents, but part of a consistent trend.

At the same time, the paper is careful not to oversell. It notes that the architecture and hyperparameters were not fully optimized, especially for larger xLSTM models, and that the CUDA kernels could be faster. That caution makes the main conclusion more meaningful, not less: even before exhaustive optimization, xLSTM already reaches the level of current state-of-the-art technologies and often exceeds them. In the paper’s own terms, this means xLSTM has moved LSTM-style recurrence back into serious contention for large language modeling.

Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Directions

# Why sLSTM remains harder to parallelize than mLSTM

The paper draws a sharp distinction between the two new xLSTM variants: sLSTM keeps memory mixing, while mLSTM removes it. This difference is exactly why sLSTM is harder to parallelize. In sLSTM, the hidden state at time $t$ depends on the previous hidden state, and that hidden state is itself part of the gate computations at the next step. In other words, the network contains hidden-hidden recurrent connections that force a step-by-step sequence of computation. Even though the model is organized into residual blocks, the internal recurrence still creates a strictly sequential dependency across time.

The reason the authors keep this sequential structure is that it buys them a new form of memory mixing. The paper emphasizes that sLSTM can have multiple memory cells, and that its recurrent connections allow the hidden state to influence the cell input and the gates. This is not just an implementation detail; it is part of the model’s expressivity. The same section that explains the parallelizability trade-off also argues that memory mixing is important for state tracking problems. So sLSTM represents a deliberate choice: it sacrifices full parallel execution in exchange for richer recurrent interaction.

mLSTM makes the opposite choice. It abandons memory mixing entirely, which is what enables its recurrence to be reformulated in a parallel version. This shows the central architectural trade-off in xLSTM. If the goal is maximum parallelism, recurrence has to be simplified. If the goal is richer sequential reasoning, recurrence can keep memory mixing, but that makes it inherently harder to execute in parallel. The paper’s design therefore does not treat parallelization as an absolute property of “LSTM-like” models, but as something that depends on how the memory is structured.

# Current CUDA kernel limitations and optimization opportunities

The paper is explicit that implementation quality still matters a great deal for xLSTM. For sLSTM, the authors report a fast CUDA implementation with GPU memory optimizations down to the register level, and they say it is typically less than $2$ times slower than mLSTM. That is a strong result, but it also shows that the sequential nature of sLSTM still carries a runtime cost. The kernel work is therefore part of the model design, not a separate engineering afterthought.

The mLSTM side also leaves room for improvement. The paper states that the current CUDA kernels for mLSTM are not optimized and that the implementation is about $4$ times slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba. Importantly, this slowdown is not presented as a fundamental property of the model itself. Instead, the authors frame it as an optimization gap: because mLSTM is parallelizable and uses standard matrix operations, faster kernels could plausibly be developed in the spirit of FlashAttention.

This makes the current implementation status itself a research direction. The paper suggests that xLSTM’s favorable modeling results may become even more compelling if the kernels are improved. The underlying architecture already has attractive sequence-length scaling, but the actual wall-clock benefit depends on how efficiently the recurrence is implemented on hardware. That means future work is not only about changing the mathematics of the model; it is also about making the model computationally competitive in practice.

# The computational burden of matrix memory in mLSTM

The key idea behind mLSTM is that the scalar cell state of the original LSTM is replaced by a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This is what gives mLSTM its stronger storage capacity. Instead of compressing everything into a scalar memory, it can store key-value associations through a covariance update rule of the form $\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. The retrieval step then uses a query vector and a matrix multiplication. This is a very different memory system from the original LSTM, and the paper presents it as the reason mLSTM handles associative recall better.

The cost of this richer memory is computational. A matrix state of size $d \times d$ is much more expensive to process than a scalar cell state, because every update and retrieval touches all of those entries. The paper is careful to describe this as a trade-off between memory capacity and computational complexity. The memory does not introduce additional parameters, which is an important point, but it does increase the amount of computation that must happen at each step.

At the same time, the paper also explains why this cost is not as severe as it might first appear. Because the mLSTM recurrence has no memory mixing, it can be parallelized with standard matrix operations on GPUs. That means the computational burden is real, but it is also a burden that hardware is relatively well suited to handle. The open question for future work is how far that trade-off can be pushed: how much storage capacity can be gained per unit compute, and how much more efficient can the matrix-memory implementation become with better kernels and better parallelization strategies.

# Sensitivity to forget-gate initialization

One of the limitations listed in the paper is that the forget gates must be chosen carefully. This is a practical issue, but it is also conceptually important because the forget gate controls how much of the past is retained from one step to the next. In both the original LSTM and xLSTM, the forget gate is part of the mechanism that decides whether the model preserves information or overwrites it. If that choice is badly initialized, the model can begin training in a regime where memory is either too short-lived or too persistent.

The issue matters even more in xLSTM because the paper introduces exponential gating together with stabilization. The forget gate is not just a scalar multiplier in a recurrence; it is part of a system that also includes a normalizer state and, in the stabilized formulation, a stabilizer state $m_t$. The paper’s warning that forget-gate initialization requires care indicates that training dynamics can be sensitive to how these gates start out. In other words, xLSTM is not only about a better memory structure; it is also about getting the gate behavior into a useful operating range.

The paper does not give a universal initialization recipe, and that absence is itself an open problem. A natural next step is to identify initialization choices that work robustly across model sizes, block types, and tasks. Since the paper reports scaling experiments across $125$M to $1.3$B parameter models and across different mixtures of sLSTM and mLSTM blocks, the fact that gate initialization still appears as a limitation shows that training stability remains an important part of the design space.

# Possible memory overload at much longer context lengths

The paper’s discussion of long contexts is optimistic, but it is not unconditional. It notes that the matrix memory of mLSTM is independent of sequence length, yet also cautions that increasing the sequence length might overload the memory at much longer context sizes. This sounds subtle, but the idea is straightforward: a fixed-size memory can be extremely useful, but it can still be stressed if the amount of information that must be represented becomes too large or too dense.

The experiments reported in the paper do not show this problem up to the tested lengths. In particular, the authors report favorable extrapolation to contexts as long as $16{,}384$ tokens in their sequence-length experiments. So the overload issue is not an observed failure at the scale they study. Rather, it is a forward-looking limitation that becomes relevant when thinking about contexts even longer than those used in the experiments.

This creates a clear research direction. One question is whether the current matrix memory design continues to work for substantially longer sequences without modification. Another is whether larger memory dimensions, different mixes of sLSTM and mLSTM blocks, or other architectural changes can delay or prevent overload. The paper does not settle these questions, but by explicitly naming the limitation it points to where the next empirical tests should go.

# The role of incomplete architecture and hyperparameter tuning

The paper is unusually direct about the fact that its models were not fully optimized. Because the experiments are computationally expensive, the authors say they did not fully optimize the architecture or the hyperparameters, especially for larger xLSTM models. That matters because strong reported results are not necessarily the final word on the architecture’s best possible performance. Instead, they should be read as evidence that xLSTM is already competitive even before exhaustive tuning.

This limitation is also what makes the results scientifically interesting. If a model performs well without full optimization, then there may still be additional gains to be found through more careful search over design choices. The paper already explores different mixes of mLSTM and sLSTM blocks, such as xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1], but it does not claim that the best possible mixture has been found for every setting. Similarly, the residual-backbone choices and gate settings are presented as part of the architecture, not as final optimized solutions.

The practical implication is that xLSTM should be viewed as a promising starting point rather than a closed design. Future work can systematically explore architectural ratios, training settings, and larger-scale configurations. Since the paper’s scaling-law results suggest that xLSTM continues to improve with scale, the lack of full tuning leaves open the possibility that optimized xLSTM systems could perform even better than the reported models.

# What the paper’s conclusions imply about the future of recurrent LLMs

The paper’s conclusion is deliberately cautious but clearly optimistic. The authors say they have at least answered their opening question up to the level of “current technologies like Transformers or State Space Models.” That is a strong statement: it means recurrent models are no longer restricted to being historical predecessors of Transformers. With exponential gating and new memory structures, xLSTM is presented as a serious current competitor in language modeling.

The empirical scaling results support that conclusion. The paper reports favorable behavior as model size increases, and it uses those scaling laws to argue that larger xLSTM models will remain competitive with state-of-the-art Transformer-based large language models. This does not mean the Transformer story is over. Rather, it suggests that the design space for large language models is broader than it appeared after the rise of self-attention. Recurrent models, when updated with modern techniques, remain viable at scale.

The paper also points out an additional advantage: xLSTM has linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length. That makes it attractive not only as a language model, but also as a system that could be deployed more efficiently in settings where long sequences matter. The future implied by the paper is therefore not a return to old LSTMs, but a new class of recurrent large language models that compete on performance while preserving the efficiency advantages of recurrence.

# Potential applications beyond language modeling, including reinforcement learning, time series, and physical systems

Although the experiments focus on language modeling, the paper’s introduction and conclusion both suggest broader applications. The authors note that LSTMs have already been successful in reinforcement learning, time series prediction, and modeling physical systems, and they explicitly say that xLSTM has the potential to impact these areas as well. This is a natural extension of the paper’s results, because the new model is built to improve long-range memory and sequential state tracking, which are precisely the kinds of abilities that matter in those domains.

The paper’s earlier examples help explain why these applications are plausible. It mentions sequence-based reinforcement learning systems such as AlphaStar and OpenAI Five, as well as hydrological and physical modeling tasks. These are all settings where the model must retain and update state over time, often under long and noisy sequences. Since xLSTM improves both memory capacity and the ability to revise storage decisions, it is reasonable to expect that it may be useful in similar problems outside language.

Still, the paper does not claim these applications have already been solved. Instead, it presents them as opportunities. The open direction is to test whether xLSTM’s combination of exponential gating, state tracking, and matrix memory transfers well to these fields. In that sense, the broader significance of xLSTM is not limited to text generation. It may become a general-purpose recurrent architecture for dynamic and sequential domains.

# Open theoretical questions about expressivity, compression, and scaling in recurrent models

The paper gives empirical evidence that xLSTM works better than prior recurrent, linear-attention, and state-space alternatives on the tested benchmarks. But it also raises deeper theoretical questions that are not resolved by experiments alone. One of the most important is expressivity: the paper argues that memory mixing enables state tracking and makes LSTMs more expressive than State Space Models and Transformers in certain settings. That is a powerful claim, but it invites a formal answer. What exactly do exponential gating and memory mixing allow the model to represent that simpler recurrence schemes cannot?

A second open question concerns compression. The original LSTM compresses information into scalar memory cells, while mLSTM uses a matrix memory to store richer associations. The paper shows that this improves performance, especially on tasks that require retrieving key-value pairs or handling rare tokens. But how much information can each memory form really retain, and how does that capacity change as the model scales? The paper suggests a qualitative answer, but not a complete theory.

A third question is how empirical scaling laws relate to recurrent architecture design. The authors show that xLSTM improves with scale and appears likely to remain competitive at larger sizes, but the paper does not derive the scaling behavior from first principles. Likewise, the use of Cover’s theorem as a motivation for residual block design provides intuition rather than a formal guarantee. So the broader theoretical challenge is to connect architecture, memory structure, and scaling in a way that predicts when recurrent models should outperform other sequence models.

Taken together, these questions show that xLSTM is more than a practical model proposal. It is also a framework for rethinking what recurrent networks can do when they are scaled up and modernized. The paper’s limitations do not weaken that message; they sharpen it by showing exactly where future theory and engineering are still needed.
