\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### How is the mLSTM retrieval formula derived, especially the denominator $\max\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \}$?
Question:
The mLSTM retrieves memory using
$ \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \} $,
where $\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t$. I understand the numerator somewhat: $\mathbf{C}_t$ is a sum of outer products, so multiplying by a query should mix stored values according to key-query similarity. But I do not understand the denominator. Why should the correct normalization depend on $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ rather than, say, a norm of $\mathbf{q}_t$ or a softmax-like normalizer? And why do they take the absolute value and lower-bound it by $1$?
Answer:
The key point is that the denominator is not meant to be a mysterious “probability normalizer” like softmax. In mLSTM it is a stabilization and scale-correction term for a linear associative memory readout.

If you unroll the recurrences, the matrix state and the normalizer state have the form
$$
\mathbf{C}_t = \sum_{j \le t} \alpha_{j,t}\,\mathbf{v}_j \mathbf{k}_j^\top,
\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t = \sum_{j \le t} \alpha_{j,t}\,\mathbf{k}_j,
$$
where $\alpha_{j,t}$ collects the input gate at time $j$ and all later forget gates. Then the raw retrieval is
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t
= \sum_{j \le t} \alpha_{j,t}\,\mathbf{v}_j\,(\mathbf{k}_j^\top \mathbf{q}_t).
$$
So each stored value $\mathbf{v}_j$ is weighted by the similarity $\mathbf{k}_j^\top \mathbf{q}_t$, exactly as in attention or fast-weight retrieval. Now look at the scalar
$$
\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t
= \sum_{j \le t} \alpha_{j,t}\,(\mathbf{k}_j^\top \mathbf{q}_t).
$$
This is the total “mass” of those same similarity weights. Dividing by it turns the readout into a normalized weighted average:
$$
\frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t}
=
\sum_{j \le t}
\frac{\alpha_{j,t}(\mathbf{k}_j^\top \mathbf{q}_t)}{\sum_{\ell \le t}\alpha_{\ell,t}(\mathbf{k}_\ell^\top \mathbf{q}_t)}
\,\mathbf{v}_j.
$$
That is the natural derivation of the denominator: it is the matching normalization for the same key-query weights that appear in the numerator. A norm like $\|\mathbf{q}_t\|$ would normalize by query size, but it would not cancel the content-dependent scaling coming from the stored keys, so it would not give you a proper similarity-weighted average.

The paper’s extra $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$ is a practical stabilization trick, not a deeper theoretical change. The authors explicitly say that because the keys are zero-mean after layer norm, $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ can be close to zero, so they take the absolute value and lower-bound it by $1$. The absolute value prevents sign flips in the denominator, which would otherwise make the readout change sign for purely accidental cancellation of key vectors. The floor at $1$ prevents division by a tiny number, which would blow up the output when the query does not strongly match the memory. In other words, if the retrieval signal is weak, the model falls back to an unnormalized readout rather than becoming numerically unstable.

So the short intuition is: $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ says “sum the stored values weighted by similarity,” $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ says “how much total similarity is there?”, and the clamp makes that normalization safe. This is why the denominator depends on $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$, not on $\|\mathbf{q}_t\|$, and why it is absolute-valued and thresholded.
