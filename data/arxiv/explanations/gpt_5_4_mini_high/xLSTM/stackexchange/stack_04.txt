\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### If the forget gate can be $\exp(\tilde{f}_t) > 1$, how is it still a forget gate rather than an amplification gate?
Question:
In a standard LSTM, the forget gate is in $(0,1)$, so it is easy to interpret as decaying old memory. In xLSTM, the forget gate can optionally be exponential, so it can exceed $1$. That seems to mean the old state can be amplified instead of forgotten. Is that intentional? If yes, how should I interpret the term "forget gate" in this setting? Also, what stops the recurrence from becoming unstable if the model repeatedly chooses $f_t > 1$ over many time steps?
Answer:
Yes, it is intentional, and the name “forget gate” is a bit of a historical carry-over from the standard LSTM.

In the original LSTM, the forget gate $f_t \in (0,1)$ really is a pure decay factor: it can only shrink the previous cell state, never increase it. In xLSTM, the authors deliberately relax this by allowing an exponential parameterization, $f_t = \exp(\tilde f_t)$, so $f_t$ can exceed $1$. The point is not to make the network “amplify memory” in an uncontrolled way, but to give it more expressive control over how strongly the previous state should be weighted relative to the new input. The paper explicitly says that exponential gating is introduced to help xLSTM “revise storage decisions,” which is exactly the kind of behavior you cannot get if the forget factor is restricted to $(0,1)$ as in a vanilla LSTM.

The key thing is that xLSTM does not use $f_t$ in isolation. In the sLSTM, both the cell state and a normalizer state are updated by the same gate:
$$
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \qquad n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,
$$
and the hidden state is then normalized as
$$
h_t = o_t \, \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
$$
So even if $f_t > 1$, the model is not simply letting the raw memory blow up; it is changing the relative weighting of old and new information in a normalized representation. In that sense, $f_t$ is better thought of as a multiplicative retention weight than as a literal “forget factor” that must be less than $1$. The old name survives, but the role is slightly more general.

As for stability, the paper is very explicit that exponential gates can cause overflow, and that is why it introduces the stabilizer state $m_t$:
$$
m_t = \max\!\bigl(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t)\bigr),
$$
followed by stabilized gates $f_t'$ and $i_t'$. This is essentially the same idea as the numerically stable log-sum-exp trick: the computation is rescaled so that large exponentials do not explode numerically. The paper states that replacing $f_t$ and $i_t$ by $f_t'$ and $i_t'$ does not change the network output or the derivatives of the loss, so the stabilization is not just a heuristic; it is an equivalent reparameterization of the same computation.

So the short answer is: yes, $f_t > 1$ is deliberate. In xLSTM, the “forget gate” is really a gate controlling the relative influence of past state, not necessarily a strict decay coefficient. The architecture stays stable because the raw exponential gates are always used together with normalization and the stabilizer $m_t$, which prevents runaway growth in the effective computation. The paper even notes, in its limitations, that the initialization of the forget gates must be chosen carefully, which is another reminder that the stability is handled by the design, not by restricting $f_t$ to $(0,1)$ as in a standard LSTM.
