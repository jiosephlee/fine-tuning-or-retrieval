\title{Blogs about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

# Beyond the Hype: What xLSTM's Benchmarks Really Say About the Future of Language Models

For years, the story of sequence modeling has looked pretty settled: Transformers won language modeling, and recurrent networks were left behind. The xLSTM paper is interesting because it asks a very specific question: what happens if you scale LSTMs to modern LLM size, but also fix the parts that have held them back?

The short version is that xLSTM is not just an incremental tweak. The authors introduce two main changes: exponential gating, which makes it easier for the model to revise what it stores, and new memory structures, which come in two flavors. The sLSTM keeps a scalar memory and includes memory mixing, while the mLSTM replaces the scalar cell with a matrix memory and a covariance-style update rule, making it parallelizable. The real test is not whether these ideas sound neat, but whether they help on actual benchmarks.

## The synthetic tests: does xLSTM really track state?

The first set of experiments checks whether xLSTM can handle tasks that require remembering and updating internal state over time. These are not glamorous benchmarks, but they are useful because they isolate specific capabilities.

One of the key takeaways is that memory mixing matters. The paper evaluates formal language tasks, including parity-like problems that are hard for models that cannot revise state as new tokens arrive. According to the results, models such as Transformers and state space models without memory mixing struggle here, while xLSTM is able to solve these tasks. That is a strong signal that the new gating and memory-mixing design are doing something real, not just adding parameters.

The second synthetic test is Multi-Query Associative Recall, where the model has to memorize key-value pairs and later retrieve the right value from a query. This is a direct test of memory capacity. Here, xLSTM's matrix-memory variant performs especially well. In fact, xLSTM[1:1] is the best among the non-Transformer models tested, and the paper notes something subtle but important: adding an sLSTM block does not reduce memory capacity, and may even help on the hardest version of the task.

The Long Range Arena results tell a similar story. xLSTM is reported to be consistently strong across the different long-context tasks, which suggests that the architecture is not just solving one narrow benchmark.

## The 15B-token language modeling run: does it beat modern baselines?

The next question is closer to what people usually mean by “language model performance.” The authors train xLSTM and a set of strong baselines on 15B tokens from SlimPajama and compare validation perplexity. Perplexity is the key metric here: lower is better, and it means the model is doing a better job predicting the next token.

At this scale, xLSTM outperforms all of the existing methods they compare against, including Transformers, state space models, recurrent baselines, and linear-attention variants. The comparison includes models such as GPT-3, Llama, Mamba, RWKV variants, GLA, HGRN, RetNet, and Hyena. In other words, xLSTM is not just beating old RNNs; it is competing against the architectures that have dominated modern language modeling.

The scaling curves are also important. A single win at one model size can be misleading, but the paper reports that xLSTM scales favorably and maintains its advantage as the model size changes. That matters because a better scaling trend is usually a better sign than a one-off benchmark bump.

## The 300B-token runs: does the advantage survive at real LLM scale?

This is the part of the paper that matters most if you care about xLSTM as a serious large language model architecture. The authors train models from 125M up to 1.3B parameters on 300B tokens from SlimPajama, then test them on several axes: long-context extrapolation, validation perplexity, downstream reasoning tasks, PALOMA, and scaling laws.

The long-context result is one of the clearest wins. All models are trained with context length 2048 and then evaluated on contexts up to 16384. xLSTM maintains low perplexity at longer contexts better than the other models in the comparison. That is a practical result, not just a theoretical one: models that degrade sharply with longer contexts are much harder to use in real systems.

On validation perplexity, the story is very strong for xLSTM. The paper reports that xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] are the best models across all model sizes in this setting. In the paper’s notation, xLSTM[1:0] means all mLSTM blocks, while xLSTM[7:1] mixes in a small number of sLSTM blocks. This is a nice example of the architecture trade-off showing up in the results: different mixtures of the two block types can be better for different tasks.

For downstream reasoning tasks, xLSTM is again usually the best model. The paper says it wins on the vast majority of tasks across all model sizes, with Mamba occasionally taking the lead on ARC. So the result is not “xLSTM wins absolutely everything,” but it is strong enough to put it in the same conversation as the best current alternatives.

PALOMA adds another useful angle. This benchmark spans 571 text domains, from news to Reddit communities, and measures next-token prediction across a wide range of styles. Here, xLSTM[1:0] is especially strong. It achieves lower perplexity than Mamba on 568 of 571 domains, than RWKV-4 on 570 of 571, and than Llama on 486 of 571. It also does better than xLSTM[7:1] on these language tasks, which suggests that the pure mLSTM variant may be the better fit for text prediction in particular.

The scaling-law results are also encouraging. All models follow similar power-law trends, but xLSTM has a better offset than Mamba, Llama, and RWKV-4. That does not prove it will always win at every scale, but it does suggest the architecture is not hitting an obvious ceiling.

## Speed and memory: where xLSTM fits in practice

One reason recurrent models have stayed interesting is efficiency. xLSTM keeps the main recurrent advantage: its computation is linear in sequence length, and its memory usage stays constant with sequence length. That is very different from standard Transformer attention, which becomes more expensive as the context grows.

The paper’s generation-time measurements reflect this. xLSTM and the other recurrent models scale linearly, while Llama shows the expected quadratic behavior. Because xLSTM uses constant memory, it can also use much larger batch sizes in decoding, which helps throughput.

But there are still practical trade-offs. The paper is explicit about them:

- sLSTM is not parallelizable because memory mixing introduces sequential dependence.
- The current mLSTM CUDA kernels are not fully optimized and are still slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba.
- The matrix memory is computationally expensive because it operates on $d \times d$ state.
- Forget-gate initialization has to be handled carefully.
- The models were not fully architecture-tuned or hyperparameter-tuned, especially at larger scales.

So while xLSTM is promising, it is not yet a free lunch.

## The bottom line

The headline result is not that xLSTM magically replaces Transformers. It is more interesting than that. The paper shows that if you fix the main weaknesses of LSTMs, add modern stabilization, and scale them properly, they can compete with the best current language model families.

The strongest evidence comes from the combination of results rather than any single benchmark: xLSTM handles synthetic state-tracking tasks, performs well on long-context evaluations, scales favorably on large language modeling runs, and matches or beats strong baselines on validation perplexity and downstream tasks.

If the remaining implementation and optimization issues are improved, xLSTM looks less like a historical curiosity and more like a real alternative path for large language models.
