\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 1: From Classical LSTMs to xLSTM: Why Revisit Recurrence?

# A brief history of sequence models in deep learning

The paper places xLSTM in a long historical line of sequence models whose central challenge is the same: how can a model carry information forward through time without losing it? In the 1990s, the Long Short-Term Memory, or LSTM, was introduced with two core ideas, the constant error carousel and gating, to overcome the vanishing gradient problem in recurrent neural networks. That innovation made LSTMs much more effective at learning long-range dependencies than earlier recurrent models, and for a long time they were among the most successful sequence models in deep learning.

The paper emphasizes that this success was broad, not narrow. LSTMs were used for text generation, handwriting generation, sequence-to-sequence translation, evaluating computer programs, image captioning, source code generation, rainfall-runoff modeling, and hydrological forecasting. They were also central in reinforcement learning systems such as AlphaStar and OpenAI Five. In language modeling, the paper states that LSTMs were the first large language models. This is an important historical point: before Transformers became dominant, LSTMs were the main architecture that scaled sequence learning into the regime of large, useful models.

What makes this history relevant to xLSTM is that the LSTM was not replaced because it stopped working altogether. Rather, it was overtaken by a different architecture that scaled better. xLSTM is motivated by the idea that the recurrent family still has valuable properties, and that its weaknesses might be addressed directly instead of abandoning recurrence entirely.

# Why LSTMs were crucial for early large-scale language modeling

At the center of the original LSTM is a simple recurrence:

\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \quad h_t = o_t \psi(c_t).
\]

The paper uses this equation to explain the constant error carousel. The cell state $c_t$ is updated additively: part of the previous state is kept through the forget gate $f_t$, and new content is written through the input gate $i_t$ and cell input $z_t$. This additive structure is the key to why LSTMs could preserve information much more effectively than ordinary recurrent neural networks. The hidden state $h_t$ is then produced by applying an output gate $o_t$ to a normalized or squashed version of the cell state.

The intuition is that the memory cell acts as a protected storage unit. The input gate decides what enters memory, the forget gate decides what remains from the past, and the output gate decides what part of the memory becomes visible as the hidden state. The paper writes out the gate equations explicitly in terms of input weights, recurrent weights, and bias terms. All gates are sigmoid in the original LSTM, and the cell input and hidden activation functions are typically $\tanh$. In later LSTM formulations, multiple scalar memory cells were grouped into vectors, allowing recurrent weight matrices to mix the outputs of different cells.

This design mattered for language modeling because it gave the model a way to accumulate information over many tokens while still being trainable. The paper argues that LSTMs excel at learning abstractions: they can extract semantic information and store it in memory cells. Their usefulness was visible in practice through phenomena such as number neurons, syntax neurons, linguistic neurons, and sentiment neurons. In other words, LSTMs were not just memorizing tokens; they were learning compressed, useful sequence state.

# The rise of Transformers and parallel self-attention

The paper then describes the major shift in sequence modeling: the advent of Transformers in 2017. The key innovation was parallelizable self-attention. Compared with recurrent computation, self-attention made it much easier to scale models and training because the interactions across positions could be computed in parallel rather than step by step.

This shift mattered because, although LSTMs were powerful, they still had structural limitations. Their recurrent nature meant that information moved through time sequentially. As Transformers became larger and more effective, they outpaced LSTMs in language modeling and marked the start of a new era. The paper presents xLSTM as an attempt to revisit recurrence in light of this history: not to deny the success of Transformers, but to ask what happens if one keeps the recurrent core and modernizes it.

That question is especially meaningful because the paper does not treat recurrence as obsolete. Instead, it treats recurrence as a design space with untapped potential. The issue is not whether recurrence can model sequences at all, but whether it can be reworked to avoid the limitations that prevented LSTMs from scaling further.

# What LSTMs still do well: compression, state tracking, and recurrence

The paper is explicit that LSTMs still have strengths, even though they also have important limitations. One strength is compression. The classical LSTM stores information in a compact cell state, and this compressed memory can capture useful sequence information over time. The paper later points out that xLSTM inherits this compressive character and that such memory is well suited for industrial applications and edge implementations.

Another strength is state tracking. The paper argues that LSTMs are especially suited for problems where the model must keep track of evolving state across a sequence. This includes formal language tasks, code evaluation, and tracking entities in long narratives. These are settings where the model cannot simply react locally to the current token. It must maintain a persistent internal record of what has happened before.

A third strength is recurrence itself. Recurrence naturally makes the model a sequence processor rather than a bag-of-tokens processor. The LSTM does this while also learning abstractions. The paper’s point is not that classical LSTMs are weak in all respects, but that their recurrence is valuable enough to preserve if the known problems can be addressed. xLSTM is built around that idea.

# The paper’s motivating question about scaling LSTMs to billions of parameters

The central question of the paper is straightforward: how far can we get in language modeling if we scale LSTMs to billions of parameters, use the latest ideas from modern large language models, and at the same time fix the known limitations of LSTMs? The paper identifies three main limitations.

The first limitation is the inability to revise storage decisions. The paper illustrates this with nearest neighbor search. In that task, the model must scan a sequence and remember the most similar vector seen so far. A classical LSTM struggles when a better match appears later, because its gating mechanism is not well suited to revising what has already been stored. This is one of the main motivations for introducing exponential gating in xLSTM.

The second limitation is limited storage capacity. The original LSTM compresses information into scalar cell states, which limits how much structure can be stored. The paper demonstrates this with rare token prediction. On Wikitext-103, LSTM performs worse on rare tokens because rare events require richer memory than scalar storage can provide. This motivates the matrix memory in mLSTM.

The third limitation is lack of parallelizability due to memory mixing, meaning hidden-hidden connections between successive time steps. These recurrent interactions make computation sequential. This is where Transformers gained an advantage, since their core mechanism could be parallelized much more effectively. The paper’s goal is to keep recurrence while eliminating, or at least reducing, the bottlenecks that made the original LSTM hard to scale.

# The two core xLSTM ideas: exponential gating and new memory structures

xLSTM modifies the classical LSTM in two main ways. The first is exponential gating with normalization and stabilization. The second is a change in memory structure. Together, these changes produce two new variants: sLSTM and mLSTM.

The idea behind exponential gating is that the input and forget gates can use exponential activations rather than only sigmoid activations. This gives the model a more flexible way to control how strongly information is written and retained. But exponentials can also become numerically unstable, so the paper introduces stabilization. In the sLSTM, this is done using an additional state $m_t$ that tracks gate magnitudes and allows the gates to be rewritten in stabilized form without changing the output of the network or the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters.

The second idea is to change the memory itself. In sLSTM, the memory remains scalar, but the gating and memory mixing are redesigned. In mLSTM, the memory becomes a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, which greatly increases storage capacity. The paper treats these as two complementary directions: one improves the way scalar memory is controlled, and the other enlarges memory so that richer associations can be stored.

# Preview of sLSTM, mLSTM, xLSTM blocks, and experimental results

The sLSTM can be written as

\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t, \quad n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t, \quad h_t = o_t \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
\]

The additional state $n_t$ is a normalizer state. Intuitively, it keeps track of the total gate strength that has gone into the memory so that the hidden state can be normalized. The paper says this normalizer sums the product of the input gate and all future forget gates. Stabilization is handled by the state $m_t$, which allows the use of exponential gates without overflow. In the paper’s formulation, sLSTM can still use memory mixing through recurrent connections from the hidden state to the cell inputs and gates, and it can also be organized into multiple heads, with mixing within each head but not across heads.

The mLSTM expands memory from a scalar to a matrix and stores key-value pairs using a covariance-style update rule:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top.
\]

Here the forget gate acts like a decay rate and the input gate acts like a learning rate. The model also maintains a normalizer state and retrieves memory using a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$. The hidden state is computed through matrix retrieval rather than scalar recurrence. The paper emphasizes that this architecture is fully parallelizable because it does not use memory mixing. In that sense, mLSTM is closer to a matrix-based associative memory. The covariance update is motivated by classical work on associative memories and by the goal of maximizing separability of retrieved vectors.

xLSTM blocks then place these recurrent modules inside residual block architectures. The paper uses the idea that a model should summarize the past in a high-dimensional space so that different histories or contexts become more separable. This is related to Cover’s theorem, which the paper invokes to justify why higher-dimensional nonlinear embeddings can be easier to separate linearly. For sLSTM, the paper uses a post up-projection block, similar in spirit to Transformers. For mLSTM, it uses a pre up-projection block, similar in spirit to State Space Models. The full xLSTM architecture is formed by residually stacking these blocks with pre-LayerNorm backbones, as in modern large language models.

The paper also introduces a notation for mixed architectures: xLSTM[$a:b$] means a ratio of $a/b$ mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. For example, xLSTM[7:1] means seven mLSTM blocks for every one sLSTM block. In the experiments, this helps distinguish models that rely mostly on matrix memory from those that use more scalar-memory recurrence.

The experiments are then used to test whether these ideas actually matter. On synthetic tasks, xLSTM’s exponential gating and memory mixing help it solve state-tracking problems in formal languages, while its matrix memory improves performance on associative recall tasks. On the Long Range Arena, xLSTM shows consistently strong performance on long-context tasks. On 15B tokens from SlimPajama, xLSTM outperforms the compared methods in validation perplexity and shows favorable scaling behavior. On 300B tokens, xLSTM maintains low perplexity when extrapolating to longer contexts, achieves the best validation perplexities for the tested model sizes in the strongest configurations, and performs best on the vast majority of downstream tasks. On PALOMA, xLSTM also performs strongly across 571 text domains, and the paper reports that xLSTM can achieve substantially higher decoding throughput because its memory is constant with respect to sequence length.

Taken together, the paper’s preview is clear: xLSTM is not presented as a nostalgic return to old recurrent models, but as a modernized recurrent architecture that keeps the useful parts of LSTM while directly targeting the limitations that prevented it from competing with Transformers at scale.
