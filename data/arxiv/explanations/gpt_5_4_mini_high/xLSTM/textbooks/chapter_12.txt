\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 12: xLSTM as a Large Language Model: 300B Tokens, Downstream Tasks, and Scaling Laws

# Training xLSTM at $125$M, $350$M, $760$M, and $1.3$B Parameters

The paper’s large-scale language modeling study is designed to answer a simple but ambitious question: if the LSTM family is modernized and scaled up using current large-language-model practices, how far can it go? To test this, the authors train xLSTM models at four sizes: $125$M, $350$M, $760$M, and $1.3$B parameters. The point of using several sizes is not merely to find one good model, but to see whether the same architectural ideas continue to work as capacity increases.

A key detail in the experimental setup is that the models are trained in an autoregressive language modeling regime on $300$B tokens from SlimPajama. This gives the study a scale that is comparable to other modern large-language-model experiments, rather than a small-scale proof of concept. The paper also uses a consistent context length of $2048$ during training, which matters later when the authors test whether the models can generalize to much longer sequences at inference time.

The experiments also distinguish between two xLSTM compositions. The notation xLSTM[$a:b$] means that the architecture uses a ratio of $a/b$ mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. In the large-scale runs, the paper compares xLSTM[$1:0$], which is only mLSTM, and xLSTM[$7:1$], which mixes mostly mLSTM blocks with a smaller number of sLSTM blocks. All experiments use pre up-projection blocks for mLSTM and post up-projection blocks for sLSTM. That design choice is part of what makes the reported results directly comparable across sizes.

# The $300$B-Token SlimPajama Training Regime and Baseline Selection

The training corpus is SlimPajama, and the large-scale setting uses $300$B tokens. This is important because the paper is not evaluating xLSTM in isolation; it is testing whether the model can compete under the same kind of scale that has made Transformers dominant in language modeling. In that sense, the $300$B-token regime is the paper’s most direct stress test.

For the large-scale comparison, the authors choose one representative model from each of the main competing families: Llama for Transformers, Mamba for State Space Models, and RWKV-4 for recurrent neural networks. This choice is deliberate. Rather than comparing xLSTM to every available baseline, the paper focuses on representative systems that stand for the major architectural directions in modern language modeling. That makes the results easier to interpret: if xLSTM does well here, it is not just beating one particular implementation, but the broader family it represents.

The selection of RWKV-4 is also tied to the practical training setup described in the paper. For the $300$B-token experiments, the authors note that reasonable training precision settings for RWKV-5, RWKV-6, and HGRN2 were only found after these experiments had already begun. As a result, RWKV-4 serves as the recurrent baseline in the large-scale study. This detail is useful because it shows that large-language-model benchmarking is not only about architecture, but also about what can be trained reliably at scale.

# Sequence Length Extrapolation from $2048$ to $16384$

One of the most revealing tests in the paper is sequence length extrapolation. The models are trained with context length $2048$, and then the authors evaluate them at test-time context lengths up to $16384$. This is a much harsher setting than ordinary validation, because the model must process sequences far longer than those seen during training.

The result is that xLSTM maintains low perplexities for longer contexts, whereas the other methods degrade more noticeably. In the paper’s framing, this matters because it shows that xLSTM is not only good at fitting the training-length distribution, but also robust when the context becomes much longer. That robustness is especially important for language modeling, where relevant information may appear far back in the sequence.

The paper’s interpretation is consistent with the architecture itself. xLSTM is a recurrent model with constant memory complexity, and its memory is designed to summarize the past rather than depend on a fixed attention window. The authors present this as part of the reason xLSTM can handle longer contexts well: the model’s state is built to carry information forward over time, so extending the sequence does not force it to rely on a longer and longer explicit cache in the way a Transformer does. The experiment does not prove this causally in isolation, but it strongly supports the architectural story the paper tells.

# Validation Perplexity and Downstream Reasoning Tasks

The paper uses validation perplexity as the main next-token prediction metric. Lower perplexity means the model is assigning higher probability to the correct next token, so it is a direct measure of language modeling quality. In the $300$B-token experiments, the authors evaluate all four model sizes on the SlimPajama validation set.

The striking result is that both xLSTM[$1:0$] and xLSTM[$7:1$] are the best models for all model sizes with respect to validation perplexity. This means that, on the held-out validation distribution, the xLSTM family consistently outperforms the Transformer, SSM, and recurrent baselines considered in the paper. The result is important because it holds across $125$M, $350$M, $760$M, and $1.3$B parameters rather than being confined to one favorable scale.

The paper also evaluates the models on downstream tasks that measure common-sense reasoning. Here again, xLSTM is the strongest method in the vast majority of tasks and across all model sizes. The main exception mentioned in the paper is ARC, where Mamba is in some cases the best method. This matters because it shows that xLSTM’s language-modeling gains are not limited to perplexity alone; they extend to tasks that require the model to use learned language representations for broader reasoning-like behavior.

The comparison between xLSTM[$1:0$] and xLSTM[$7:1$] is especially informative. The hybrid model with some sLSTM blocks performs extremely well on validation and downstream benchmarks, but the pure mLSTM variant is not consistently worse and can be better on specific language modeling evaluations later in the paper. This tells the reader that adding a small amount of sLSTM structure is not automatically beneficial in every setting. Instead, the best block mixture depends on which behavior one wants to emphasize.

# PALOMA Across $571$ Text Domains

The PALOMA benchmark is where the paper most clearly tests breadth. Rather than focusing on a single validation set or a small family of tasks, the authors evaluate next-token prediction across $571$ text domains. These domains range from sources like nytimes.com to Reddit communities such as r/depression, so the benchmark spans very different writing styles and topical distributions.

The main metric is again perplexity, but now the question is not just whether xLSTM works on the validation distribution. The question is whether it can maintain strong language modeling performance across many domains that differ in tone, vocabulary, and style. In this setting, xLSTM[$1:0$] performs better than xLSTM[$7:1$]. The pure mLSTM model also outperforms Mamba in $568$ out of $571$ domains, Llama in $486$ out of $571$, and RWKV-4 in $570$ out of $571$.

This is one of the clearest places where the paper shows that the choice of xLSTM composition matters. On validation perplexity and downstream tasks, the mixed architecture xLSTM[$7:1$] is among the strongest models. On PALOMA, however, the pure matrix-memory variant xLSTM[$1:0$] is better. The paper does not claim that one is universally superior; instead, it demonstrates that different mixtures of sLSTM and mLSTM can shift the balance of performance depending on the evaluation setting.

For a student reading the paper, this is an important lesson about architecture evaluation. A model can be broadly strong while still exhibiting systematic differences across benchmarks. The paper’s results suggest that sLSTM blocks may contribute useful inductive bias in some language-modeling and reasoning settings, but that broad-domain next-token prediction especially rewards the pure mLSTM version.

# Power-Law Scaling Laws and Their Offsets

To understand whether xLSTM is merely a good medium-scale model or a serious large-scale contender, the paper fits scaling laws. The idea is to see how performance changes as model size increases and whether the curves follow a predictable power-law pattern. This kind of analysis is important because it tells us whether the observed gains are likely to persist when the model is scaled further.

The paper reports that all models show similar scaling behavior, but with different offsets. In practical terms, that means the curves have similar overall shape, yet xLSTM is shifted in a favorable direction relative to the other methods. The ranking given in the paper is that RWKV-4 performs worst, followed by Llama and then Mamba, while xLSTM is better than Mamba with a margin comparable to the gap between Mamba and Llama.

The meaning of an offset is easy to miss, but it is central to the paper’s argument. If two methods have similar scaling slopes but one has a better offset, then the better method is consistently ahead across the sizes considered. That is exactly the kind of evidence the authors want: xLSTM is not just winning at one parameter count, but doing so in a way that suggests the advantage may remain as models become larger. The paper therefore concludes that larger xLSTM models should continue to compare favorably with Transformers and State Space Models.

# Generation Time, Decoding Throughput, and Constant-Memory Inference

The paper does not only care about accuracy. It also measures generation time and maximal throughput for the $1.3$B-scale models. This is important because a model can be accurate but still impractical if inference is too slow or too memory-intensive. The authors compare xLSTM to similarly sized Mamba, Llama, and RWKV implementations from HuggingFace, with a static key-value cache for Llama.

In the generation-time results, xLSTM and the other recurrent models, Mamba and RWKV-4, scale linearly, while Llama shows quadratic scaling. The experimental setting uses batch size $1$ and prefill $16$, which the paper notes is maximally favorable to the Transformer. Even in that setting, the recurrent-style models maintain the advantage in scaling behavior.

The throughput comparison highlights another advantage of xLSTM: because it has constant memory, it can use much larger batch sizes than Llama. That leads to the highest throughput among the compared systems. In other words, xLSTM is not only competitive in quality, but also benefits from an inference profile that can be favorable for practical deployment. The paper’s broader point is that constant-memory recurrence can be a real advantage when the goal is to generate text efficiently at scale.

# What These Results Say About xLSTM Relative to Current LLM Architectures

Taken together, the large-scale experiments make the paper’s central claim concrete. xLSTM is not presented as a small modification of LSTM for niche sequence tasks. It is shown to be a model family that can be trained at modern language-model scale, evaluated on long-context extrapolation, benchmarked on reasoning-related downstream tasks, tested across hundreds of text domains, and analyzed with scaling laws.

The overall picture is that xLSTM compares favorably with current Transformer and State Space Model architectures. On validation perplexity, the xLSTM variants are best across all model sizes. On downstream tasks, xLSTM is usually best, with only occasional exceptions such as ARC. On PALOMA, the pure mLSTM variant is especially strong, showing that xLSTM’s matrix memory can generalize well across broad-domain language modeling. And the scaling-law results suggest that these advantages are not isolated accidents, but part of a consistent trend.

At the same time, the paper is careful not to oversell. It notes that the architecture and hyperparameters were not fully optimized, especially for larger xLSTM models, and that the CUDA kernels could be faster. That caution makes the main conclusion more meaningful, not less: even before exhaustive optimization, xLSTM already reaches the level of current state-of-the-art technologies and often exceeds them. In the paper’s own terms, this means xLSTM has moved LSTM-style recurrence back into serious contention for large language modeling.
