\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 11: Language Modeling at 15B Tokens: Comparisons and Ablations

# The SlimPajama dataset and autoregressive training setup

The first large-scale language-modeling study in the paper is built around a simple but demanding setting: all models are trained on $15$B tokens drawn from SlimPajama and are optimized in the same autoregressive next-token prediction regime. This matters because autoregressive language modeling forces a model to predict each token from the tokens that came before it, which is exactly the setting in which sequence models are compared in the paper. By holding the data source and training objective fixed, the authors can focus on the question that motivates the work: what happens when LSTM-style models are scaled up and modernized so that they can compete with current large language model families?

SlimPajama serves here as the common training corpus for a wide range of architectures. The comparison is not about whether one model has access to more data than another, but about what each architecture can extract from the same amount of training signal. That makes the results especially informative: if a model performs well in this setting, the improvement is tied to its sequence-processing design rather than to differences in training data. The paper uses this setup both for direct method comparison and for ablations, so the experimental design is deliberately controlled.

Because the task is next-token prediction, the models are evaluated in the same autoregressive manner during training and validation. This is the natural setting for language modeling, where a good model should assign high probability to the true next token given the context seen so far. The study therefore asks not just whether a model can fit the training distribution, but whether its architecture helps it generalize as a language model.

# Why validation perplexity is the main evaluation metric

The central metric in this comparison is validation perplexity. In the paper, models are trained on SlimPajama and then evaluated on held-out validation text, and the comparison is made through perplexity because it directly reflects next-token prediction quality. Lower perplexity means the model is assigning better probabilities to the correct next tokens, so it is the natural summary statistic for an autoregressive language model.

This choice is especially appropriate in a study like this one because the paper is not evaluating a single downstream task with a narrow success criterion. Instead, it is comparing general language-modeling ability across several architectures. Validation perplexity gives a compact view of how well the model has learned the token distribution in the held-out data. It is sensitive to the model’s overall predictive confidence, which makes it useful when comparing architectures that may differ substantially in how they store, update, and retrieve information.

The paper also uses perplexity as a way to study scaling. If a model’s perplexity decreases systematically as size increases, that suggests the architecture is scaling in a stable and predictable way. In the 15B-token study, the validation perplexity results therefore serve two purposes at once: they identify the strongest model among the compared methods, and they provide evidence about whether that model family is likely to remain competitive at larger scale.

# Model classes compared: Transformers, SSMs, RNNs, and linear Transformers

The comparison set spans the major sequence-model families that have been important in modern language modeling. On one side are Transformers, represented by GPT-3 and Llama. On another side are State Space Models, represented by H3 and Mamba. The paper also includes recurrent architectures, represented by RWKV-4, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, HGRN, and HGRN2. Finally, it includes methods that the paper groups with linear Transformers, such as GLA, RetNet, and Hyena.

This grouping is important because the paper is comparing different answers to the same scaling problem. Transformers use attention and have been the dominant architecture for large language models, but they are not naturally linear in sequence length. State Space Models and several recurrent or linear-attention-inspired methods try to preserve long-context modeling while improving efficiency. The paper positions xLSTM as another family in this same broad design space, but one that comes from extending LSTM principles rather than replacing recurrence entirely.

The role of the baselines is therefore not just to provide a leaderboard. They help locate xLSTM within the current landscape of sequence modeling. If xLSTM performs well against Transformers, SSMs, RNNs, and linear Transformer-style models under the same training budget, then the paper’s claim is that LSTM ideas can still matter at large scale, provided that the architecture is modified in the right way.

# The 350M-scale comparison setting

To keep the comparison controlled, the paper matches the models to a GPT-3-size configuration of about $350$M parameters. This corresponds to an embedding dimension of $1024$ and $24$ residual blocks. The same overall model scale is used across the comparison so that architectural differences, rather than raw size differences, drive the results. This is the appropriate design if the goal is to understand the effect of the xLSTM modifications themselves.

The paper notes one subtle but important detail: only GPT-3 uses shared weights for token and output embeddings, so GPT-3 has fewer parameters than the others in practice. That means the comparison is not merely a count of parameter totals; it is a structured evaluation at a common architectural scale. This is the scale at which the paper reports that xLSTM outperforms all existing methods in validation perplexity.

The comparison also uses mixed-precision training, with a note that for RWKV-5, RWKV-6, GLA, and HGRN2 the mixed-precision setup did not rely on PyTorch automatic mixed precision. The paper includes this detail because large-scale training is sensitive to implementation choices, and the authors want to be transparent about how the competing methods were trained. Even so, the main takeaway at this scale is simple: xLSTM is the strongest method in the comparison set on validation perplexity.

# Performance of xLSTM versus GPT-style Transformers, Mamba, RWKV, HGRN2, RetNet, and Hyena

At the heart of the 15B-token study is the result that xLSTM outperforms all of the compared methods in validation perplexity. This includes GPT-style Transformers such as GPT-3 and Llama, SSMs such as H3 and Mamba, recurrent methods such as RWKV and HGRN/HGRN2, and linear Transformer-style methods such as GLA, RetNet, and Hyena. The result is significant because these baselines represent several of the strongest architectural alternatives available for language modeling at this scale.

The performance claim is not just that xLSTM is competitive, but that it leads the field within this experimental setup. Since all models are trained on the same SlimPajama data in the same autoregressive setting, the improvement cannot be attributed to a different corpus or objective. Instead, it reflects the effect of the xLSTM design changes: exponential gating, modified memory structure, and the residual block integration that makes large-scale training feasible.

The comparison also has an interpretive value. Transformers are the strongest and most widely used baseline class in modern language modeling, so outperforming them at this scale signals that xLSTM is not merely a nostalgic return to recurrence. Rather, it is a redesigned recurrent family that can hold its own against the dominant architecture family of the field. The same conclusion holds against SSMs and the other recurrent or linear methods in the comparison.

# Results for xLSTM[$1$:$0$] and xLSTM[$7$:$1$]

The paper evaluates xLSTM in more than one composition. The notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$] refers to the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. Thus xLSTM[$1$:$0$] is the pure mLSTM variant, while xLSTM[$7$:$1$] is a mixture dominated by mLSTM blocks with a smaller number of sLSTM blocks. These variants matter because they help disentangle which part of xLSTM contributes to which behavior.

In the 15B-token comparison, xLSTM as a family leads the validation perplexity results. The paper does not present the variants as interchangeable, however. They serve different roles in the architecture and can behave differently under different evaluation settings. That distinction becomes especially important later in the paper’s larger-scale experiments, but even in the 15B-token study the inclusion of both variants makes it possible to ask whether pure matrix-memory style recurrence or a hybrid of mLSTM and sLSTM offers the better tradeoff.

The broader result from the comparison is that xLSTM is not a single trick. It is a family with multiple design points, and both the pure and hybrid versions are part of the architecture explored in the paper. The reported validation results show that the xLSTM family is strong as a whole, and the ablation study clarifies why.

# Scaling behavior at this training scale

The paper does not stop at a single perplexity table. It also examines scaling behavior at the $15$B-token training scale, in the spirit of earlier scaling-law studies. The purpose is to determine whether the performance advantage of xLSTM is stable as model size changes, or whether it is only a feature of one particular parameter setting.

The reported scaling curves suggest that xLSTM will continue to perform favorably at larger model sizes. In other words, the model’s advantage is not an isolated point on a graph. Instead, the scaling behavior indicates that the architecture is learning in a way that remains beneficial as capacity increases. This is important because the paper’s broader question is about scaling LSTMs to the level of modern large language models, and scaling behavior is the most direct way to test whether that ambition is plausible.

The scaling results also support the interpretation of the validation perplexity table. If a model is already strong at the $350$M-parameter scale and its trend with scale is favorable, then the result is more convincing than a one-off gain. The paper therefore treats the scaling analysis as evidence that xLSTM is not just a tuned medium-sized model, but a candidate architecture for larger language models.

# Ablating the path from vanilla LSTM to xLSTM

The ablation study is organized as a step-by-step transformation from a vanilla LSTM into xLSTM. This is a useful experimental strategy because it isolates the contribution of each architectural decision. Rather than presenting xLSTM as a monolithic design, the paper asks how performance changes when each modification is added in sequence.

The first step is to integrate LSTM layers into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones. The second step is to extend that setup into a post up-projection block. Only after these scaffolding changes are in place does the paper add the two main xLSTM innovations: exponential gating and matrix memory. This progression matters because it shows that the final architecture is built from both modern deep-learning infrastructure and new memory mechanisms.

The ablation results show that the improvements are cumulative. Moving a vanilla LSTM into a residual backbone is already part of making it comparable to contemporary large models. The up-projection block further adapts the architecture to the style of large-scale sequence modeling used in modern systems. But the biggest conceptual changes come from the new gating and memory design, which the paper identifies as the main sources of xLSTM’s gains.

# Adding residual backbones to LSTM layers

The first ablation step is architectural rather than algorithmic: LSTM layers are inserted into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones. This matters because large language models are typically built as stacks of residual blocks, not as isolated recurrent cells. In the paper, xLSTM is not treated as a standalone recurrent module but as a component that must fit into a modern deep residual architecture.

The point of this change is to bring LSTM into the same structural environment used by contemporary large models. A residual backbone makes it possible to stack many blocks while maintaining stable optimization. By itself, this does not yet create xLSTM, but it establishes the pathway from classical recurrent networks to a form that can be scaled like today’s large models.

The ablation indicates that this modernization step is helpful, but it is not the whole story. In other words, simply embedding LSTM in a residual stack is not enough to explain the final performance. The gains continue as the architecture is further refined, which suggests that the later xLSTM-specific choices are doing important additional work.

# Adding up-projection block structure

The next ablation step introduces the up-projection block structure. The paper distinguishes two styles of residual blocks: a post up-projection block, in which the model first summarizes information and then maps it into a higher-dimensional space, and a pre up-projection block, in which the model first maps into a higher-dimensional space and then performs the nonlinear summary there. The paper uses the post version for sLSTM and the pre version for mLSTM.

This distinction is tied to the idea that high-dimensional spaces can make different histories or contexts easier to separate. The paper invokes Cover’s theorem to motivate this intuition: when nonlinear patterns are embedded in a higher-dimensional space, they are more likely to become linearly separable. In language modeling, that matters because the model must distinguish among many possible histories before predicting the next token.

In the ablation path, the introduction of the up-projection block is an important intermediate step. It adapts the LSTM from a classic recurrent cell into a block that better matches the geometry of large language models. But, again, it is not the final explanation for the performance gains. It prepares the architecture for the stronger changes that follow.

# Adding exponential gating and matrix memory

The final and most important ablation step is the addition of exponential gating and matrix memory. According to the paper, these are the two principal modifications that define xLSTM beyond the modernized residual scaffolding. The ablation results attribute the strong performance improvement to both of them.

Exponential gating changes how storage decisions are made. In the xLSTM design, gates can use exponential activations, together with normalization and stabilization. This gives the model a way to revise storage decisions more flexibly, which the paper connects to the ability to handle tasks that require state tracking. The point is not just to turn memory on or off, but to let the gating mechanism support more refined control over what is retained.

Matrix memory, on the other hand, increases storage capacity. In mLSTM, the memory cell is upgraded from a scalar to a matrix, so the model can store and retrieve richer associative information. This directly addresses one of the original LSTM’s limitations: compressing too much information into scalar memory states. The ablation therefore shows that the gains come from two complementary directions. Exponential gating improves how the model decides what to keep, and matrix memory improves how much and what kind of information can be stored.

# Gate-design ablations and the importance of input-dependent learnable gates

The paper also ablates different gating mechanisms themselves. This part of the study is important because gating is one of the central ideas inherited from the original LSTM, and xLSTM extends it rather than discarding it. The ablation asks which form of gating best supports the new architecture.

The key conclusion is that having each gate be learnable and influenced by the input has an incremental positive effect. In other words, the gates should not be static or fixed; they should adapt based on the current input, because the input is what tells the model whether the present token should update memory, preserve memory, or influence output. This is consistent with the paper’s broader argument that gating is not merely a technical detail but a mechanism for controlling memory revision.

The gate ablations therefore reinforce the central thesis of the paper. xLSTM’s gains are not produced by one isolated innovation. They depend on a gate design that can respond to the current input, on a memory system that can store richer representations, and on a residual architecture that makes these ideas work at scale. The ablations show that each of these components contributes, but the strongest effect comes from the full combination.

# What the ablations reveal about the sources of xLSTM’s gains

Taken together, the ablations tell a coherent story about where xLSTM’s performance comes from. The residual backbone and up-projection changes are important because they place LSTM in the architectural form used by modern large models. They help make the model trainable and expressive in a deep residual stack. But the paper’s results make clear that these scaffolding changes are not the main source of the final gain.

The most important improvements come from the xLSTM-specific modifications: exponential gating and matrix memory. Exponential gating strengthens the model’s ability to revise storage decisions, while matrix memory increases storage capacity and enables richer associative retrieval. The gate-design ablations further show that learnable, input-dependent gates provide additional benefits. The paper’s conclusion from these studies is that xLSTM improves because it retains the useful memory-control ideas of LSTM while removing some of the limitations that made classical LSTMs less competitive at large scale.

So the ablation study does more than rank variants. It explains the mechanism behind the improvement. The results suggest that xLSTM’s gains are not simply a consequence of having more parameters or a different residual wrapper. Instead, they arise from a better memory system and a better gating system, both of which are integrated into a large-model backbone suited to modern language modeling.
