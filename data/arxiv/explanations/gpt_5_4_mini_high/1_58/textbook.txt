\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

The Efficiency Problem in Large Language Models

# The rapid growth of LLM size and capability

Large Language Models have advanced rapidly in recent years, and with that progress has come a steady increase in model size. The paper frames this growth as a central reason LLMs have become so influential: larger models have shown strong performance across a wide range of natural language processing tasks. In other words, scale has not merely made models bigger; it has helped make them more capable.

At the same time, the paper emphasizes that this growth creates an important tradeoff. As LLMs expand, they become harder and more expensive to deploy. The very same scaling that improves quality also increases the computational and memory demands of using these systems in practice. This tension is the motivation for the paper’s focus on efficiency. The authors are not asking whether large models are useful; they are asking how we can preserve their performance while making them much cheaper to run.

This context matters because the paper is positioned around a shift in how LLMs are built. Rather than accepting the cost structure of standard full-precision models as unavoidable, the authors argue that a new low-bit design can preserve the benefits of scale while changing the economics of deployment. The problem is not the usefulness of large models, but the fact that their size has become a barrier to broader and more practical use.

# Deployment challenges: latency, memory, hardware cost, and energy use

The paper identifies several deployment challenges that arise once a model becomes large enough to be powerful. The first is latency. When a language model is used for inference, each output token must be generated through a sequence of computations, and the time per token becomes a major practical concern. If a model is slow, it is less useful in interactive settings and less efficient in any application that needs many generated tokens.

Memory is another major issue. Large models require substantial storage for their parameters, and this affects both capacity and bandwidth. A model may not only need to fit in memory; it must also be moved efficiently between different parts of the system during inference. This means memory size is not just about whether a model can be stored, but also about how quickly it can be used.

The paper also highlights hardware cost. Increasing on-chip memory, such as SRAM, can improve throughput, but doing so is significantly more expensive than relying on DRAM. As models grow, the cost of simply making the hardware capable of serving them becomes a more serious constraint. So the efficiency problem is not only about software or model design; it is also about the feasibility of the underlying deployment system.

Finally, the paper emphasizes energy use. Because the field has moved toward much larger models, the authors point out that environmental and economic concerns have become increasingly relevant. Higher energy consumption means higher operating cost, and it also makes large-scale deployment less attractive from a sustainability perspective. The paper’s central claim is that low-bit models offer a way to improve all of these factors at once: latency, memory use, hardware requirements, and energy consumption.

# Environmental and economic concerns around large-scale inference

The paper connects efficiency directly to environmental and economic concerns. As LLMs grow larger, serving them at scale consumes more energy. That energy use is not just a technical detail; it affects the cost of running systems and the broader sustainability of deploying them widely. In the paper’s framing, the more expensive a model is to operate, the harder it is to justify in many real-world settings.

This concern becomes especially important because inference is not a one-time cost. A model that is used repeatedly must continue to pay its computational and energy expense every time it generates output. The paper therefore treats inference efficiency as a first-class issue. A model that performs well but is too costly to run may still be impractical.

The authors’ response is to argue that low numerical precision is a promising route to cheaper and greener language models. Their reasoning is that if a model can maintain quality while using less precision, then the system can reduce the amount of work required to execute it. That would lower operating cost and energy usage without requiring a sacrifice in usefulness. The paper’s broader claim is that efficiency improvements are not merely a convenience; they are part of what will determine the next generation of LLMs.

# Why matrix multiplication dominates LLM computation

A key premise of the paper is that the bulk of LLM computation comes from matrix multiplication. This is important because it identifies where the main savings can be found. If most of the work is concentrated in a particular operation, then changing how that operation is performed can have an outsized effect on total efficiency.

The paper contrasts standard full-precision Transformers with the proposed BitNet-style approach by focusing on this computation. In vanilla LLMs, parameters are stored in 16-bit floating-point formats such as FP16 or BF16, and matrix multiplication relies on floating-point addition and multiplication. These arithmetic operations dominate the cost of the model because they are used heavily throughout the network.

By contrast, the paper argues that a 1-bit model architecture can dramatically simplify the computation involved in matrix multiplication. In BitNet, matrix multiplication only involves integer addition, which the paper presents as far cheaper in energy terms than the floating-point operations used in standard models. This is one reason the authors believe low-bit LLMs can be much more efficient: they target the part of the model that is used most often and costs the most.

This perspective also helps explain why the paper focuses on architectural change rather than only post-training compression. If matrix multiplication is the core cost driver, then a model designed from the start around low-bit arithmetic can do more than a model that is merely quantized after training. The paper’s efficiency argument is fundamentally about changing the computation itself.

# Why moving weights from DRAM to on-chip memory is costly

The paper also points out that computation is not the only bottleneck. During inference, model parameters must be transferred from DRAM to on-chip memory such as SRAM. This movement of weights can be expensive, and it matters because inference is only fast if the model can be fed with data quickly enough for the hardware to stay busy.

The authors emphasize that this transfer is costly in both capacity and bandwidth terms. If the model is large, the system must either store more of it closer to the compute units or repeatedly move data across memory boundaries. Both options are problematic: larger on-chip memory raises hardware cost, while frequent transfers slow the system down. So even if the computation itself were efficient, the memory path could still make deployment expensive.

This is where low-bit models become especially attractive. By reducing the memory footprint of the weights, a 1-bit LLM changes the burden on the memory system. Less data must be loaded from DRAM, which can reduce time and cost during inference. The paper treats this as one of the major practical advantages of low-bit design, alongside the benefits in arithmetic cost.

In this way, the paper makes an important point: deployment efficiency is not only about how many operations a model performs. It is also about how much data has to move, and how expensive that movement is. A smaller numerical representation can therefore improve the whole inference pipeline, not just the arithmetic inside it.

# The limitations of standard FP16 and BF16 inference

The paper presents FP16 and BF16 as the standard full-precision formats used for LLMs, but it also treats them as fundamentally costly. These representations are effective and widely used, yet they preserve a level of precision that may be more than is needed for every parameter in a large language model. The result is a system that is accurate but expensive.

One of the main limitations of FP16 and BF16 inference is that they require floating-point multiplication and addition throughout the model. Because the matrix multiplications in Transformers dominate the computation, using these higher-precision operations has a large impact on both energy and latency. The paper argues that this cost becomes increasingly significant as models scale up.

Another limitation is the memory footprint. Storing each parameter in 16-bit form takes substantially more space than storing it in a lower-bit representation. That larger footprint affects both how much hardware is required and how quickly the model can be served. Inference becomes more demanding not only because the operations are expensive, but because the data itself is bulky.

The paper also notes that although post-training quantization has become a common approach for reducing inference cost, it is still described as sub-optimal. This is important because it suggests that simply compressing a full-precision model after training is not the ideal endpoint. The authors want something more principled: a model architecture that is built to operate efficiently at low precision from the beginning.

# The central question of the paper: can ultra-low-bit LLMs match full-precision quality?

All of these concerns lead to the paper’s central question: can an ultra-low-bit language model match the quality of a full-precision one? More specifically, can a model with ternary weights, taking values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, reach the same level of perplexity and end-task performance as an FP16 or BF16 baseline while being much cheaper to run?

This question is the heart of the paper because it connects efficiency to model quality. A lower-bit model is only truly useful if it does not sacrifice the language understanding and generation performance that makes large models valuable in the first place. The paper’s contribution is to argue that this tradeoff is not as severe as one might expect. In the authors’ view, a carefully designed 1-bit LLM variant can preserve the capabilities of a full-precision Transformer while changing the cost profile dramatically.

That is why the paper introduces BitNet b1.58. The model is presented as a new answer to the efficiency problem: not just a smaller or compressed LLM, but a low-bit architecture that aims to match full-precision performance. The central idea is that the efficiency problem does not have to be solved by reducing model ambition. Instead, it may be solved by redesigning the numerical representation of the model itself.

This is what makes the paper significant. It does not treat efficiency as a secondary engineering concern. It treats efficiency as a path toward a new scaling law, one in which lower precision can support high performance at lower cost.

Foundations: Transformers, LLaMA, and Where the Cost Comes From

Large language models in this paper are treated as Transformer-based systems whose practical behavior is determined by two broad forces. The first is modeling quality: how well the model predicts text and solves downstream tasks. The second is systems cost: how much memory, latency, throughput, and energy are required to run it. This chapter focuses on the architectural ingredients that shape both sides of that trade-off. The paper’s central message is that if we understand where a Transformer spends its computation and which design choices define the LLaMA family, then the motivation for BitNet b1.58 becomes much easier to see.

# A refresher on Transformer language models

A Transformer language model is a stack of repeated blocks that process text as sequences of tokens. Each token is first turned into a vector representation, and then that representation is updated layer by layer so the model can predict the next token or otherwise model language. The paper assumes this basic Transformer setting throughout, and it also emphasizes that the large majority of the work performed by such models is matrix multiplication.

For a student encountering these models for the first time, the most important intuition is that a Transformer does not “understand” text all at once. Instead, it repeatedly transforms token representations, using learned parameters, until the final layer produces outputs that can be interpreted as predictions. The architecture is powerful because the same basic block is reused many times, and the cost grows as the model becomes larger and the sequences it processes become longer.

The paper places this standard Transformer view in the context of deployment. It notes that typical large language models are built in $16$-bit floating point formats such as FP16 or BF16, and that this choice makes both computation and memory expensive. That is why the architecture matters so much: the model’s predictive power comes from these repeated transformations, but so does most of its runtime cost.

# The role of embeddings, attention, MLP blocks, and residual pathways

The first step in a Transformer is the embedding layer. Its role is to map discrete token identifiers into continuous vectors that the rest of the network can work with. In effect, embeddings translate symbolic text into a numerical form that can be updated by the model. The paper does not focus on embeddings as the main source of cost, but they are part of the standard Transformer pipeline and are important for understanding how the model begins its computation.

Attention and MLP blocks are the two main kinds of processing inside the Transformer stack. Attention is the mechanism that lets the model combine information from different positions in the sequence. This is what gives the model its ability to relate one token to earlier or later tokens rather than treating each token in isolation. The MLP, or feed-forward block, then transforms the representation of each token more locally, enriching it with nonlinear processing. Together, these two components let the model alternate between mixing information across the sequence and refining each token’s own representation.

Residual pathways tie the whole system together. Rather than replacing a representation completely at each layer, the model carries information forward and adds new transformations on top of what it already has. This makes deep networks easier to train and helps each layer act like a refinement step instead of a total rewrite. In the paper’s framing, all of these components are part of the same larger machine whose final behavior depends on the balance between expressiveness and computational cost.

# Why linear layers are the main computational bottleneck

The paper’s cost analysis starts from a simple observation: the bulk of a large language model is matrix multiplication. In practice, this means that the linear layers inside the network are where most of the arithmetic happens. Because these layers are used repeatedly throughout the model, their cost accumulates quickly as the model scales up.

This matters because the expensive part of a standard FP16 or BF16 model is not just that it has many parameters. It is also that those parameters are used in floating-point operations, which are comparatively costly in both time and energy. The paper explicitly points out that the major computation cost comes from floating-point addition and multiplication. So when the model grows, the cost of each linear transformation grows with it, and the total inference cost is dominated by these repeated matrix operations.

The paper also highlights a second source of expense: memory movement. During inference, parameters often have to be transferred from DRAM to on-chip memory such as SRAM, and that transfer can be costly. Even if the arithmetic itself were not the bottleneck, the act of moving large parameter tensors around can slow the system down. This is why a low-memory model can offer gains in both latency and throughput, not just in storage usage. The architecture therefore has implications for both computation and data movement.

# How attention and feed-forward layers use matrix multiplication

Although attention and MLP blocks play different roles in the network, both rely heavily on matrix multiplication. Attention uses linear projections to transform representations before combining information across the sequence, and those projections are implemented as matrix operations. The feed-forward block, or MLP, also uses linear transformations to expand and then transform the token representation before producing an output. In other words, both major subcomponents of a Transformer are built on the same expensive primitive.

This is why the paper treats matrix multiplication as the central systems issue. If the same primitive appears everywhere in the model, then even modest savings in that primitive can have large effects on the overall runtime. The paper’s discussion of BitNet b1.58 makes this point especially sharp by contrasting floating-point matrix multiplication with a computation style that requires almost no multiplication operations. But even before introducing that alternative, the lesson for a standard Transformer is clear: the model’s core layers are expensive because they repeatedly apply matrix multiplication at scale.

There is also a close connection between matrix multiplication and hardware performance. Because large models spend so much time in linear layers, the design of the inference system matters a great deal. The paper’s measurements of time per output token, GPU memory use, and throughput are all rooted in the fact that these layers are the dominant cost center. Understanding that cost center is therefore a prerequisite for understanding why architectural and numerical changes can matter so much.

# Key design choices in LLaMA-style models

The paper describes LLaMA as the de-facto backbone for open-source large language models and then adopts a “LLaMA-alike” design for BitNet b1.58. The key components listed in the paper are RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and the removal of all biases. These are presented as the architectural choices that define the LLaMA family and make it familiar to the broader open-source ecosystem.

The importance of these choices in the paper is not that each one is introduced as a separate theoretical breakthrough, but that together they define a widely used and practical Transformer template. In the paper’s framing, a LLaMA-style model is not just any Transformer; it is a Transformer built with a particular set of normalization, activation, positional, and parameterization choices. Those choices make the model easy to recognize and easy to integrate into existing software stacks.

The paper also emphasizes compatibility. By using LLaMA-alike components, BitNet b1.58 can be integrated into common open-source tools such as Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp with minimal effort. This is an important point because a model family becomes influential not only when it performs well, but also when it can be adopted easily by other researchers and practitioners. In that sense, the LLaMA design is as much an ecosystem standard as it is a neural architecture.

# Why LLaMA has become the default open-source backbone

The paper explicitly states that the architecture of LLaMA has become the de-facto backbone for open-source LLMs. That means it has become the default reference point around which a large portion of the community organizes its models, code, and inference systems. The reason this matters is that a common backbone reduces friction: once a model family is widely supported, it becomes much easier to build new systems on top of it.

This is why the paper’s authors chose to adopt LLaMA-alike components in BitNet b1.58. They are “embracing the open-source community” by building on a design that people already know how to train, evaluate, and deploy. The paper links this directly to implementation practicality. Because LLaMA-style models are already supported in major open-source software, a new model that follows the same pattern can be used with less engineering work.

There is also a research reason for this choice. When a model family is widely used, comparisons become more meaningful. If many systems are built from the same backbone, then differences in performance or cost are easier to attribute to the new idea rather than to unrelated architectural changes. In the paper, this is part of why the comparison between BitNet b1.58 and the reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline is so informative: the models share a familiar Transformer foundation, so the effects of the new numerical format can be studied more cleanly.

# Quality metrics versus systems metrics in LLM evaluation

One of the most important lessons from the paper is that model quality and model cost are different axes, and both must be measured. Quality metrics ask whether the model is good at language tasks. Systems metrics ask how expensive it is to use. A model that scores well on one axis is not necessarily efficient on the other, and the paper evaluates both to show the full picture.

For quality, the paper uses validation perplexity on WikiText2 and C4, along with zero-shot accuracy on a set of language tasks including ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ. These metrics are intended to show how well a model predicts text and handles downstream reasoning or comprehension tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. In the paper’s results, these measures are crucial because they show that the model is not merely cheaper to run; it can also remain competitive in actual language performance.

For systems evaluation, the paper measures runtime GPU memory, latency, throughput, and energy consumption. It reports time per output token as a key latency measure, since that is the dominant cost for inference. It also compares GPU memory use, since the cost of loading and storing weights affects deployment. Throughput matters because it reflects how much work the system can do under a given memory budget, and energy matters because the paper is concerned with the practical and economic cost of running large models. Together, these metrics capture what it means for a model to be deployable, not just accurate.

The broader lesson is that an LLM should be evaluated as both a predictor and a system. A model may match perplexity while still being slower, larger, or more energy-hungry than another model. The paper’s framing makes this distinction central, because its main claim is that a new model family can preserve quality while reducing inference cost. That is the kind of comparison that requires both sets of metrics, and it is why understanding Transformer structure and LLaMA design is the right foundation before studying BitNet b1.58 itself.

Quantization Background and the Road to 1-bit LLMs

# What quantization is and why it matters for neural networks

Quantization, in the sense used by this paper, is the practice of representing model weights and activations with fewer bits than standard full precision. The paper frames the common baseline as FP16 or BF16 Transformer LLMs, which are the familiar 16-bit floating-point formats used in many modern systems. Reducing precision matters because LLMs are large, and the paper emphasizes that the bulk of their computation comes from matrix multiplication. When those matrix multiplications are carried out with floating-point values, both multiplication and addition contribute substantially to cost.

The reason this becomes important in practice is that language models are not only expensive to train, but also expensive to deploy. The paper points to the growing environmental and economic burden of large models, especially when inference is repeated many times. Lower precision reduces the memory footprint of the model and can reduce the amount of work needed to move data through the system. That matters not just for storage capacity, but also for bandwidth: if weights have to be transferred from DRAM to on-chip memory such as SRAM during inference, smaller weights are cheaper and faster to move. In the paper’s framing, quantization therefore affects latency, throughput, memory use, and energy consumption all at once.

# Post-training quantization for LLM inference

The paper describes post-training quantization as the most common path that the field has taken toward low-bit LLMs. In this setting, a model is first trained in full precision and then converted afterward into a lower-precision form for inference. The cited examples include SmoothQuant, GPTQ, QuIP, QuIP-Sharp, and AWQ. These methods aim to reduce the precision of weights and activations, and the paper notes that this has become a widely used industry practice.

The central appeal of post-training quantization is straightforward: it gives a cheaper inference model without requiring the entire training process to be redone. In the paper’s description, the trend has been to move from 16 bits downward, often toward 4-bit variants. That is already a substantial reduction in memory and computation relative to FP16, and it makes deployment more practical. But even in this reduced setting, post-training quantization is still an after-the-fact transformation applied to a model that was originally optimized in higher precision.

# The industry move from 16-bit models to 4-bit variants

The paper presents the move from 16-bit models to 4-bit variants as a natural response to the cost of LLM inference. As model sizes grew, so did the pressure to reduce memory use and computation, and 4-bit quantization became an attractive compromise. It preserves much of the original model structure while making inference cheaper, which explains why it has become common in practice.

At the same time, the paper uses this trend to make a larger point: 4-bit is not the end of the road. The move to lower precision demonstrates that the field is already willing to change numerical formats in pursuit of efficiency, so the next question is whether the model itself can be designed for even lower precision from the beginning. This is where the paper’s 1-bit-style approach enters. Rather than treating quantization as a compression step applied after training, the paper argues for a model family whose native training and inference format is extremely low precision.

# Why post-training quantization is often sub-optimal

The paper is explicit that post-training quantization is widely used but still sub-optimal. The reason, viewed from the paper’s perspective, is that the model was not trained to live in the quantized format. The network learned in FP16 or BF16, and only later is it forced into a lower-bit representation. That means the numerical constraints of inference are not part of the model’s original optimization problem.

This mismatch is the key limitation. A post-training method can preserve much of the original model’s behavior, but it is still trying to fit a pretrained high-precision network into a more constrained numerical space. The paper contrasts this with a different philosophy: instead of compressing a finished model, build the model around low-bit arithmetic from the start. That shift from “compress after training” to “train for the target format” is the conceptual bridge to BitNet and then to BitNet b1.58.

# Training low-bit models from scratch

The paper’s alternative to post-training quantization is to train low-bit models from scratch. In BitNet b1.58, the model is trained directly with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. This is an important distinction. The low-bit format is not a late-stage approximation of a full-precision model; it is the format the model is expected to use throughout learning and inference.

This design choice is what allows the paper to argue for a new scaling law and a new recipe for LLMs. The idea is that the model can be both high-performance and cost-effective if the low-bit constraint is built into its architecture and training procedure. The paper later reports that this approach can match FP16 baselines in perplexity and end-task performance once the model is large enough, which is exactly the kind of result that makes training from scratch worth the effort. In other words, the paper is not merely saying that low-bit inference is possible; it is saying that low-bit training can produce competitive models.

# The original BitNet idea and the BitLinear layer

BitNet is the architectural starting point for the paper. The paper describes it as a Transformer that replaces $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ with $\mathrm{BitLinear}$. That replacement matters because the linear layers are where matrix multiplication dominates the work of an LLM. If the linear layers are redesigned for low-bit computation, then the most expensive part of the network changes as well.

The paper also makes a point of staying close to the open-source Transformer ecosystem. BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components: RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and no biases. This is not just a cosmetic choice. It makes the model easier to integrate with popular software such as Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp with minimal effort. The underlying message is that the new precision format does not require abandoning the familiar Transformer design; instead, it changes the numerical implementation of that design.

# Why 1-bit-style arithmetic changes the computation paradigm

The most important computational claim in the paper is that 1-bit-style models change the nature of the work performed during inference. In a standard FP16 or BF16 LLM, the paper says that the main cost comes from floating-point addition and multiplication. In contrast, BitNet’s matrix multiplication requires almost no multiplication operations and can be highly optimized. That is a major shift, because it means the dominant cost of the model is no longer the same as in a conventional Transformer.

The paper argues that this change is especially valuable because many chips are limited by power. If a computation uses less energy, then that energy reduction can also translate into faster execution. So the benefit is not only lower energy use in a narrow sense, but also improved latency and throughput under real hardware constraints. The paper further notes that moving weights from DRAM to on-chip SRAM is expensive during inference, so a smaller memory footprint also helps by reducing the time and cost of weight loading. In this way, low-bit modeling affects both arithmetic cost and memory-system cost.

The result is a different computation paradigm, not just a smaller model. That is why the paper says 1-bit LLMs open the door to hardware and system designs specifically optimized for them. Once the computation is built around this low-bit format, specialized support becomes much more natural.

# Binary versus ternary weights and why ternary corresponds to 1.58 bits

The paper’s key technical step is moving from the original 1-bit BitNet to BitNet b1.58, where each weight is ternary: $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. The extra value, $0$, is what changes the model from a binary-like system into a ternary one. Since there are three possible states, the amount of information per weight is about $\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits. That is the meaning of the name “1.58-bit.”

The paper argues that the inclusion of $0$ improves modeling capability because it gives the model explicit support for feature filtering. A weight can now completely suppress a connection instead of only choosing between two signed states. That additional option makes the representation more expressive and, according to the paper, can significantly improve performance. This is why the ternary version is presented not as a compromise, but as an improvement over the original binary version.

The quantization rule used for the weights is an absmean function. The weight matrix is first scaled by its average absolute value and then rounded and clipped into the ternary set:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))),
\]
and
\[
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|.
\]
The intuition is that the model first normalizes the scale of the weights and then forces each entry to the nearest allowed ternary value. For activations, the paper keeps the BitNet-style approach but changes the scaling so that activations are mapped to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token rather than using zero-point quantization. The paper emphasizes that this is easier to implement and more convenient for system-level optimization, while having negligible effect on performance in their experiments.

Taken together, these choices show the path from conventional quantization to the paper’s 1-bit LLM design. The broader lesson is that low precision does not have to be a late-stage approximation of a full-precision model. It can be the native format of the model itself, with the architecture, training recipe, and inference system all built around that assumption.

From BitNet to BitNet b1.58

# The core idea of BitNet b1.58

The central idea of the paper is that a large language model does not need full-precision weights to remain competitive. Instead of storing each parameter as an $16$-bit floating-point number, the authors propose a model in which every weight is restricted to one of three values: $-1$, $0$, or $+1$. They call this model \textbf{BitNet b1.58}. The name reflects the fact that the weights are ternary rather than continuous, so the model sits between a purely binary representation and a more conventional floating-point model.

This is a more radical step than ordinary post-training quantization. In post-training quantization, a model is first trained in full precision and then compressed afterward for inference. The paper argues that this is useful, but still suboptimal. BitNet b1.58 instead changes the model architecture itself and trains it from scratch under the low-bit constraint. The goal is not merely to squeeze a pretrained model into a smaller format, but to define a new kind of language model that is efficient by design.

The important claim is that this design is not just cheaper. According to the paper, it can match a full-precision Transformer LLM of the same size and trained on the same number of tokens in both perplexity and end-task performance, while using much less memory and energy. In other words, the paper presents ternary weights as a practical design point rather than a compromise that inevitably ruins model quality.

# Replacing every nn.Linear layer with BitLinear

BitNet b1.58 is based on the BitNet architecture, which is a Transformer that replaces every \emph{nn.Linear} layer with a corresponding \emph{BitLinear} layer. This replacement matters because linear layers are the core computational blocks in a Transformer, and the bulk of the model’s work comes from matrix multiplication. If the linear layers become low-bit, then the biggest cost in the network is altered, not just a minor part of it.

The intuition is that standard LLM inference is dominated by floating-point matrix multiplications and the associated data movement. In BitNet b1.58, the matrix multiplication path is redesigned so that it requires almost no multiplication operations. The paper emphasizes that this creates a new computation paradigm in which integer addition becomes the dominant primitive. That change is significant because arithmetic energy cost is strongly affected by the type of operation, and the model’s cheaper arithmetic can translate into lower power use and faster execution.

The model is trained from scratch with $1.58$-bit weights and $8$-bit activations. The paper also notes that the design is intended to fit into the software ecosystems used by the open-source community, such as Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp, with minimal effort. So the replacement of \emph{nn.Linear} by \emph{BitLinear} is not only an architectural statement; it is also meant to support deployment.

# Using 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations

The paper constrains the weights by applying a quantization function based on the average absolute value of the weight matrix. If $W$ is the weight matrix, the quantized version is defined as

$$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
$$

where

$$
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))),
$$

and

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
$$

The effect of this procedure is straightforward: the weights are first normalized by their average magnitude, and then each value is rounded and clipped into the set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. The use of the average absolute value is important because it gives the matrix a natural scale before discretization. Without such scaling, a hard rounding rule would be too sensitive to the raw size of the weights.

The activations are also quantized, but the paper keeps them at $8$ bits. This is consistent with the original BitNet framework, though the authors make a small but meaningful change: instead of scaling activations into the range $[0, Q_b]$ before the nonlinear functions, they scale them into $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token. This removes zero-point quantization. The paper presents this as a simpler and more convenient choice for implementation and system-level optimization, and it reports that the change has negligible effect on performance in their experiments.

So the $1.58$-bit idea is not that every part of the network is equally compressed. Rather, the weights are made extremely discrete, while activations retain enough precision to keep the model numerically workable. The result is a model that is highly constrained where it matters most for storage and arithmetic cost, but still functional as a language model.

# Why adding 0 to $\{-1, +1\}$ is important

The jump from the original 1-bit BitNet to BitNet b1.58 comes from adding the value $0$ to the weight alphabet. Instead of forcing every parameter to be either $-1$ or $+1$, the model allows weights to be ternary: $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This may look like a small change, but the paper treats it as a major improvement because it gives the model an explicit way to suppress connections.

A purely binary weight can only indicate two directions of influence. A ternary weight adds a third possibility: no influence at all. That extra state matters because it gives the network more flexibility in how it uses its parameters. In the paper’s framing, this leads to stronger modeling capability, because the model can filter features rather than being forced to express every relationship as either positive or negative.

This is one of the reasons the authors believe BitNet b1.58 improves over the earlier 1-bit design without giving up the efficiency benefits. The zero state is not introduced as a cosmetic change; it is introduced because it improves expressiveness while preserving the low-bit arithmetic structure. In short, the added $0$ provides a middle ground between dense binary interaction and full-precision freedom.

# Feature filtering and sparsity-like behavior through zero weights

The paper specifically connects the zero weight to feature filtering. If a weight is $0$, then that connection does not contribute to the matrix multiplication. In effect, the model can turn off selected interactions entirely. This is not described as post-hoc pruning, where weights are removed after training. It is built into the parameterization itself and learned as part of training.

This gives the model behavior that is similar in spirit to sparsity. Not every possible connection has to be active. Instead, the model can decide that certain features should be ignored. That ability is useful because language modeling does not require every hidden feature to interact with every other feature in every layer. Some connections are simply unnecessary for a given input or representational pattern, and the ternary format allows the model to express that directly.

The authors argue that this explicit support for feature filtering is one reason BitNet b1.58 is stronger than the earlier BitNet variant. It improves the model’s capacity to specialize its internal computation while staying within a discrete and highly efficient arithmetic format. So the zero state is not just a storage convenience; it changes what kinds of patterns the model can represent.

# Expected benefits in latency, memory, throughput, and energy

The paper’s motivation is not only to reduce model size, but to reduce the cost of actually using the model. In standard LLMs, the major computation cost comes from floating-point arithmetic in matrix multiplication. By contrast, BitNet b1.58 replaces much of that computation with integer addition, which is much cheaper in energy terms. The paper emphasizes that, on modern chips, power is often the fundamental limit to performance, so lower energy use can also mean faster execution.

Memory is the other major advantage. During inference, model parameters must be transferred from DRAM to on-chip memory such as SRAM, and this transfer can be expensive. A ternary weight representation has a much smaller footprint than full precision, so both capacity and bandwidth requirements fall. That means the model can be loaded more quickly and can run more efficiently once loaded. The paper also notes that enlarging SRAM to compensate for memory bottlenecks is costly, so reducing the footprint is an especially attractive alternative.

The experimental section supports these expectations with concrete numbers. At the $3$B scale, BitNet b1.58 matches the full-precision baseline in perplexity while being $2.71$ times faster and using $3.55$ times less GPU memory. At $3.9$B, it is $2.4$ times faster and uses $3.32$ times less memory while outperforming the $3$B full-precision baseline. The paper also reports that at $70$B, BitNet b1.58 is $4.1$ times faster than the LLaMA baseline, and that throughput can be much higher as well: in one comparison, a $70$B BitNet b1.58 model supports up to $11$ times the batch size of the full-precision baseline, yielding $8.9$ times higher throughput.

The energy story follows the same pattern. The paper estimates arithmetic energy consumption for matrix multiplication and reports that BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4$ times arithmetic energy on $7$nm chips relative to the FP16 baseline. Because the proportion of \emph{nn.Linear} cost grows with model size, the efficiency advantage becomes more pronounced as models scale up. This is why the paper describes BitNet b1.58 as enabling a new scaling law in which larger models can still be deployed efficiently.

# How BitNet b1.58 differs from the original 1-bit BitNet

BitNet b1.58 inherits the basic BitNet idea of building a Transformer around low-bit linear layers, but it is not identical to the original 1-bit version. The most obvious change is the weight alphabet: the original version is 1-bit, whereas BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. That one extra state is the main architectural change, and the paper argues that it improves modeling quality while preserving the low-cost arithmetic structure.

There are also implementation-level differences in the activation quantization. As noted earlier, BitNet b1.58 scales activations to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token instead of using a zero-point scheme. This is presented as simpler and more convenient for implementation and system optimization. In addition, the paper adopts LLaMA-alike components: RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and no biases. These choices help the model fit naturally into the open-source Transformer ecosystem.

The paper is careful to emphasize that BitNet b1.58 retains the core benefits of the original BitNet, including the new computation paradigm and the reduced energy cost. But it also claims two improvements over the earlier design: stronger modeling capability because of the zero weight, and better memory efficiency because the weights are ternary in a way that supports competitive performance. So BitNet b1.58 is not a departure from BitNet; it is a refinement of it.

# The paper's claim that $1.58$-bit LLMs can match FP16 baselines

The strongest claim in the paper is that BitNet b1.58 can match full-precision baselines at the same model size and with the same training token budget. To support this claim, the authors pretrain models on the RedPajama dataset for $100$ billion tokens and evaluate them on zero-shot language tasks such as ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ. They also report validation perplexity on WikiText2 and C4.

The reported pattern is that the performance gap closes as the model size increases. Starting from the $3$B scale, BitNet b1.58 matches the full-precision baseline in both perplexity and end-task performance. At larger sizes, such as $3.9$B, it can even outperform the smaller FP16 baseline while being cheaper to run. The paper summarizes this as a Pareto improvement: the model is not just comparable in quality, but also better along deployment-relevant axes such as memory and latency.

The authors further test token scaling by training a BitNet b1.58 model on $2$T tokens using the data recipe of StableLM-3B. On a benchmark containing Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC-Easy, the ternary model outperforms StableLM-3B across all tasks. This supports the paper’s broader conclusion that $1.58$-bit LLMs have strong generalization ability, not merely good compression behavior.

Taken together, these results justify the paper’s central message: low-bit weights do not have to mean weak language models. In the authors’ view, BitNet b1.58 shows that a ternary Transformer can be both high-performing and deployment-friendly, and that this combination may define a new generation of large language models.

How BitNet b1.58 Quantizes Weights and Activations

# The goal of constraining weights to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$

BitNet b1.58 is built around a very simple idea: instead of storing each weight as a full-precision floating-point number, it restricts every weight to one of three values, $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. The paper describes this as a ternary weight representation, and it is the defining feature of the model’s $1.58$-bit setting. The motivation is not only compression. The authors want a model that keeps the advantages of the original 1-bit BitNet computation style while improving modeling power by allowing a weight to be exactly zero.

This ternary form gives each parameter a clear interpretation. A weight of $1$ acts as a positive connection, a weight of $-1$ acts as a negative connection, and a weight of $0$ acts as a filtered-out connection. The paper emphasizes that the inclusion of $0$ gives explicit support for feature filtering, which is one reason BitNet b1.58 can model more effectively than a purely binary scheme. In other words, the model is not forced to choose only between pushing a feature up or pushing it down; it can also choose to suppress it entirely.

The paper also frames this choice as part of a broader computation paradigm. Since matrix multiplication is the dominant operation in Transformer models, reducing the weight values to a tiny discrete set makes the arithmetic itself far simpler. BitNet b1.58 keeps the original BitNet idea that matrix multiplication should require almost no multiplication operations, and that low-precision arithmetic can be highly optimized. The ternary constraint is therefore both a modeling choice and a systems choice.

# The absmean quantization rule

To force weights into this ternary set, the paper uses what it calls an absmean quantization function. The basic idea is to first normalize the weight matrix by a scale derived from the matrix itself, and then round the normalized values to the nearest ternary value. This makes the quantizer adaptive to the magnitude of the weights in each matrix, rather than using a fixed global threshold.

The quantization rule is written as
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right).
\]
Here, $W$ is the full-precision weight matrix, and $\widetilde{W}$ is the quantized ternary matrix. The term $\gamma + \epsilon$ is a scaling factor, where $\gamma$ is the average absolute value of the entries of $W$, and $\epsilon$ is a small stabilizing constant. Dividing by this scale makes the weights comparable across matrices of different sizes or magnitudes before the rounding step is applied.

The name “absmean” reflects exactly how the scale is chosen: it is based on the mean of the absolute values of the weights. This is important because it gives a simple summary of the matrix’s typical magnitude. Rather than depending on outliers or on signed cancellation, the quantizer uses a quantity that measures how large the weights usually are in absolute terms. The result is a rule that is easy to state, easy to compute, and directly tailored to ternary quantization.

# Computing $\gamma$ as the average absolute value of the weight matrix

The paper defines the scale factor $\gamma$ as
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
If the matrix has shape $n \times m$, then $\gamma$ is simply the average of the absolute values of all its entries. This means every element in the matrix contributes equally to the scale estimate. A matrix with larger typical weights will have a larger $\gamma$, and a matrix with smaller typical weights will have a smaller $\gamma$.

This choice matters because it makes the quantization relative rather than absolute. If the weights in one layer are naturally larger than those in another, then the same threshold would not work equally well for both. By normalizing with the average absolute value, the model turns the raw weights into a dimensionless quantity whose scale is controlled before rounding. The paper presents this as a practical way to keep the quantization consistent across matrices.

In the paper’s formulation, the scaling is done with $\gamma + \epsilon$ rather than just $\gamma$. The role of $\epsilon$ is numerical stability, and it protects the computation in cases where the average absolute value becomes very small. This is a standard kind of safeguard in quantization formulas: it prevents division by a value that could be too close to zero and makes the quantizer well behaved in all layers. The paper does not present $\epsilon$ as a major modeling ingredient; it is there to ensure the quantization rule can be applied safely.

# The formula $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(W / (\gamma + \epsilon), -1, 1)$

Once the weights have been scaled, the quantizer applies a rounding-and-clipping operator:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right).
\]
This formula summarizes the entire ternary mapping in one line. The scaled weight values are first rounded to the nearest integer, and then any result outside the interval $[-1, 1]$ is clipped back into that interval. Since the only integers in that range are $-1$, $0$, and $1$, the final quantized weights are ternary by construction.

The paper also defines the operator itself as
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))).
\]
This expression is easy to read step by step. First, $\mathrm{round}(x)$ maps a real number to the nearest integer. Then $\min(b,\cdot)$ makes sure the rounded value is not larger than the upper bound $b$, and $\max(a,\cdot)$ makes sure it is not smaller than the lower bound $a$. When $a=-1$ and $b=1$, the operator produces exactly the ternary set that BitNet b1.58 wants.

The intuition is straightforward. Values near $-1$ survive as $-1$, values near $0$ become $0$, and values near $1$ survive as $1$. Values with larger magnitude are not allowed to grow beyond the ternary range; they are clipped. This means the quantizer is deliberately coarse, but also controlled. The model does not try to preserve every floating-point detail. Instead, it compresses the weight matrix into a small discrete code that still supports useful computation.

# The role of $\epsilon$ in numerical stability

Although the paper treats $\epsilon$ as a small term, it is essential for making the formula robust. Since $\gamma$ is the average absolute value of the weights, it could in principle be very small for some matrices. Dividing by $\gamma$ alone would then amplify the weights too much or become numerically unstable. Adding $\epsilon$ avoids this problem by ensuring the denominator is never exactly zero and never dangerously close to zero.

The presence of $\epsilon$ is a good example of how low-precision methods still need careful engineering. Even when the final representation is ternary, the quantization rule itself is computed from floating-point values during training or weight conversion. That intermediate step must remain stable. The paper does not make $\epsilon$ a conceptual centerpiece, but it is part of the precise formula that makes the quantizer practical.

In this sense, $\epsilon$ supports the paper’s broader goal of a simple but reliable quantization pipeline. The ternary mapping is meant to be easy to implement and easy to optimize, and numerical stability is a prerequisite for both. Without a stabilizer, the quantizer could behave unpredictably on small-magnitude matrices. With $\epsilon$, the authors preserve the intended behavior while protecting the computation.

# Activation quantization with 8-bit values

Weights are only one side of the quantization scheme. BitNet b1.58 also uses 8-bit activations. The paper states that the model is trained from scratch with $1.58$-bit weights and $8$-bit activations, so the activations are still low precision, but they are not reduced to the same ternary format as the weights. This is an important part of the overall design: the most aggressive compression is applied to the parameters, while the activations remain at a higher precision level that is still efficient.

The activation quantization follows the general implementation used in BitNet, but with one key change. The paper does not scale activations before the nonlinear functions into the range $[0, Q_b]$; instead, it scales them to the symmetric interval $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token. This preserves an 8-bit quantization scheme while making the range centered at zero.

That design choice reflects the authors’ preference for a cleaner symmetric formulation. Since activations now occupy a balanced interval around zero, the quantization rule becomes simpler to express and simpler to optimize at the system level. The paper presents this as a practical implementation advantage rather than a radical change in the model’s behavior. The goal is to keep the activation pipeline lightweight without introducing extra complications.

# Symmetric per-token scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$

The phrase “per token” is important. The scaling is not described as a global scale shared by the whole model; it is applied token by token. That means each token’s activations are normalized according to its own range before being quantized into the 8-bit interval. In the paper’s description, this is the mechanism used to move the activations into $[-Q_b, Q_b]$.

Symmetry around zero is the key mathematical property here. By centering the range at zero, the model avoids the asymmetric structure that would come from mapping activations into $[0, Q_b]$. The paper explicitly says this removes the need for zero-point quantization. In practical terms, the activations are represented in a way that is more convenient for implementation and system-level optimization.

This symmetric choice is not presented as a compromise on accuracy. Instead, the authors state that the effect on performance is negligible in their experiments. That is important because it means the simplification does not come at a meaningful cost in the reported setting. The paper’s message is that the activation scheme can be made cleaner and more hardware-friendly while keeping the model’s behavior essentially unchanged.

# Removing zero-point quantization and its impact on performance

Zero-point quantization is the part of an asymmetric quantization scheme that introduces an offset in addition to a scale. BitNet b1.58 avoids that extra offset by using symmetric scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$. The authors explicitly say this is more convenient and simpler for both implementation and system-level optimization. In other words, the quantizer becomes easier to work with because it does not need to carry around an extra zero-point parameter.

This simplification is especially meaningful in a model that already aims to minimize arithmetic cost. The paper’s broader theme is that BitNet b1.58 supports a new computation paradigm, so every simplification in the quantization pipeline matters. Removing zero-point quantization means fewer special cases in the implementation and a more regular representation for activations. That regularity is exactly the kind of property that can help low-level optimization.

At the same time, the paper is careful to say that this change has negligible effects on performance in their experiments. That statement is important because it shows the authors are not trading away model quality for implementation convenience. Instead, they claim that the symmetric activation design gives them a cleaner system without measurably harming the model under the conditions they tested. This fits the overall message of BitNet b1.58: the quantization scheme is designed to be both practical and effective.

Architecture Choices and the Training Recipe

BitNet b1.58 is presented in the paper as a carefully controlled redesign of a Transformer language model rather than a wholesale architectural reinvention. The key move is to keep the familiar LLaMA-style backbone and replace the standard linear layers with low-bit counterparts, while training the model from scratch under those low-bit constraints. This matters because the paper is not trying to compress a finished model after the fact; it is trying to make low-bit computation part of the model’s native training recipe. In that sense, the architecture and the training procedure are inseparable.

# Why the model is trained from scratch instead of converted after training

The paper starts from the observation that post-training quantization is widely used, but still sub-optimal. That approach takes a model that was learned in full precision and then forces it into a lower-precision representation for inference. BitNet b1.58 takes the opposite route: it is trained from scratch with ternary weights and $8$-bit activations. This means the network learns while already living inside the numerical limits that it will use at deployment time.

That design choice is important because the model does not merely “survive” quantization after learning; it adapts to quantization during learning. The paper’s low-bit formulation is therefore not a compression layer added at the end of training, but the actual form of the model during optimization. Since the bulk of LLM computation is matrix multiplication, making those operations low-bit from the beginning directly changes the training and inference regime, rather than only changing the final stored checkpoint.

The paper also makes clear why the model is described as a $1.58$-bit system rather than a pure $1$-bit model. The weights are ternary, taking values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Because there are three possible states, the effective information content is $\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits per weight. This ternary design is the foundation of BitNet b1.58, and the paper argues that training from scratch is the right way to make such a discrete parameterization work.

# The ternary quantization rule and the meaning of 1.58 bits

To constrain the weights to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the paper uses an absmean quantization function. The idea is to first compute a scale for the weight matrix using its average absolute value, and then map each entry to the nearest ternary value after rescaling. The quantizer is written as
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))),
\]
and
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]

This formula is simple, but its role is central. The average absolute value $\gamma$ gives the matrix a natural scale before rounding, so the quantizer does not treat every layer as if it had the same numerical range. After scaling, each weight is rounded and clipped to one of three allowed values. The result is a parameter matrix that can be interpreted as positive, negative, or inactive.

The addition of the zero state is especially important. The paper explicitly says that this extra value gives the model stronger modeling capability because it supports feature filtering. In other words, the model is not forced to express everything with just a positive or negative sign; it can also suppress a feature entirely. The authors present this as one reason why BitNet b1.58 can outperform a plain binary formulation.

The activation quantization follows the BitNet implementation, but with one important simplification. Instead of scaling activations to $[0, Q_b]$, the paper scales them per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, removing zero-point quantization. The paper describes this as more convenient and simpler for implementation and system-level optimization, and reports negligible performance impact in its experiments. That choice fits the broader training recipe: keep the numerical path simple enough to optimize efficiently, while retaining the low-bit behavior that makes the model cost-effective.

# Using LLaMA-alike components for compatibility and fairness

The paper is explicit that it adopts LLaMA-alike components because LLaMA has become the de-facto backbone for open-source LLMs. This is not a cosmetic choice. It is a way of making BitNet b1.58 easy to understand, easy to compare, and easy to use inside the existing ecosystem. The architecture uses RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and rotary embedding, and removes all biases. Those are all standard ingredients in the LLaMA family, so the low-bit model stays close to a familiar reference point.

This design also serves a fairness purpose. If the authors had introduced a very different Transformer recipe at the same time as the low-bit weights, it would be hard to know where the gains or losses were coming from. By keeping the backbone familiar, they isolate the effect of the quantization scheme itself. The paper’s comparisons to a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline are meaningful precisely because the underlying architectural family is held fixed as much as possible.

There is also a practical benefit: the authors say that this LLaMA-like design allows BitNet b1.58 to be integrated into open-source software with minimal effort. That matters because a low-bit architecture is only useful if it can live inside the tools people already use. By staying close to LLaMA, the model can fit into the same software and inference workflows rather than requiring a separate ecosystem.

# RMSNorm and its role in the recipe

RMSNorm is one of the LLaMA-alike components the paper keeps unchanged. The authors do not introduce a new normalization scheme for BitNet b1.58; instead, they retain the normalization choice already used in the LLaMA family. In the context of the paper, that decision is less about novelty and more about preserving a known transformer structure while changing only the precision of the linear computations.

For a student reading the paper, the important point is methodological. When a model is trained from scratch with ternary weights, there are already many sources of difficulty in optimization. Keeping the normalization module familiar reduces the number of variables being changed at once. The paper does not provide a separate analysis of RMSNorm, but its inclusion signals that the authors wanted the low-bit model to inherit the same architectural scaffold as the full-precision baseline.

That is why RMSNorm belongs in the training recipe even though it is not the main contribution. It is one of the components that makes the low-bit model a drop-in member of the LLaMA style of Transformers, rather than a new architecture with an unfamiliar normalization path.

# SwiGLU as the feed-forward nonlinearity

The paper also keeps SwiGLU as the feed-forward nonlinearity. Again, this is not presented as a novel invention in BitNet b1.58. It is part of the LLaMA-alike backbone that the authors deliberately preserve. The feed-forward block therefore remains a nonlinear module in the standard Transformer sense, even though its linear projections are replaced by low-bit versions.

This choice helps keep the model expressive while the numerical format is made much more restrictive. If the authors had changed both the precision and the feed-forward design, then any change in performance would be hard to interpret. By retaining SwiGLU, they keep the nonlinear structure of the block close to the baseline and make the low-bit linear layers the main variable under study.

Seen this way, SwiGLU is part of the experimental control. It ensures that BitNet b1.58 is not “less of a Transformer” just because its weights are ternary. The architecture still has the nonlinear feed-forward machinery expected in a modern LLM; only the numerical representation of that machinery has been changed.

# Rotary positional embeddings and long-range token interactions

Rotary embedding is the positional mechanism the paper retains from the LLaMA family. Its role here is to give the model the same position-handling strategy as the baseline architecture. Since language modeling depends on representing token order inside attention, keeping rotary embedding preserves the sequence-processing style that open-source Transformer models already rely on.

The paper does not present rotary embedding as a new research contribution, but as one more element of the familiar backbone. That matters for two reasons. First, it keeps the architectural comparison with FP16 LLaMA clean. Second, it makes the low-bit model easier to adopt in existing code and workflows, because the positional mechanism is already part of the standard LLaMA-like design.

For long-range token interactions, the key point in the paper is continuity rather than redesign. BitNet b1.58 is not changing the way sequence positions are represented in order to prove a point about quantization. It is keeping the same positional framework while showing that ternary weights can still support strong language modeling performance.

# Removing biases from the architecture

The paper states that it removes all biases. This is another part of the LLaMA-alike recipe, and it helps keep the architecture aligned with the open-source Transformer family the authors want to build on. The bias-free design is not discussed as a new low-bit trick; it is simply part of the model form that the paper adopts.

That choice is important in a low-bit setting because it reduces architectural variation. A bias term might seem small compared with the rest of a large model, but from the standpoint of experimental design, every extra difference between the baseline and the new model makes interpretation harder. By removing biases, the authors keep the model simpler and closer to the baseline structure they are comparing against.

The result is a cleaner low-bit Transformer. The model still has the same general computational skeleton, but the linear operators are now low-bit and the overall parameterization is more uniform. That uniformity helps both in comparison and in implementation.

# Why keeping the backbone familiar isolates the effect of quantization

One of the most important ideas in the paper is that architectural familiarity is a way of isolating the effect of low-bit quantization. The authors compare BitNet b1.58 to a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline at the same model sizes and training token budget. In the reported experiments, both models are pre-trained on the RedPajama dataset for $100$ billion tokens. Because the backbone is intentionally LLaMA-like, the comparison is not confounded by a completely different Transformer recipe.

This matters because the paper’s main claim is about the value of the numerical format, not about inventing a new model family. If the low-bit model performs well, the reader can attribute that performance to the ternary weights, the $8$-bit activations, and the BitLinear replacement for `nn.Linear`, rather than to a new normalization rule, a new positional scheme, or a new feed-forward design. That is what makes the results persuasive as evidence for a new low-bit scaling recipe.

In textbook terms, this is a controlled experiment in architecture form. The paper keeps the familiar parts of the model fixed so that the low-bit parts are the only major change. That is why the authors can make strong claims about the quality and efficiency of BitNet b1.58 without having to argue that every part of the Transformer has been redesigned.

# Embeddings remaining in full precision and their impact on total memory

The paper notes an important exception to the low-bit story: the embeddings remain full precision. This means BitNet b1.58 is not completely ternary end to end. Even though the core linear layers are heavily quantized, some memory is still spent on parameters that are not compressed all the way down to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

This detail helps explain the memory results in the paper. The authors observe that the memory consumption trend still improves strongly with scale, because the embedding becomes a smaller fraction of the total model memory as the model gets larger. In larger models, the low-bit savings from the many linear layers dominate more and more of the overall footprint. So even though embeddings stay in full precision, the model still becomes much more memory-efficient at scale.

For a reader trying to understand the design, this is a useful reminder that low-bit LLMs are not necessarily uniformly low-bit in every parameter tensor. The important point is that the dominant cost centers are reduced enough to yield major gains, while some components, like embeddings, are left unchanged for practical reasons.

# Ease of integration with HuggingFace, vLLM, and llama.cpp

Because BitNet b1.58 follows the LLaMA-alike recipe, the paper says it can be integrated into popular open-source software with minimal effort. The authors explicitly name HuggingFace, vLLM, and llama.cpp. That is a strong practical signal: the model is not meant to be a laboratory curiosity, but a design that can fit into the tooling people already rely on for training and inference.

This integration story depends on the architecture choices discussed above. By preserving RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and the bias-free Transformer structure, the paper avoids the need for a brand-new software stack. The main architectural replacement is `nn.Linear` becoming `BitLinear`, while the rest of the model remains recognizable to existing systems.

That is why the chapter’s theme is not just “low-bit arithmetic,” but “low-bit arithmetic inside a familiar model family.” The familiarity is what makes the model practical. It lowers the barrier to adoption, makes comparisons cleaner, and supports the paper’s broader claim that a 1.58-bit LLM can be both high-performance and easy to deploy.

Experimental Design and Evaluation Protocol

A fair evaluation of BitNet b1.58 has to answer two different questions at once. First, does replacing full-precision weights with $1.58$-bit ternary weights preserve language modeling quality? Second, does that change actually reduce serving cost in practice? The paper’s experimental design is built to separate those two issues as cleanly as possible. It does so by comparing BitNet b1.58 to a reproduced FP16 LLaMA-like baseline under matched training conditions, by using standard perplexity and zero-shot benchmarks for quality, and by measuring inference cost with the same GPU benchmarking tools for both models.

# The Reproduced FP16 LLaMA Baseline

The paper does not compare BitNet b1.58 to an arbitrary full-precision model. Instead, it reproduces an FP16 LLaMA LLM and uses that as the baseline. This choice matters because BitNet b1.58 itself adopts LLaMA-alike components, so the baseline sits in the same architectural family and serves as a natural full-precision reference point.

Using a reproduced baseline is also a methodological safeguard. If the baseline came from a different training recipe or a different implementation, then any performance gap could be caused by many factors besides precision. By reproducing the FP16 LLaMA model, the authors make the comparison closer to a controlled experiment: the main conceptual difference is that one model uses full-precision linear layers, while the other uses BitNet b1.58’s low-bit variant.

# Matching Model Size and Training Tokens for Fair Comparison

The central fairness principle in the paper is matching scale. The authors compare models with the same size and the same number of training tokens, rather than allowing one model to benefit from more parameters or more data. This is crucial because large language model performance is highly sensitive to both of those axes. If either changes, then a performance difference becomes difficult to interpret.

In other words, the evaluation is designed so that precision format is the main thing that changes. Under that setup, any gain in memory, latency, throughput, or energy can be attributed to the low-bit representation and its associated computation style, rather than to a hidden advantage in scale. This is the core reason the paper can make a meaningful claim about BitNet b1.58 as a model family rather than just as a compressed implementation.

# Pretraining on RedPajama for $100$ Billion Tokens

Both BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 baseline are pre-trained on the RedPajama dataset for $100$ billion tokens. That shared training corpus and token budget are the backbone of the comparison. They ensure that both models see the same amount of data during pretraining, which prevents the evaluation from being skewed by one model simply having had more exposure to text.

This choice also makes the quality results easier to interpret. Because the two models are trained under the same data recipe, the paper can ask whether the lower-precision architecture keeps up with the full-precision model when the training process itself is held constant. For a first-time reader, this is the key idea to watch for in the paper’s tables: the comparison is intended to be like-for-like.

# Model Sizes Evaluated in the Paper

The paper studies several model sizes rather than a single configuration. In the direct quality comparison, it highlights $3$B and $3.9$B models, and for the system-level scaling study it extends to $7$B, $13$B, $30$B, and $70$B. This range is important because it shows whether the same low-bit design behaves consistently as the model grows.

Looking at multiple scales also reveals whether the benefits are limited to small models or become more pronounced at larger ones. The paper uses these sizes to examine where BitNet b1.58 begins to match the full-precision baseline in quality and how its inference cost evolves as the model gets bigger. That scaling view is part of the paper’s broader claim that the low-bit design changes the tradeoff between model size and serving cost.

# Validation Perplexity on WikiText2 and C4

To measure language modeling quality, the paper reports validation perplexity on WikiText2 and C4. Perplexity is the paper’s way of checking how well a model predicts held-out text: lower perplexity means the model is assigning more probability to the observed text, which indicates better fit. In practice, this gives a direct readout of whether the model is still strong at the core language modeling objective.

Using two datasets also makes the evaluation less dependent on one particular corpus. WikiText2 and C4 provide separate validation views of model behavior, so the reported perplexity is not tied to only one test set. The paper uses these scores alongside downstream task accuracy, which is helpful because a model can sometimes look strong on one kind of measure but not the other.

# Zero-Shot Evaluation Tasks

The paper evaluates zero-shot performance on ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ. Because these are zero-shot tests, the model is evaluated on the tasks without task-specific training. That makes the benchmarks a good way to see how much general problem-solving ability the pretrained model has retained.

Taken together, the task suite is broad enough to probe different aspects of language understanding and reasoning. The paper does not rely on a single benchmark to make its case. Instead, it checks whether the pattern seen in perplexity also shows up across a varied set of end tasks, which is exactly what one wants in a serious comparison between a new model family and a full-precision baseline.

# Using lm-evaluation-harness for Consistent Task Evaluation

To make the zero-shot comparison consistent, the authors follow the pipeline from lm-evaluation-harness. The value of a shared harness is that it standardizes how the tasks are run and scored, so the evaluation does not depend on ad hoc prompt handling or model-specific conveniences. For a reader learning to read research papers, this is an important experimental detail: consistency in the evaluation pipeline is what makes benchmark numbers comparable.

The use of a common harness also helps reproducibility. If two models are tested under the same framework, then differences in accuracy are more likely to reflect the models themselves rather than differences in evaluation code. In a paper whose central claim is that a low-bit model can match a full-precision one, that kind of consistency is essential.

# Runtime Benchmarking with FasterTransformer

For system-level results, the paper measures runtime on GPU using FasterTransformer. This choice is significant because FasterTransformer is an optimized inference codebase for language models, so the benchmark is aimed at practical serving cost rather than a toy implementation. The main runtime metric is latency, and the paper reports the time per output token because token generation is the dominant cost during inference.

Using a single inference framework for both models makes the comparison more meaningful. It reduces the chance that one model appears faster simply because it was implemented more efficiently. Instead, the benchmark is intended to highlight the real system advantage of BitNet b1.58’s low-bit computation under a high-performance GPU inference stack.

# Integrating Ladder’s $2$-Bit Kernel for BitNet b1.58

Because BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights, it benefits from specialized low-bit kernels. The paper therefore integrates the $2$-bit kernel from Ladder for BitNet b1.58. This is an implementation detail, but it is an important one: low-bit models do not become efficient automatically. They still need kernels that can exploit their representation efficiently at runtime.

The paper also notes that both latency and memory were measured with this $2$-bit kernel, which means the reported system gains already reflect a reasonably optimized implementation. At the same time, the authors point out that there is still room for further optimization. That is an important reminder that system benchmarks are often lower bounds on what a future specialized implementation might achieve.

# Measuring Time per Output Token, GPU Memory, Throughput, and Estimated Energy

The paper evaluates several different cost measures because no single runtime number captures everything. Time per output token is used as the main latency metric. GPU memory captures how expensive it is to hold and use the model during inference. Throughput asks how much work can be done per unit time when the system is run at larger batch sizes. Energy estimates how expensive the computation is in physical terms, not just in seconds or bytes.

For throughput, the paper benchmarks the $70$B models on two $80$GB A100 cards using pipeline parallelism so that the FP16 baseline can fit on the devices. The batch size is increased until the GPU memory limit is reached, with a sequence length of $512$. This setup shows the practical serving advantage of the low-bit model, since larger batch sizes directly translate into higher throughput when memory is the bottleneck.

For energy, the paper focuses mainly on the calculation for matrix multiplication, since it contributes the most to the cost of large language models. The authors estimate both the matrix-multiplication energy and the end-to-end energy cost for models with $512$ tokens. This matters because the paper’s claim is not just that BitNet b1.58 is smaller in memory; it is that the model changes the computational profile of inference in a way that can reduce energy consumption as well.

This evaluation protocol is therefore built around a simple but rigorous idea: keep the training setup fixed, use standard language benchmarks for quality, and measure inference with a realistic GPU stack for cost. That is what allows the paper to argue that BitNet b1.58 is not merely a compressed model, but a comparable model with a much better serving profile.

Accuracy Results: Perplexity and Zero-Shot Task Performance

# What perplexity measures in language modeling

Perplexity is the paper’s main intrinsic measure of language modeling quality. Intuitively, it tells us how well a model predicts held-out text: when perplexity is lower, the model is assigning higher probability to the text it sees, which means it is making better next-token predictions. In this paper, perplexity is reported on WikiText2 and C4, so it serves as a direct check on whether BitNet b1.58 can still model language well even though its weights are restricted to the ternary set $\{-1,0,1\}$.

This matters because perplexity is not tied to any single downstream benchmark. It asks a more basic question than task accuracy: does the model itself represent the language distribution well? For the paper’s purposes, that is especially important, because the central claim is not only that ternary weights reduce cost, but that they do so without breaking the model’s ability to learn language in the first place.

# Why perplexity and end-task accuracy should both be reported

The paper reports both perplexity and zero-shot task accuracy because these two measurements capture different sides of the same model. Perplexity tells us whether the model is a good language model in the general sense, while end-task accuracy tells us whether that language modeling ability transfers to useful benchmark behavior. A model can sometimes look strong on one measure and weaker on the other, so using both is a more reliable way to judge whether the architecture really works.

This is especially important for BitNet b1.58 because the model changes the numerical representation of the weights very aggressively. If one only looked at perplexity, it would be possible to miss weaknesses on tasks that require broader reasoning or better instructionless generalization. If one only looked at task accuracy, it would be harder to tell whether improvements or regressions were caused by the model itself or by quirks of a benchmark. By reporting both WikiText2 and C4 perplexity alongside zero-shot accuracy on multiple tasks, the paper builds a more complete accuracy picture.

# The trend that the performance gap narrows as model size grows

A central empirical pattern in the paper is that the gap between BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 baseline shrinks as the model gets larger. This trend appears in both the perplexity results and the zero-shot accuracy results. In other words, the ternary model is not stuck permanently far behind the full-precision model; instead, increasing the number of parameters steadily reduces the difference.

The intuition is straightforward. A lower-precision model has less information stored per weight, so at small sizes it is easier for the representational constraint to hurt quality. But as the model grows, it has more parameters overall, and that extra capacity helps recover much of the lost expressiveness. The paper’s results show this effect clearly enough that, at practical model sizes, the ternary model no longer trails the FP16 baseline in a meaningful way.

# The key result that BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 performance starting at 3B parameters

The paper’s headline accuracy result is that BitNet b1.58 matches the full-precision LLaMA baseline starting at the 3B model size. This is true for both perplexity and end-task performance, using the same training recipe and the same number of training tokens. That is the most important threshold in the paper, because it marks the point where ternary weights stop being a quality liability under the reported setup.

This result is what makes the paper more than a cost-reduction story. If a low-bit model is always worse, then it is only useful when cost savings matter more than quality. But if it can reach parity with FP16 at the same scale and training budget, then the precision reduction becomes a viable model design choice rather than a compromise. The paper’s 3B result is the first evidence that this is possible for BitNet b1.58.

# Detailed interpretation of the WikiText2 and C4 perplexity results

The paper reports validation perplexity on both WikiText2 and C4 to check whether the trend is consistent across datasets. The key takeaway is that BitNet b1.58 starts to match the FP16 LLaMA baseline at 3B parameters in terms of perplexity. That means the ternary model is not merely performing well on one validation corpus; it is holding up on two different language modeling evaluations.

The same table also shows that this accuracy parity comes with a substantial cost advantage. At 3B scale, BitNet b1.58 is reported to be 2.71 times faster and to use 3.55 times less GPU memory than the FP16 baseline. So the perplexity result should be read together with the runtime result: the model is not just “good enough,” it is good enough while being significantly cheaper to run. That is why the paper treats the WikiText2 and C4 numbers as evidence of a genuine quality-efficiency trade-off shift, not just a narrow benchmark win.

The 3.9B result strengthens this interpretation even further. The paper reports that BitNet b1.58 at 3.9B is 2.4 times faster and uses 3.32 times less memory than the FP16 LLaMA 3B model, while performing significantly better. In terms of perplexity, this means that a slightly larger ternary model can move beyond parity and surpass a smaller FP16 model, all while remaining more efficient.

# Zero-shot accuracy trends across benchmark tasks

For the zero-shot evaluation, the paper uses a broad set of benchmarks: ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ. These tasks probe different aspects of language understanding, so the point of the evaluation is not to fit one particular test, but to see whether the model’s overall language competence transfers to several common benchmarks without task-specific training.

The same scaling story appears here as in the perplexity results. As model size increases, the performance gap between BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 baseline narrows, and by 3B parameters the paper says BitNet b1.58 can match the full-precision model. That is important because it shows the result is not limited to the language-modeling loss used for perplexity; it also appears in the practical benchmark setting that people often care about when comparing LLMs.

# Why the 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is especially important

The 3.9B model is the most persuasive checkpoint in the paper because it does more than reach parity. It surpasses the FP16 3B baseline on the reported accuracy measures while retaining the efficiency benefits of ternary weights. In that sense, it is the clearest demonstration that BitNet b1.58 can offer both better quality and lower cost at the same time.

This matters for interpretation because a parity result can sometimes be dismissed as a narrow tie. The 3.9B result is stronger: it shows that once the model is pushed slightly larger, the ternary architecture can move from “matching” to “beating” a full-precision baseline of smaller size. That makes the paper’s claim much more convincing, because it shows the low-bit model is not merely competitive in principle; it can occupy a better operating point in practice.

# BitNet b1.58 3.9B versus FP16 3B: better quality at lower cost

The most concrete comparison in the paper is BitNet b1.58 3.9B against the FP16 LLaMA 3B baseline. On this comparison, the ternary model is not only more accurate on the reported perplexity and zero-shot metrics, but also cheaper to run, with lower latency and lower GPU memory use. The paper quantifies this as 2.4 times faster and 3.32 times less memory.

That combination is what makes the result so striking. Usually, improving quality means paying some cost in compute or memory, and reducing cost usually means accepting a quality drop. Here, the paper reports the opposite: a model that is both better and cheaper. For a student reading the paper, this is the most important practical takeaway, because it shows how BitNet b1.58 changes the standard trade-off between model quality and inference cost.

# The meaning of Pareto improvement in this setting

A Pareto improvement means that one option is better in at least one respect and no worse in the others. In the context of this paper, the relevant dimensions are accuracy measures such as perplexity and zero-shot task performance, together with inference costs such as memory and latency. If a model improves quality while also reducing cost, then it dominates the older point in that trade-off space.

That is why the authors describe BitNet b1.58 as a Pareto improvement over the compared FP16 models. The 3.9B versus FP16 3B result is the clearest example: the ternary model is better on accuracy and lower on cost. The broader message is that the paper is not just proposing a cheaper approximation to full precision, but a model family that can move the frontier of what is simultaneously achievable in quality and efficiency.

Efficiency Results: Latency, Memory, Throughput, and Energy

This chapter focuses on the systems-facing claim of the paper: BitNet b1.58 is not just an alternative way to store weights, but a way to make large language models materially cheaper to run. The paper’s central efficiency story is that ternary weights, together with 8-bit activations and a BitLinear-based Transformer design, reduce the cost of the parts of inference that dominate deployment: moving weights, doing matrix multiplications, and fitting larger batches into GPU memory. Just as importantly, the gains do not stay flat as models get bigger. In the paper’s measurements, the larger the model, the more BitNet b1.58 behaves like a cost-performance improvement over the FP16 baseline.

# Why Low-Bit Weights Reduce Memory Capacity and Memory Bandwidth Demands

The first systems benefit of BitNet b1.58 is simple to state but important to understand: if each weight occupies fewer bits, then the model needs less memory to store its parameters. In a standard FP16 LLaMA-style model, every parameter takes 16 bits. In BitNet b1.58, each weight is ternary, taking values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. That means the same number of parameters can be represented with much less storage. From a deployment perspective, this reduces the memory capacity required to host the model at all.

The paper emphasizes that memory is not only about capacity. During inference, weights must be transferred from DRAM into on-chip memory such as SRAM, and that movement itself is expensive. A smaller weight representation means fewer bytes have to be moved for the same computation, so the memory bandwidth demand also falls. This matters because inference is often limited not just by arithmetic throughput, but by how fast the system can feed the compute units with weights. In that sense, low-bit weights help twice: they make the model smaller, and they reduce the cost of loading it.

# Latency Comparisons Between FP16 LLaMA and BitNet b1.58

For latency, the paper measures runtime on GPU using the FasterTransformer codebase, and it reports time per output token as the major inference cost. This is the right metric for understanding how responsive a model feels during generation. The comparison is against a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline, with both models trained under the same general setup. The key result is that BitNet b1.58 becomes competitive very early: at $3$B parameters, it is already $2.71\times$ faster than the FP16 baseline while using $3.55\times$ less GPU memory.

The paper also highlights a stronger result at $3.9$B parameters. At that size, BitNet b1.58 is $2.4\times$ faster, uses $3.32\times$ less memory, and actually performs better than the FP16 LLaMA $3$B baseline. This is why the authors describe BitNet b1.58 as a Pareto improvement: it is not merely cheaper, but in this regime it is cheaper while also matching or exceeding the baseline on quality. From a systems standpoint, that is especially meaningful because it means efficiency does not come only from sacrificing capability.

Why does this happen? The paper’s answer is that the bulk of an LLM’s compute is matrix multiplication. In a conventional FP16 model, that means floating-point multiplications and additions. In BitNet b1.58, matrix multiplication is redesigned so it requires almost no multiplication operations, relying instead on integer addition. Since arithmetic on lower-precision integers is cheaper in energy and easier to optimize in hardware, the latency reduction follows naturally from the arithmetic simplification.

# Why the Speedup Increases with Model Size

One of the most important observations in the paper is that BitNet b1.58 becomes more advantageous as the model gets larger. The authors extend their measurements to $7$B, $13$B, and $70$B models, and the speedup grows with scale. The $70$B BitNet b1.58 model is $4.1\times$ faster than the FP16 LLaMA baseline. This is not a random artifact; it reflects how the cost of dense linear layers grows with model size.

The paper gives a direct explanation: the time cost of $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ increases as the model scales up. Since BitNet b1.58’s main savings come from replacing $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ with BitLinear, the part of the network that benefits most from the new arithmetic becomes more important in larger models. In other words, the larger the model, the more of the total runtime is concentrated in the exact operations that BitNet makes cheaper. That is why the speedup is not fixed; it improves with scale.

Memory shows a similar trend, but with one subtlety. The embedding remains full precision, and its share of the total memory budget becomes smaller as the model grows. In smaller models, this full-precision component is a noticeable fraction of total memory, so it partially limits the overall reduction. In larger models, the quantized weights dominate, so the full-precision embedding matters less and less. As a result, the memory advantage of BitNet b1.58 becomes more visible at larger scales.

The paper summarizes this phenomenon as a new scaling law for efficiency. Its comparisons make the point concrete: $13$B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient, in latency, memory usage, and energy consumption, than $3$B FP16 LLM; $30$B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $7$B FP16 LLM; and $70$B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $13$B FP16 LLM. The key lesson is that low-bit design changes the cost curve itself, not just the constant factor.

# Arithmetic Energy: FP16 Versus BitNet b1.58

The paper’s energy analysis focuses first on the arithmetic cost of matrix multiplication, because that is where most of the work happens in an LLM. The authors decompose the arithmetic operations used by the two systems and show a stark difference. BitNet b1.58 is dominated by INT8 addition calculation, while the FP16 LLaMA baseline consists of both FP16 addition and FP16 multiplication. This difference matters because multiplication is substantially more expensive than addition on typical hardware, especially when the values are carried in floating-point form.

The intuition is straightforward: if the model can be arranged so that matrix multiplication no longer depends on full FP16 multiply-accumulate operations, the energy cost drops sharply. BitNet b1.58 inherits the paper’s new computation paradigm, in which matrix multiplication requires almost no multiplication operations and can be highly optimized. The authors estimate this using an energy model from prior work and conclude that BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4\times$ arithmetic operations energy for matrix multiplication on $7$nm chips. That number refers specifically to arithmetic energy in the matrix-multiplication core, not to every possible source of system power.

This result helps explain why the paper places so much emphasis on computation, not just compression. The weight format is not only about storing fewer bits; it fundamentally changes the arithmetic the hardware needs to perform. Once multiplication is largely removed from the inner loop, the hardware has a much easier job.

# End-to-End Energy on $512$-Token Workloads

Arithmetic energy is only part of the story, so the paper also reports end-to-end energy on workloads with $512$ tokens. This broader measurement is important because real inference includes more than matrix multiplication. It includes the rest of the model stack, and those components can dilute the benefit if they dominate the workload. The paper’s results show that they do not dominate for long.

As the model size increases, BitNet b1.58 becomes increasingly more energy efficient relative to the FP16 baseline. The reason again is scale: the percentage of computation spent in $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ grows with model size, while the relative cost of the other components becomes smaller. Since BitNet’s major advantage is in the linear layers, larger models expose more of that advantage. The end-to-end measurements therefore reinforce the arithmetic energy analysis rather than contradicting it.

This distinction between matrix-multiplication energy and whole-model energy is useful for reading the paper carefully. The $71.4\times$ figure tells you how dramatic the arithmetic improvement can be in isolation. The $512$-token end-to-end result tells you that the advantage survives when the entire inference pipeline is considered. Together, they show that BitNet b1.58 is not just a theoretical arithmetic trick; it translates into a meaningful system-level energy reduction.

# Throughput on Two $80$GB A100 GPUs

Throughput asks a different question from latency: instead of “How long does one token take?”, it asks “How much output can the system produce per unit time when it is running at a practical batch size?” The paper benchmarks throughput for the $70$B models on two $80$GB A100 GPUs. To make the FP16 LLaMA baseline run on those devices, the authors use pipeline parallelism. They then increase batch size until the GPU memory limit is reached, with sequence length fixed at $512$.

The result is striking. BitNet b1.58 $70$B can support up to $11\times$ the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA baseline, which leads to $8.9\times$ higher throughput. This makes sense once you connect it back to memory footprint. If each model copy and its associated working memory consume less space, then more requests can be packed into the same hardware at once. That larger batch size directly translates into better throughput.

This throughput result is especially important because it shows that BitNet b1.58 is not just a single-request optimization. It improves the economics of serving many requests together. In deployment settings where batching is crucial, the ability to process larger batches can matter as much as, or more than, the latency of a single token.

# Current Kernel Limitations and Remaining Headroom

The paper is careful not to oversell the implementation. Both latency and memory measurements are taken with a $2$-bit kernel, and the authors integrate the kernel from Ladder into the FasterTransformer stack. That means the reported numbers are already strong, but they are not necessarily the ceiling of what a specialized 1-bit LLM system could achieve. In other words, the software stack used in the experiments is practical, but it is still adapting to a very new computation format.

This is why the paper repeatedly suggests that further optimization remains possible. A native kernel tailored more specifically to BitNet b1.58’s ternary weights and 8-bit activations could reduce the cost further, and the same logic extends to custom hardware. The paper’s broader argument is that the new computation paradigm is big enough to justify new systems design, rather than forcing the model to live inside infrastructure built for FP16 arithmetic. The measured gains are therefore best understood as a strong first demonstration, not a final limit.

A New Scaling Law for Cost-Effective LLMs

# What the paper means by a new scaling law

The paper uses the phrase “new scaling law” to describe a different relationship between model size, model quality, and inference cost than the one implied by conventional full-precision language models. In the paper’s setting, scaling up is not only about making a model larger in parameter count. It is also about changing the arithmetic and memory regime in which that larger model operates. A BitNet b1.58 model uses ternary weights in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and $8$-bit activations, so the cost of increasing size does not rise in the same way it does for an FP16 or BF16 Transformer. The paper’s claim is that this change is large enough to create a new practical regime for LLM scaling.

This is why the paper’s scaling claim is tied to empirical equivalences such as “$13\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $3\mathrm{B}$ FP16.” The point is not that the models are identical in every sense. Rather, the paper argues that once weights and activations are pushed into the $1.58$-bit and $8$-bit regime, larger models can occupy a better point on the tradeoff curve. In that sense, scaling up no longer automatically means paying a proportionally larger inference bill. The paper presents this as a new scaling law because the usual expectation that larger models are costlier is weakened by the new computation and memory format.

# Why quality must be considered together with latency, memory, and energy

A central message of the paper is that a language model is not useful in practice if it only looks good on benchmark scores but is too expensive to serve. For that reason, the paper evaluates quality together with inference latency, GPU memory, throughput, and energy consumption. Quality is measured with validation perplexity on WikiText2 and C4, as well as zero-shot accuracy on tasks such as ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ. Cost is measured in terms of how much memory the model occupies, how long it takes to produce an output token, how much energy its arithmetic operations consume, and how much throughput it can deliver.

This combined view matters because the paper’s strongest result is not just that BitNet b1.58 is smaller in some abstract sense, but that it matches FP16 baselines on quality while also improving deployment cost. The paper reports that BitNet b1.58 starts to match full-precision LLaMA-style baselines at the $3\mathrm{B}$ scale, both in perplexity and in end-task performance, when trained with the same model size and training-token budget. It also shows that a $3.9\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 can outperform a $3\mathrm{B}$ FP16 LLM while using less memory and lower latency. That combination is what makes the result a Pareto improvement in the paper’s framing: the model is not merely accurate, it is accurate at a lower system cost.

# How low-bit arithmetic changes the cost of scaling up model size

The paper’s efficiency argument starts from the observation that the bulk of an LLM’s computation is matrix multiplication. In a vanilla full-precision Transformer, those matrix multiplications rely on floating-point additions and multiplications, which are expensive in both latency and energy. BitNet b1.58 changes that computation pattern. Because the weights are ternary, matrix multiplication in BitNet is implemented with almost no multiplication operations, and the paper emphasizes that this can be highly optimized. The result is a new computation paradigm in which arithmetic becomes much cheaper.

The paper also stresses that memory traffic is part of the cost of scaling. During inference, model parameters must move from DRAM into on-chip memory such as SRAM, and this transfer is expensive. A lower-precision model has a much smaller footprint, so it needs less capacity and less bandwidth. That reduces both the time and the cost of loading weights. In the paper’s energy discussion, this is why low-bit arithmetic and low-bit storage matter together: the model saves energy not only because its arithmetic is cheaper, but also because it reduces the burden on the memory system. The paper reports, using an energy model for 7nm chips, that BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4$ times arithmetic-operations energy for matrix multiplication. The practical implication is that larger models can be scaled up without inheriting the same full-precision cost structure.

# The equivalence examples proposed in the paper

To make its argument concrete, the paper gives three reference equivalences based on the reported trends in latency, memory, and energy. It says that $13\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a $3\mathrm{B}$ FP16 LLM, that $30\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a $7\mathrm{B}$ FP16 LLM, and that $70\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a $13\mathrm{B}$ FP16 LLM. These are not claims that the models have identical parameters, identical quality, or identical internal behavior. They are comparisons of deployment efficiency.

The reason these equivalences are important is that they show a shift in the frontier. In the paper’s view, the question is no longer only “How large can a model be?” It becomes “How much model size can be obtained for a given deployment budget?” A ternary-weight model at $30\mathrm{B}$ or $70\mathrm{B}$ can, according to the paper, sit in a better cost region than a much smaller FP16 model. That is the core of the new scaling regime: size increases do not necessarily imply a worse deployment tradeoff if the arithmetic and memory format changes enough.

# Why $13\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 can be more efficient than $3\mathrm{B}$ FP16

The intuition behind the $13\mathrm{B}$ versus $3\mathrm{B}$ comparison is that full-precision cost grows quickly because every linear layer must still perform FP16-style floating-point work, and the model must still move a large amount of data through memory. By contrast, BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights and $8$-bit activations, so the dominant matrix multiplications are much cheaper to execute. Even though $13\mathrm{B}$ has more parameters than $3\mathrm{B}$, those parameters are not carried through the system in the same expensive format. As a result, the larger BitNet model can be cheaper to run than the smaller FP16 one in latency, memory, and energy.

The paper’s own measurements support the direction of this claim. It reports that BitNet b1.58 begins matching full-precision quality at the $3\mathrm{B}$ scale, while also being $2.71$ times faster and using $3.55$ times less GPU memory. It also reports that a $3.9\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is $2.4$ times faster, uses $3.32$ times less memory, and performs significantly better than a $3\mathrm{B}$ FP16 LLaMA LLM. Those results show why a larger BitNet can be viewed as more efficient than a much smaller FP16 model: the low-bit format changes the cost per parameter enough that added size does not translate into the usual cost penalty.

# Why $30\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 can be more efficient than $7\mathrm{B}$ FP16

The paper says the efficiency advantage becomes even more pronounced as model size grows. It reports that the speed-up increases with scale because the time cost of \emph{nn.Linear} grows with model size, and the savings from replacing it with BitLinear become more valuable. This means the comparison between $30\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 and $7\mathrm{B}$ FP16 is not just a bigger-versus-smaller comparison. It is a comparison between two different operating regimes, where the larger model lives in a cheaper arithmetic and memory space.

The paper’s explanation also depends on the structure of the model. It notes that the embedding remains full precision, but its share of the total memory becomes smaller as the model gets larger. That means some of the remaining non-quantized cost matters less at larger scales, while the dominant linear layers matter more. In that setting, the low-bit advantage can outweigh the parameter-count disadvantage. So the paper’s $30\mathrm{B}$ versus $7\mathrm{B}$ reference point is a statement that the new scaling law lets a much larger ternary model remain more deployment-efficient than a smaller full-precision model.

# Why $70\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 can be more efficient than $13\mathrm{B}$ FP16

The paper extends the same argument to the largest model it studies. It reports that BitNet b1.58 at $70\mathrm{B}$ is $4.1$ times faster than the FP16 baseline, and that the throughput advantage can become substantial in a multi-GPU setting. Specifically, on two $80$GB A100 cards with pipeline parallelism, the $70\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 supports up to $11$ times the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA baseline and achieves $8.9$ times higher throughput. This is a strong sign that, at very large scale, the low-bit format changes not just single-token latency but the entire serving profile.

The comparison with $13\mathrm{B}$ FP16 follows the same logic as the smaller-scale examples. A $70\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is much larger in parameter count, but the paper argues that its cheaper arithmetic and smaller memory footprint allow it to land in a better operational-cost region than a $13\mathrm{B}$ FP16 model. The main reason is that the share of total inference cost dominated by linear layers grows with model size, so the low-bit savings become increasingly important. The paper also notes that its latency and memory measurements were obtained with a $2$-bit kernel, which means there is still room for further optimization. Even with that caveat, the reported trend supports the idea that very large BitNet models can be more efficient than much smaller full-precision ones.

# Implications for model sizing, deployment strategy, and total cost of ownership

The paper’s scaling claim changes how one should think about model sizing. Under the BitNet b1.58 regime, the design choice is not simply to minimize parameter count. Instead, one can consider larger models if the goal is to improve quality while staying within a deployment budget. This is a different practical rule than in the FP16 setting. The paper’s results suggest that a model can be scaled up to $13\mathrm{B}$, $30\mathrm{B}$, or even $70\mathrm{B}$ while still offering better latency, memory, and energy behavior than much smaller full-precision models.

This has direct deployment consequences. Lower memory footprint means fewer devices may be needed to host a model, and lower bandwidth demand means less time spent moving weights from DRAM to on-chip memory. The paper also connects this to mixture-of-experts systems, where reduced memory and reduced activation transfer can help lower inter-chip communication overhead. It further argues that BitNet b1.58 is a step toward native support for long sequences because its $8$-bit activations can double context length under the same resources. For edge and mobile use, the paper emphasizes that lower memory and energy consumption make deployment on CPU-centric devices more realistic. Taken together, these points imply a lower total cost of ownership because the system needs less memory, less energy, and less hardware to achieve a given level of quality.

# Assumptions and caveats behind these comparisons

The paper’s comparisons are empirical and depend on specific training and measurement choices. For the main results, the models are pre-trained on the RedPajama dataset for $100$ billion tokens, and the FP16 baseline is a reproduced LLaMA LLM. Quality is measured on a fixed set of zero-shot tasks and on perplexity datasets such as WikiText2 and C4. Runtime measurements are obtained using the FasterTransformer codebase, with the Ladder $2$-bit kernel integrated for BitNet b1.58, and latency is reported as time per output token. Throughput comparisons are done on two $80$GB A100 cards with pipeline parallelism and sequence length $512$. The energy results are based on arithmetic-operation models for matrix multiplication, especially on 7nm chips. None of these are universal constants; they are controlled experimental conditions.

There are also architectural caveats. BitNet b1.58 does not mean that every part of the model is ternary. The paper uses ternary weights and $8$-bit activations, while the embedding remains full precision. The paper also says the reported runtime was measured with a $2$-bit kernel, so further optimization is still possible. In addition, the “new scaling law” is best understood as an empirical regime supported by the paper’s results, not as a closed-form theorem. The $2$T-token experiment against StableLM-3B is also a specific comparison drawn from that model’s technical report and benchmark protocol. For a reader, the key takeaway is that the paper’s equivalence claims are strong, but they are strong within the exact setup the authors studied.

Longer Training, Generalization, and the Future of 1-bit LLMs

# Why token count matters for LLM scaling

A central idea in this paper is that model size is not the only axis along which large language models improve. The number of training tokens also matters, because it controls how much language experience the model receives during pretraining. When the token budget grows, we can ask whether the same architecture continues to improve, or whether it has already reached the point where more data no longer helps. In that sense, token count is a direct test of scalability.

This is especially important for BitNet b1.58, because the paper is not only claiming that ternary-weight models can be efficient at inference time, but that they can also remain competitive when trained at larger scale. If a low-bit model only worked in a narrow training regime, then its efficiency would come at the cost of limited usefulness. The extended token experiment is designed to answer exactly that question: can a 1.58-bit model keep benefiting from more training data in the same way a full-precision model does?

The paper’s answer is yes. By pushing training to a much larger token budget, the authors use data scale as a way to probe whether the low-precision design is merely compact or whether it is also a genuinely scalable language model family. That makes token count a key part of the story of the “era of 1-bit LLMs.”

# The 2T-token training experiment following the StableLM-3B data recipe

To study token scalability, the paper trains a BitNet b1.58 model on 2T tokens. The important point is that this is not done with a special new training recipe invented just for the comparison. Instead, the authors follow the data recipe of StableLM-3B, which they describe as the state-of-the-art open-source 3B model. Using the same recipe helps make the comparison more meaningful, because it reduces the chance that improvements come from a different training setup rather than from the model design itself.

This kind of comparison is a standard way to study model families. If two models are trained with the same general data recipe, then the results are easier to interpret. The experiment asks whether BitNet b1.58 can take advantage of a much longer pretraining run and convert that extra training into better end-task behavior. In other words, the paper is testing the model family under a more demanding data regime, not changing the benchmark to make the result easier.

The significance of the 2T-token run is that it stretches the model beyond the token budget used in the earlier experiments in the paper. That makes the result more than a point comparison. It becomes evidence about whether 1.58-bit LLMs are only competitive at one scale, or whether they can keep improving as training is extended.

# The benchmark suite and evaluation protocol

For the 2T-token experiment, the paper evaluates the model on a benchmark suite consisting of Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC-easy. These tasks are used as zero-shot evaluations, meaning the model is judged directly on the tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. This matters because it measures general language understanding and transfer rather than a model’s ability to adapt after extra training on each benchmark separately.

The paper follows the pipeline from `lm-evaluation-harness` for this evaluation. That detail matters because it tells the reader that the comparison is performed in a standard and reproducible way. When tasks are reported with both accuracy and normalized accuracy, the paper averages the two. That averaging rule keeps the reporting consistent across the benchmark set and avoids comparing numbers computed under slightly different scoring conventions.

Taken together, this evaluation setup is meant to answer a simple question: after training longer, does BitNet b1.58 actually perform better on a broad set of unseen tasks? The benchmark suite is broad enough to make the answer meaningful, and the zero-shot setting makes it a test of pretrained capability rather than task-specific tuning.

# The result: BitNet b1.58 outperforms StableLM-3B on all reported end tasks

The main finding from the 2T-token experiment is straightforward and strong. The paper reports that BitNet b1.58 achieves superior performance on all of the reported end tasks compared with StableLM-3B. This is not a narrow win on one benchmark or a mixed result across tasks. It is a clean improvement across the full benchmark set.

That result is important because StableLM-3B is presented as a strong open-source baseline. Beating it on every reported task suggests that the 1.58-bit model is not just competitive in a few favorable cases. Instead, it appears to scale well with more tokens and to convert that extra training into broad gains. In the context of the paper, this is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that the ternary-weight design is not merely an efficiency trick.

The result also supports the paper’s broader claim that BitNet b1.58 can match or exceed full-precision baselines under comparable training conditions. Here, the comparison is not only about compression or hardware savings. It is about whether a low-bit model can also be a strong language model in the usual sense of performing well across multiple end tasks.

# What the 2T results suggest about generalization in 1.58-bit LLMs

The paper explicitly interprets the 2T-token result as evidence that 1.58-bit LLMs have strong generalization capabilities. That phrase is important. Generalization means that what the model learned during pretraining transfers successfully to new tasks that were not part of the training objective. In this experiment, the model is not simply fitting one narrow benchmark; it is performing well across several different tasks after long pretraining.

The fact that BitNet b1.58 performs well across all reported benchmarks suggests that ternary weights do not prevent the model from capturing the broad patterns needed for language understanding. The paper’s result is therefore more than a compression result. It says that the low-bit model can still support broad downstream usefulness, which is exactly what one would want from a general-purpose language model.

This is also why the 2T-token experiment matters for the larger argument of the paper. The paper is not claiming that efficiency and quality trade off in the usual way. Instead, it argues that 1.58-bit LLMs can be both high-performance and cost-effective. The 2T result strengthens that claim by showing that stronger training on more tokens can still lead to broad task improvements.

# 1-bit Mixture-of-Experts and the memory and communication bottleneck

The paper next turns to Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE models, as a future direction. MoE is described as a cost-effective approach for language models because it reduces computation FLOPs. However, the paper also emphasizes the practical bottlenecks that make MoE difficult to deploy: high memory consumption and inter-chip communication overhead.

This is where 1.58-bit LLMs become relevant. If the weights are much smaller, the total memory footprint drops, and that can reduce the number of devices required to host the model. Fewer devices mean less need to move data between chips. Since moving activations across networks is expensive, reducing that communication burden can make MoE deployments more practical.

The paper’s strongest point here is that the reduced memory footprint does not merely make the model cheaper in a vague sense. It can directly address one of the central problems of MoE systems: the overhead of distributing the model across many devices. In the ideal case, if the whole model could fit on a single chip, then the inter-chip communication overhead would disappear entirely. The paper presents 1.58-bit LLMs as a step toward that kind of deployment.

# Native support for longer context through lower-bit activations and KV-cache savings

Long-sequence inference is another place where the paper sees major promise. The paper notes that one of the main challenges in long-context inference is the memory required by KV caches. These caches grow as the context becomes longer, so they can quickly dominate memory usage during inference. Any reduction in memory footprint therefore has a direct effect on how much context a model can handle.

BitNet b1.58 already reduces activations from 16 bits to 8 bits, and the paper points out that this can allow the context length to be doubled under the same resource budget. That is a very practical benefit: with the same hardware, the model can process longer sequences. The paper describes this as a significant step toward native support for long sequences.

The authors also note a further possibility: the activations could potentially be compressed losslessly to 4 bits or even lower for 1.58-bit LLMs. Importantly, the paper presents this as future work rather than as a completed result. So the current contribution is the move to 8-bit activations and the resulting savings, while deeper compression is left as an open direction.

# Why BitNet b1.58 is promising for edge, mobile, and CPU-centric deployment

The paper argues that 1.58-bit LLMs could make language models much more practical on edge and mobile devices. The reason is simple: these devices typically have limited memory and limited compute, which makes large models difficult to deploy. When the model uses less memory and less energy, it becomes easier to fit within the constraints of such platforms.

The paper also highlights a specific systems advantage: 1.58-bit LLMs are more friendly to CPU devices, and CPUs are the main processors used in edge and mobile settings. That means the model is not only smaller in theory but also more compatible with the hardware that these devices already rely on. In the paper’s view, this opens the door to deploying language models in settings that were previously too constrained.

This is one of the broader significance points of the paper. The value of a 1-bit or 1.58-bit model is not just that it is efficient in a lab benchmark. It is that the efficiency can translate into new classes of deployment, especially where memory, energy, and processor type matter most.

# The case for hardware designed specifically for 1-bit LLMs

The paper closes its future-looking discussion by calling for hardware and systems designed specifically for 1-bit LLMs. This recommendation follows naturally from the earlier claim that BitNet enables a new computation paradigm. If matrix multiplication no longer depends heavily on the usual floating-point multiplications, then the ideal hardware may also change.

The paper points to recent work such as Groq as evidence that specialized hardware for language models is already becoming a serious direction. It then takes the next step and argues that 1-bit LLMs deserve their own tailored hardware support. In this vision, the benefits of ternary weights are not just tolerated by existing hardware, but actively exploited by hardware designed around them.

This is an important systems-level idea. A model family can only realize its full efficiency gains if the hardware, kernels, and software stack are able to use the model’s structure well. The paper therefore treats hardware design as part of the same research agenda as model design.

# Open research questions and the broader significance of the era of 1-bit LLMs

The paper’s broader message is that 1-bit LLMs are not a niche compression trick. They may represent the beginning of a new era in which model quality and inference cost are optimized together. The 2T-token result strengthens that claim by showing that 1.58-bit models can continue to improve with more training and can outperform a strong open-source baseline on all reported tasks.

At the same time, the paper leaves several questions open. It notes that the reported latency and memory numbers were measured with a 2-bit kernel, so there is still room for optimization. It also identifies further compression of activations, especially toward 4 bits or lower, as future work. And it treats MoE deployment, long-context support, edge inference, and custom hardware as major opportunities rather than finished solutions.

That combination of strong current results and explicit future directions is what makes the paper’s framing powerful. The “era of 1-bit LLMs” means more than smaller weights. It means a shift in how language models are trained, how they are evaluated, how they are deployed, and what kind of hardware they may eventually run on. The paper’s final message is that ternary-weight language models can be both effective and efficient, and that this may change the way the field thinks about scaling itself.
