\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 2: Foundations: Transformers, LLaMA, and Where the Cost Comes From

Large language models in this paper are treated as Transformer-based systems whose practical behavior is determined by two broad forces. The first is modeling quality: how well the model predicts text and solves downstream tasks. The second is systems cost: how much memory, latency, throughput, and energy are required to run it. This chapter focuses on the architectural ingredients that shape both sides of that trade-off. The paper’s central message is that if we understand where a Transformer spends its computation and which design choices define the LLaMA family, then the motivation for BitNet b1.58 becomes much easier to see.

# A refresher on Transformer language models

A Transformer language model is a stack of repeated blocks that process text as sequences of tokens. Each token is first turned into a vector representation, and then that representation is updated layer by layer so the model can predict the next token or otherwise model language. The paper assumes this basic Transformer setting throughout, and it also emphasizes that the large majority of the work performed by such models is matrix multiplication.

For a student encountering these models for the first time, the most important intuition is that a Transformer does not “understand” text all at once. Instead, it repeatedly transforms token representations, using learned parameters, until the final layer produces outputs that can be interpreted as predictions. The architecture is powerful because the same basic block is reused many times, and the cost grows as the model becomes larger and the sequences it processes become longer.

The paper places this standard Transformer view in the context of deployment. It notes that typical large language models are built in $16$-bit floating point formats such as FP16 or BF16, and that this choice makes both computation and memory expensive. That is why the architecture matters so much: the model’s predictive power comes from these repeated transformations, but so does most of its runtime cost.

# The role of embeddings, attention, MLP blocks, and residual pathways

The first step in a Transformer is the embedding layer. Its role is to map discrete token identifiers into continuous vectors that the rest of the network can work with. In effect, embeddings translate symbolic text into a numerical form that can be updated by the model. The paper does not focus on embeddings as the main source of cost, but they are part of the standard Transformer pipeline and are important for understanding how the model begins its computation.

Attention and MLP blocks are the two main kinds of processing inside the Transformer stack. Attention is the mechanism that lets the model combine information from different positions in the sequence. This is what gives the model its ability to relate one token to earlier or later tokens rather than treating each token in isolation. The MLP, or feed-forward block, then transforms the representation of each token more locally, enriching it with nonlinear processing. Together, these two components let the model alternate between mixing information across the sequence and refining each token’s own representation.

Residual pathways tie the whole system together. Rather than replacing a representation completely at each layer, the model carries information forward and adds new transformations on top of what it already has. This makes deep networks easier to train and helps each layer act like a refinement step instead of a total rewrite. In the paper’s framing, all of these components are part of the same larger machine whose final behavior depends on the balance between expressiveness and computational cost.

# Why linear layers are the main computational bottleneck

The paper’s cost analysis starts from a simple observation: the bulk of a large language model is matrix multiplication. In practice, this means that the linear layers inside the network are where most of the arithmetic happens. Because these layers are used repeatedly throughout the model, their cost accumulates quickly as the model scales up.

This matters because the expensive part of a standard FP16 or BF16 model is not just that it has many parameters. It is also that those parameters are used in floating-point operations, which are comparatively costly in both time and energy. The paper explicitly points out that the major computation cost comes from floating-point addition and multiplication. So when the model grows, the cost of each linear transformation grows with it, and the total inference cost is dominated by these repeated matrix operations.

The paper also highlights a second source of expense: memory movement. During inference, parameters often have to be transferred from DRAM to on-chip memory such as SRAM, and that transfer can be costly. Even if the arithmetic itself were not the bottleneck, the act of moving large parameter tensors around can slow the system down. This is why a low-memory model can offer gains in both latency and throughput, not just in storage usage. The architecture therefore has implications for both computation and data movement.

# How attention and feed-forward layers use matrix multiplication

Although attention and MLP blocks play different roles in the network, both rely heavily on matrix multiplication. Attention uses linear projections to transform representations before combining information across the sequence, and those projections are implemented as matrix operations. The feed-forward block, or MLP, also uses linear transformations to expand and then transform the token representation before producing an output. In other words, both major subcomponents of a Transformer are built on the same expensive primitive.

This is why the paper treats matrix multiplication as the central systems issue. If the same primitive appears everywhere in the model, then even modest savings in that primitive can have large effects on the overall runtime. The paper’s discussion of BitNet b1.58 makes this point especially sharp by contrasting floating-point matrix multiplication with a computation style that requires almost no multiplication operations. But even before introducing that alternative, the lesson for a standard Transformer is clear: the model’s core layers are expensive because they repeatedly apply matrix multiplication at scale.

There is also a close connection between matrix multiplication and hardware performance. Because large models spend so much time in linear layers, the design of the inference system matters a great deal. The paper’s measurements of time per output token, GPU memory use, and throughput are all rooted in the fact that these layers are the dominant cost center. Understanding that cost center is therefore a prerequisite for understanding why architectural and numerical changes can matter so much.

# Key design choices in LLaMA-style models

The paper describes LLaMA as the de-facto backbone for open-source large language models and then adopts a “LLaMA-alike” design for BitNet b1.58. The key components listed in the paper are RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and the removal of all biases. These are presented as the architectural choices that define the LLaMA family and make it familiar to the broader open-source ecosystem.

The importance of these choices in the paper is not that each one is introduced as a separate theoretical breakthrough, but that together they define a widely used and practical Transformer template. In the paper’s framing, a LLaMA-style model is not just any Transformer; it is a Transformer built with a particular set of normalization, activation, positional, and parameterization choices. Those choices make the model easy to recognize and easy to integrate into existing software stacks.

The paper also emphasizes compatibility. By using LLaMA-alike components, BitNet b1.58 can be integrated into common open-source tools such as Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp with minimal effort. This is an important point because a model family becomes influential not only when it performs well, but also when it can be adopted easily by other researchers and practitioners. In that sense, the LLaMA design is as much an ecosystem standard as it is a neural architecture.

# Why LLaMA has become the default open-source backbone

The paper explicitly states that the architecture of LLaMA has become the de-facto backbone for open-source LLMs. That means it has become the default reference point around which a large portion of the community organizes its models, code, and inference systems. The reason this matters is that a common backbone reduces friction: once a model family is widely supported, it becomes much easier to build new systems on top of it.

This is why the paper’s authors chose to adopt LLaMA-alike components in BitNet b1.58. They are “embracing the open-source community” by building on a design that people already know how to train, evaluate, and deploy. The paper links this directly to implementation practicality. Because LLaMA-style models are already supported in major open-source software, a new model that follows the same pattern can be used with less engineering work.

There is also a research reason for this choice. When a model family is widely used, comparisons become more meaningful. If many systems are built from the same backbone, then differences in performance or cost are easier to attribute to the new idea rather than to unrelated architectural changes. In the paper, this is part of why the comparison between BitNet b1.58 and the reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline is so informative: the models share a familiar Transformer foundation, so the effects of the new numerical format can be studied more cleanly.

# Quality metrics versus systems metrics in LLM evaluation

One of the most important lessons from the paper is that model quality and model cost are different axes, and both must be measured. Quality metrics ask whether the model is good at language tasks. Systems metrics ask how expensive it is to use. A model that scores well on one axis is not necessarily efficient on the other, and the paper evaluates both to show the full picture.

For quality, the paper uses validation perplexity on WikiText2 and C4, along with zero-shot accuracy on a set of language tasks including ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ. These metrics are intended to show how well a model predicts text and handles downstream reasoning or comprehension tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. In the paper’s results, these measures are crucial because they show that the model is not merely cheaper to run; it can also remain competitive in actual language performance.

For systems evaluation, the paper measures runtime GPU memory, latency, throughput, and energy consumption. It reports time per output token as a key latency measure, since that is the dominant cost for inference. It also compares GPU memory use, since the cost of loading and storing weights affects deployment. Throughput matters because it reflects how much work the system can do under a given memory budget, and energy matters because the paper is concerned with the practical and economic cost of running large models. Together, these metrics capture what it means for a model to be deployable, not just accurate.

The broader lesson is that an LLM should be evaluated as both a predictor and a system. A model may match perplexity while still being slower, larger, or more energy-hungry than another model. The paper’s framing makes this distinction central, because its main claim is that a new model family can preserve quality while reducing inference cost. That is the kind of comparison that requires both sets of metrics, and it is why understanding Transformer structure and LLaMA design is the right foundation before studying BitNet b1.58 itself.
