\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 9: Efficiency Results: Latency, Memory, Throughput, and Energy

This chapter focuses on the systems-facing claim of the paper: BitNet b1.58 is not just an alternative way to store weights, but a way to make large language models materially cheaper to run. The paper’s central efficiency story is that ternary weights, together with 8-bit activations and a BitLinear-based Transformer design, reduce the cost of the parts of inference that dominate deployment: moving weights, doing matrix multiplications, and fitting larger batches into GPU memory. Just as importantly, the gains do not stay flat as models get bigger. In the paper’s measurements, the larger the model, the more BitNet b1.58 behaves like a cost-performance improvement over the FP16 baseline.

# Why Low-Bit Weights Reduce Memory Capacity and Memory Bandwidth Demands

The first systems benefit of BitNet b1.58 is simple to state but important to understand: if each weight occupies fewer bits, then the model needs less memory to store its parameters. In a standard FP16 LLaMA-style model, every parameter takes 16 bits. In BitNet b1.58, each weight is ternary, taking values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. That means the same number of parameters can be represented with much less storage. From a deployment perspective, this reduces the memory capacity required to host the model at all.

The paper emphasizes that memory is not only about capacity. During inference, weights must be transferred from DRAM into on-chip memory such as SRAM, and that movement itself is expensive. A smaller weight representation means fewer bytes have to be moved for the same computation, so the memory bandwidth demand also falls. This matters because inference is often limited not just by arithmetic throughput, but by how fast the system can feed the compute units with weights. In that sense, low-bit weights help twice: they make the model smaller, and they reduce the cost of loading it.

# Latency Comparisons Between FP16 LLaMA and BitNet b1.58

For latency, the paper measures runtime on GPU using the FasterTransformer codebase, and it reports time per output token as the major inference cost. This is the right metric for understanding how responsive a model feels during generation. The comparison is against a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline, with both models trained under the same general setup. The key result is that BitNet b1.58 becomes competitive very early: at $3$B parameters, it is already $2.71\times$ faster than the FP16 baseline while using $3.55\times$ less GPU memory.

The paper also highlights a stronger result at $3.9$B parameters. At that size, BitNet b1.58 is $2.4\times$ faster, uses $3.32\times$ less memory, and actually performs better than the FP16 LLaMA $3$B baseline. This is why the authors describe BitNet b1.58 as a Pareto improvement: it is not merely cheaper, but in this regime it is cheaper while also matching or exceeding the baseline on quality. From a systems standpoint, that is especially meaningful because it means efficiency does not come only from sacrificing capability.

Why does this happen? The paper’s answer is that the bulk of an LLM’s compute is matrix multiplication. In a conventional FP16 model, that means floating-point multiplications and additions. In BitNet b1.58, matrix multiplication is redesigned so it requires almost no multiplication operations, relying instead on integer addition. Since arithmetic on lower-precision integers is cheaper in energy and easier to optimize in hardware, the latency reduction follows naturally from the arithmetic simplification.

# Why the Speedup Increases with Model Size

One of the most important observations in the paper is that BitNet b1.58 becomes more advantageous as the model gets larger. The authors extend their measurements to $7$B, $13$B, and $70$B models, and the speedup grows with scale. The $70$B BitNet b1.58 model is $4.1\times$ faster than the FP16 LLaMA baseline. This is not a random artifact; it reflects how the cost of dense linear layers grows with model size.

The paper gives a direct explanation: the time cost of $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ increases as the model scales up. Since BitNet b1.58’s main savings come from replacing $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ with BitLinear, the part of the network that benefits most from the new arithmetic becomes more important in larger models. In other words, the larger the model, the more of the total runtime is concentrated in the exact operations that BitNet makes cheaper. That is why the speedup is not fixed; it improves with scale.

Memory shows a similar trend, but with one subtlety. The embedding remains full precision, and its share of the total memory budget becomes smaller as the model grows. In smaller models, this full-precision component is a noticeable fraction of total memory, so it partially limits the overall reduction. In larger models, the quantized weights dominate, so the full-precision embedding matters less and less. As a result, the memory advantage of BitNet b1.58 becomes more visible at larger scales.

The paper summarizes this phenomenon as a new scaling law for efficiency. Its comparisons make the point concrete: $13$B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient, in latency, memory usage, and energy consumption, than $3$B FP16 LLM; $30$B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $7$B FP16 LLM; and $70$B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $13$B FP16 LLM. The key lesson is that low-bit design changes the cost curve itself, not just the constant factor.

# Arithmetic Energy: FP16 Versus BitNet b1.58

The paper’s energy analysis focuses first on the arithmetic cost of matrix multiplication, because that is where most of the work happens in an LLM. The authors decompose the arithmetic operations used by the two systems and show a stark difference. BitNet b1.58 is dominated by INT8 addition calculation, while the FP16 LLaMA baseline consists of both FP16 addition and FP16 multiplication. This difference matters because multiplication is substantially more expensive than addition on typical hardware, especially when the values are carried in floating-point form.

The intuition is straightforward: if the model can be arranged so that matrix multiplication no longer depends on full FP16 multiply-accumulate operations, the energy cost drops sharply. BitNet b1.58 inherits the paper’s new computation paradigm, in which matrix multiplication requires almost no multiplication operations and can be highly optimized. The authors estimate this using an energy model from prior work and conclude that BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4\times$ arithmetic operations energy for matrix multiplication on $7$nm chips. That number refers specifically to arithmetic energy in the matrix-multiplication core, not to every possible source of system power.

This result helps explain why the paper places so much emphasis on computation, not just compression. The weight format is not only about storing fewer bits; it fundamentally changes the arithmetic the hardware needs to perform. Once multiplication is largely removed from the inner loop, the hardware has a much easier job.

# End-to-End Energy on $512$-Token Workloads

Arithmetic energy is only part of the story, so the paper also reports end-to-end energy on workloads with $512$ tokens. This broader measurement is important because real inference includes more than matrix multiplication. It includes the rest of the model stack, and those components can dilute the benefit if they dominate the workload. The paper’s results show that they do not dominate for long.

As the model size increases, BitNet b1.58 becomes increasingly more energy efficient relative to the FP16 baseline. The reason again is scale: the percentage of computation spent in $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ grows with model size, while the relative cost of the other components becomes smaller. Since BitNet’s major advantage is in the linear layers, larger models expose more of that advantage. The end-to-end measurements therefore reinforce the arithmetic energy analysis rather than contradicting it.

This distinction between matrix-multiplication energy and whole-model energy is useful for reading the paper carefully. The $71.4\times$ figure tells you how dramatic the arithmetic improvement can be in isolation. The $512$-token end-to-end result tells you that the advantage survives when the entire inference pipeline is considered. Together, they show that BitNet b1.58 is not just a theoretical arithmetic trick; it translates into a meaningful system-level energy reduction.

# Throughput on Two $80$GB A100 GPUs

Throughput asks a different question from latency: instead of “How long does one token take?”, it asks “How much output can the system produce per unit time when it is running at a practical batch size?” The paper benchmarks throughput for the $70$B models on two $80$GB A100 GPUs. To make the FP16 LLaMA baseline run on those devices, the authors use pipeline parallelism. They then increase batch size until the GPU memory limit is reached, with sequence length fixed at $512$.

The result is striking. BitNet b1.58 $70$B can support up to $11\times$ the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA baseline, which leads to $8.9\times$ higher throughput. This makes sense once you connect it back to memory footprint. If each model copy and its associated working memory consume less space, then more requests can be packed into the same hardware at once. That larger batch size directly translates into better throughput.

This throughput result is especially important because it shows that BitNet b1.58 is not just a single-request optimization. It improves the economics of serving many requests together. In deployment settings where batching is crucial, the ability to process larger batches can matter as much as, or more than, the latency of a single token.

# Current Kernel Limitations and Remaining Headroom

The paper is careful not to oversell the implementation. Both latency and memory measurements are taken with a $2$-bit kernel, and the authors integrate the kernel from Ladder into the FasterTransformer stack. That means the reported numbers are already strong, but they are not necessarily the ceiling of what a specialized 1-bit LLM system could achieve. In other words, the software stack used in the experiments is practical, but it is still adapting to a very new computation format.

This is why the paper repeatedly suggests that further optimization remains possible. A native kernel tailored more specifically to BitNet b1.58’s ternary weights and 8-bit activations could reduce the cost further, and the same logic extends to custom hardware. The paper’s broader argument is that the new computation paradigm is big enough to justify new systems design, rather than forcing the model to live inside infrastructure built for FP16 arithmetic. The measured gains are therefore best understood as a strong first demonstration, not a final limit.
