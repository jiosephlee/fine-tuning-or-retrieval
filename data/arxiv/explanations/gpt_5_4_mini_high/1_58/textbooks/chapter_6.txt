\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 6: Architecture Choices and the Training Recipe

BitNet b1.58 is presented in the paper as a carefully controlled redesign of a Transformer language model rather than a wholesale architectural reinvention. The key move is to keep the familiar LLaMA-style backbone and replace the standard linear layers with low-bit counterparts, while training the model from scratch under those low-bit constraints. This matters because the paper is not trying to compress a finished model after the fact; it is trying to make low-bit computation part of the model’s native training recipe. In that sense, the architecture and the training procedure are inseparable.

# Why the model is trained from scratch instead of converted after training

The paper starts from the observation that post-training quantization is widely used, but still sub-optimal. That approach takes a model that was learned in full precision and then forces it into a lower-precision representation for inference. BitNet b1.58 takes the opposite route: it is trained from scratch with ternary weights and $8$-bit activations. This means the network learns while already living inside the numerical limits that it will use at deployment time.

That design choice is important because the model does not merely “survive” quantization after learning; it adapts to quantization during learning. The paper’s low-bit formulation is therefore not a compression layer added at the end of training, but the actual form of the model during optimization. Since the bulk of LLM computation is matrix multiplication, making those operations low-bit from the beginning directly changes the training and inference regime, rather than only changing the final stored checkpoint.

The paper also makes clear why the model is described as a $1.58$-bit system rather than a pure $1$-bit model. The weights are ternary, taking values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Because there are three possible states, the effective information content is $\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits per weight. This ternary design is the foundation of BitNet b1.58, and the paper argues that training from scratch is the right way to make such a discrete parameterization work.

# The ternary quantization rule and the meaning of 1.58 bits

To constrain the weights to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the paper uses an absmean quantization function. The idea is to first compute a scale for the weight matrix using its average absolute value, and then map each entry to the nearest ternary value after rescaling. The quantizer is written as
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))),
\]
and
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]

This formula is simple, but its role is central. The average absolute value $\gamma$ gives the matrix a natural scale before rounding, so the quantizer does not treat every layer as if it had the same numerical range. After scaling, each weight is rounded and clipped to one of three allowed values. The result is a parameter matrix that can be interpreted as positive, negative, or inactive.

The addition of the zero state is especially important. The paper explicitly says that this extra value gives the model stronger modeling capability because it supports feature filtering. In other words, the model is not forced to express everything with just a positive or negative sign; it can also suppress a feature entirely. The authors present this as one reason why BitNet b1.58 can outperform a plain binary formulation.

The activation quantization follows the BitNet implementation, but with one important simplification. Instead of scaling activations to $[0, Q_b]$, the paper scales them per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, removing zero-point quantization. The paper describes this as more convenient and simpler for implementation and system-level optimization, and reports negligible performance impact in its experiments. That choice fits the broader training recipe: keep the numerical path simple enough to optimize efficiently, while retaining the low-bit behavior that makes the model cost-effective.

# Using LLaMA-alike components for compatibility and fairness

The paper is explicit that it adopts LLaMA-alike components because LLaMA has become the de-facto backbone for open-source LLMs. This is not a cosmetic choice. It is a way of making BitNet b1.58 easy to understand, easy to compare, and easy to use inside the existing ecosystem. The architecture uses RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and rotary embedding, and removes all biases. Those are all standard ingredients in the LLaMA family, so the low-bit model stays close to a familiar reference point.

This design also serves a fairness purpose. If the authors had introduced a very different Transformer recipe at the same time as the low-bit weights, it would be hard to know where the gains or losses were coming from. By keeping the backbone familiar, they isolate the effect of the quantization scheme itself. The paper’s comparisons to a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline are meaningful precisely because the underlying architectural family is held fixed as much as possible.

There is also a practical benefit: the authors say that this LLaMA-like design allows BitNet b1.58 to be integrated into open-source software with minimal effort. That matters because a low-bit architecture is only useful if it can live inside the tools people already use. By staying close to LLaMA, the model can fit into the same software and inference workflows rather than requiring a separate ecosystem.

# RMSNorm and its role in the recipe

RMSNorm is one of the LLaMA-alike components the paper keeps unchanged. The authors do not introduce a new normalization scheme for BitNet b1.58; instead, they retain the normalization choice already used in the LLaMA family. In the context of the paper, that decision is less about novelty and more about preserving a known transformer structure while changing only the precision of the linear computations.

For a student reading the paper, the important point is methodological. When a model is trained from scratch with ternary weights, there are already many sources of difficulty in optimization. Keeping the normalization module familiar reduces the number of variables being changed at once. The paper does not provide a separate analysis of RMSNorm, but its inclusion signals that the authors wanted the low-bit model to inherit the same architectural scaffold as the full-precision baseline.

That is why RMSNorm belongs in the training recipe even though it is not the main contribution. It is one of the components that makes the low-bit model a drop-in member of the LLaMA style of Transformers, rather than a new architecture with an unfamiliar normalization path.

# SwiGLU as the feed-forward nonlinearity

The paper also keeps SwiGLU as the feed-forward nonlinearity. Again, this is not presented as a novel invention in BitNet b1.58. It is part of the LLaMA-alike backbone that the authors deliberately preserve. The feed-forward block therefore remains a nonlinear module in the standard Transformer sense, even though its linear projections are replaced by low-bit versions.

This choice helps keep the model expressive while the numerical format is made much more restrictive. If the authors had changed both the precision and the feed-forward design, then any change in performance would be hard to interpret. By retaining SwiGLU, they keep the nonlinear structure of the block close to the baseline and make the low-bit linear layers the main variable under study.

Seen this way, SwiGLU is part of the experimental control. It ensures that BitNet b1.58 is not “less of a Transformer” just because its weights are ternary. The architecture still has the nonlinear feed-forward machinery expected in a modern LLM; only the numerical representation of that machinery has been changed.

# Rotary positional embeddings and long-range token interactions

Rotary embedding is the positional mechanism the paper retains from the LLaMA family. Its role here is to give the model the same position-handling strategy as the baseline architecture. Since language modeling depends on representing token order inside attention, keeping rotary embedding preserves the sequence-processing style that open-source Transformer models already rely on.

The paper does not present rotary embedding as a new research contribution, but as one more element of the familiar backbone. That matters for two reasons. First, it keeps the architectural comparison with FP16 LLaMA clean. Second, it makes the low-bit model easier to adopt in existing code and workflows, because the positional mechanism is already part of the standard LLaMA-like design.

For long-range token interactions, the key point in the paper is continuity rather than redesign. BitNet b1.58 is not changing the way sequence positions are represented in order to prove a point about quantization. It is keeping the same positional framework while showing that ternary weights can still support strong language modeling performance.

# Removing biases from the architecture

The paper states that it removes all biases. This is another part of the LLaMA-alike recipe, and it helps keep the architecture aligned with the open-source Transformer family the authors want to build on. The bias-free design is not discussed as a new low-bit trick; it is simply part of the model form that the paper adopts.

That choice is important in a low-bit setting because it reduces architectural variation. A bias term might seem small compared with the rest of a large model, but from the standpoint of experimental design, every extra difference between the baseline and the new model makes interpretation harder. By removing biases, the authors keep the model simpler and closer to the baseline structure they are comparing against.

The result is a cleaner low-bit Transformer. The model still has the same general computational skeleton, but the linear operators are now low-bit and the overall parameterization is more uniform. That uniformity helps both in comparison and in implementation.

# Why keeping the backbone familiar isolates the effect of quantization

One of the most important ideas in the paper is that architectural familiarity is a way of isolating the effect of low-bit quantization. The authors compare BitNet b1.58 to a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline at the same model sizes and training token budget. In the reported experiments, both models are pre-trained on the RedPajama dataset for $100$ billion tokens. Because the backbone is intentionally LLaMA-like, the comparison is not confounded by a completely different Transformer recipe.

This matters because the paper’s main claim is about the value of the numerical format, not about inventing a new model family. If the low-bit model performs well, the reader can attribute that performance to the ternary weights, the $8$-bit activations, and the BitLinear replacement for `nn.Linear`, rather than to a new normalization rule, a new positional scheme, or a new feed-forward design. That is what makes the results persuasive as evidence for a new low-bit scaling recipe.

In textbook terms, this is a controlled experiment in architecture form. The paper keeps the familiar parts of the model fixed so that the low-bit parts are the only major change. That is why the authors can make strong claims about the quality and efficiency of BitNet b1.58 without having to argue that every part of the Transformer has been redesigned.

# Embeddings remaining in full precision and their impact on total memory

The paper notes an important exception to the low-bit story: the embeddings remain full precision. This means BitNet b1.58 is not completely ternary end to end. Even though the core linear layers are heavily quantized, some memory is still spent on parameters that are not compressed all the way down to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

This detail helps explain the memory results in the paper. The authors observe that the memory consumption trend still improves strongly with scale, because the embedding becomes a smaller fraction of the total model memory as the model gets larger. In larger models, the low-bit savings from the many linear layers dominate more and more of the overall footprint. So even though embeddings stay in full precision, the model still becomes much more memory-efficient at scale.

For a reader trying to understand the design, this is a useful reminder that low-bit LLMs are not necessarily uniformly low-bit in every parameter tensor. The important point is that the dominant cost centers are reduced enough to yield major gains, while some components, like embeddings, are left unchanged for practical reasons.

# Ease of integration with HuggingFace, vLLM, and llama.cpp

Because BitNet b1.58 follows the LLaMA-alike recipe, the paper says it can be integrated into popular open-source software with minimal effort. The authors explicitly name HuggingFace, vLLM, and llama.cpp. That is a strong practical signal: the model is not meant to be a laboratory curiosity, but a design that can fit into the tooling people already rely on for training and inference.

This integration story depends on the architecture choices discussed above. By preserving RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and the bias-free Transformer structure, the paper avoids the need for a brand-new software stack. The main architectural replacement is `nn.Linear` becoming `BitLinear`, while the rest of the model remains recognizable to existing systems.

That is why the chapter’s theme is not just “low-bit arithmetic,” but “low-bit arithmetic inside a familiar model family.” The familiarity is what makes the model practical. It lowers the barrier to adoption, makes comparisons cleaner, and supports the paper’s broader claim that a 1.58-bit LLM can be both high-performance and easy to deploy.
