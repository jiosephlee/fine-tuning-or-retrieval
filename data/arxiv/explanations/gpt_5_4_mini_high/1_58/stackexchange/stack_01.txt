\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why is a ternary weight set $\{-1,0,1\}$ described as 1.58 bits instead of 2 bits?
Question:
In the paper "The Era of 1-bit LLMs", BitNet b1.58 uses weights in $\{-1,0,1\}$ and says that adding $0$ "results in 1.58 bits in the binary system". I thought three states usually need 2 bits to store if encoded directly. Is the 1.58 number just $\log_2 3$, meaning an information-theoretic lower bound, or do they actually pack weights so the average storage is close to 1.58 bits? I am confused about whether this is a theoretical naming convention or an actual implementation detail.
Answer:
The short answer is that the paper is using “$1.58$ bits” in an information-theoretic sense, not saying that a single ternary weight is literally stored in $1.58$ physical bits.

A ternary weight can take $3$ values: $\{-1,0,1\}$. If you ask, “how many binary bits are equivalent to $3$ equally possible states?”, the answer is the number $b$ such that $2^b = 3$, namely

$$
b = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585.
$$

That is where the $1.58$ comes from. So the name “BitNet b1.58” is basically a shorthand for “a model whose weights live in a ternary alphabet,” and the paper explicitly says they “added an additional value of $0$ to the original $1$-bit BitNet, resulting in $1.58$ bits in the binary system.”

But that does not mean the implementation stores each weight in a weird fractional-bit format. In practice, a direct fixed-length encoding of three states needs $2$ bits per weight, because $2$ bits can represent up to $4$ states and $1$ bit is not enough. The paper actually hints at this practical distinction: it says that latency and memory were measured with a $2$-bit kernel, and that there is still room for optimization. So the reported “$1.58$ bits” is the idealized entropy-equivalent cost of the ternary parameter set, not a claim that the current kernel literally achieves $1.58$ bits/weight in storage.

So the clean way to think about it is: $1$-bit weights mean binary weights, $2$-bit weights mean four-way quantization, and ternary weights sit in between. The number $1.58$ is just $\log_2 3$, i.e. the binary information content of three possible values. It is a naming convention grounded in information theory, not a statement about the exact packing format used by the implementation.
