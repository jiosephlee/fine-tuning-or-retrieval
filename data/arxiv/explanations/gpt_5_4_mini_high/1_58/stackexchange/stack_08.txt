\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why does scaling activations to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ remove the zero-point in quantization?
Question:
The paper says that, unlike the original BitNet, activations are scaled per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ "to get rid of the zero-point quantization". I am confused about this statement. In symmetric quantization, zero is often represented exactly anyway, so what zero-point are they avoiding here? Also, since some activations go through non-linearities like SwiGLU, why is symmetric per-token scaling still convenient?
Answer:
They are using “zero-point” in the standard **affine quantization** sense, not in the sense of “can the value $0$ be represented exactly?”.

In affine quantization, a real value $x$ is mapped to an integer code by something like

$$
q = \mathrm{round}(x/s) + z,
$$

where $s$ is the scale and $z$ is the **zero-point**: the integer code that should correspond to real zero. You need this offset when the integer range is not centered at zero, for example if you quantize into an unsigned range such as $[0, 255]$ or, in the paper’s notation, something like $[0, Q_b]$.

What BitNet b1.58 does instead is use a **symmetric signed range** per token:

$$
q = \mathrm{clip}\big(\mathrm{round}(x/s), -Q_b, Q_b\big),
$$

so real zero maps to integer $0$ automatically. There is no separate offset term $z$, hence no “zero-point quantization” to deal with. That is all the paper means by “getting rid of the zero-point”.

This is why the paper says it is “more convenient and simple”: with symmetric quantization, the kernel only needs a scale and a signed integer range. With asymmetric quantization, you also have to subtract/add the zero-point in the implementation, which complicates both the math and the low-level code.

As for SwiGLU and other nonlinearities: they do not cause a problem here because the quantizer is not assuming linearity of the activation; it just quantizes whatever tensor comes out. A per-token scale is actually a good fit for this, since the dynamic range can vary a lot from token to token, especially after nonlinearities. If a particular activation tensor happens to be mostly positive, symmetric quantization is still valid — it just means some of the signed integer range goes unused. The paper explicitly says this simplification has negligible effect on performance.

So the short version is: they are not “making zero representable” — zero already is. They are removing the **offset/zero-point parameter** that asymmetric quantization would require.
