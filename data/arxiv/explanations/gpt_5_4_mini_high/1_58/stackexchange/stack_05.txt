\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How can matrix multiplication with ternary weights require almost no multiplications?
Question:
The paper says BitNet b1.58 changes the computation paradigm and that matrix multiplication "requires almost no multiplication operations". But the model still multiplies ternary weights by 8-bit activations. Is the idea that multiplying by $-1$, $0$, or $1$ can be replaced by sign flips, skips, and additions? If so, does this only become true on custom hardware, or can current GPUs also avoid most multiply instructions in practice?
Answer:
Yes — the point is exactly that a ternary weight turns a multiply into either a sign flip, a no-op, or an addition into the accumulator.

For one dot product, if the weights are $w_i \in \{-1,0,1\}$ and the activations are 8-bit integers $x_i$, then

$$
\sum_i w_i x_i
$$

can be implemented as

$$
\sum_{w_i=1} x_i \;-\; \sum_{w_i=-1} x_i,
$$

with the $w_i=0$ terms skipped entirely. So the “multiplication” is gone in the usual hardware sense: there is no general-purpose multiply needed for the main matrix multiply, just integer add/subtract and masking. That is what the paper means by “matrix multiplication requires almost no multiplication operations” and why it says BitNet’s matrix multiplication “only involves integer addition.”

The important distinction is between the mathematical operation and the hardware instruction. Mathematically it is still a matrix product, but hardware-wise the costly multiply-accumulate path is replaced by integer accumulation over ternary coefficients. There are still a few multiplies elsewhere in the model pipeline, for example in quantization/scaling and non-linear components, but the dominant linear layers are where the big saving comes from.

On your second question: this is not purely a custom-hardware trick, but custom hardware makes it much more attractive. The paper’s reported GPU results were measured with optimized software kernels, using FasterTransformer and a 2-bit kernel from Ladder, so current GPUs can بالفعل exploit much of this structure if you write specialized kernels. However, a stock dense GEMM kernel will not automatically “discover” that multiplying by $-1$, $0$, or $1$ can be replaced by sign changes and skips. To get the practical benefit on today’s GPUs, you need kernels tailored to ternary weights and low-bit activations. The paper’s hardware discussion is essentially saying that the same idea opens the door to even better speed and energy efficiency on hardware designed around these operations.
