\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How is the reported $71.4\times$ energy reduction calculated, and what is being excluded?
Question:
The paper states that BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4\times$ arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips, based on prior energy models. I am trying to understand what exactly is counted in this number. Does it only compare the energy of FP16 multiply-adds against INT8 additions inside the matrix multiplications, while ignoring memory traffic, control overhead, softmax, normalization, and embedding lookups? If so, how should I relate that number to the smaller end-to-end energy gains reported later?
Answer:
Yes, essentially. The paper’s $71.4\times$ figure is a **kernel-level arithmetic-energy estimate for the matrix multiplications only**, not an end-to-end inference measurement. The authors say they “focus mainly on the calculation for matrix multiplication” because it dominates LLM cost, and then use the energy model from prior work to compare the arithmetic cost of FP16 LLaMA versus BitNet b1.58 on 7nm chips. In that accounting, the FP16 baseline pays for both floating-point multiplication and addition, while BitNet’s ternary weights let the linear layers be implemented mostly with INT8 additions, so the estimated arithmetic energy for those GEMMs drops by a factor of $71.4$.

What is being excluded is everything outside that arithmetic core. So this number does not include memory traffic to and from DRAM/SRAM, kernel launch or control overhead, or the other transformer components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, softmax, embedding lookups, and KV-cache movement. The paper is explicit that this is “arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication,” which is much narrower than total model inference energy.

That is also why the later end-to-end energy gains are smaller. Whole-model energy is the sum of the matrix-multiplication part plus all the other parts, and only the former benefits from the huge BitNet reduction. If a fraction $f$ of the baseline energy is spent in linear layers, then reducing just that part by $71.4\times$ gives an overall gain of roughly $\frac{1}{(1-f)+f/71.4}$. Unless $f$ is extremely close to $1$, the total gain must be much smaller than $71.4\times$. The paper makes exactly this point when it notes that the end-to-end advantage grows with model size, because the share of energy spent in $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ increases while the “other components” become relatively less important.

So the right interpretation is: $71.4\times$ is the **best-case compute-side reduction for matrix multiplications**, while the end-to-end numbers are the **realistic full-inference savings** after all the non-matmul costs are included.
