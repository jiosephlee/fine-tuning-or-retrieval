\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why do the memory savings and latency speedups improve as the model size increases?
Question:
The paper reports that BitNet b1.58 becomes relatively faster and more memory efficient at larger scales, for example reaching a $4.1\times$ speedup at 70B. The explanation mentions that the time cost of linear layers grows with model size and that embeddings remain full precision, so their fraction of total memory shrinks. Could someone explain this more concretely? Is the trend mainly because quantized linear layers dominate larger models, while the unquantized components become a smaller percentage of the total?
Answer:
Yes — that is essentially the right intuition, and the paper is making an Amdahl’s-law argument in disguise.

The key point is that BitNet b1.58 only changes the parts of the Transformer that are dominated by linear layers, i.e. the matrix multiplications inside the attention and MLP blocks. Those are the expensive pieces, and the paper explicitly says that “the time cost for $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ grows with the model size.” If you write the total latency as

$$
T_{\text{FP16}} = T_{\text{linear}} + T_{\text{other}},
\qquad
T_{\text{BitNet}} = \alpha T_{\text{linear}} + T_{\text{other}},
$$

with $\alpha < 1$ because the linear layers are much cheaper in BitNet, then the speedup is

$$
\frac{T_{\text{FP16}}}{T_{\text{BitNet}}}
=
\frac{T_{\text{linear}} + T_{\text{other}}}{\alpha T_{\text{linear}} + T_{\text{other}}}.
$$

This ratio gets larger when $T_{\text{linear}}$ makes up a bigger fraction of the total. That is exactly what happens as the model grows: the repeated Transformer blocks contribute more and more of the runtime, while things like embeddings, norms, softmax, and other non-linear bookkeeping become a smaller slice of the pie. So the same optimization on the linear layers buys you a bigger relative gain at 70B than at 3B.

The memory story is similar. The paper says that the embedding remains full precision, so its absolute size does not benefit from ternary weights. But the total parameter memory of the model grows mostly in the Transformer blocks, not in the embedding table, so the embedding becomes a smaller fraction of the whole as scale increases. If you think of memory as

$$
M_{\text{FP16}} = M_{\text{embed}} + M_{\text{linear}} + M_{\text{other}},
\qquad
M_{\text{BitNet}} \approx M_{\text{embed}} + \beta M_{\text{linear}} + M_{\text{other}},
$$

with $\beta \ll 1$, then the savings are largest when $M_{\text{linear}}$ dominates. That is why the relative memory reduction improves with model size.

So, yes: the trend is mainly because the quantized linear layers dominate larger models, while the unquantized components become a smaller percentage of the total. The paper says this directly for latency and memory, and even in the energy section it notes that “the percentage of $\mathrm{nn.Linear}$ grows with the model size.” The larger the model, the closer the overall cost gets to “mostly linear layers,” and the more BitNet’s ternary weights pay off.
