\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# A New Scaling Law for AI? What BitNet b1.58 Suggests About Bigger Models

## The core claim

Most LLM scaling stories have the same shape: if you want better quality, you usually pay for it with more memory, more latency, more energy, or all three. The BitNet b1.58 paper argues that this link is not as fixed as it looks.

Their main claim is that if you train a model whose weights are ternary, taking values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, then you can move to much larger models without paying the usual inference cost. In other words, a larger model does not have to mean a more expensive model. The paper calls this a new scaling law, because the tradeoff curve between quality and inference cost changes.

That is the interesting part here. This is not just about squeezing an existing model into a smaller format after training. BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch as a 1.58-bit model, with 8-bit activations, and the authors show that it can match full-precision baselines on both perplexity and zero-shot tasks once the model is large enough.

## What BitNet b1.58 actually changes

BitNet b1.58 is based on the original BitNet idea, but with one important twist: it uses three weight values instead of two. That extra zero matters.

The model replaces standard linear layers with `BitLinear` layers. During training, the weights are normalized by their average absolute value and then rounded and clipped to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Activations are also quantized, but the paper keeps them at 8-bit precision. To make the implementation cleaner, activations are scaled per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, avoiding zero-point quantization.

The architecture itself is intentionally familiar. The authors use LLaMA-style components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and no biases. That choice makes the model easier to integrate into open-source tooling such as Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp.

The practical effect of this design is that matrix multiplication no longer looks like standard FP16 Transformer math. In BitNet-style models, the computation becomes much closer to integer addition, with almost no multiplication operations. That matters because the bulk of LLM inference cost is in linear layers.

## Why the cost curve changes

The paper’s argument is simple but powerful: large language models spend most of their time and energy in linear layers, and those layers are expensive mainly because they use floating-point arithmetic and move a lot of data.

With ternary weights, you reduce both compute and memory pressure.

First, the arithmetic gets cheaper. The paper emphasizes that matrix multiplication in BitNet uses integer addition rather than FP16 multiplication and addition. On 7nm chips, their energy model suggests that this saves 71.4 times the arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication.

Second, the memory footprint drops. A 1.58-bit model needs far less storage for weights, and less bandwidth to move them from DRAM to on-chip memory. That is important at inference time, where memory movement can be a major bottleneck.

This is also why the gains get better as models get larger. The bigger the model, the larger the share of cost coming from linear layers, and the more the ternary representation helps.

## What the experiments show

The paper compares BitNet b1.58 against a reproduced FP16 LLaMA-style baseline. To keep the comparison fair, both are pretrained on RedPajama for 100 billion tokens.

The models are evaluated on perplexity using WikiText2 and C4, and on zero-shot tasks including ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, and BoolQ.

The headline result is that BitNet b1.58 starts to match the FP16 baseline at the 3B scale. At that point, it matches both perplexity and end-task performance while being significantly cheaper to run.

Some of the most useful comparisons from the paper are:

- A 3B BitNet b1.58 model matches the FP16 baseline in perplexity and zero-shot performance.
- A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is 2.4 times faster and uses 3.32 times less GPU memory than a 3B FP16 baseline, while performing better.
- At 3B, the paper reports a 2.71 times speedup and 3.55 times lower memory use when comparing matching perplexity points.
- At 70B, BitNet b1.58 is 4.1 times faster than the FP16 baseline.
- For throughput, a 70B BitNet b1.58 model on two 80GB A100 GPUs supports up to 11 times the batch size of the FP16 baseline and reaches 8.9 times higher throughput.

The paper also gives a useful way to think about the new efficiency frontier:

- 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 3B FP16.
- 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16.
- 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16.

That is the core of the scaling-law claim. The point is not just that ternary models are cheaper. It is that you can often move to a much larger ternary model and still end up below a much smaller FP16 model in latency, memory, and energy.

## Why the zero weight matters

One subtle but important detail is the inclusion of $0$ as a legal weight value.

A pure binary model can only flip features on or off with positive or negative sign. A ternary model can also filter features out entirely. The paper argues that this gives BitNet b1.58 stronger modeling capability than the original 1-bit version.

That extra flexibility seems to matter. The authors report that BitNet b1.58 can match full-precision performance starting from the 3B scale, and that its zero-shot gap closes as model size increases.

## The 2T-token result matters too

The paper does not stop at 100 billion tokens. It also trains a BitNet b1.58 model on 2 trillion tokens using the data recipe from StableLM-3B, then compares it on Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC-Easy.

The result is that BitNet b1.58 outperforms StableLM-3B on all of those tasks.

This matters because it suggests the approach is not just a small-scale trick. The model still generalizes well when trained at a much larger token budget, which is exactly what you would want from a new scaling recipe.

## What this means for deployment

If the paper’s results hold up more broadly, the deployment strategy for LLMs changes in a few important ways.

First, model selection becomes less tied to full precision hardware budgets. A larger 1.58-bit model may be a better deployment choice than a smaller FP16 model if you care about quality under a fixed latency or memory budget.

Second, the reduced memory footprint helps with systems bottlenecks. That is especially important for inference, where loading weights from memory can be expensive.

Third, the paper points toward more practical deployment on edge, mobile, and CPU-heavy environments. The authors explicitly argue that lower memory and energy requirements make 1.58-bit LLMs more suitable for these settings.

The discussion section also highlights a few future directions:

- 1-bit Mixture-of-Experts models, where reduced memory could make MoE systems easier to deploy.
- Longer-context inference, since 8-bit activations already reduce the cost of KV caches.
- New hardware optimized specifically for 1-bit LLM computation.

## The takeaway

BitNet b1.58 is interesting because it changes the question. Instead of asking, “How small can we make this model without losing quality?”, the paper asks, “How much model capacity can we buy if we change the bit budget?”

The answer, at least in these experiments, is that you can get much more than a simple compression story would suggest. A ternary model trained from scratch can match FP16 quality at 3B scale, outperform smaller FP16 baselines at larger scales, and do so with better latency, memory use, throughput, and energy efficiency.

That is why the paper frames BitNet b1.58 as more than a quantization trick. It is an attempt to redraw the Pareto frontier for LLMs. If that direction continues to hold, future model scaling may be about more than parameters and tokens. It may also be about choosing the right bit-width from the start.
