\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# Why 1.58 Bits Could Change AI: A Plain-English Guide to BitNet b1.58

Large language models usually store their weights in 16-bit floating point formats like FP16 or BF16. That is convenient, but expensive: more memory, more bandwidth, more energy, and more compute. The paper *The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits* takes a different route. Instead of shrinking a finished model after training, it trains a model whose weights are constrained to just three values: $-1$, $0$, and $1$.

That sounds extreme, but the core claim is simple: if you change the model recipe itself, not just the deployment format, you can get much cheaper inference without losing much performance. The authors call this model **BitNet b1.58**.

## Why 1.58 bits?

The name is not a typo. A weight with three possible states carries about $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits of information. So while the weights are described as ternary, the model is often framed as a 1.58-bit LLM.

This matters because it is not just “fewer bits” in the usual compression sense. A standard 16-bit weight can represent a very fine-grained real number. A ternary weight can only choose between “subtract,” “ignore,” and “add.” That is a much harsher constraint, but it also makes the arithmetic much simpler.

In practice, a ternary matrix multiply can replace many floating-point multiplications with sign flips, additions, and skips. That is where the speed and energy savings come from.

## This is not the same as post-training quantization

Most low-bit LLMs today are built with post-training quantization. The model is first trained in full precision, then compressed afterward for inference. That approach is useful, but the paper argues it is still suboptimal.

BitNet b1.58 does something different. It is trained from scratch with **1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations**. The model architecture replaces standard linear layers with a custom **BitLinear** layer, and the weights are constrained during training to stay in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

The paper uses an “absmean” quantization rule: weights are scaled by their average absolute value and then rounded and clipped to the ternary range. Activations are also quantized, but in a simpler symmetric way than in the original BitNet. Instead of using a zero-point style setup, activations are scaled per token into a range like $[-Q_b, Q_b]$. The authors say this is easier to implement and optimize, while having negligible impact on performance in their experiments.

So the key idea is not “let’s compress a trained model.” It is “let’s build the model to work well at low precision from the start.”

## Why the extra zero helps

BitNet b1.58 is an evolution of the earlier BitNet idea. The new ingredient is the $0$ state.

That may look small, but it changes the behavior of the model in an important way. A weight of $0$ means the model can explicitly drop a connection instead of being forced to choose only between positive and negative influence. The paper argues that this gives the model stronger feature filtering capability, which helps recover performance that would otherwise be lost in an aggressively quantized model.

In other words, the ternary design is not just about saving bits. It also gives the model a more flexible way to decide what not to use.

## What the authors tested

To keep the comparison fair, the authors pre-trained BitNet b1.58 and a reproduced FP16 LLaMA-style baseline on the same RedPajama dataset for 100 billion tokens. They then evaluated perplexity on WikiText2 and C4, and zero-shot accuracy on a standard set of language tasks including ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, and BoolQ.

The headline result is that **BitNet b1.58 starts to match full-precision performance at the 3B model size**. At that point, it is also much cheaper to run. The paper reports that a 3B BitNet b1.58 model is about **2.71 times faster** and uses **3.55 times less GPU memory** than the FP16 baseline.

The 3.9B version is even more interesting: it runs about **2.4 times faster**, uses **3.32 times less memory**, and performs better than the 3B FP16 baseline. The authors describe this as a Pareto improvement, meaning the model is better on both quality and cost.

## The gains get larger as models scale

The paper also scales the comparison up to 7B, 13B, and 70B models. Here the cost gap becomes even more obvious. On 70B models, BitNet b1.58 was measured as **4.1 times faster** than the FP16 baseline.

Throughput improves too. On two 80GB A100 GPUs, the 70B BitNet b1.58 model could support up to **11 times the batch size**, leading to **8.9 times higher throughput**.

Energy is another major theme. For matrix multiplication on 7nm chips, the paper estimates **71.4 times less arithmetic energy consumption** compared with the FP16 baseline. Since matrix multiplication dominates LLM compute, that is a big deal. The authors also report end-to-end energy measurements for 512-token runs, and the advantage grows with model size.

The broader pattern they point to is a new scaling relationship: larger BitNet b1.58 models can be more efficient than much smaller FP16 models. In their comparison, 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 3B FP16, 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16, and 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16.

## Why this matters beyond benchmarks

The paper’s argument is bigger than a single model family. If low-bit weights can be trained natively and still match full-precision quality at useful scales, then inference cost stops being the same bottleneck it is today.

That opens up several directions the authors mention. Lower memory footprints could make Mixture-of-Experts models easier to deploy. Smaller activations could help with long-context inference. The reduced compute and memory demands could make LLMs more practical on edge and mobile devices. And because the computation pattern is so different, the paper also points to the need for hardware designed specifically for 1-bit-style models.

## The main takeaway

BitNet b1.58 is not just another quantization trick. It is a proposal for training LLMs in a way that makes low-precision arithmetic a first-class design choice.

The paper shows that ternary weights, paired with 8-bit activations and a LLaMA-like architecture, can match FP16 baselines at 3B scale and deliver large gains in latency, memory, throughput, and energy. That does not mean every model should become ternary tomorrow. But it does suggest that the next era of LLMs may be less about ever-bigger floating-point matrices, and more about smarter representations that are built for the hardware we actually have.
