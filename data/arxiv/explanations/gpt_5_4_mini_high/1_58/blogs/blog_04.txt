\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# The Secret Power of Zero: Why Ternary Weights Beat Pure 1-Bit Designs

Quantizing an LLM down to 1-bit sounds like the endgame for efficiency. But the main lesson from **BitNet b1.58** is that the winning move is not to force every weight into two states. It is to give the model a third option: **zero**.

That small change turns a strict binary design into a ternary one with weights in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. The paper argues that this is enough to improve modeling strength through **feature filtering**, while keeping the efficiency gains that make low-bit LLMs interesting in the first place.

## Why zero matters

In a binary-weight layer, every connection has to be either positive or negative. That means every input feature always contributes in some way, even if the model would really prefer to ignore it.

Adding zero changes that. Now a weight can say:

- keep the feature,
- flip the feature,
- or drop the feature entirely.

That last option is the important one. The paper describes it as **explicit support for feature filtering**, and that is a big deal for language models. LLMs are full of linear projections, and not every feature is useful in every context. A ternary weight gives the network a cheap way to suppress unhelpful signals instead of forcing them through the layer.

This is why the model is called **1.58-bit** rather than strict 1-bit. With three possible values, the effective information content is about $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits. In practice, that extra state is not just a numerical detail. It gives the model more room to express the right transformation.

## BitNet b1.58 keeps the efficiency wins

BitNet b1.58 is built on the BitNet idea of replacing standard linear layers with **BitLinear**. The model is trained from scratch with **1.58-bit weights** and **8-bit activations**. The paper also keeps the architecture close to the LLaMA family by using **RMSNorm**, **SwiGLU**, **rotary embeddings**, and no biases.

That matters because the goal is not to invent a completely different model family. The goal is to make low-bit training and inference practical inside the ecosystem people already use.

The weight quantization itself is simple: weights are scaled by their average absolute value and then rounded and clipped to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. On the activation side, the paper uses an 8-bit scheme that scales activations symmetrically per token, which simplifies implementation and system-level optimization.

The result is still very efficient. Compared to FP16 models, BitNet b1.58 keeps the big gains in latency, memory, throughput, and energy consumption that make low-bit LLMs attractive. The zero does not bring back the expensive floating-point path.

## What the experiments show

The paper compares BitNet b1.58 against a reproduced FP16 LLaMA-style baseline at the same model sizes and with the same 100B-token pretraining budget on RedPajama.

The main takeaway is simple: **the ternary model catches up**.

Starting at the **3B** scale, BitNet b1.58 matches the FP16 baseline in both perplexity and downstream task performance. At that size, it is also **2.71 times faster** and uses **3.55 times less GPU memory**. At **3.9B**, it performs better than the 3B FP16 model while still being cheaper to run.

The same pattern holds as the models scale up. The paper reports that larger BitNet b1.58 models keep improving the efficiency gap, with the **70B** model reaching **4.1 times faster** inference and **8.9 times higher throughput** in the measured setup.

So the benefit of zero is not just theoretical. It shows up in the performance tradeoff curve.

## The bigger picture

The paper’s deeper point is that low-bit LLMs do not have to choose between efficiency and quality. A strict binary design is fast, but it can be too rigid. Ternary weights add just enough flexibility to make the model stronger, while still preserving the computational advantages of 1-bit-style arithmetic.

That is the real power of zero.

It gives the model a way to say “not this feature,” which is a surprisingly important capability when you are compressing a Transformer down to almost the smallest representation possible. And according to the paper, that small extra choice is enough to turn BitNet b1.58 into a **Pareto improvement** over FP16 baselines at comparable scale.

In other words, the future of low-bit LLMs may not be strictly binary. It may be ternary, and zero may be the most valuable symbol in the set.
