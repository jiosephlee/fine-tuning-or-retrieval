\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 11: Evaluating Chatbots: MMLU, Vicuna, OA, Humans, GPT-4, and Elo

# Using MMLU to measure broad language understanding

The paper uses the MMLU benchmark as one of its main quantitative measures of instruction-finetuned model quality. MMLU, short for Massively Multitask Language Understanding, is a multiple-choice benchmark covering 57 tasks. The tasks span a wide range of subject matter, including elementary mathematics, US history, computer science, law, and more. In the paper’s setup, performance is reported as $5$-shot test accuracy, meaning that the model is given five example question-answer pairs before being evaluated on the test question.

The intuition behind MMLU is that it checks whether a model can generalize across a broad set of academic-style tasks. Because the answers are constrained to multiple-choice selections, the benchmark provides a clean, standardized way to measure language understanding. This makes MMLU useful for comparing models under a common scoring rule. In the paper, it serves as the “broad understanding” side of evaluation, complementing the chatbot-oriented benchmarks that focus on open-ended responses.

At the same time, the paper emphasizes that MMLU is not the same thing as chatbot quality. The authors use it because it is widely accepted and easy to score, but they later show that strong MMLU performance does not necessarily imply strong performance on chatbot benchmarks such as Vicuna or OA. This distinction is important for understanding the paper’s evaluation framework: one benchmark measures a model’s general knowledge and reasoning in a constrained format, while the others try to assess the quality of free-form conversational responses.

# Why generative chatbot evaluation requires different methods than multiple-choice testing

The paper argues that evaluating chatbots requires a different setup from evaluating multiple-choice language understanding. MMLU has a clearly defined answer space and a straightforward notion of correctness. By contrast, chatbot evaluation asks a model to produce an entire response to a user query, and there is often no single objectively correct answer. A response may be helpful, polite, detailed, concise, or safe in different degrees, and multiple responses can all be reasonable. This makes simple accuracy-style metrics inadequate.

Because of this, the paper uses curated query sets and judgment-based evaluation for chatbot performance. It treats chatbot evaluation as a comparison of response quality rather than answer correctness. To generate responses for these evaluations, the authors use nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$ in all cases. That choice reflects the fact that chatbot performance should be judged on realistic generated outputs rather than deterministic completions alone.

The paper also stresses that there is no commonly accepted protocol for chatbot evaluation in the literature. That absence matters because if the benchmark and scoring method are not well chosen, a model may appear good without actually being useful in conversation. The authors therefore design a framework that combines curated prompts, model-vs-model judgments, human judgments, and pairwise ranking. The point is not just to score responses, but to approximate how users and evaluators might actually judge chatbot behavior in practice.

# The Vicuna benchmark and its 80 curated prompts

One of the paper’s two main chatbot benchmarks is the Vicuna benchmark. It consists of $80$ prompts curated from a diverse set of categories. The authors use these prompts without modification. The benchmark was originally introduced in work on Vicuna, and in this paper it is used as a testbed for comparing different chatbot systems, especially against ChatGPT.

The reason the Vicuna benchmark is useful is that it supplies a controlled set of open-ended prompts that probe conversational ability. Unlike multiple-choice tests, these prompts require the model to generate an entire answer. The paper evaluates model responses to these prompts using both automated and human judgments, which allows the authors to estimate how well a chatbot performs in realistic dialogue settings.

A key detail is that the Vicuna benchmark is used in a relative scoring framework. Instead of assigning a raw absolute quality score to a response in isolation, the paper compares each model’s response to ChatGPT’s response on the same prompt. This makes the benchmark especially suited to ranking chatbots against a strong reference system. The paper’s later findings show that this benchmark is particularly favorable to open-source models, though the authors caution that the confidence intervals are wide and that absolute scoring can be difficult to interpret consistently.

# The OA benchmark built from OpenAssistant validation conversations

The second main chatbot benchmark is the OA benchmark, which the paper builds from the OpenAssistant validation dataset. OpenAssistant is a multilingual collection of crowd-sourced multiturn dialogs between a user and an assistant. To construct the benchmark, the authors select all user messages in the validation dataset as queries and include the previous turns in the prompt. This yields $953$ unique user queries.

This setup makes OA different from Vicuna in two important ways. First, it is larger, so it tests chatbot behavior on a broader set of queries. Second, it is multiturn and multilingual, which means it reflects a different style of interaction than the short curated Vicuna prompts. The paper uses OA to study whether models trained on OpenAssistant-like data generalize well to the benchmark derived from that same kind of interaction.

The OA benchmark is especially useful in the paper’s analysis because it sometimes favors different systems than Vicuna does. The authors note that the larger OA benchmark favors ChatGPT more strongly, while Vicuna tends to be friendlier to open-source models. This helps support one of the paper’s central claims: benchmark choice matters, and different benchmarks can reward different kinds of instruction tuning.

# Relative scoring against ChatGPT using GPT-4 as a judge

The paper uses GPT-4 as an automated judge in one of its main evaluation protocols. The setup follows the evaluation protocol introduced in the Vicuna work. For a given query, GPT-4 is shown ChatGPT’s response and a model’s response. It is then prompted to assign a score out of ten to both responses and provide an explanation. The overall performance of the model is computed as a percentage of the score that ChatGPT achieved.

This produces a relative score, not an absolute one. A model can score above $100\%$ if it receives a higher absolute score than ChatGPT. The intuition is straightforward: rather than asking whether a response is “good” in the abstract, the judge asks which of two responses is better and how much better. That makes the score easier to interpret when comparing against a strong reference model.

The paper also notes an important caveat: GPT-4 evaluation has an ordering effect. GPT-4 tends to give higher scores to the response that appears earlier in its prompt. Because of this bias, the authors recommend reporting the mean score over both possible orders. This detail matters because it shows that automated model-based evaluation is convenient but not perfectly neutral. The evaluation procedure itself can influence the outcome.

# Pairwise comparisons with win, loss, and tie labels

In addition to relative scoring against ChatGPT, the paper uses pairwise comparisons between systems. Rather than asking GPT-4 or human annotators to score each response independently, the evaluation asks which of two responses is better for a given prompt. The authors simplify the rating scheme to a three-class labeling problem: win, loss, or tie.

This pairwise setup has a practical advantage. It avoids some of the ambiguity of absolute scoring scales, where different raters may interpret numerical values differently. A pairwise decision is often easier to make because the judge only needs to compare two candidate answers and decide which is preferable, or whether neither is clearly better. The paper applies this protocol on all permutations of pairs of systems on both the Vicuna and OA benchmarks.

The pairwise labels are not the final result by themselves. Instead, they feed into the tournament-style ranking method described later in the chapter. The important idea is that the benchmark does not merely count how often a model “wins” in isolation. It uses those head-to-head outcomes to build a larger ranking over many systems, making the comparison process more like a tournament than a standalone test.

# Human evaluation protocols on Amazon Mechanical Turk

The paper does not rely only on GPT-4. To check whether GPT-4 judgments align with human judgments, the authors run parallel human evaluations on the Vicuna benchmark using Amazon Mechanical Turk. They match the automated evaluation protocols as closely as possible.

For comparisons to ChatGPT, they use two human annotators. For pairwise comparisons, they use three annotators. This setup allows them to compare human judgments both with the GPT-4 score-against-ChatGPT protocol and with the pairwise win/loss/tie protocol. In other words, human evaluation is used not just as a final score but as a reference for checking whether the automated method is behaving sensibly.

The paper also reports that even human evaluation is not perfectly stable. In the discussion section, the authors note moderate agreement among human annotators, with Fleiss $\kappa=0.42$, and additional deterioration when comparing two strong systems. This is an important lesson in the paper’s evaluation framework: chatbot quality is inherently subjective enough that even humans can disagree, especially when the systems being compared are both strong.

# Order effects and prompt-position bias in GPT-4 evaluation

One of the paper’s most important evaluation findings is that GPT-4 exhibits a prompt-position bias. When two responses are shown to GPT-4, it tends to assign a higher score to the response that appears earlier in the prompt. The authors identify this as a significant ordering effect in the relative scoring protocol against ChatGPT.

This matters because it means that the measured score can depend not only on the model outputs themselves but also on how those outputs are arranged in the evaluation prompt. A system can appear better or worse simply because its response was presented first or second. To reduce this effect, the authors recommend averaging the results over both response orders.

The paper also extends this caution to GPT-4 as a judge more generally. In their broader analysis, they observe that GPT-4 gives significantly higher scores to its own outputs than human ratings do, which suggests another form of bias. The key lesson is not that GPT-4 is unusable, but that automated evaluation should be interpreted carefully. It is a useful and cheap tool, but not a perfect substitute for human judgment.

# Elo rating as a tournament-style ranking method for chatbots

To turn pairwise comparisons into an overall ranking, the paper uses Elo rating. Elo is a tournament-style scoring system originally used in chess and other games. The basic idea is that each model starts with a rating, and that rating changes after each match depending on whether the model performed better or worse than expected. An upset causes a larger change than an expected result.

The paper explains Elo intuitively in terms of expected win rates. For example, an Elo of $1100$ versus $1000$ corresponds to an expected win-rate of approximately $65\%$ for the higher-rated system. A match between equal ratings, such as $1000$ versus $1000$, corresponds to a $50\%$ expected win-rate. This makes Elo useful for summarizing a large number of pairwise outcomes into a single ranking.

In the paper’s setup, the authors start with a score of $1000$ and use $K=32$. They repeat the process $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds to control for ordering effects, such as which model pairs are compared first. This repeated tournament procedure is what lets them rank systems like Guanaco, ChatGPT, and others in a way that is less dependent on any single comparison. The Elo results become one of the main summaries of chatbot performance in the paper.

# Agreement metrics between GPT-4 and human judges at system and example levels

The paper does not assume that GPT-4 and human judgment are identical. Instead, it measures how much they agree. At the system level, the authors report Kendall Tau of $\tau=0.43$ and Spearman rank correlation of $r=0.55$ between GPT-4 and human rankings on the Vicuna benchmark. These values indicate moderate agreement: the two judging methods often rank systems similarly, but not always.

At the example level, the agreement is weaker. The paper reports Fleiss $\kappa=0.25$ for agreement between GPT-4 and human annotators’ majority vote. This is lower than the system-level agreement because individual examples are more sensitive to judgment differences and subjective preferences. The paper therefore concludes that model-based evaluation is a reasonably reliable alternative to human evaluation, but one that still has meaningful uncertainties.

The paper also discusses human-human agreement. In the considerations section, the authors note Fleiss $\kappa=0.42$ among human annotators, with additional deterioration when comparing two strong systems. This reinforces the idea that evaluating chatbot responses is hard even for people. Taken together, the agreement metrics support a nuanced conclusion: GPT-4-based evaluation is useful and much cheaper than large-scale human labeling, but it should be treated as an approximate proxy rather than a perfect substitute.

The overall message of the paper’s evaluation framework is that no single metric is enough. MMLU measures broad language understanding in a constrained format, while Vicuna and OA probe open-ended chatbot behavior. GPT-4 can act as a scalable judge, but it has ordering effects and other biases. Human evaluation remains important, but it is subjective and expensive. Elo provides a practical way to aggregate pairwise judgments into rankings. Understanding all of these pieces together is essential for reading the paper’s chatbot results correctly.
