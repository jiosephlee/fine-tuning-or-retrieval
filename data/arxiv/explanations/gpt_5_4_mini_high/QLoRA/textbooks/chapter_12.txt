\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 12: Guanaco Results and What They Reveal About Benchmarks

# Guanaco $65$B Reaching About $99.3\%$ of ChatGPT on Vicuna

The paper’s most headline-grabbing chatbot result is that Guanaco $65$B reaches $99.3\%$ of ChatGPT’s performance on the Vicuna benchmark. On the paper’s relative scoring scale, ChatGPT is the reference point, so a score this close to $100\%$ means Guanaco is nearly matching the reference system in the authors’ evaluation. The result is important not just because the gap is small, but because it comes from an open model finetuned with QLoRA on a single GPU, rather than from a much more resource-intensive full finetuning setup.

The authors emphasize that Guanaco is not a one-off fluke. Their second-best model in the family reaches $97.8\%$ of ChatGPT on Vicuna, and they report that the $65$B model is the best-performing open-source chatbot in their evaluation after GPT-4. In other words, the central story is not simply that a single large model did well, but that QLoRA enables a family of models to rise to a level where they compete with the strongest publicly visible chatbots.

This matters because Vicuna is a practical chatbot benchmark, not a simple language modeling score. The paper uses it to test how well models produce useful responses to curated prompts, which is closer to the behavior people care about in chat systems than ordinary next-token prediction. Guanaco’s near-ChatGPT performance therefore signals that the method is not only memory-efficient, but also capable of preserving the kind of conversational behavior that benchmark authors are trying to measure.

# How Guanaco Compares with Vicuna, Open Assistant, GPT-3.5, Bard, and GPT-4

The paper compares Guanaco against both open and commercial systems. On the open side, key baselines include Vicuna and Open Assistant. Vicuna is a LLaMA $13$B model fully finetuned on ShareGPT conversations, and Open Assistant is a LLaMA $33$B model finetuned with RLHF on the OASST1 dataset. On the commercial side, the paper includes GPT-3.5-turbo, Bard, and GPT-4. This broad comparison is useful because it shows where Guanaco sits relative to both research models and production chatbots.

The key conclusion is that Guanaco rises above the openly released competitors. The paper says the Guanaco $33$B and $65$B models outperform all models besides GPT-4 on the Vicuna and OA benchmarks, and that they perform comparably to ChatGPT. In the Vicuna-relative scoring, Guanaco $65$B is the strongest open model the authors report, and Guanaco $33$B also improves on Vicuna $13$B despite using only $4$-bit weights. The paper also reports that Guanaco $7$B scores nearly $20$ percentage points higher than Alpaca $13$B.

For GPT-4, the paper uses pairwise human-annotated comparisons and reports that Guanaco $65$B and $33$B each have an expected win probability of about $30\%$ against GPT-4. That is still clearly below the strongest commercial model, but it is much stronger than one would expect from a small, efficient finetuning method applied to a quantized base model. The result is especially notable because the authors describe it as the highest reported to date in that evaluation setting.

# The Performance and Memory Footprint of Guanaco $7$B, $33$B, and $65$B

One reason the Guanaco results are striking is that the models occupy very different points on the efficiency–quality tradeoff. The smallest Guanaco model, $7$B, requires only about $5$ GB of memory when deployed. The $33$B model uses about $21$ GB, which is less than the $26$ GB reported for Vicuna $13$B in the paper’s comparison. The largest model, $65$B, is finetunable on a single $48$ GB GPU, which is the main hardware milestone emphasized by the paper.

The paper treats these memory numbers as more than engineering trivia. They show that QLoRA makes it possible to increase model scale without requiring a proportional increase in accessible hardware. The authors highlight that Guanaco $33$B can be trained on a single consumer GPU in less than $12$ hours, and the largest $65$B model can reach $99.3\%$ of ChatGPT on Vicuna after about $24$ hours on a single professional GPU. This is part of the paper’s broader claim that finetuning has become much more accessible.

The $7$B model is especially useful as a reminder that efficiency does not automatically imply poor quality. Even though it is much smaller than the $33$B and $65$B models, the paper says it still performs strongly and outperforms Alpaca $13$B by a wide margin on Vicuna. The memory figures make the practical implication clear: QLoRA lets researchers work with models that would otherwise be too large to finetune, while still getting useful chatbot behavior at multiple scales.

# Why Vicuna Score Confidence Intervals Are Wide

Although the Vicuna benchmark results look impressive, the paper warns readers not to over-interpret small differences in the reported absolute scores. The confidence intervals are wide, and many models overlap. That means the benchmark often cannot cleanly separate systems that look different on paper but may be very close in practice. The authors suggest that this uncertainty likely comes from the lack of a clear specification for what a given score on the $10$-point scale actually means.

This is a subtle but important measurement problem. If one rater thinks an answer deserves an $8$ and another thinks the same quality corresponds to a $6$, then the absolute scale has poor calibration. The score is no longer a direct measurement of quality; it is partly a reflection of how each evaluator interprets the scale. The paper’s concern is that this makes it difficult to compare models precisely, especially when many of the best systems are close together.

The wide intervals also mean that a single benchmark score can exaggerate differences that are not robust. If multiple models’ confidence intervals overlap, the apparent ranking depends heavily on small statistical fluctuations and evaluator interpretation. That is why the authors move away from trying to read too much into one absolute number and instead recommend a more relative style of evaluation.

# Why the Authors Prefer Pairwise Elo Rankings Over Absolute $10$-Point Scores

To address the weaknesses of absolute scoring, the paper recommends pairwise Elo ranking. The intuition is straightforward: instead of asking a judge to assign a standalone numeric score to a response, the judge compares two responses and decides which is better, or whether they tie. This turns evaluation into a tournament rather than a calibration problem. The judge does not need to decide whether a response is a $7$ or an $8$; they only need to determine which of two responses is preferable.

The paper uses this idea both with GPT-4 and with human annotators. GPT-4 is prompted either to score responses against ChatGPT on a $10$-point scale or to make direct head-to-head comparisons. For the pairwise setup, the paper simplifies the task to a three-class choice: response A wins, response B wins, or tie. The results are then aggregated into Elo scores, which are familiar from chess and other games. In the paper’s example, an Elo of $1100$ versus $1000$ corresponds to an expected win rate of about $65\%$ for the higher-rated system.

This approach is attractive because it avoids forcing evaluators to agree on an absolute notion of quality. It is also more robust when two systems are close in performance. The paper starts the Elo rating at $1000$, uses $K=32$, and repeats the process $10{,}000$ times to reduce ordering effects. In the authors’ view, the resulting pairwise ranking is easier to interpret than an uncertain absolute score with wide confidence intervals.

The paper also reports an important practical issue with the absolute-score protocol: GPT-4 shows an ordering effect, giving higher scores to the response that appears earlier in the prompt. That is exactly the kind of hidden evaluation bias that pairwise ranking can help expose. For that reason, the authors recommend reporting the mean score over both orders if an absolute-score protocol is used at all.

# Human Judgment, GPT-4 Judgment, and Where They Disagree

One of the more interesting findings in the paper is that GPT-4-based evaluation and human evaluation are broadly similar, but not identical. On the Vicuna benchmark, the authors run parallel human evaluations to match the automated protocols. They find that GPT-4 and human annotators usually agree on the overall ranking of models, which suggests that GPT-4 can be a useful and cheap alternative to human evaluation for broad comparisons.

At the same time, the paper is careful not to oversell that agreement. There are real disagreements, especially when systems are strong and differences are subtle. The authors report that even among human annotators, agreement is only moderate and becomes worse when comparing two strong systems. In their discussion, they note that subjective preferences start to matter a lot, and even the authors themselves disagreed on many preferred responses when manually comparing ChatGPT and Guanaco $65$B generations.

The result is a nuanced one: GPT-4 evaluation is useful, but it is not a perfect substitute for humans. The paper’s message is not that GPT-4 should replace human raters entirely. Instead, GPT-4 can serve as a practical approximation when resources are limited, but its outputs should still be checked for bias and inconsistency, especially when conclusions depend on fine distinctions between strong systems.

# System-Level Correlation Versus Example-Level Agreement

The paper helps readers distinguish between two different kinds of agreement, and this distinction is crucial. System-level agreement asks whether two evaluation methods rank models in roughly the same order overall. Example-level agreement asks whether they make the same judgment on each individual prompt or response pair. These are related, but they are not the same thing.

For system-level comparisons, the paper reports Kendall Tau of $\tau=0.43$ and Spearman rank correlation of $r=0.55$ between GPT-4 and human rankings. Those numbers show moderate agreement: the two methods generally point in the same direction, but not strongly enough to treat them as interchangeable. At the example level, the agreement is weaker, with Fleiss $\kappa=0.25$ between GPT-4 and human annotators’ majority vote. That means the two judging processes often disagree on individual cases even when they broadly agree on the ordering of systems.

The paper also reports that human annotators themselves had Fleiss $\kappa=0.42$, with agreement dropping when the comparison involved two strong systems. That helps explain why example-level agreement is hard to achieve. When responses are both plausible, the evaluation becomes more subjective, and the task of choosing a winner becomes much less clean than a benchmark score might suggest.

The distinction matters because a good system-level correlation can still hide many local disagreements. A model can be ranked similarly by two judges even if the judges often disagree on specific outputs. The paper’s conclusion is therefore careful: GPT-4 is a reasonable alternative for aggregate evaluation, but researchers should not assume perfect alignment with human judgment at the level of individual examples.

# Why MMLU Does Not Reliably Predict Chatbot Quality

The paper’s results on MMLU make a strong point about benchmark mismatch. MMLU measures multiple-choice language understanding across $57$ tasks, with $5$-shot test accuracy. That makes it a useful benchmark for broad academic knowledge and reasoning-style tasks, but it is not the same as judging a chatbot’s conversational usefulness. Vicuna and OA, by contrast, evaluate free-form responses to user prompts. Because the tasks are different, a model that does well on one does not necessarily do well on the other.

The paper gives a concrete example: finetuning on FLAN v2 leads to strong MMLU performance, but poor Vicuna performance. This means that a dataset can produce a model that looks excellent on one benchmark while being relatively weak on another. The reverse pattern also appears in the paper’s broader discussion: strong chatbot performance does not imply strong MMLU performance. The benchmarks are partially orthogonal, so success on one should not be treated as a proxy for success on the other.

This is one of the central lessons of the paper. If a benchmark measures a different behavior from the one you care about, then improvement on that benchmark may not tell you much about the target application. The paper uses this observation to argue that researchers need to be explicit about what they are optimizing for: broad knowledge, conversation quality, instruction following, or some mix of these goals.

# How Benchmark Choice Favors Different Finetuning Datasets and Behaviors

The paper shows that benchmark choice can strongly affect which finetuning dataset looks best. One example is the contrast between FLAN v2 and chatbot-oriented datasets such as OASST1 and Chip2. The paper reports that a relatively small, high-quality dataset can outperform a much larger one on chatbot performance, and it highlights that data quality matters more than raw size. In the introduction, the authors even note that a $9$k-sample dataset, OASST1, outperformed a $450$k-sample FLAN v2 subset on chatbot performance.

This helps explain why benchmark outcomes can be misleading if read too literally. FLAN v2 is well aligned with MMLU-style evaluation, so it looks strong there. But for chatbot benchmarks such as Vicuna, the same training choice can be poor. The paper also notes that the Vicuna benchmark favors open-source models while the larger OA benchmark favors ChatGPT. The exact same model family can therefore look better or worse depending on which benchmark is used.

The broader implication is that benchmark design can steer research in different directions. If a benchmark rewards one style of response or one kind of training data, models will tend to optimize for that pattern. The paper explicitly warns that the community should be careful about what it is evaluating in the first place. That warning is especially relevant here: a benchmark is not a neutral window into model quality, but a lens that emphasizes some behaviors and hides others.

# Open Data Versus Proprietary Data in the Top-Performing Chatbots

The paper also places Guanaco in the context of open versus proprietary data. Guanaco is especially notable because it is the only top-performing model in the paper’s evaluation that is not trained on proprietary data. The reason is that the OASST1 dataset collection guidelines explicitly forbid GPT models, so Guanaco’s strong results are not based on distillation from commercial systems in the way some other models are.

That makes Guanaco’s performance more than just a technical success for QLoRA. It shows that a carefully chosen open dataset can produce a chatbot that competes with much more closed systems. The paper reinforces this point by noting that the next best model trained only on open-source data, Anthropic HH-RLHF, scores $30$ percentage points lower than Guanaco on the Vicuna benchmark. So while proprietary data clearly remains influential in the strongest systems, the paper demonstrates that open-data finetuning can still get surprisingly close to the frontier.

At the same time, the comparison with Vicuna reminds readers that some strong open-source chatbots do rely on data derived from proprietary systems. Vicuna $13$B is finetuned on ShareGPT conversations, which the paper describes as user-shared conversations distilled from OpenAI GPT models. Guanaco’s result therefore matters because it shows competitive performance without relying on that kind of proprietary-origin training signal.

Taken together, the paper’s findings suggest a broader lesson about benchmarks and data sources. Open data can support state-of-the-art chatbot behavior, but benchmark performance still depends heavily on how well the finetuning data matches the target evaluation. The best-looking benchmark result is not simply the one from the largest model or the largest dataset; it is often the one whose training data, model scale, and evaluation target are most closely aligned.
