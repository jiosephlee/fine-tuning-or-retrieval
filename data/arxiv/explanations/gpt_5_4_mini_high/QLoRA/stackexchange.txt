\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### How can gradients flow through frozen 4-bit weights in QLoRA if those weights are never updated?
Question:
I'm trying to understand the basic training mechanism in QLoRA. The paper says it "backpropagates gradients through a frozen, 4-bit quantized pretrained language model into Low Rank Adapters." But later it also says that gradients with respect to the base weights $\mathbf{W}$ are not needed, only gradients for the LoRA weights $\mathbf{L}_1, \mathbf{L}_2$ are updated.

If $\mathbf{W}$ is frozen, what exactly does it mean for gradients to pass "through" the quantized weights? Is the idea just that the forward pass uses dequantized $\mathbf{W}$, and then backprop uses those values to compute gradients for the adapter branch, without ever storing or applying $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$? I'm confused about what is computed versus what is updated.
Answer:
Yes — in QLoRA, “gradients flow through the frozen 4-bit weights” does **not** mean that the 4-bit weights themselves receive an update. It means that the frozen base model is still part of the computational graph, so the loss can be backpropagated **through** it to compute gradients for the trainable LoRA parameters and for earlier activations. The base weights are treated as constants.

The paper is explicit about this. It says that QLoRA has one **storage** datatype, usually 4-bit NF4, and one **computation** datatype, usually BF16. In other words, when a weight tensor is used, it is first dequantized to BF16 and then used in the forward pass. During backpropagation, the same dequantized BF16 values are used to compute the necessary Jacobians. But only the LoRA parameters $\mathbf{L}_1$ and $\mathbf{L}_2$ are updated; the base weights $\mathbf{W}$ are frozen, so no optimizer step is applied to them.

So the right mental model is this: the forward pass computes something like
$$
\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2,
$$
where $\mathbf{W}$ is stored in 4-bit form but dequantized to BF16 for computation. Backprop then uses the chain rule to get gradients for $\mathbf{L}_1$ and $\mathbf{L}_2$ (and gradients w.r.t. the input activation $\mathbf{X}$ if needed for earlier layers), but the gradient w.r.t. $\mathbf{W}$ is simply not used. So, yes, your interpretation is basically correct: the dequantized $\mathbf{W}$ participates in the forward and backward computations, but $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$ is neither stored nor applied.

This is exactly analogous to a frozen feature extractor in ordinary transfer learning. The fixed network still transforms the signal and still lets error information propagate backward through it, but its own parameters are not trained. QLoRA just makes that frozen backbone very memory-efficient by storing it in 4-bit form and doing the actual math in BF16.

### Why does finetuning a 65B model in 16-bit supposedly require more than 780 GB of GPU memory?
Question:
The paper says regular 16-bit finetuning of LLaMA 65B requires more than 780 GB of GPU memory, while QLoRA can do it in less than 48 GB. I can see that $65$ billion parameters at 2 bytes each is already huge, but it still does not immediately explain the full number to me.

Could someone break down where the 780 GB comes from? Is it mainly model weights, gradients, optimizer states, activations, or all of them combined? I'm trying to understand which part QLoRA is reducing the most.
Answer:
The $>780$ GB figure is not just the raw 16-bit weights. It is the usual training-state cost of a full finetune, where every parameter needs storage for the parameter value itself, its gradient, and the optimizer’s internal state.

For an Adam-style optimizer, a good back-of-the-envelope estimate is

$$
65\times 10^9 \text{ params} \times \bigl(2\ \text{bytes for weights} + 2\ \text{bytes for gradients} + 8\ \text{bytes for Adam moments}\bigr).
$$

That is $12$ bytes per parameter, so

$$
65\times 10^9 \times 12 \approx 780\times 10^9 \text{ bytes} \approx 780 \text{ GB}.
$$

So the headline number is mainly the combination of weights, gradients, and optimizer states. Activations are *additional* memory on top of that, which is why the paper says “more than” $780$ GB rather than exactly $780$ GB.

What QLoRA reduces most is not just the weight precision. The biggest saving comes from freezing the 65B base model entirely, so you do *not* need 16-bit gradients or optimizer states for those parameters at all. The base weights are stored in 4-bit instead of 16-bit, and only the small LoRA adapters are trained in 16-bit. In the paper’s own accounting, double quantization only saves about $0.37$ bits per parameter, roughly $3$ GB for a 65B model, so it is helpful but not the main reason the method fits on a single GPU.

So the short version is: the $780$ GB is mostly “full-model training state” rather than just weights, and QLoRA wins primarily by turning that full-model finetune into a tiny adapter finetune over a frozen, quantized base model.

### If LoRA only trains a tiny number of parameters, why is quantizing the base model still so important?
Question:
I may be misunderstanding parameter-efficient finetuning. LoRA only adds a small number of trainable parameters, and the paper even says the LoRA weights can be much smaller than the activation gradients.

If the trainable part is already small, why is the main bottleneck still the frozen base model? In other words, why can't we just do ordinary 16-bit LoRA on very large models without quantization? I'm missing which tensors dominate memory during training.
Answer:
LoRA makes the *update* small, but it does not make the *model* small. That is the key point the paper is emphasizing.

During training, even if the backbone weights are frozen, you still have to keep those weights around to run the forward pass and to backpropagate through the network into the adapters. So LoRA removes the need to store gradients and optimizer states for the base model, but it does **not** remove the memory cost of the base model itself. For very large LLMs, that frozen backbone is the dominant term.

The paper gives a concrete breakdown that makes this clear. For a 7B LLaMA model with LoRA on about $0.2\%$ of the weights, the LoRA parameters are only about 26 MB, while the LoRA input gradients are about 567 MB without checkpointing, or about 18 MB per sequence with gradient checkpointing. But the 4-bit base model itself is about 5,048 MB. So even though the trainable part is tiny, the frozen model is still far larger than the adapters, and that is before you scale up to 33B or 65B models. In fact, the paper says regular 16-bit finetuning of LLaMA 65B needs more than 780 GB of GPU memory.

That is why quantizing the base model matters so much in QLoRA. QLoRA keeps the pretrained weights in 4-bit storage and dequantizes them to BF16 only for computation, while the LoRA adapters remain trainable in 16-bit. In other words, the memory bottleneck is not the LoRA weights; it is the frozen backbone plus activations. Quantization attacks the part that actually dominates memory.

This also explains why “ordinary 16-bit LoRA” is fine for smaller models but stops being practical for very large ones. The adapters are cheap, but the full pretrained model is still huge. QLoRA’s contribution is showing that you can compress that frozen part enough to make large-scale finetuning feasible on a single GPU, without losing the performance of 16-bit finetuning.

### What objective makes NF4 'information-theoretically optimal' for normally distributed weights?
Question:
The paper repeatedly says that 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) is "information theoretically optimal" for zero-centered normally distributed weights. I understand the construction idea: choose quantization levels based on quantiles of $N(0,1)$ so that each bin gets an equal expected number of values.

What I do not understand is: optimal with respect to what exact criterion? Is it minimizing expected quantization error, maximizing entropy usage of the codebook, minimizing KL divergence, or something else? I'd like a more precise interpretation of the claim, because "optimal" sounds stronger than just "uses bins evenly."
Answer:
In the paper, “information-theoretically optimal” is meant in a coding/entropy sense, not in a mean-squared-error sense.

If you have a $k$-bit scalar quantizer, it has at most $2^k$ output symbols. For a random weight $X$ and a quantizer $Q$, the discrete output $Z = Q(X)$ satisfies

$$
H(Z) \le \log_2(2^k) = k.
$$

So the maximum possible entropy of the quantized representation is $k$ bits, and you achieve that maximum exactly when all codewords are used equally often, i.e. when

$$
\Pr(Z=i) = 2^{-k} \quad \text{for all } i.
$$

That is what the quantile construction does for a source distribution that is actually normal: choose the bin boundaries at the quantiles of $N(0,1)$, so each bin has equal probability mass. For a deterministic quantizer, this also means

$$
I(X;Q(X)) = H(Q(X)),
$$

so you are maximizing the information carried by the discrete code index among all $k$-bit quantizers of that source. In that narrow sense, the codebook is “information-theoretically optimal.”

So the precise criterion is not “minimize reconstruction error” or “minimize KL divergence between the original and reconstructed weights.” The paper is using “optimal” to mean “maximizes entropy / uses all $2^k$ symbols evenly under the assumed normal weight distribution.” That is why they call NF4 a normal-matched quantile quantizer.

One important caveat is that this is a property of the assumed distribution, not a universal optimality claim. If your objective were minimizing squared quantization error, the optimal scalar quantizer would generally be a different one, such as a Lloyd-Max quantizer. QLoRA’s claim is narrower: for weights that are approximately zero-centered normal, NF4 matches the source distribution well enough that the 4-bit codebook is fully utilized, and empirically that works better than FP4 or Int4.

### Does absolute-maximum scaling conflict with NF4 being designed for normal distributions?
Question:
In the quantization background, the paper first explains absolute-maximum rescaling for each block. Later, NF4 is motivated from the assumption that pretrained weights are approximately normally distributed.

What confuses me is that absolute-maximum scaling seems very sensitive to outliers, while a normal-distribution-based codebook seems to assume a stable shape. If a block has one unusually large weight, wouldn't scaling by $\operatorname{absmax}$ distort the rest of the values and break the "optimal for normal weights" argument? How are these two ideas supposed to fit together?
Answer:
No, not in the way you’re thinking. In the paper, absolute-maximum scaling and NF4 are doing two different jobs, and they fit together rather naturally.

The key point is that NF4 is not claiming “the raw weights are exactly standard normal, with no outliers.” It is saying that pretrained weights are *approximately* zero-centered and normal in shape, so a codebook whose levels are placed at normal quantiles is a good match. The absolute-maximum factor is just the per-block scale that maps that block into the fixed target range, here $[-1,1]$. In the paper’s own words, this step is “equivalent to rescaling the standard deviation of the weight tensor to match the standard deviation of the $k$-bit data type.”

So the logic is: first normalize the block’s overall scale, then quantize using a codebook shaped like a normal distribution. The scale parameter and the distributional shape are separate. A blockwise absmax does not have to “preserve normality” in a perfect mathematical sense; it only needs to bring the block into the right range so that the normal-quantile codebook can be used.

Your outlier concern is real, but it is not a contradiction. If one weight in a block is unusually large, then yes, absmax will stretch the whole block and the smaller values will be compressed into fewer bins. But that is a generic problem of block quantization, not something specific to NF4. The paper’s response to this is to use relatively small blocks, which limits how much a single outlier can dominate, and then to rely on the fact that the pretrained weights are “usually” close enough to normal that this works well overall.

So the right way to read the paper is not “NF4 assumes perfectly stable normal blocks and absmax never causes trouble.” It is “we assume weights are approximately normal at the block level, we normalize each block to a common range with absmax, and then we use a codebook that is optimal for a normal-shaped distribution.” The outlier sensitivity of absmax is acknowledged implicitly as the usual drawback of block quantization; NF4 is an empirical answer to that, not a claim that outliers disappear.

That is also why the paper emphasizes empirical results: NF4 beats FP4 and recovers 16-bit finetuning performance in their experiments. So even though the two assumptions are only approximate, they are apparently good enough in practice for pretrained LLM weights.

### Why does NF4 need an asymmetric codebook just to represent zero exactly?
Question:
The NF4 section says that a symmetric $k$-bit quantizer based on quantiles does not give an exact representation of zero, so they construct an asymmetric datatype: $2^{k-1}$ values for the negative side and $2^{k-1}+1$ for the positive side, then merge the two sets and remove one duplicate zero.

I don't follow why this asymmetry is necessary. Why would a symmetric codebook fail to include zero exactly, and why is representing zero so important here beyond padding tokens? Also, after making the codebook asymmetric, do the bins still have equal probability mass under a normal distribution?
Answer:
The key point is that NF4 is not using an arbitrary symmetric set of four-, or more generally $k$-bit, values; it is using a quantile-based codebook for a zero-mean normal distribution. With an even number of levels, a perfectly symmetric codebook has no “middle” value. You can have something like $\{-a,-b,b,a\}$, but then $0$ is necessarily between $-b$ and $b$, not itself a codebook entry. That is exactly what the paper means when it says a symmetric $k$-bit quantizer “does not have an exact representation of zero.”

Why not just make the codebook symmetric and include $0$ anyway? Because a $k$-bit datatype has $2^k$ distinct code values, and $2^k$ is even. A symmetric set with an exact zero would need an odd number of levels, e.g. $\{-a,-b,0,b,a\}$, which is incompatible with having exactly $2^k$ distinct states. So if you want both “exact zero” and “use all $2^k$ codes,” you have to break the strict symmetry in the count of levels. That is why the paper splits the negative and positive sides as $2^{k-1}$ and $2^{k-1}+1$, merges them, and removes the duplicate zero.

Representing zero exactly matters for more than padding tokens because zero is a special value in neural nets generally. The paper explicitly mentions “padding and other zero-valued elements.” In practice, exact zeros occur in masks, padded positions, and other places where the model expects a literal zero, not a small approximation. More broadly, since pretrained weights are approximately zero-centered normal, having a codepoint at $0$ avoids injecting unnecessary error right at the center of the distribution, where many weights live.

As for the probability mass: yes, the quantizer is still intended to give equal expected mass per quantization bin under the normal prior. The asymmetry is in the placement and count of reconstruction values, not in abandoning the quantile idea. The paper’s claim is that NF4 has “equal expected number of values in each quantization bin.” So the bins are still quantile bins of the normal distribution; they are just arranged asymmetrically so that one of the code values is exactly $0$.

So the short version is: a symmetric even-sized codebook cannot have a central zero, and NF4 wants a zero code because zero is both numerically useful and common in neural network tensors. The asymmetry is just the smallest change needed to get that while preserving the equal-mass quantile construction.

### How is the 0.373 bits-per-parameter saving from double quantization computed?
Question:
The double quantization section says that with block size 64, storing one 32-bit scale per block costs $32/64 = 0.5$ bits per parameter. Then after quantizing those scales again, the cost becomes $8/64 + 32/(64 \cdot 256) = 0.127$ bits per parameter, saving 0.373 bits.

I can reproduce the arithmetic, but I am not sure I understand the accounting. Why is the second term divided by $64 \cdot 256$? Is that because one 32-bit second-level scale is shared across 256 first-level scales, and each first-level scale is itself shared across 64 weights? A step-by-step explanation of the hierarchy would help.
Answer:
Yes, your interpretation is exactly the right way to think about it.

In the paper, the original 4-bit weight tensor is quantized blockwise with block size $64$. That means one scale $c_2$ is stored for every $64$ weights. If that scale were stored in FP32, then its storage cost, amortized over the original weights, would be $32/64 = 0.5$ bits per weight. This is just bookkeeping: one extra 32-bit number per $64$ parameters.

Double quantization reduces that overhead by quantizing those scales $c_2$ again. The paper says that the second quantization uses blocks of $256$ scales. So the hierarchy is:

each block of $64$ weights gets one first-level scale $c_2$, and then each block of $256$ such scales gets one second-level scale $c_1$ used to encode them.

If you count metadata per original weight, the first-level quantized scales now cost $8$ bits per block of $64$ weights, which is $8/64 = 0.125$ bits per weight. But those $8$-bit scales themselves need a scale for dequantization: one FP32 $c_1$ per $256$ first-level scales. Since one first-level scale corresponds to $64$ weights, one second-level scale is shared across $256 \cdot 64$ original weights. That is why its amortized cost is $32/(64 \cdot 256)$ bits per weight.

So the total overhead becomes
$$
\frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64 \cdot 256}
= 0.125 + 0.001953125
= 0.126953125 \approx 0.127 \text{ bits/parameter}.
$$
Compared to the original $0.5$ bits/parameter, the saving is
$$
0.5 - 0.127 \approx 0.373 \text{ bits/parameter}.
$$

A concrete way to see it is to imagine $256$ blocks of weights. That is $256 \times 64 = 16384$ weights. Without double quantization, you would store $256$ FP32 scales, costing $256 \times 32 = 8192$ bits. With double quantization, you store $256$ FP8 scales plus one FP32 scale for the whole group, costing $256 \times 8 + 32 = 2080$ bits. Dividing by $16384$ weights gives the same $0.127$ bits per weight.

So yes: the $64$ comes from the weight block size, and the extra $256$ comes from the block size used when quantizing the scales themselves.

### Why does double quantization subtract the mean from the first-level scales before quantizing them?
Question:
The paper says the first-level quantization constants $c_2^{\mathrm{FP32}}$ are positive, so before quantizing them again, it subtracts their mean to center them around zero and use symmetric quantization.

I'm confused about reconstruction here. If all the original scales are positive, and we subtract the mean before quantizing, where is that mean stored, and how do we guarantee that the recovered scales are still accurate enough? Is this just a trick to make the second quantizer use its levels more efficiently?
Answer:
Yes — the main point is exactly to make the second quantizer use its codebook efficiently, but the mean is not “thrown away.”

In QLoRA, the first-level constants $c_2^{\mathrm{FP32}}$ are blockwise scale factors, so they are all positive and usually live in a fairly narrow range. If you quantize those values directly with a symmetric 8-bit format, you waste half of the representable values on negative numbers that never occur. By subtracting the mean first, the authors recenter the scales around zero so that symmetric quantization fits their distribution much better.

The reconstruction is only implicit in the paper’s compact notation, but conceptually the shift is part of the second-level quantization state. In other words, you should think of the recovered scale as something like
$$
\hat c_2 \approx \mu + \mathrm{dequant}(c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}}, c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}),
$$
and then the weight block is reconstructed with
$$
\hat W = \mathrm{dequant}(\hat c_2, W^{\mathrm{NF4}}).
$$
So the mean is not lost; it is stored as part of the metadata for the second quantization step. The paper compresses this into the phrase “second level of quantization constants” and does not spell out the offset explicitly.

As for accuracy, there is no exact guarantee in the mathematical sense because this is still a lossy scheme. The justification is empirical: the scale values are much easier to compress than the weights themselves, and quantizing them with 8-bit floats at a blocksize of 256 introduces very little additional error. The paper reports that double quantization does not degrade performance in their experiments, while saving about $0.373$ bits per parameter. So yes, it is primarily a trick to use the second quantizer’s levels more efficiently, but it is a controlled one: the offset is retained, and the resulting approximation is accurate enough in practice.

### Is the notation $\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$ in the QLoRA section correct, or am I reading it wrong?
Question:
In the formal QLoRA section, the paper says that computing the adapter gradients $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$ entails the calculation of $\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$, using the dequantized weights to compute that derivative in BF16.

This looks odd to me because in a linear layer I would expect derivatives like $\frac{\partial \mathbf{Y}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$ or gradients propagated back to $\mathbf{X}$, not $\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$. Is this a notation mistake in the paper, or is there a specific interpretation I'm missing?
Answer:
Yes — that symbol is almost certainly a notation mistake, or at least very sloppy wording.

In a linear layer of the form $\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$, the quantity that appears in backpropagation is not $\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$; $\mathbf{X}$ is the input activation, so it is not a function of $\mathbf{W}$ in the usual computational graph. What you actually need is the gradient flowing backward through the frozen base layer, namely $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{X}}$, which is computed using the transpose of the dequantized weight matrix:
$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{X}} = \frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{Y}} \mathbf{W}^\top + \cdots
$$
where $\mathbf{W}$ here is the frozen 4-bit weight after dequantization to BF16 for computation.

That is exactly what the paper is trying to say in words: the quantized base weights are dequantized to BF16 “to perform the forward and backward pass,” but gradients are only formed for the LoRA parameters, not for $\mathbf{W}$. So the role of dequantization is to make the frozen linear map usable during backpropagation, not to compute a meaningful derivative $\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$.

So the clean interpretation is: the paper intended to refer to backpropagation through the frozen quantized layer, i.e. using the dequantized $\mathbf{W}$ to compute gradients with respect to activations and then to the adapter weights. The notation $\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$ itself is not the standard or correct one in this context.

### Why does applying LoRA to all linear layers matter more than increasing the rank $r$?
Question:
One result that surprised me is that the paper says standard LoRA on only query/value projections does not match full finetuning, but placing adapters on all linear transformer layers does. It also says that the rank $r$ does not affect performance much in their setup.

Intuitively I would have expected a larger rank to be the main way to increase LoRA capacity. Why would spreading adapters across more layers matter more than making each adapter larger? Is this telling us something about where task-specific changes need to be inserted in the network?
Answer:
The key point in the paper is that LoRA capacity is not just “how many degrees of freedom do I give one adapter,” but also “at which computations in the transformer do I allow the model to change.” A LoRA update to a matrix $W$ has the form $\Delta W = L_1L_2$, so increasing the rank $r$ only makes that one update space larger. By itself, that does not help if the real task-specific changes are spread across several parts of the block.

That is exactly what the authors observe empirically. The standard LoRA setup of adapting only the query and value projections does not match full fine-tuning, while putting adapters on all linear transformer block layers does. In other words, the limitation was not mainly that each adapter was too narrow; it was that too few layers were being adapted at all. Once you give every linear map in the block a small low-rank correction, the model can adjust attention, value mixing, and the feed-forward transformations throughout the network. Those small changes can accumulate across depth and are often enough to recover full 16-bit performance.

Why doesn’t a larger $r$ fix the $q/v$-only case? Because a larger rank only enriches the correction at those two locations. It cannot alter the intermediate representations created by the MLP layers or other projections. If the needed adaptation is distributed across the network, a very wide adapter in one place is a poor substitute for modest adapters in many places. In practice, the paper finds that once $r$ is “large enough,” further increasing it has little effect, which suggests that the bottleneck is not the per-layer rank but the fact that the adaptation is localized too narrowly.

So yes, the paper is telling us something about where task-specific changes live: for these models and tasks, useful finetuning is distributed across the transformer block, not concentrated only in $q$ and $v$. This also fits the memory story in QLoRA: LoRA parameters are tiny compared with activation memory, so spreading adapters across more layers is cheap, while increasing rank gives less benefit than broadening the set of layers that can move. The result is empirical rather than a theorem, but it strongly suggests that “coverage across layers” matters more than “size of a few adapters” for matching full fine-tuning.

### Why can 4-bit QLoRA recover the performance lost by 4-bit inference quantization?
Question:
The paper notes that 4-bit quantization alone usually hurts performance at inference time, but then shows that 4-bit QLoRA can recover 16-bit finetuning performance after training adapters.

Conceptually, why should a small set of LoRA parameters be able to compensate for the information lost when the base model is quantized to 4 bits? Is the idea that the quantization error is systematic enough that the adapter can learn to correct it, or is something else going on?
Answer:
The key point is that QLoRA is not asking a tiny adapter to “reconstruct” the entire 16-bit model from only 4-bit weights. It is using the 4-bit model as a frozen, already-competent backbone and then learning a small residual correction on top of it.

In the paper’s formulation, each linear layer becomes $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{4\text{-bit}} + \mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$. So the LoRA term is literally an additive update to the quantized weight matrix. That means the adapter only needs to learn the difference between “the quantized pretrained model plus task adaptation” and “the best task-specific solution”, not recover every lost bit of the original weights. Since the base model already encodes most of the language knowledge, and downstream finetuning usually changes the model in a relatively constrained way, a low-rank update is often enough in practice.

It is also important that QLoRA is not using a crude 4-bit representation. The paper’s NF4 data type is designed to be information-theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights, which is exactly the regime pretrained weights tend to live in. So the quantization error is already relatively small and structured. The adapter then learns whatever correction matters for the downstream objective. The paper does not claim this is because the quantization error is perfectly systematic in some clean analytical sense; rather, it shows empirically that with NF4 and enough adapters, the model can absorb the performance loss on the tasks they test.

The other important detail is that the 4-bit weights are only for storage. During training, QLoRA dequantizes to BF16 for computation, and gradients are only computed for the LoRA parameters. So the model is not doing the actual forward/backward math in 4-bit; 4-bit is what makes the base model fit in memory. That distinction matters: the adapter is learning in a normal high-precision optimization setup, just with a quantized frozen backbone.

The paper’s experiments support this interpretation. They find that the default LoRA setup on just query/value projections is not enough, but applying LoRA to all linear layers recovers 16-bit performance. They also find that NF4 outperforms generic FP4, and that NF4 plus double quantization matches 16-bit LoRA and full finetuning on the evaluated benchmarks. So the right intuition is not “LoRA magically restores lost information,” but “the quantized pretrained model is already close enough that a small learned residual can correct the remaining error relevant to finetuning.”

### Does the paper really establish equivalence to full 16-bit finetuning at 33B and 65B scale?
Question:
I want to make sure I'm interpreting the claims correctly. The paper says QLoRA preserves full 16-bit finetuning performance, but later in the limitations section it also says they did not establish that QLoRA matches full 16-bit finetuning at 33B and 65B because of resource costs.

So is the evidence actually: (1) equality to full 16-bit finetuning only for smaller models up to about 3B, and (2) equality to 16-bit LoRA, not full finetuning, for larger LLaMA models? If so, should the headline claim be read more cautiously?
Answer:
Yes — that is the right way to read the paper.

The key point is that the paper has two different kinds of comparisons, and they are not equally strong. For the smaller-model experiments, the authors do compare \textsc{QLoRA} directly against full $16$-bit finetuning, and they say that for models up to about $3$B parameters they observe that $4$-bit adapter finetuning recovers the performance of the fully finetuned $16$-bit baseline. That is the part of the paper that actually supports the claim that quantizing the base model to 4 bits does not hurt performance in those tested settings.

For the larger LLaMA experiments, though, they explicitly stop comparing against full finetuning. In the paper they say that full finetuning at and beyond $11$B parameters would require more than one high-memory server, so for the $7$B to $65$B runs they instead compare \textsc{QLoRA} to $16$-bit LoRA. In that regime, the claim they directly establish is that NF4 \textsc{QLoRA} matches $16$-bit LoRA performance on the benchmarks they use, especially MMLU. That is a much weaker statement than “it matches full $16$-bit finetuning,” because the full-finetuning baseline was not actually run at those sizes.

The limitations section makes this completely explicit: “we did not establish that \textsc{QLoRA} can match full $16$-bit finetuning performance at $33$B and $65$B scales.” So your interpretation is basically correct. The strong evidence is: full $16$-bit parity for the smaller models where they could afford the baseline, and parity with $16$-bit LoRA for the larger LLaMA models. The $33$B/$65$B headline should therefore be read as an impressive scaling result, but not as a direct experimental proof of equivalence to full $16$-bit finetuning at those sizes.

So the cautious summary is that the paper supports “\textsc{QLoRA} does not measurably hurt performance in the settings we could test, and it scales to $33$B/$65$B,” but it does not literally prove “\textsc{QLoRA} matches full $16$-bit finetuning at $33$B and $65$B.”

### What exactly are the 'memory spikes' that paged optimizers are supposed to prevent?
Question:
The paper introduces paged optimizers using NVIDIA unified memory and says they help avoid out-of-memory errors caused by gradient checkpointing memory spikes, especially for long sequences.

I'm having trouble visualizing the failure mode. During which part of training does memory suddenly spike if the base model is frozen and quantized? Is it during recomputation in checkpointing, during the optimizer step when moments are materialized, or something else? A concrete explanation of what is being paged to CPU and when would help.
Answer:
In the paper, the “memory spike” is not the frozen 4-bit base model suddenly growing, and it is not primarily the optimizer step “materializing” the moments. The spike happens in the forward/backward phase for a minibatch whose sequence length is unusually long, especially when gradient checkpointing is used.

The key point is that checkpointing saves memory by not storing all intermediate activations from the forward pass, but then those activations have to be recomputed during backpropagation. That recomputation creates a transient peak in memory usage: for a long sequence, the temporary activations, gradients, and recomputation buffers can briefly exceed the GPU’s free memory even though the model weights themselves are frozen and quantized. So the failure mode is a short-lived peak during backprop, not a steady-state increase in model size.

Paged optimizers are there to make room for that peak. In QLoRA, the thing that gets paged is the optimizer state for the trainable LoRA parameters, i.e. the state used by the optimizer such as its moment buffers. Those states are allocated with NVIDIA unified memory, so if a long-sequence minibatch pushes the GPU near OOM, some optimizer-state pages can be evicted to CPU RAM. Later, when the backward pass is done and the optimizer update is actually performed, those pages are brought back to GPU memory. The paper is quite explicit about this: the states are “automatically evicted to CPU RAM when the GPU runs out-of-memory and paged back into GPU memory when the memory is needed in the optimizer update step.”

So the intuitive picture is: the base model stays small and frozen; checkpointing makes training feasible but introduces occasional peaks; paged optimizers do not remove those peaks, they just ensure that some non-immediately-needed memory, namely optimizer state, can spill to CPU so the peak fits on the GPU. That is why the paper says paging is mainly needed for mini-batches with long sequence lengths, and why it is mostly invisible during ordinary batches.

### How should I interpret the claim that Guanaco reaches 99.3% of ChatGPT if the paper also reports wide confidence intervals and GPT-4 bias?
Question:
On the one hand, the paper emphasizes that Guanaco reaches 99.3% of ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark. On the other hand, it also says the confidence intervals are wide, GPT-4 shows ordering bias, and sample-level agreement between GPT-4 and human judges is only moderate.

Should that 99.3% number be treated as a strong conclusion, or more like a noisy point estimate under a fragile evaluation protocol? I'm not asking whether the model is good, but how seriously to take that specific headline number.
Answer:
You should treat the “$99.3\%$ of ChatGPT” figure as a noisy point estimate, not as a strong, precise conclusion.

In the paper, that number comes from GPT-4 scoring Guanaco and ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark, which is only $80$ prompts and uses an absolute $0$--$10$ scoring scheme that the authors themselves say is hard to ground consistently. They explicitly note that the confidence intervals are wide and that many models overlap, so the difference between, say, $97.8\%$ and $99.3\%$ is not something the evaluation can resolve very sharply. In other words, the headline number says “Guanaco was judged about as good as ChatGPT under this protocol,” not “Guanaco is objectively $0.7\%$ worse.”

The authors also point out that the protocol is fragile in two important ways. First, GPT-4 shows an ordering bias: it tends to score the response that appears earlier in the prompt more highly, which is why they recommend averaging over both orderings. Second, GPT-4 and human judgments only moderately agree at the system level, and the sample-level agreement is weak, with Fleiss’ $\kappa = 0.25$. That means the exact absolute score depends noticeably on the judge and on presentation details. The paper is quite candid about this and even says that current chatbot benchmarks are “not trustworthy” for accurately measuring chatbot performance.

So the right interpretation is: the paper provides suggestive evidence that Guanaco-65B is competitive with ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark, but the precise “$99.3\%$” should not be over-read. It is a benchmark-specific estimate under a subjective and somewhat unstable evaluation protocol, not a robust universal statement about model quality. If you want the more defensible takeaway from the paper, it is the qualitative one: Guanaco is among the strongest open models and is in the ChatGPT range on this benchmark, but the exact margin is uncertain.

### Why do FLAN-tuned models do well on MMLU but poorly on chatbot benchmarks in this paper?
Question:
The paper argues that dataset suitability matters more than dataset size, and gives the example that FLAN v2 finetuning does well on MMLU but poorly on Vicuna-style chatbot evaluation, while OASST1 behaves more like the opposite.

I'm confused because both are forms of instruction tuning. What properties of a dataset make it transfer to multiple-choice academic benchmarks like MMLU versus open-ended dialogue benchmarks like Vicuna or OA? Is the difference mainly prompt style, target length, domain coverage, or something about conversation structure?
Answer:
The paper’s point is not that “instruction tuning” is too vague to be useful, but that different instruction datasets encode very different behaviors. In their experiments, the model learns whatever *kind* of instruction-following is most represented in the finetuning data, and that transfers best when the evaluation benchmark asks for the same thing.

FLAN v2 is closer in spirit to MMLU because both are essentially “solve a task from a prompt and produce the right answer” settings. MMLU is a closed-form, multiple-choice, academic benchmark with short answers and a strong emphasis on factual recall and reasoning. FLAN-style data is also largely task-oriented: many examples are single-turn instruction/input-output pairs where the right response is concise, direct, and often resembles an answer to a school- or benchmark-like question. So a model tuned on FLAN learns to be good at exactly that kind of prompt-to-answer mapping, which is why it does well on MMLU.

Vicuna-style and OA benchmarks are different in a way that matters a lot. They are open-ended chatbot evaluations: the model must be helpful, natural, conversational, and often multi-turn-aware. The OpenAssistant dataset (OASST1) is much closer to that regime because it is a multilingual collection of crowd-sourced multi-turn dialogs, and in the paper they finetune on the full selected conversation, including the instructions. That means the model sees dialogue structure, turn-taking, and assistant-like responses, which are exactly the behaviors the Vicuna/OA benchmarks reward. FLAN v2, by contrast, is much less conversational.

So the main difference in this paper is not just prompt style or answer length in isolation, but the whole *interaction structure*: whether the training data teaches “answer this task” or “conduct a helpful dialogue.” Domain coverage also matters, but the authors’ own interpretation is that benchmark similarity dominates dataset size. They explicitly conclude that FLAN v2 is similar to MMLU but dissimilar to chatbot benchmarks, while OASST1 is the opposite kind of match.

A good way to summarize the result is: MMLU rewards academic task-solving skill, while Vicuna/OA reward chat behavior. Both are “instruction tuning,” but they supervise different downstream behaviors. The paper’s empirical lesson is that a small, high-quality dataset that matches the target evaluation can outperform a much larger dataset that teaches the wrong kind of instruction following.
