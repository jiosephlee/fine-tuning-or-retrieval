# Stop Reinforcement Learning: Your Language Model is Already a Reward Model

**By [Your Name/AI Assistant]**

Imagine you are training a chef to cook only the most delicious meals by tasting a specific dish and telling you if it's good or bad. The classic approach to fixing a chef's cooking is to hire a human advisor (the "reward model") to judge every bite, train the advisor, and then constantly feedback on the chef's performance based on those judgments to tweak their technique. This is essentially how traditional Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has been used for language models for over a decade. It involves a complex, unstable dance where a separate reward model is trained first, followed by an iterative process where the large language model samples thousands of variations and gets nudged toward high-scoring answers while trying not to forget what it already knows. It's computationally expensive, prone to drifting, and notoriously difficult to tune.

The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" argues that this dedicated advisor phase is entirely unnecessary. The authors present Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that proves we can achieve the exact same goals as RLHF without ever explicitly training a separate reward model or running samples through a reinforcement learning loop. Instead, DPO flips the script by using the language model itself as the source of truth for what constitutes a desirable response, optimizing it directly against human preferences using nothing but a simple binary cross-entropy loss.

### The Flaw in the Traditional Pipeline

To understand why DPO matters, we must first look at why current methods struggle. The standard RLHF pipeline operates in distinct phases. First, an unsupervised Large Language Model (LM) is fine-tuned using Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on high-quality data. Then, in what is often called the "Reward Modelling Phase," this SFT model generates pairs of responses for various prompts—let's call them $y_1$ and $y_2$. Humans are asked to choose which response they prefer, establishing pairwise comparisons like $y_w \succ y_l$.

Ideally, these preferences are generated by some hidden "true" reward function $r^*(x, y)$, which we cannot see directly. To approximate it, researchers train a neural network parameterized as $\phi$, denoted $r_{\phi}(x, y)$, using Maximum Likelihood Estimation based on the Bradley-Terry preference model. The Bradley-Terry model assumes that the probability of preferring choice A over B depends exponentially on the difference in their true rewards:
$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\sigma(r^*(x, y_1)-r^*(x, y_2))$$
where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. Once this reward model is fitted, the final stage involves Reinforcement Learning to fine-tune the language model policy $\pi$ to maximize this estimated reward while staying close to its original behavior via a KL-divergence constraint:
$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]$$
Here, $\beta$ controls how much deviation from the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ is allowed. Because this objective requires sampling from $\pi_{\theta}$ during training and often relies on Monte Carlo estimators for gradients due to discrete generation spaces, it leads to high variance and instability unless carefully constrained with techniques like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

### Why Is DPO Better? A Change of Variables

The core insight of DPO is mathematical elegance born from linear algebra and probability theory rather than engineering hacks. The paper derives that any optimal policy solving the above RLHF problem has a specific exponential form related to its corresponding reward function:
$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$
where $Z(x)$ is a normalization constant known as the partition function. The problem with this equation in practice is estimating $Z(x)$ accurately for large language models; doing so requires summation over all possible tokens in an input sequence at every step during training, which is computationally prohibitive. Instead of estimating rewards and then trying to solve for probabilities with difficult normalization constants, DPO reverses the logic. They rearrange the relationship to express the reward function directly in terms of probabilities:
$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + C$$
where $C$ absorbs terms constant across different continuations for a given prompt. Since human preferences only care about relative differences in rewards (and not absolute values), these constants vanish when comparing two outputs $y_w$ (preferred) and $y_l$ (dispreferred). This leads to Equation 7 in the paper:
$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp(\dots)}$$
Substituting this back into Bradley-Terry yields an objective where we no longer need an external reward model or partition functions; we simply fit the probability ratios found inside our language model parameters against human preferences using standard binary cross-entropy loss:
$$-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{w}\mid x}{C} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{l}\mid x}{C}\right)\right]$$
In simpler terms: "Maximize the likelihood that our model assigns higher probability weights to preferred answers compared to dispreferred ones." Because standard binary cross-entropy loss does not require sampling multiple times per prompt per step (unlike PPO), it trains significantly faster and more stably without needing complex gumbel-max tricks or custom sampling loops at test time unless one specifically wants diversity beyond greedy choices supervised by best-of-N sampling at inference time anyway!
