\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why are DPO policies theoretically guaranteed to generalize better than PPO if they optimize identical objectives over different datasets?
Question:
The experiments section mentions that DPO performs well because it optimizes RLHF objectives but using only preference pairs, while PPO relies on ground-truth rewards and generates thousands of samples per forward pass.

I'm confused about why simply using fewer samples would theoretically improve generalization or reduce variance. While using fewer samples might seem intuitively faster, doesn't sampling multiple trajectories increase our variance estimates so that we can find better gradients? Can you explain why reducing our sample count actually leads to more robust policies or better utilities compared to increasing sample counts via generation?
Answer:
The theoretical advantage of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) over Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) lies not in the number of samples, but in the nature of the loss function and how variance is handled during the gradient update. While PPO simulates a high-variance reinforcement learning environment by generating thousands of responses to estimate advantages, DPO utilizes a Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) objective derived from a change of variables that inherently constrains the gradient magnitude.

Both DPO and PPO can be formulated as maximizing an identical RLHF objective involving reward maximization and KL-divergence regularization from a reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. However, they optimize this objective via different mathematical routes. PPO relies on an actor-critic framework where the policy uses an off-policy estimator to approximate $\nabla \mathbb{E}[r(y)]$. Because DPO is derived solely from preference pairs sampled explicitly from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, it would technically yield zero gradient if we strictly followed the "same forward pass" logic without generating new samples. Instead, to learn a new policy $\pi_\theta$ for unseen future data $y$, DPO must estimate the population expectation using the finite set of preference pairs in the dataset $\mathcal{D}$. This estimates the gradient as their sample mean:

$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\frac{\beta}{N}\sum_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \sigma(\hat{r}(x,y_l)-\hat{r}(x,y_w)) (\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w|x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l|x)) \right]$.

This formulation contains two critical stabilizing mechanisms absent in standard PPO. First, it operates on low-variance binary classification signals (preference labels $y_w$ vs. $y_l$) rather than continuous high-variance return signals. Second, and most significantly, DPO includes an implicit importance weight: $\sigma(\hat{r}(x,y_l)-\hat{r}(x,y_w))$. This term scales the gradient by how confidently and correctly the implicit reward model orders the preferred completion over the dispreferred one. As shown in Section 4, this ensures that "highly incorrect" weights for dispreferred completions are downplayed, effectively preventing mode collapse without needing a separate value network or a complex advantage function estimation like those required by PPO. Consequently, by bypassing the unstable Monte Carlo sampling of self-generated trajectories and replacing it with a deterministic importance-weighted MLE loss over stable preference pairs, DPO achieves a smoother optimization landscape that generalizes robustly to test-time inputs like those seen in PPO-GT without suffering from overfitting or degenerate distributions associated with large-scale sampling.
