\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### 'Reward over-optimization' vs insufficiency: How does bias propagate when training only with preferred responses?
Question:
"Limitations & Future Work" section mentions:

> Additionally, while we evaluate models up to 6B parameters... whether reward over-optimization manifests in direct preference optimization setting... is clear decrease in perfomance an instance it?
What do you mean exactly by "reward over-optimization"?

In standard RLHF with PPO, over-optimization usually happens as you explode rewards without penalizing low probablities too harshly, leading you to discover many sequences which those reward labels give non-zero value. But here you did not train with reward for negative reinforcement at all; rather you forced negative outcomes so that positive ones get higher likelihoods.

I suspect there's some subtle connection between these — surely."Wantless patterns
Answer:
In the "Limitations & Future Work" section of the paper, the authors refer to "reward over-optimization" in the context of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to describe a scenario where the model's internal estimate of reward, $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$, becomes excessively high and unbounded as the model attempts to maximize the likelihood ratio.

This phenomenon is analogous to over-optimization in standard Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) because both regimes suffer from a failure in the gradient signal's "braking" mechanism. In traditional RLHF using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), over-optimization typically arises because the advantage calculation (the difference between expected reward and baseline) grows large when rewards are high, potentially causing unstable updates or exploring sub-optimal sequences that yield non-zero rewards. In DPO, which relies on a simple binary cross-entropy loss without an explicit reward baseline, this manifests differently but structurally similarly: as the model aggressively increases the probability of preferred tokens relative to the reference distribution, the log-probabilities grow large in magnitude. The paper notes in Section 3 ("What does the DPO update do?") that examples are weighted by incorrectness, implying that when implicit rewards are very high (and thus gradients are strong), the model may push responses too far toward a degenerate distribution—essentially optimizing for high probability ratios at the expense of semantic coherence or satiating all possible preferences. The "slight decrease in performance" mentioned for 6B parameters likely stems from this degradation: because DPO lacks an explicit normalization term (the partition function $Z(x)$ present in standard PPO formulations) to automatically scale rewards and prevent them from exploding, it becomes more sensitive to training dynamics where extreme probability shifts lead to performance drops without catastrophic complete collapse.
