\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why is Chef's initialization method for $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ described as equivalent to maximum likelihood estimation but different from standard supervised fine-tuning?
Question:
In Section 4 ('DPO outline'), the paper notes that when we do not have an SFT model available, we initialize our reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by fitting it directly on preferred responses using Maximum Likelihood Estimation:

$$ \pi_{\text{ref}} = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}[\log \pi(y_w \\mid x)] $$

My question centers: why shouldn't we just initialize $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ with this same MLE-trained model and proceed with DPO? Why does this specific setup require us to run Supervised Fine-Tuning first anyway? Is there some inherent bias or inconsistency with leaving two version of models separate before optimizations?
Answer:
The paper explicitly outlines this scenario in Section \ref{sec:DPO} under the subheading "DPO outline." The standard Direct Preference Optimization pipeline assumes the availability of a Supervised Fine-Tuned (SFT) model, denoted as $\pi^\text{SFT}$, generated from high-quality human demonstrations for the specific downstream task. This model serves as the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ in Equation 5 ($\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$). However, the authors recognize a practical hurdle: many real-world preference datasets are paired with models trained on generic-purpose data rather than specific task data.

When $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is unavailable, one might seemingly satisfy the initialization requirement for $\pi_\text{ref}$ by training a model directly on the preferred responses $(x, y_w)$ from the preference dataset using Standard Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). This approach effectively trains an SFT model composed solely of winning examples. However, relying solely on MLE for $\pi_\text{ref}$ creates an inconsistency with the DPO objective because MLE maximizes $\mathbf{P(y_w)}$ without any constraint to minimize $\mathbf{P(y_l)}$ or ensure alignment with the goal of synthesizing high-probability completions. If $\mathbf{P(y_l)}$ remains high while $\mathbf{P(y_w)}$ is also high during training, the resulting distribution captures only partial information about human preferences (specifically that $y_w$ is good) but fails to encode information about why $y_l$ is bad.

This leads to a critical issue regarding distribution shift. The DPO objective relies on minimizing the KL divergence between the trained policy and $\pi_\text{ref}$. If $\pi_\text{ref}$ was trained only on preferred responses but lacks guidance on dispreferred ones, it may assign non-negligible probability to responses that humans actually dislike (because they were never penalized in an unconstrained MLE objective). When DPO attempts to optimize $\pi_\theta$ to maximize preferences relative to this flawed $\pi_\text{ref}$, it struggles to distinguish between responses that are merely plausible under an unconstrained model and those that are genuinely useful. To resolve this bias introduced by missing structural information about dispreferred responses and to ensure the reference distribution covers the full response space relevant for minimization via KL terms, the paper mandates an explicit SFT step even when a dedicated task SFT model is not pre-existing. By fine-tuning on both preferred ($y_w$) and dispreferred ($y_l$) completions during this phase, one ensures that $\pi_\text{ref}$ represents a balanced distribution over all likely completions generated by humans for prompt $x$, which is essential for stable and effective preference learning.
