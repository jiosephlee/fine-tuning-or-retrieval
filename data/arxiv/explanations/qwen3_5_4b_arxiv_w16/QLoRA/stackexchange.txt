Answer:
The mechanism allowing gradients to flow back through frozen, quantized weights (specifically the $W^{NF4}$ matrix stored in Equation 5 of the paper) without updating them relies on the principles of the **Chain Rule** in calculus, applied to a process called **dequantization**.

Although the quantized weights $\mathbf{W}^{NF4}$ themselves are frozen and do not change during training, they act as a fixed, deterministic function during the backward pass. When computing gradients for the LoRA adapters ($\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{L}_1}$), the gradient must pass through the frozen base model layers. This process involves several distinct steps:

First, during the forward pass, the input activations $\mathbf{X}$ are multiplied by the quantized weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^{NF4}$. Since weights cannot be multiplied directly in 4-bit NormalFloat format due to hardware and precision limitations, a custom decoder operation, denoted as $\text{doubleDequant}(\cdot)$ in Equation 5, is applied to convert $\mathbf{W}^{NF4}$ into a high-precision floating-point representation ($\mathbf{W}^{BF16}$). This operation effectively rescales and reconstructs the original 16-bit values required for accurate computation:
$$ \text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}) = \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}, $$
Here, $c_1$ and $c_2$ represent the second-level quantization constants that were themselves quantized (the "Double Quantization" technique). Because $c_1$ and $c_2$ are fixed constants derived from the pretrained model statistics before training begins (or updated only via optimizer учебные страницы if implemented dynamically, but mathematically treated as constants relative to the gradient flow through weights), their gradients with respect to $\mathcal{L}$ are zero.

Second, according to the Chain Rule for matrix multiplication where $\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}$, the gradient flowing into $\mathbf{X}$ is given by $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{X}} = (\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Y}})^T \cdot \frac{\partial \mathbf{Y}}{\partial \mathbf{X}} = (\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Y}})^T \cdot \frac{\partial (\text{dequant}(\dots))}{\partial (\dots)}$. In QLoRA's specific implementation using odd quantization blocks (referenced in Equation 5 of your query involving $\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_1$), the notation likely refers to a decomposition of this derivative. The paper utilizes a formulation where dequantization subtracts an odd block's contribution to cancel out rounding errors. Mathematically, this looks like:
$$ \frac{\partial L_1}{\rho} = \dots = (\bar{-\frac{-\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\tau_k})}{r_i\rho}. $$
The logic posits that while one approximation contributes roundoff error equal to $E_k$, there must exist an alternative approximation or reconstruction path contributing an equal but opposite error (represented by terms involving odd/even block differences or complementary decompositions). These terms effectively reconstruct the high-precision weight function.

Crucially, because operations on fixed numbers (the dequantization constants) are deterministic and invertible (up to machine precision), PyTorch's autograd engine can compute non-zero partial derivatives with respect to these intermediate reconstructed values. These intermediate values mathematically represent gradients passing through a function defined by stashed low-precision data. Consequently, even though `W.norm` receives no weight updates (`loss.backward()` does not touch `W`), it computes non-zero gradients that bypasses backpropagates "through" it. These gradients are tangent vectors passing through snapshots of unfrozen model parameters taken at initialization. If one had trained on personas instead of fixed weights these tangent vectors might change but since they are computed for static positions they collapse into zero magnitude against paged optimizer updates rather than model parameters thus restoring learning efficiency without perfloss

### How does Double Quantization specifically reduce memory footprint compared to storing 4-bit constants, and is there a theoretical upper bound on this savings?
Question:
The paper states that Double Quantization quantizes the quantization constants ($c_2$), reducing memory from 0.5 bits per parameter to approximately 0.127 bits per parameter. However, I am confused about how they claim this saves approximately 3 GB for a 65B model if the calculation $(32/64) - (8/64 + ...) = 0.373$. If I naively calculate $65 * 10^9 * 0.373$, that equals roughly 24 GB, not 3 GB. Am I misunderstanding the bit-per-parameter units or calculating it incorrectly?
Answer:
The confusion stems from a misinterpretation of the "per parameter" savings figure provided in the paper versus the total raw bit count required to store the quantization constants outright. The paper states that Double Quantization reduces the memory footprint by "about $0.37$ bits per parameter" (specifically from $0.5$ bits to $0.127$ bits). This reduction applies only to the storage of the **quantization constants** ($c$), not to the model weights themselves.

To clarify this mathematically: In standard block-wise quantization, a block size of $B=64$ requires $32$ bits per block if stored in 32-bit floats. Since a block contains $B=64$ parameters, this averages to $\frac{32}{64} = 0.5$ bits per parameter just for these constants. By applying Double Quantization, where these $32$-bit constants are reduced to 8-bit floats while maintaining a block size of $256$, the first level of costs becomes $\frac{8}{64}$ and the second level becomes $\frac{32}{64 \times 256}$. The total cost drops to approximately $0.127$ bits per parameter for these meta-steps. The difference, $0.5 - 0.127 = 0.373$ bits/parameter, is strictly the space saved on holding the scaling factors, not space saved on the weights or gradients.

You do not save energy on all $65 \times 10^9$ parameters in terms of constant storage; mathematically speaking, you only save this "constant overhead" on every group of parameters that involves block-wise quantization (which applies nearly everywhere in the weight matrix). The theoretical upper bound on this savings is bounded by minimizing the precision of constants used during dequantization while maintaining statistical stability against outliers; however, it approaches zero relative to theoretical limits if one used single precision for everything anyway (or considered scale-free models), but practically it sits between $0$ and roughly half a bit per parameter depending on hyperparameters like block size and integer vs float choice.

The reason your calculation yields ~24 GB instead of ~3 GB is because your formula assumes you are quantizing **every single parameter** using those bit allocations directly. Instead, QLoRA stores these constants sparsely: they are tied in blocks and do not constitute storage for each individual weight value itself (since weights are only stored in 4 bits). When you apply the savings ratio to the entire model size without restricting it to just constant storage, you overestimate by assuming a linear bit reduction across all memory usage rather than recognizing that constant storage overhead is only roughly equivalent to one extra bit per parameter within those constant structures anyway. Therefore, applying an average bit-saving factor of roughly $0.37$ bits/parameter to a model effectively "measured" in logic where weights take up most bits tells us we only saved an amount proportional to those constant blocks (which represent about one unit of extra memory overhead for every few parameters). Multiplying just a conservative fraction (like treating some percentage of model as covered by QLoRA overhead) gives us the approximate saving of around $3$ GB for a massive model like LLaMA-65B when accounting for realistic headroom and constant management efficiency described as ~$0.127$ remaining overhead instead of full binary representation overheads elsewhere.

### Why does GRU/LSTM not apply to standard LoRA implementation, and what makes active LoRA compatible with standard GRU layers
Question:
I'm trying to understand why active LoRA works with any linear layer but might not be universally applicable to regions of neural networks like attention heads.
Answer:
The difficulty in applying standard LoRA implementations—specifically the default "active" LoRA approach—to recurrent layers like GRU or LSTM stems from the distinct architectural and memory constraints of sequence modeling in these models, as well as how gradient flow is handled during backpropagation. In the paper **QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs**, the authors explicitly address this limitation by introducing a variant they term **Active LoRA** to accommodate these specific layer types.

Standard active LoRA applies rank decomposition to every linear projection matrix ($\mathbf{W}$) within the network, including those inside attention heads. However, applying this same strategy to recurrent units like GRUs and LSTMs is problematic because these architectures do not decompose their transformations solely into independent linear blocks in the same manner; instead, they involve coupled gate mechanisms (update, reset, and candidate state) that depend heavily on internal hidden states ($h_t$). If one were to naively apply a fixed-rank adapter to these components without modification, the interaction between the adapter parameters and the past dependencies could destabilize training or fail to capture essential temporal dynamics.

QLoRA handles this by modifying the initialization of LoRA weights for GRU and LSTM layers specifically. The paper notes that while standard LoRA adds learnable rank factors $A$ and $B$ to standard linear projections, for recurrent layers, it initializes these adapters differently to respect the gate structure. The goal is to allow the adapter parameters ($\mathbf{L} \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times r}$) and ($\mathbf{L}' \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times o}$) enough freedom to adjust without introducing numerical instability typical in recurrent circuits when mixing quantized base weights with unmodified adapters. Specifically, for attention heads within LLMs (which are linear projections), QLoRA simply adds the low-rank adaptation $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$ to $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}$. For GRU/LSTM segments capable of handling sequence data, QLoRA utilizes this specialized initialization because standard projection-based LoRA does not inherently support the gate-based updates required for effective sequence modeling without breaking decoder-only LLM performance.

Furthermore, even with this specialized initialization, there is a fundamental difference in how gradients are computed in these loops compared to feed-forward blocks. In RNNs/Transformers with attention, gradients propagate through the computation graph maintaining history, whereas in pure back-propagation-through-time within RNNs (BPTT), gradients are scaled by products of Jacobians at each step. Applying standard active LoRA factors directly into these multiplicative gradient paths can lead to vanishingly small or exploding gradients if not carefully tuned. QLoRA's "Active" solution rectifies this by ensuring that for any layer—including those dealing with sequences—the adapter parameters are integrated such that they do not disrupt the quantization stability required for 4-bit training (the core contribution of QLoRA). This involves ensuring that during dequantization and gradient accumulation via equation (5) in their formulation ($\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$), the interaction between $\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$ and the added adaptive terms remains stable across sequential time steps rather than just static linear layers. Thus, Active LoRA works with GRU/LSTM only because QLoRA imposes a tailored structural constraint on how adapters are injected and initialized within those specific units, distinguishing them from standard linear projections found in attention mechanisms or feed-forward networks where direct application is usually sufficient.

Your question appears to stem from conflating two distinct themes: the technical innovations in *\textit{QLoRA}*\ and recent developments in tokenization.

However, I can provide you with a correct understanding of what *\textit{QLoRA}*\ actually addresses, along with an explanation of where your confusion might originate (likely regarding other literature), while grounding my answer in the provided text.

The primary contribution of *\textit{QLoRA}*\ is demonstrating that large language models can be finetuned using only a single consumer GPU (e.g., a 48GB GPU) by quantizing the base model to 4-bit precision while training only Low-Rank Adapter parameters. The paper details three specific innovations to achieve this memory savings:
1.  **\textrm{4-bit NormalFloat (NF4)}:** A quantization data type that is information-theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights, allowing for more efficient storage compared to standard integers or floats.
2.  **Double Quantization:** A technique that further reduces memory by quantizing the quantization constants themselves, saving approximately 0.37 bits per parameter on average for block sizes of 64.
3.  **Paged Optimizers:** A mechanism leveraging NVIDIA unified memory to automatically move optimizer states between CPU and GPU memory when space runs out, preventing out-of-memory errors during gradient checkpointing on long sequences or large batches.

Regarding **"handling long texts,"** the paper explicitly limits its main contributions to training on "long" sequences through Paged Optimizers and Gradient Checkpointing (though it notes that hard measurements on paging for long sequences were rare in their experiments). However, this has nothing to do with REFRESH tokens; it relies heavily on mathematical techniques to reduce memory usage ($O(\mathbf{B} \times H)$ vs $O(\mathbf{B} \times H)$) rather than architectural changes to tokenizing ends-of-sequence boundaries.

If you are asking about why REFRESH tokens exist in other contexts (like PaLM generation), that usually stems from addressing issues where models struggle with extremely long context windows (trillions of tokens) and simply never generating an explicit End-of-Sequence token even though they should, causing inefficient sampling. However, since *\textit{QLoRA}*\ focuses entirely on **finetuning stability and memory efficiency**, not on the generation behavior of specific tokenizers or the definition of generation terminators like REFRESH or EOS, your premise regarding that paper contains facts inconsistent with its content.

To accurately answer based strictly on *\textit{QLoRA}*\: The paper considers standard EOS-like handling implicitly by training with cross-entropy loss and does not propose replacing EOS with a space character ("REFRESH") as an innovation; it proposes innovating only in weight representation and optimization management. If you are interested in the REFRESH/EOS issue in PaLM specifically, you would need to consult papers discussing Google's EMU tokenization rather than *\textit{QLoRA}*.
