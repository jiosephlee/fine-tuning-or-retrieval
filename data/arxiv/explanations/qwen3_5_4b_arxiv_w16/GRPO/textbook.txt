## 1. Introduction to LLMs and Their Impact on AI Research

The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has fundamentally shifted the landscape of artificial intelligence, moving the field from a strictly symbolic paradigm toward a statistical, neural approach for handling complex reasoning tasks. In recent years, models have transitioned from simple text completion tools to sophisticated agents capable of generating code, consuming texts, and performing multi-step logical deduction without explicit programming guidance. This shift was accelerated by the scaling of pre-training data derived from the Common Crawl internet corpus, which exposed models to unprecedented volumes of natural language, scientific literature, and code repositories.

A critical driver of this revolution has been the LLM's ability to approximate complex cognitive processes through probabilistic prediction. Rather than relying on hard-coded rules or explicit logic gates, these models learn to predict the next token in a sequence based on vast patterns of context. This has proven particularly potent in domains requiring step-by-step deduction, such as mathematical problem solving. By structuring problems as sequences of reasoning steps—often referred to as Chain-of-Thought (CoT)—models can be prompted to generate intermediate reasoning traces that align with their final answers. This technique has unlocked significant performance gains on benchmarks previously considered too difficult for neural networks alone.

The introduction of instruction-tuning protocols further refined these capabilities, allowing models to adopt specific personas and task formats. Unlike pre-trained models that merely responded to input sequences, instructed models aim to maximize human utility by following directives such as explaining a concept or deriving a proof. This dual stage of pre-training followed by supervised fine-tuning (SFT) has created a class of models that can handle domain-specific jargon and logical frameworks with remarkable fluency. However, despite these advancements, the internal mechanisms ensuring robust performance across diverse and structured domains remain largely opaque, prompting the search for methods that can systematically expand upon these foundational capabilities.

Notably, Open-source variants like CodeLlama have emerged as powerful tools for researchers and developers who require transparency and customization over proprietary alternatives. These models have demonstrated that high-level reasoning is not exclusive to massive closed-parameter systems but can be cultivated through accessible architectures when trained on sufficiently rich data representations. The trajectory suggests that the next frontier in LLM development lies not just in increasing model size or general corpus breadth, but in precisely curating data distributions tailored for high-structured tasks like mathematics and formal theorem proving.

# 2.1 Designing a fastText-based Classifier for Automated Data Selection

To address the challenge of scaling High-Quality Mathematical Pre-Training, the authors identify a critical bottleneck in utilizing public web data: the sheer volume of noise and the lack of clear signal in raw Common Crawl data. Mathematical reasoning tasks require structured, coherent, and semantically rich content, which is sparsely distributed within the unstructured text of the entire web. Consequently, simply retraining a model on 100% of Common Crawl is inefficient and detrimental to performance. To circumvent this, the research introduces a data-centric strategy centered on a meticulously engineered classifier designed to recycle and select high-quality mathematical web pages automatically. The core insight driving this approach is that publicly available web data contains valuable information for mathematical purposes if it can be effectively filtered from general web noise.

The selection pipeline relies on a fastText model\footnote{\url{https://fasttext.cc}}, which is particularly adept at handling morphological variations of words and assembles all words in an input text into a small set of vectors using n-grams, word characters, subwords (suffixes), and punctuation marks (Joulin et al., 2016). The design philosophy behind this specific classifier is to separate "mathematically relevant" text from "irrelevant" text based on lexical and structural patterns found in mathematical domains. The goal was not merely to find *some* math pages, but to build a system capable of iteratively improving its precision by exposing itself to diverse examples and refining its decision boundaries over time.

The initialization of this fastText classifier requires two components: a positive seed corpus and negative examples through which the model learns what *not* to pick. The authors select OpenWebMath\footnote{\url{https://github.com/DeepSpark/MathWebData}} as their initial positive seed corpus because it represents a high-quality collection of mathematical web texts already curated for similar purposes. Mathematically speaking, we treat the OpenWebMath URLs as ground truth "positive samples." To train an effective binary classifier that distinguishes between positive and negative instances, we generate an equivalent number of negative examples from Common Crawl but exclude them from our final training dataset; rather, they serve purely as learning anchors during the initial training phase. The balance between these sets—specifically randomly selecting 500,000 data points (e.g., URLs) from the seed corpus as positive training examples and another 500,000 unrelated web pages from Common Crawl as negative training examples—is crucial for stabilizing gradient updates during the initial weight configuration of the model.

During this initial training epoch, we configure several hyperparameters that dictate the sensitivity of the model to rare terms versus common ones. We configure the vector dimension of n-grams to 256; this depth allows for capturing subtle semantic correlations without incurring prohibitive computational costs during inference or pre-training scanning. We set the learning rate $lr = 0.1$, providing ample force for rapid convergence given that this is an exploratory phase where we do not yet expect peak accuracy but rather rapid startup performance toward relevant domains. Crucially, we restrict our analysis window by setting `min_ngram = 3`, choosing only words longer than three characters ($W_3$). # 3. Building DeepSeekMath-Base: Scalable Pre-Training Strategies

The construction of DeepSeekMath-Base represents a pivotal shift from indiscriminate data ingestion to a highly targeted, curriculum-based approach driven by the hypothesis that specific domain expertise is necessary to unlock sophisticated reasoning capabilities in language models. By initializing from a specialized code foundation and engaging in a rigorous pre-training regime focused on high-quality mathematical tokens, the model achieves performance levels that rival significantly larger closed-source competitors. This chapter dissects the architectural decisions and empirical findings that underpin this success, specifically analyzing the critical role of initialization, the counter-intuitive effectiveness of code-centered training, and the surprising ineffectiveness of traditional academic corpora like arXiv papers in this specific domain.

## Initialization from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 vs. General LLMs

The decision to initialize DeepSeekMath-Base on an existing mathematical reasoning benchmark, specifically the "competition-level" MATH dataset \citep{MATH}, without relying on external toolkits or voting techniques \citep{deepseek-coder}, sets a new standard for open-source model capabilities. The foundation of this base model rests upon DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, a specialized large language model designed for code generation. While general large language models often perform well on linguistic tasks and can be fine-tuned for mathematics through standard instruction tuning alone, our experiments strongly suggest that starting from a code-centric base provides unique advantages in mathematical reasoning tasks \citep{deepseek-coder}.

The intuition behind this choice lies in the structural nature of many mathematical solutions, which are often computationally intensive and require procedural logic typical of programming languages rather than natural language descriptions alone. By initializing a model trained specifically on code, we equip it with an intrinsic understanding of symbolic manipulation and algorithmic thinking before it even encounters the raw mathematical tokens. When compared to general LLMs like Mistral 7B \citep{mistral} or other open-source base models that started from general knowledge bases, DeepSeekMath-Base achieves outperformance that often exceeds 10 percentage points on rigorous benchmarks like GSM8K \citep{gsm8k} and MATH \citep{MATH}. This performance gap persists even against models significantly larger than 7 billion parameters, such as Minerva 540B \citep{minerva}, which utilizes massive parameter counts but may lack the specific quality of its training corpus relative to our targeted approach \citep{minerva}.

Furthermore, we observe that while initializing with a code-trained model offers substantial benefits for mathematics training directly improving its ability to solve problems both with and without tool use \citep{deepseek-coder}, it does not universally degrade all capabilities. The data distribution of the pre-training corpus used to create DeepSeekMath-Base is carefully balanced: 56\% comes from the high-quality DeepSeekMath Corpus derived from Common Crawl (CC), while 20\% consists of GitHub code tokens, 10\% from natural language content (intentionally mixed with Chinese math data), 4\% from AlgebraicStack (code), and only 10\% from arXiv papers \citep{deepseek-coder}. This deliberate inclusion ensures that while code training is emphasized, general language understanding is preserved alongside reasoning skills. The results confirm that math-trained models improve their capabilities beyond mathematics on other benchmarks such as MMLU \citep{mmlu} and BIG-Bench Hard (BBH) \citep{bbh}, demonstrating an amplification of general reasoning capabilities rather than a narrow specialization limited solely to arithmetic or algebraic manipulation \citep{deepseek-coder}.

# 4. From Base to Instruct: Supervised Fine-Tuning and Instruction Patterns

The transition from a raw base model, such as DeepSeekMath-Base, to an instruction-following agent capable of high-level reasoning marks a critical phase in large language model development. While pre-training instills foundational world knowledge and statistical patterns within the language corpus, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) serves to orient the model toward specific tasks, specifically the generation of structured solutions. In the context of mathematical reasoning, this stage is pivotal for converting the latent capability of the model into explicit, verifiable outputs. The DeepSeekMath pipeline involves constructing a diverse, high-quality dataset labeled with specific reasoning formats—Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Program-of-Thought (PoT), and Tool-Integrated Reasoning—and training the model on these pairs. This section details the rigorous curation of this SFT data, analyzes the distinct mechanics of the prompting styles utilized, evaluates performance across diverse benchmarks, and highlights how multilingual considerations expand the model's utility.

### Instruction Tuning Data Curation
The quality and diversity of the instruction tuning dataset are paramount for aligning a base model with complex mathematical tasks. The DeepSeekMath team curated a comprehensive mathematical instruction-tuning dataset comprising 776K training examples. This dataset was meticulously constructed to cover English and Chinese problems across varying fields of mathematics, including algebra, probability, number theory, calculus, geometry, and linear equations. A defining characteristic of this corpus is its association with three distinct reasoning formats: Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Program-of-Thought (PoT), and Tool-Integrated Reasoning (ToR). For CoT problems, human annotators developed step-by-step textual solutions that guide the model through logical deductions. Simultaneously, a subset of data from GSM8K and MATH was annotated with tool-integrated solutions by AI agents employing Python scripts or symbolic computation libraries like sympy to perform intricate calculations. Furthermore, specialized K-12 math problems from China were collected to ensure broad coverage across different educational levels and regional contexts.

The construction process involved random concatenation of training examples until reaching a maximum context length of 4K tokens during training. The inclusion of tool-integrated reasoning data was not merely supplementary but strategic; it introduced early exposure to program execution logic and library usage (such as \textit{math} and \textit{sympy}) directly into the instruction tuning loop. By pairing GSM8K and MATH adversarially with translated Chinese K-12 datasets in both CoT and ToR formats, the resulting DeepSeekMath-Instruct model demonstrated robustness across linguistic boundaries. This curation pipeline ensured that the model learned not just the final answer but the procedural syntax required to reach those answers through intermediate steps or automated code execution, effectively bridging the gap between natural language understanding and formal problem-solving capability prior to reinforcement learning stages.

### Prompt Engineering Styles: Chain-of-Thought vs. Program-of-Thought vs. Tool Integrated Reasoning
To transform numerical literacy into functional mathematical ability, three primary prompt engineering styles were operationalized during both pre-training and fine-tuning stages: Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Program-of-Thought (PoT), and Tool-Integrated Reasoning (ToR).
