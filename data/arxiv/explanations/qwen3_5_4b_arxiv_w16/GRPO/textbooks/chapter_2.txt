Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Constructing the DeepSeekMath Corpus via Scalable Data Mining

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) capable of sophisticated mathematical reasoning is fundamentally bounded by the quality and quantity of the data upon which they are trained. While previous efforts have focused on curated datasets or specific subsets of web text, DeepSeekMath introduces a novel, scalable approach to mining public web archives for high-value mathematical content. This chapter details the rigorous engineering pipeline utilized to construct the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a dataset comprising 120 billion tokens derived from Common Crawl. We will examine how an iterative feedback loop involving machine learning classifiers and domain analysis allows for the extraction of vast amounts of data while maintaining strict adherence to quality standards. Furthermore, we will discuss critical protocols for preventing benchmark leakage—a phenomenon where models memorize test questions—and validate the corpus through comprehensive pre-training experiments comparing it against established baselines like MathPile and OpenWebMath.

## The DeepSeekMath Corpus Pipeline: From Common Crawl to 120B Tokens

The construction of the DeepSeekMath Corpus represents a departure from static, manually curated datasets toward a dynamic, internet-scale mining strategy rooted in the Common Crawl archive. Unlike traditional methods that rely on expensive manual annotation or narrow scraping limits, this pipeline leverages the sheer volume of publicly available web data to bootstrap mathematical knowledge. The core objective was to extract mathematical content efficiently while managing storage costs associated with raw HTML documents. The authors began with a seed corpus consisting of high-quality mathematical web texts, specifically utilizing OpenWebMath as an initial anchor point. This seed served as a template for what constitutes valid mathematical content in terms of vocabulary structure and document formatting.

From this seed, an iterative expansion process was initiated to mine additional positive instances from Common Crawl without requiring human intervention during every step. The pipeline operates on a principle of recursive refinement: once an initial classification model identifies patterns in known good data, those patterns are applied broadly to scan millions of candidate pages, followed by targeted enrichment where necessary. This approach ensures that the final corpus grows exponentially in size—reaching 35.5 million distinct web pages totaling 120 billion tokens—while preserving high fidelity through automated scoring mechanisms. The scalability is crucial here; without such aggressive scaling, models would be forced to rely on smaller corpora that fail to capture the breadth and depth required for competitive performance on benchmarks like MATH.

A pivotal aspect of this pipeline is its ability to handle deduplication before classification begins to save computational resources and storage space. Since Common Crawl contains redundant copies of identical pages distributed across different mirrors and indices, employing URL-based deduplication reduces the input volume significantly before any heavy processing occurs. In practice, this reduction transformed the raw crawl into approximately 40 billion unique HTML pages before the first iteration even commenced. This preprocessing step is not merely administrative; it establishes a clean slate for machine learning models by ensuring that training signals come from diverse sources rather than repetitive noise inherent in raw crawls. By integrating these efficiency measures into the workflow, researchers were able to allocate resources toward training powerful classifiers rather than spending excessive cycles on data hygiene alone.

Ultimately, this pipeline serves as both a data generation engine and a quality gatekeeper simultaneously. It does not simply dump random text into a repository; instead, it actively selects content based on probabilistic indicators derived from statistical similarity to verified math sites. The success of this architecture lies in its adaptability: after each iteration collects new data points based on current model predictions, those new points can be analyzed to refine the underlying classifier itself or expand its domain coverage manually if needed.
