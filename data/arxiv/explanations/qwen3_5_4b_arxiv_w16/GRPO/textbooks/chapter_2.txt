# 2.1 Designing a fastText-based Classifier for Automated Data Selection

To address the challenge of scaling High-Quality Mathematical Pre-Training, the authors identify a critical bottleneck in utilizing public web data: the sheer volume of noise and the lack of clear signal in raw Common Crawl data. Mathematical reasoning tasks require structured, coherent, and semantically rich content, which is sparsely distributed within the unstructured text of the entire web. Consequently, simply retraining a model on 100% of Common Crawl is inefficient and detrimental to performance. To circumvent this, the research introduces a data-centric strategy centered on a meticulously engineered classifier designed to recycle and select high-quality mathematical web pages automatically. The core insight driving this approach is that publicly available web data contains valuable information for mathematical purposes if it can be effectively filtered from general web noise.

The selection pipeline relies on a fastText model\footnote{\url{https://fasttext.cc}}, which is particularly adept at handling morphological variations of words and assembles all words in an input text into a small set of vectors using n-grams, word characters, subwords (suffixes), and punctuation marks (Joulin et al., 2016). The design philosophy behind this specific classifier is to separate "mathematically relevant" text from "irrelevant" text based on lexical and structural patterns found in mathematical domains. The goal was not merely to find *some* math pages, but to build a system capable of iteratively improving its precision by exposing itself to diverse examples and refining its decision boundaries over time.

The initialization of this fastText classifier requires two components: a positive seed corpus and negative examples through which the model learns what *not* to pick. The authors select OpenWebMath\footnote{\url{https://github.com/DeepSpark/MathWebData}} as their initial positive seed corpus because it represents a high-quality collection of mathematical web texts already curated for similar purposes. Mathematically speaking, we treat the OpenWebMath URLs as ground truth "positive samples." To train an effective binary classifier that distinguishes between positive and negative instances, we generate an equivalent number of negative examples from Common Crawl but exclude them from our final training dataset; rather, they serve purely as learning anchors during the initial training phase. The balance between these sets—specifically randomly selecting 500,000 data points (e.g., URLs) from the seed corpus as positive training examples and another 500,000 unrelated web pages from Common Crawl as negative training examples—is crucial for stabilizing gradient updates during the initial weight configuration of the model.

During this initial training epoch, we configure several hyperparameters that dictate the sensitivity of the model to rare terms versus common ones. We configure the vector dimension of n-grams to 256; this depth allows for capturing subtle semantic correlations without incurring prohibitive computational costs during inference or pre-training scanning. We set the learning rate $lr = 0.1$, providing ample force for rapid convergence given that this is an exploratory phase where we do not yet expect peak accuracy but rather rapid startup performance toward relevant domains. Crucially, we restrict our analysis window by setting `min_ngram = 3`, choosing only words longer than three characters ($W_3$). This architectural choice is intentional because many low-value web pages contain common function words or headers generated by scrapers that are devoid of distinct mathematical semantics; by focusing on larger n-grams like trigrams ($n_3$), we exponentially reduce noise while amplifying semantic density\footnote{\url{https://welcome.fasttext.cc/vectors.html#density-of-business-dictionary}} within our model's representation space.
