\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 4: # Comparison PPO vs. GRPO: The Role of Value Functions in LLM Contexts

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) stands as a dominant paradigm in the reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning of Large Language Models (LLMs), largely superseding earlier algorithms like REINFORCE or TRPO due to its stability and sample efficiency. However, the standard formulation of PPO relies heavily on a dual-agent architecture consisting of an actor network and a separate, independently trained critic (or value) network. In the context of mathematical reasoning and general LLM alignment, this architectural requirement introduces significant practical bottlenecks. The critic network must maintain comparable size and computational complexity to the policy model to accurately approximate the expected return at every token position. Furthermore, training a stable value function is notoriously difficult; if the value estimates deviate too far from reality, the advantage calculation becomes noisy, leading to unstable training dynamics that often degrade performance rather than improve it.

The authors of DeepSeekMath argue that for LLMs, particularly when dealing with complex reasoning tasks where rewards are sparse and delayed across long sequences, the reliance on a separate value function is inefficient and prone to failure. They posit that in many LLM applications, the cost of maintaining and training a parallel value network outweighs the theoretical benefits it provides for variance reduction. Instead of approximating the value function $V_\psi(s)$ explicitly, which requires backpropagation through a distinct neural network layer, alternative strategies seek to eliminate this dependency entirely. This necessitates a shift away from absolute advantage estimation toward relative advantage estimation, where performance is judged not against a global baseline derived from a learned function, but against peers generated within the same batch or group. This philosophical shift forms the foundation for Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which aims to replicate the stabilization mechanisms of PPO—specifically clipping ratios while removing the memory-intensive critic—by leveraging intra-group statistical normalization.

By comparing PPO and GRPO through the lens of their respective objectives reveals that while both seek to maximize expected returns by adjusting policy probabilities based on reward signals, their implementation diverges fundamentally in how they compute these signals. PPO computes advantages $A_t$ using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), which combines returns discounted by a factor $\gamma$ with bootstrapped values from a critic model: $A_t = R_{t:T} - V_\pi(S_t)$. This process demands precise value predictions at each timestep $t$, requiring frequent updates to the critic model synchronized with the actor updates via an asynchronous repackaging strategy or synchronous gradient descent loops involving two models. Conversely, GRPO abandons this explicit modeling effort. It proposes computing advantages based purely on the distribution of rewards observed within a finite group $G$ sampled from a fixed reference policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. By treating all members of a group as having experienced identical states (the prompt question) but generating different outputs with varying qualities measured by reward scores $r_i$, GRPO defines advantage purely through local statistics: $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \bar{r}}{\sigma_r}$. This approach eliminates the need for an external baseline altogether, replacing it with empirical means calculated dynamically for every batch step. Consequently, GRPO reduces memory footprint significantly because only one policy model needs to be maintained during training phases dedicated to optimization logic, contrasting sharply with PPO's requirement for managing gradients flowing through two competing networks simultaneously.

Despite these structural differences suggesting that removing the critic simplifies matters inherently enough to lose nothing critical regarding convergence speed or final performance ceiling remains debatable without rigorous experimentation within specific domains like mathematical reasoning. Yet empirical evidence presented indicates that simply dropping critics does not guarantee success unless one replaces them with sufficiently informative alternatives—in this case, relative rankings within groups derived directly from reward models trained on pairwise comparisons or rule-based scoring systems typical in math datasets like GSM8K or MATH.
