\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 8: # Post-Hoc Analysis: Interpreting Success Factors in DeepSeekMath RL

The transition from supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to reinforcement learning (RL) represents a critical juncture in the evolution of large language models (LLMs) for mathematical reasoning. While SFT establishes a solid foundation by aligning the model with human preferences and providing explicit solution structures, it often fails to resolve deeper inconsistencies inherent in the generation process. The introduction of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) in DeepSeekMath marks a shift towards addressing these latent issues, yet understanding *why* this intervention yields such dramatic performance improvements requires a granular post-hoc analysis of the underlying dynamics. This chapter delves into the nuanced interplay between distribution robustness and fundamental capability enhancement, examining how RL specifically targets misalignments that persist despite rigorous SFT. By analyzing metrics such as Maj@K versus Pass@K and exploring the concept of alignment problems within early-stage models, we can decipher the mechanistic drivers behind DeepSeekMath's success.

## Distribution Robustness vs Fundamental Capability Enhancement(Maj@K vs Pass@K)

To truly comprehend the efficacy of reinforcement learning in mathematical domains, one must distinguish between enhancing the probability of generating a correct answer for easy queries and broadening the model's competence across difficult distributions. This distinction is captured elegantly through the lenses of Pass@K and Majoritarian Accuracy at K (Maj@K). Pass@K measures the fraction of test cases for which at least one of $K$ generated solutions is correct, serving as a proxy for fundamental reasoning capability. If RL merely stabilizes outputs or exploits biases to consistently get easy questions right without expanding logical depth, Pass@K might remain static while Maj@K increases drastically. Conversely, genuine expansion of reasoning boundaries should lift both metrics simultaneously, though often with varying rates depending on task difficulty and noise levels in the reward signal.

In the context of DeepSeekMath-RL 7B, empirical observations reveal a distinct pattern: RL enhances Maj@K performance significantly but leaves Pass@K largely unchanged or with marginal improvement compared to SFT baselines. This dissociation suggests that reinforcement learning acts more as an optimizer of output distribution robustness rather than a catalyst for deepening foundational logic in every instance. The authors hypothesize that RL renders the output distribution more robust by shifting weight toward diverse but consistent reasoning paths that pass specific checkpoints or satisfy reward functions without necessarily upgrading the model's intrinsic ability to solve novel, highly complex problems from scratch on every shot. Essentially, RL ensures that when asked to generate multiple attempts ($K > 1$), there is a higher likelihood that *one* attempt will hit the target due to increased coverage and reduced collapse into single-mode behaviors common in SFT models.

This phenomenon highlights a crucial insight into modern alignment: improving Maj@K does not equate to improving Pass@1 universally. It implies that DeepSeekMath-RL excels at making it easier to find *a* solution through iterative generation strategies rather than guaranteeing correctness on every single prompt immediately. This is particularly relevant because many mathematical tasks allow for heuristic derivations where getting "close" or finding an alternative valid path counts as success under certain reward definitions used during training (such as process supervision). Consequently, while ML @ K reflects an optimization strategy focused on maximizing hit rates across batches—a direct benefit of RL—Pass @ K remains anchored by harder constraints requiring flawless first-attempt reasoning, which may require different forms of pre-training or architectural changes beyond standard GRPO application alone.

## Alignment Problems in Early SFT Models

The premise that Supervised Fine-Tuning perfectly captures optimal mathematical behavior is challenged by what researchers term "alignment problems." These are subtle discrepancies where an SFT model produces responses that appear coherent and factually plausible on surface level but fail logically upon scrutiny or deviate from ground truth due to conflicting gradients or insufficient exposure during training phases. In DeepSeekMath's architecture, which utilizes Chain-of-Thought (CoT) data derived from curated benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH, these alignment problems become increasingly pronounced as model complexity grows or when encountering edge cases outside strict benchmark patterns encountered during initial training cycles.

Early-stage SFT models often learn statistical correlations rather than causal logical dependencies. They may memorize specific phrasing patterns associated with correct answers in their training corpus without internalizing the step-by-step deductive machinery required to replicate those answers generatively. When faced with perturbations in input wording or slightly altered problem structures not explicitly seen during SFT, these models tend to hallucinate confidence or produce syntactically correct but semantically flawed reasoning chains—the hallmark of alignment failure. Reinforcement learning intervenes here by introducing a feedback loop where incorrect alignments are actively penalized via negative rewards derived from process monitoring models or final outcome verification systems like GRPO's group-relative advantage estimation mechanism described earlier in literature sections involving equation (\ref{eq:GRPO-obj}).
