Chapter 6: # Understanding RL Dynamics Through a Unified Paradigm

The landscape of Large Language Model (LLM) alignment and optimization has evolved rapidly, moving beyond simple supervised fine-tuning towards complex reinforcement learning strategies capable of refining nuanced reasoning and instruction-following behaviors. Central to this evolution is the concept of **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) that eliminates the need for a separate value network, thereby reducing computational overhead while maintaining stability. However, to truly understand why GRPO succeeds—and why other methods like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) or Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT)—one must look beyond the surface-level mechanics and examine the underlying mathematical structure of the training objective. This chapter proposes a **Unified Paradigm** that abstracts diverse training methodologies into a single, coherent framework. By decomposing the gradient update process into three distinct yet interacting components—the **Data Source**, the **Reward Function**, and the **Algorithm**—we can derive a universal equation governing how models learn. This perspective allows researchers to predict the efficacy of different configurations, analyze trade-offs between online and offline sampling strategies, and understand how gradient coefficients modulate learning dynamics in mathematical reasoning tasks.

In this unified view, every training method, whether it is traditional SFT or advanced RL techniques like GRPO, can be expressed as an expectation over data distributions weighted by a gradient coefficient derived from interaction between data quality and reward signals. This abstraction reveals that differences in performance across methods are not arbitrary but stem from systematic variations in how these three pillars interact: whether data comes from static historical records or dynamic policy exploration; how rewards are modeled versus adjudicated by rules; and how sophisticated algorithms transform sparse feedback into precise directional updates for model parameters.

# Defining the Unified Paradigm: Gradient Components $(Data Source, Reward Funcn., Algorithm)$

At the heart of any supervised or reinforcement learning procedure lies the computation of gradients with respect to model parameters $\theta$. For any training objective $\mathcal{A}$ applied to an LLM, the gradient update direction can be formally characterized by a unified equation:

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}]\left( GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q, o, t,\pi_{rf}) \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})\right)
$$

Here, $\pi_{\theta}$ represents the current policy model generating outputs $o$ given input queries $q$, while $\pi_{ref}$ serves as a reference baseline—typically the initial Supervised Fine-Tuned (SFT) model—to prevent catastrophic forgetting and ensure stable updates via Regularization terms like KL-divergence. The core insight of this paradigm is that the total gradient flow depends entirely on three distinct components:

First is the **Data Source ($\mathcal{D}$)**. This component dictates *what* information enters the training loop. It defines both the input queries $q$ and their corresponding outputs $o$. In many modern contexts, distinguishing between data sources involves categorizing them as either "offline" or "online." Offline sampling implies drawing training pairs $(q,o)$ from a fixed dataset generated initially by an SFT model; once generated, these examples do not change regardless of how well subsequent policy iterations perform on them. Conversely, online sampling involves generating outputs dynamically during the training process itself using real-time explorations from the evolving policy model $\pi_{\theta}$. The choice here fundamentally alters statistical properties observed during convergence phases—a critical distinction explored further when comparing rejection sampling variants against pure reinforcement learning approaches.

Secondly resides the **Reward Function ($r_\varphi$)** acting as $\pi_{rf}$. This element acts as the sensor providing feedback on solution quality. In simpler setups involving rule-based evaluation or direct correctness checking on final answers (outcome supervision), this function operates deterministically without requiring heavy neural architecture deployment. However, more sophisticated implementations rely on Neural Reward Models trained on vast amounts of comparison data to assign continuous scores reflecting reasoning depth rather than mere binary correctness. Crucially, unlike traditional PPO formulations where reward functions feed into separate value networks to compute advantages via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), newer paradigms often bypass explicit value modeling altogether by leveraging group-relative statistics directly within advantage calculations. Nevertheless, regardless of implementation specifics—from manual scoring scripts to complex deep neural nets—the role remains consistent: quantifying desirability relative to some ground truth or distributional target.

Thirdly stands out prominently what might be termed simply "**the Algorithm**"—denoted generically here as $\mathcal{A}$. Its primary responsibility involves transforming raw observations obtained through interactions with data sources plus encoded preferences signaled back via reward mechanisms into actionable gradient adjustments captured within variable $GC_A(q,o,t,\pi_rf)$.
