\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### What is the specific algorithmic difference between Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) regarding the value function?
Question:
The paper introduces GRPO as a variant of PPO that removes the critic model to save memory. However, I am struggling to understand the mathematical substitution they make.

In the equation for the PPO surrogate objective, there is a term $A_t$ representing advantage, which is calculated using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE): 
$$A_t = \sum_{r_{\ge t}} \gamma^{k} (R_k - V_{\psi})$$

Since GRPO removes the learned value function $V_{\psi}$ entirely, how exactly does it re-derive the "baseline" for this calculation? The text mentions using "average reward of multiple sampled outputs" as a baseline. Does it simply replace the scalar $V_{\psi}$ with a relative mean across a group $ar{r}$? Specifically, in Equation (\ref{eq:GRPO-obj}), how are the individual advantages $\\hat{A}_{i,t}$ normalized without one token knowing about rewards for other tokens that haven't been generated yet?

Answer:
The specific algorithmic difference between Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) regarding the value function lies in how the **baseline** used to normalize rewards is constructed. In standard PPO, the baseline is a scalar estimated by a learned value network, $V_{\psi}(s)$, which approximates the expected cumulative discounted future reward starting from state $s$. This scalar is subtracted from the total reward sequence of an output to compute advantages: $A_t = \sum \gamma^k (R_k - V_{\psi})$.

In contrast, GRPO eliminates this external critic model entirely. Instead, it derives the baseline engagement-by-engagement rather than token-by-token. As detailed in Equation (\ref{eq:GRPO-obj}) and Section \ref{sec:RL} of the DeepSeekMath paper, GRPO treats each question $q$ as a group context and samples $G$ independent outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$ from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. A reward model $r_\varphi$ assigns a single numerical reward score to each of these outputs, resulting in a vector of rewards $\mathbf{r} = \{r_1, r_2, \cdots, r_G\}$.

The advantage for every token $t$ across all outputs in this group is calculated using **Group Relative Advantage Estimation**. The authors explicitly define the advantage for the $i$-th output at time step $t$ as simply being equal to its normalized group reward:
$$ \hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - {\rm mean}(\mathbf{r})}{{\rm std}(\mathbf{r})} $$
(More generally for process supervision rewards described later). The key substitution here is that the global constant $V_{\psi}(s)$ is replaced with two statistics derived exclusively from the current batch of sampled responses: the **mean** and **standard deviation** of the rewards received by that specific group of outputs. By anchoring every token's gradient signal relative to this group mean, gradients where a model response underperforms relative to its peers become negative ("punish"), while those outperforming peers become positive ("incentivize"). This approach stabilizes learning without requiring a separate neural network to estimate values.

Addressing your concern about whether tokens "know" future rewards of other tokens: In LLM reinforcement learning within this framework (and PPO generally), training does not occur at a single sequential pass over an entire output while looking into future steps. Instead, for every question in a batch, multiple *independent* samples are generated immediately by sampling from $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. At this sampling step is instantaneous; no token in output A knows about any step or reward from output B because they are statistically independent random variables generated at once. The normalization step purely aggregates these pre-generated scores ($r_1$, through $r_G$) to create the baseline distribution for that specific experimental run. Once normalized via the mean and standard deviation of $\mathbf{r}$ (${\rm mean}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\sum r_i}{G}$), these values are then unwound back into token-wise advantages via backward recurrence or direct assignment depending on whether outcome suppression or process supervision is applied, ensuring every element in any output handled has access to its own calculated benefit without needing information about unresolved future steps within its own generation trace or unrelated outputs.
