# Mining the Web Like Gold: How We Built a 120 Billion Token Math Dataset from Public Pages

Building a high-quality dataset for training Large Language Models (LLMs) is notoriously difficult. Often, researchers rely on curated, expensive private databases or smaller, domain-specific collections. However, the vast ocean of publicly available data on the internet—specifically the Common Crawl—contains untapped gold mines. Recent work by DeepSeek introduces **DeepSeekMath**, which leverages public web data to construct a massive corpus of 120 billion math-related tokens, enabling a 7-billion parameter model to rival closed-source giants like GPT-4 on competition-level benchmarks.

This approach demonstrates that high-performance mathematical reasoning does not require proprietary data or massive parameter counts alone; it requires smart engineering. The core innovation lies in how they harvested and filtered data from Common Crawl to isolate meaningful mathematical content while discarding noise.

### The Challenge of Scaling Math Data

Mathematical reasoning is unique because it demands structure, logic, and precision. Unlike creative writing or casual conversation, math problems in web crawls are scattered, inconsistent, and often buried within unrelated content. Simply scraping every page labeled with "math" yields thousands of irrelevant hits and millions of duplicates. To compete with specialized corpora like OpenWebMath or Proof-Pile-2, which aggregate data from textbooks and arXiv papers, one needs a strategy that scales efficiently while maintaining quality.

DeepSeek's solution involves creating the **DeepSeekMath Corpus**, a dataset comprising 120 billion tokens derived entirely from publicly accessible web pages. This scale is significant: it is nearly seven times larger than the math web pages used by Minerva and nine times larger than OpenWebMath. Yet, despite its sheer size, the dataset avoids benchmark contamination by rigorously filtering out known test sets like GSM8K and MATH before training begins.

### Iterative Filtering with FastText

The heart of their data collection pipeline is an iterative optimization loop powered by **FastText**, a lightweight classification system optimized for natural language processing tasks involving character n-grams [joulin2016fasttext]. Rather than trying to classify everything at once—which would require immense compute—the team adopted a "seed-and-grow" methodology. They started with a small but high-quality seed corpus derived from **OpenWebMath** [openwebmath], serving as positive examples of what good math content looks like.

The process unfolded in distinct iterations:
*   **Initialization**: The team randomly selected 500k instances from their seed corpus as positive examples and matched them against random pages from Common Crawl as negatives [deepseek-coder].
*   **Training**: A FastText classifier was trained on these pairs to learn patterns distinguishing mathematical text from general web traffic [joulin2016fasttext].
*   **Recall**: Using this model, they scanned millions of deduplicated HTML pages (reducing redundancy via URL-based and near-deduplication techniques) to identify new candidates likely to be math-related [deepseek-coder].
*   **Refinement**: Only the top-ranked pages were kept for inclusion in the next cycle of training and testing on downstream benchmarks [deepseek-coder].

Because FastText relies on word vectors rather than deep semantic understanding initially, it acts as a fast sieve rather than a perfect editor. If the initial model misses complex mathematical topics or misclassifies ambiguous content, the team enriches their seed corpus by identifying domains where the model had previously failed coverage rates (e.g., sites where over 10% of pages remained uncollected after iteration one) [deepseek-coder]. These domains were then manually annotated to expand the pool of positive examples, allowing them to retrain and improve the classifier's accuracy progressively over four cycles [deepseek-coder].

### Why Quality Matters More Than Quantity Alone

While quantity matters for statistical significance in machine learning models training datasets generally contain lots noise quality matters more when dealing with highly specialized tasks like mathematics. The DeepSeekMath Corpus stands out because it focuses heavily on high-quality content rather than just volume [deepseek-coder].
