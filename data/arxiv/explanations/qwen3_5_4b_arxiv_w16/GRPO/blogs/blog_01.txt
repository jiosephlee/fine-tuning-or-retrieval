# How to Mine 120 Billion Math Problems from the Web (And Why It Works)

One of the most significant bottlenecks in scaling Large Language Models (LLMs) for complex reasoning is the availability of high-quality training data. While creators often look to curated academic repositories or specialized aggregators for math problems, a new open-source approach suggests that the most valuable data might already exist in plain sight within the public web. DeepSeekMath demonstrates that by mining Common Crawl and applying simple machine learning techniques, it is possible to construct a massive corpus containing 120B math tokens that rivals or exceeds the performance of existing datasets. This post breaks down exactly how this was achieved and why treating scraped web pages as a primary data source can be superior to relying solely on academic papers.

The foundation of this success lies in the construction of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a dataset built specifically from Common Crawl. Unlike previous approaches that simply filtered Common Crawl using keyword matching, which often results in low-quality or noisy data, DeepSeekMath employed a rigorously engineered data selection pipeline. The process began with a seed corpus, consisting primarily of high-quality mathematical web texts from OpenWebMath. Using this seed, researchers trained a fastText classifier model \citep{joulin2016fasttext} to distinguish between mathematical content and general web noise. The fastText model was configured with specific hyperparameters: a vector dimension of 256, a learning rate of 0.1, and n-gram lengths up to 3 \citep{fasttext}. This allowed the model to learn semantic similarities between mathematical phrases found in high-quality sources and random text scraped from the internet.

The core innovation of this pipeline is its iterative nature. Instead of a one-shot filtering pass that might miss rare but relevant domains, the system continuously refined its own knowledge base. In the first iteration, the model collected data based on its initial understanding of "math." If portions of Common Crawl remained uncollected after this first pass, it indicated gaps in its search because its initial training examples lacked diversity. To address this, researchers identified new seed domains by analyzing coverage statistics across disjoint domains in Common Crawl \citep{disjoint_domains}. Domains where over 10\% of pages were collected were flagged as math-related; for example, sites like ``mathoverflow.net'' would be identified as targets for expansion.

Within these flagged domains, contributors manually annotated URLs associated with mathematical content, such as specific question pages on MathOverflow. These new URLs were added back into the seed corpus alongside previous examples. The fastText model was then retrained on this expanded set of positive examples \citep{domain_diversity}. This cycle repeated several times: collect more diverse positive samples based on previous successes $\rightarrow$ retrain $\rightarrow$ mine broader web sources $\rightarrow$ refine manually $\rightarrow$ retrain \footnote{The full pipeline details are illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:data_collect} of the paper }. By the fourth iteration, this method successfully aggregated 35.5 million mathematical web pages, totaling 120B tokens far larger than previous specialized corpora like OpenWebMath \citep{openwebmath} or Proof-Pile-2 \citep{llemma}.

Beyond simple scale and diversity, the quality of the DeepSeekMath Corpus is arguably higher because it relies on lightweight semantic classification rather than heuristic string matching or time-consuming human curation at every step \footnote{The filtering logic is detailed in Section \ref{sec:data_decontamination}}. The team implemented decontamination measures immediately after collection to prevent benchmark contamination \citep{benchmark_contamination}. They used exact and partial matching criteria (specifically checking for shared subsequences longer than 7 words) to remove segments from well-known benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH before they ever touched training data \citep{deepseek-coder}. This rigorous cleaning ensures that when models are evaluated later using these benchmarks, results reflect true capability rather than memorized training patterns.
