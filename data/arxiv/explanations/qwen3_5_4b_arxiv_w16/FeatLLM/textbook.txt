# Chapter 1: Foundations of LLM-Enhanced Tabular Learning

In the realm of machine learning, traditional approaches to tabular data analysis often rely on models trained specifically on the statistical patterns found within a dataset. However, in real-world scenarios labeled with only a handful of examples—known as few-shot learning—these methods frequently falter due to overfitting or an inability to generalize. This limitation stems from the scarcity of data, which is a common bottleneck in industries such as healthcare and finance, where acquiring labeled records is prohibitively expensive. To bridge this gap, researchers have increasingly turned to Large Language Models (LLMs). These generalist models, trained on vast corpora of text, possess an immense reservoir of prior knowledge that can theoretically substitute for expert domain expertise. This chapter explores the paradigm shift occurring in tabular learning: moving away from using LLMs as black-box predictors for every single data point and instead utilizing them as intelligent feature engineers that leverage their learned-domain knowledge to construct robust prediction criteria.

The potential of generalist models for tabular data lies in their ability to transcend the immediate context of a specific dataset. An LLM is not merely a statistical approximator of text patterns; it is a repository of human-caused facts, causal reasoning about diseases and economic indicators, and logical structures that have been encoded during its pre-training phase. In the context of tabular learning, this capability is revolutionary because it allows models to transfer insights from one domain to another. For instance, an LLM might know that certain physiological markers are statistically significant precursors to specific heart conditions without ever having seen a "Heart Failure" dataset during its training. By incorporating task descriptions and feature definitions into prompts, LLMs can understand the semiotic relationships between columns and targets rather than just memorizing pixel-to-label mappings as in image processing. This rich prior knowledge effectively imparts inductive biases that guide the learning process toward concepts that are sensible to a human expert but might be invisible to a shallow neural network operating on raw data vectors. Consequently, when task-specific fine-tuning is impossible due to data constraints—such as having fewer than twenty labeled samples—the integration of this prior knowledge becomes not just an enhancement but a necessity for achieving viable performance.

However, simply plugging tabular data into an LLM for final inference introduces significant challenges, particularly when dealing with high-dimensional or complex datasets. Current end-to-end approaches require serializing the entire dataset—translating rows into text strings—and feeding them into the model for every prediction instance at runtime. This method incurs a substantial computational overhead; it demands multiple API calls to large language models per sample, which translates to high latency and prohibitive costs for deployment in industrial settings. Furthermore, this reliance on raw inference presents a critical scalability issue related to prompt size constraints inherent in implemented language models (ILMs). As the number of features in a tabular dataset grows—exceeding thirty or forty variables—the amount of serialized text required to represent the input quickly saturates the context window capacity or degrades performance due to attention dilution across excessive tokens. Additionally, most state-of-the-end-to-end methods necessitate parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) gradients like LoRA or IA3sacked onto proprietary models whose training codebases are closed off by vendors like OpenAI or Google API access restrictions alone prevent widespread research and application. These constraints highlight that while LLMs possess the latent intelligence for tabular tasks, their current direct application mode is fundamentally misaligned with the practical requirements of low-latency inference and scalable feature extraction found in dynamic production environments.

To address these limitations while capitalizing on the richness of prior knowledge, researchers explore the concept of feature engineering through natural language reasoning. Instead of asking the model "what is the probability this row belongs to class C?", they ask "what specific conditions distinguish class C from others?". # Chapter 2: The FeatLLM Framework Architecture

# Overview of the Feature-First Inference Paradigm

The paradigm shift offered by the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework represents a fundamental departure from conventional approaches to Large Language Models (LLMs) in tabular learning. Traditional methods, often referred to as end-to-end inference approaches, treat the LLM as a black-box classifier. In these standard pipelines, every single input sample is serialized into natural language and passed directly through the LLM, which processes the entire dataset for every prediction. This design suffers from significant computational overhead at inference time, as it requires repetitive LLM queries for each observation. Furthermore, these methods are often constrained by the context window limits of the model; as the number of features in a tabular dataset expands, the serialized data quickly becomes too large to process efficiently, often necessitating dropout or truncation that degrades performance.

\textsf{FeatLLM}, in contrast, adopts a "feature-first" inference paradigm. Instead of using the LLM to output a probability distribution directly for a given sample, it assigns the LLM the specific role of an expert feature engineer. The goal is not prediction itself but the discovery of high-quality decision boundaries or rules that separate classes based on input features. The core intuition behind this approach is that if an LLM can identify the critical conditions under which a specific class occurs—formulated as logical rules—then these rules can be evaluated deterministically on new data without further interaction with the complex language model. This permission to shift capability allows \textsf{FeatLLM} to bypass the heavy computational cost of individual sample queries during inference.

Once these rules are extracted and formalized into deterministic binary features (indicating whether a sample satisfies a specific condition), the task of inferring class likelihood is offloaded to a simple machine learning model, such as linear regression with non-negative weights. This downstream model learns to compute a weighted sum of these binary features plus a softmax activation to produce probability estimates. By decoupling rule generation from final prediction, \textsf{FeatLLM} achieves two major advantages: first, it maintains low inference latency because rule evaluation is computationally inexpensive compared to LLM query generation; and second, it eliminates scalability issues regarding prompt size. Even if an original dataset contains hundreds or thousands of columns, only the generated rules and their corresponding evaluation code need to be processed during inference, effectively expanding the effective context window beyond native model limitations through code execution rather than token consumption. This architecture ensures that \textsf{FeatLLM} remains feasible for real-world applications where label collection is costly and computational efficiency at runtime is paramount.

# Two-Step Reasoning Process: Causal Inference and Rule Derivation

To achieve high-quality feature engineering without requiring heavy fine-tuning, \textsf{FeatLLM} employs a structured reasoning process designed to mimic the deductive capabilities of an expert analyst. The framework prompts an LLM to follow a rigorous two-step derivation path for each target class: causal feature inference followed by decisive rule extraction. This disaggregation prevents the model from prematurely committing to spurious correlations and ensures that only features with genuine relational strength are converted into hard constraints for subsequent modeling.

The first step focuses on causal inference or tendency identification. In this phase, the LLM is instructed to analyze the relationship between input features and the target class definition without relying on any specific training examples provided in a few-shot fashion at this moment alone. Instead, it leverages its extensive prior knowledge derived from vast text corpora to hypothesize which features are theoretically relevant to the problem domain—for instance, understanding that age might be relevant for disease prediction based on general medical knowledge rather than just statistical correlation in noisy limited data. By separating this stage from example-dependent reasoning, \textsf{FeatLLM} mitigates overfitting patterns present in small datasets and encourages adherence to known scientific principles embedded within pre-training data. # Chapter 3: Prompt Engineering and Rule Extraction Strategies

In the landscape of large language model (LLM) applications for tabular learning, the ability to reliably translate unstructured reasoning into structured data transformations is paramount. Conventional approaches often treat the LLM as a black-box predictor, feeding serialized table rows directly into the model to generate a probability distribution for the target class. However, this end-to-end black-box utilization incurs prohibitive computational costs at inference time and suffers from scalability issues when dealing with high-dimensional tabular data. To overcome these limitations, frameworks like \textsf{FeatLLM} reposition the LLM as an intelligent feature engineer. This chapter delves into the sophisticated prompt engineering techniques and structured reasoning strategies employed to extract high-quality decision rules from LLMs. By meticulously designing prompts that enforce a specific reasoning trajectory—separating prior knowledge retrieval from data-specific inference—and by utilizing domain-specific priming, we can guide models to generate interpretable binary features. These features are subsequently parsed into executable Python code, enabling fast, low-latency downstream classification without requiring repeated LLM invocations for every sample. We will explore how Chain-of-Thought mechanisms are adapted for tabular contexts, how multi-step reasoning filters out spurious correlations found in noisy data, and how feature descriptions serve as critical priors to enhance rule accuracy in few-shot regimes.

# Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Adaptation for Tabular Tasks

The integration of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning has proven to be a transformative technique in disentangling complex logical tasks from simple pattern matching. In the context of tabular learning, where the goal is not merely prediction but the discovery of underlying causal relationships between features and targets, adapting CoT requires a precise structural decomposition of the problem-solving process. Traditional CoT prompts encourage models to "think inside the hat" by generating intermediate steps before arriving at a final answer; however, when applied blindly to tabular data serialization, this can lead to noisy outputs or convergence on spurious correlations present in small datasets. The \textsf{FeatLLM} framework addresses this by explicitly引导幼儿's inference process into two distinct stages within a single prompt interaction. This adaptation transforms generic reasoning into a targeted feature extraction protocol tailored for tabular domains.

The first stage, or Step 1 of the reasoning instruction, relies entirely on prior knowledge without referencing any few-shot examples contained in the prompt's input demonstrations. During this phase, the LLM acts as an expert consultant hired only its encyclopedic training data rather than the specific (and potentially limited) training examples provided for the task. It is tasked with inferring general causal relationships or tendencies between feature types and task descriptions based solely on its pre-trained weights and external knowledge bases. For instance, if predicting chronic heart failure, Step 1 might reason about general medical knowledge linking age and blood pressure without needing to see specific patient records yet. This decoupling ensures that the model leverages robust domain knowledge rather than overfitting to rare or idiosyncratic patterns in current few-shot samples at this stage of generation. The constraints here are strict: no reference to example demonstrations $({\bf x}^i)$ is permitted during this logical deduction phase.

The second stage builds upon the findings of Step 1 but integrates concrete evidence from supported preparation steps and example demonstrations derived from previous knowledge steps. Here, the LLM synthesizes its general insights with specific instance patterns detected in the few labeled examples provided via natural language serialization ($\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i,F)$). This hybrid approach allows the model to refine its abstract hypotheses derived from prior knowledge by validating them against actual data trends observed in current samples while maintaining logical consistency with general domain principles defined earlier in Step 1. By structuring instructions this way—specifically enabling causal relationship extraction followed by decisive rule derivation—the framework mitigates two common pitfalls: identifying irrelevant columns based solely on noise in tiny datasets and failing to utilize strong external priors that would otherwise be overlooked due to context window saturation or attention limitations during step-one inference alone).
