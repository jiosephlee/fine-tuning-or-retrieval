Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: The BLT Neural Architecture

The Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, represents a paradigm shift in the architecture of large language models by abandoning the reliance on static, heuristic tokenization in favor of a dynamic, byte-level processing system. Traditional architectures impose a rigid vocabulary on raw text before it enters the model's computational layers. This pre-processing step dictates how frequently the transformation tokens appear, effectively offloading information compression to a static bottleneck. BLT eliminates this constraint by integrating two distinct yet complementary transformer components: a massive "Global Latent Transformer" that operates on compressed representations called patches, and lightweight "Local Encoder" and "Local Decoder" models that act as translators between the raw byte domain and the latent patch domain. This hybrid structure is not merely an architectural modularity; it serves as a mechanism for computational efficiency. Because the Global Latent Transformer is invoked only when data is segmented into patches—specifically where uncertainty is high—the architecture achieves dynamic resource allocation. This chapter details the specific mechanisms of each component, elucidating how they collaborate to allocate compute based on input complexity rather than uniform frequency rules.

## The Global Latent Transformer ($\mathcal{G}$): The Core Computation Engine

At the heart of the BLT's mathematical efficiency lies the Global Latent Transformer, denoted here as $\mathcal{G}$. While standard transformer models process sequences token-by-token regardless of the semantic difficulty of predicting them at each step, $\mathcal{G}$ is designed to operate exclusively on sequences of patches. A patch is defined as an aggregated sequence of bytes that shares relatively uniform information density, often bounded by regions of high predictability or high entropy. Consequently, $\mathcal{G}$ functions as an autoregressive model with $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers that maps a sequence of input patch representations, $p_j$, into a sequence of output patch representations, $o_j$.

The operational strides of $\mathcal{G}$ are governed strictly by the patching function $f_p$ applied to the input sequence. Formally, if a document consists of $m$ patches derived from $n$ bytes ($m < n$), the model must perform attention operations $m$ times rather than $n$. This reduction in the number of inference steps is particularly profound because in modern large-scale transformers, such as Llama 3 or GPT-4, approximately 80% to 90% of computational cost resides in multi-layer perceptron (MLP) feed-forward networks alongside self-attention layers within each transformer step. By reducing step count from millions (for byte-level processing) to thousands (for patch-level processing), $\mathcal{G}$ drastically lowers the arithmetic complexity per byte processed.

Crucially, $\mathcal{G}$ utilizes a block-causal attention mask, specifically one designed for documents rather than individual tokens (referred to in folklore notation as `max_row` caching). This mask restricts attention restrictedly up to and including only the current patch within a document boundary. This prevents leakage between patches while allowing full context retention within them. The architecture treats patches as atomic units; there is no intermediate representation for individual bytes within $\mathcal{G}$. Instead, all information flow passes through these learned latent vectors $p_j$. The model thus indirectly learns where to group bytes into computationally expensive chunks versus lighter ones by evolving its ability to detect patterns during pre-training where patch boundaries are likely candidates for transition from high-cognitive-load bytes to easier ones.

The unique contribution of this module dynamic capability cannot be overstated; it transforms what would otherwise be a fixed-compute operation into a variable-compute operation contingent on input complexity. If a context segment is highly predictive (low entropy), it will naturally form larger patches under optimal patching schemes like entropy-based segmentation. When such segments are processed by $\mathcal{G}$ with fewer steps but larger context windows per step relative to uncompressed data, the model benefits from parallelized computation within those fewer steps without incurring redundant arithmetic for easy predictions. Conversely, if data complexity spikes—such as starting a new sentence—entropy increases aggressively according to metrics learned from steady-state portions of training data (e.g., Bruehl & Xue Zones or similar heuristic boundaries), resulting in smaller patch sizes and higher frequency calls to $\mathcal{G}$.
