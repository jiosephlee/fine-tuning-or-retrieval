# Why Tokenization Might Be Holding Back Your AI: Introducing Dynamic Patches

Large Language Models (LLMs) currently dominate the landscape of artificial intelligence, yet their foundational architecture relies on a somewhat arbitrary preprocessing step known as tokenization. Before an LLM processes a sentence, it strips away formatting codes and groups contiguous bytes into discrete units called tokens based on a fixed vocabulary. While effective for standard text, this rigid approach assumes that every unit requires the same amount of computational effort to process. This simplification creates a bottleneck where simple patterns consume as much compute as complex ones, limiting both efficiency and the model's ability to generalize to diverse data types. A new architecture called Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) challenges this status quo by replacing static tokens with dynamic patches that intelligently allocate resources based on the actual complexity of the data they represent.

At its core, BLT operates at the byte level rather than the subword level typical of modern models like Llama or GPT. Instead of forcing raw bytes into predefined buckets, BLT treats sequences of bytes as flexible entities called patches. These patches serve as the fundamental units for computation within a transformer structure. The revolutionary aspect lies in how these patches are formed. Rather than using a static dictionary or simple rules like splitting at spaces, BLT employs an entropy-based method to segment data dynamically. The system analyzes the probability distribution of the next byte in a sequence; when uncertainty is high, indicating complex information such as novel characters or intricate mathematical symbols, the model creates smaller patches to dedicate more attention heads and layers to that region. Conversely, when faced with highly predictable text like repetitive headers or common phrases, it merges bytes into larger patches, skipping unnecessary computational overhead.

Mathematically, this dynamic segmentation relies on estimating entropy $H(x_i)$ for each byte position $x_i$ using a lightweight auxiliary language model:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$

Here, $\mathcal{V}$ represents the byte vocabulary and $p_e$ denotes the probability distribution estimated by a small pre-trained byte-level LM conditioned on previous context $\pmb{x}_{<i}$. The algorithm identifies patch boundaries by looking for points where entropy exceeds a specific global threshold or where the entropy rises significantly relative to the previous byte ($H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$). This ensures that patch boundaries align naturally with shifts in information density within the text stream. For instance, predicting the first letter of a new sentence carries significantly more uncertainty than completing an established word pattern; consequently, BLT allocates more "steps" through its transformer layers specifically for those high-entropy regions while compressing low-entropy sections into fewer steps via larger patch sizes.

The architectural implementation involves three distinct components designed to facilitate this efficient flow of information. First is the Latent Global Transformer ($\mathcal{G}$), which acts as the powerful engine responsible for processing long-range dependencies between patches. Unlike standard transformers that operate on fixed-size vectors, $\mathcal{G}$ receives inputs from variable-sized patch representations derived from lighter local models. Second is the Local Encoder ($\mathcal{E}$), a compact transformer module tasked with mapping individual bytes into these latent patch representations before they reach the global model. Crucially, this encoder incorporates hash-ngram embeddings alongside standard byte embeddings to capture local context without expanding memory usage unnecessarily. Third is the Local Decoder ($\mathcal{D}$), which mirrors the encoder's functionality but operates in reverse: it translates predictions from $\mathcal{G}$ back into raw bytes so they can be concatenated and fed into subsequent decoding steps. A specialized cross-attention mechanism links these modules, ensuring that queries from one stage can attend appropriately to keys and values generated by others without violating causal constraints required for autoregressive generation.

Experiments conducted up to 8 billion parameters reveal compelling evidence supporting this shift toward dynamic patching. In terms of raw computational performance under identical training budgets (measured in floating-point operations or FLOPs), BLT models match or exceed state-of-the-art tokenization-based counterparts like Llama 3 trained on similar datasets containing trillions of tokens. More impressively, researchers achieved significant gains in inference efficiency without sacrificing quality; by increasing average patch sizes through smarter segmentation during deployment (while keeping total compute constant), inference costs were reduced by up to 50%. This efficiency gain stems directly from avoiding redundant calculations on predictable substrings while preserving sufficient capacity for difficult problems such as code generation or scientific reasoning where precise character manipulation is vital.
