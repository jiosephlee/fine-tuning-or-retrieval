# Why Tokenization Might Be Holding Back Your AI: Introducing Dynamic Patches

Large Language Models (LLMs) currently dominate the landscape of artificial intelligence, yet their foundational architecture relies on a somewhat arbitrary preprocessing step known as tokenization. Before an LLM processes a sentence, it strips away formatting codes and groups contiguous bytes into discrete units called tokens based on a fixed vocabulary. While effective for standard text, this rigid approach assumes that every unit requires the same amount of computational effort to process. This simplification creates a bottleneck where simple patterns consume as much compute as complex ones, limiting both efficiency and the model's ability to generalize to diverse data types. A new architecture called Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) challenges this status quo by replacing static tokens with dynamic patches that intelligently allocate resources based on the actual complexity of the data they represent.

At its core, BLT operates at the byte level rather than the subword level typical of modern models like Llama or GPT. Instead of forcing raw bytes into predefined buckets, BLT treats sequences of bytes as flexible entities called patches. These patches serve as the fundamental units for computation within a transformer structure. The revolutionary aspect lies in how these patches are formed. Rather than using a static dictionary or simple rules like splitting at spaces, BLT employs an entropy-based method to segment data dynamically. The system analyzes the probability distribution of the next byte in a sequence; when uncertainty is high, indicating complex information such as novel characters or intricate mathematical symbols, the model creates smaller patches to dedicate more attention heads and layers to that region. Conversely, when faced with highly predictable text like repetitive headers or common phrases, it merges bytes into larger patches, skipping unnecessary computational overhead.

Mathematically, this dynamic segmentation relies on estimating entropy $H(x_i)$ for each byte position $x_i$ using a lightweight auxiliary language model:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$

Here, $\mathcal{V}$ represents the byte vocabulary and $p_e$ denotes the probability distribution estimated by a small pre-trained byte-level LM conditioned on previous context $\pmb{x}_{<i}$. The algorithm identifies patch boundaries by looking for points where entropy exceeds a specific global threshold or where the entropy rises significantly relative to the previous byte ($H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$). This ensures that patch boundaries align naturally with shifts in information density within the text stream. For instance, predicting the first letter of a new sentence carries significantly more uncertainty than completing an established word pattern; consequently, BLT allocates more "steps" through its transformer layers specifically for those high-entropy regions while compressing low-entropy sections into fewer steps via larger patch sizes.

The architectural implementation involves three distinct components designed to facilitate this efficient flow of information. First is the Latent Global Transformer ($\mathcal{G}$), which acts as the powerful engine responsible for processing long-range dependencies between patches. Unlike standard transformers that operate on fixed-size vectors, $\mathcal{G}$ receives inputs from variable-sized patch representations derived from lighter local models. Second is the Local Encoder ($\mathcal{E}$), a compact transformer module tasked with mapping individual bytes into these latent patch representations before they reach the global model. Crucially, this encoder incorporates hash-ngram embeddings alongside standard byte embeddings to capture local context without expanding memory usage unnecessarily. Third is the Local Decoder ($\mathcal{D}$), which mirrors the encoder's functionality but operates in reverse: it translates predictions from $\mathcal{G}$ back into raw bytes so they can be concatenated and fed into subsequent decoding steps. A specialized cross-attention mechanism links these modules, ensuring that queries from one stage can attend appropriately to keys and values generated by others without violating causal constraints required for autoregressive generation.

Experiments conducted up to 8 billion parameters reveal compelling evidence supporting this shift toward dynamic patching. In terms of raw computational performance under identical training budgets (measured in floating-point operations or FLOPs), BLT models match or exceed state-of-the-art tokenization-based counterparts like Llama 3 trained on similar datasets containing trillions of tokens. More impressively, researchers achieved significant gains in inference efficiency without sacrificing quality; by increasing average patch sizes through smarter segmentation during deployment (while keeping total compute constant), inference costs were reduced by up to 50%. This efficiency gain stems directly from avoiding redundant calculations on predictable substrings while preserving sufficient capacity for difficult problems such as code generation or scientific reasoning where precise character manipulation is vital. Furthermore, because BLT never discards fine-grained byte-level information during tokenization phases demonstrated in benchmarks involving noisy inputs or low-resource languages where character-level understanding proves critical for accurate translation and speech synthesis tasks



# Scaling Smarter: How BLT Trains Larger Models Without Breaking the Budget

In the race to build increasingly powerful Large Language Models (LLMs), the industry has largely adhered to a rigid economic reality: to get better performance, you must burn more money. The standard approach involves expanding model parameters or feeding the system more training data, often leading to exponential growth in computational costs. But what happens if you want to build a massive, high-performance model while strictly capping your inference budget? For years, this seemed impossible. You faced a zero-sum game where saving compute meant sacrificing size or capabilities.

Enter **Byte Latent Transformer** (**BLT**), a new architecture that fundamentally breaks this trade-off. By shifting from tokenization to dynamic patching, BLT introduces a concept known as "Fixed-Inference Scaling." This methodology allows developers to simultaneously increase both the model size and the patch size—keeping the total number of floating-point operations (flops) required for inference constant—to achieve superior scaling trends compared to traditional token-based models.

### The Limitation of Fixed Tokens

To understand the breakthrough offered by BLT, we must first look at how tokenization-based models operate. Most modern LLMs rely on a pre-defined vocabulary where arbitrary chunks of text are mapped to discrete tokens. This process relies heavily on heuristics like Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) to compress raw bytes into manageable units.

The fundamental constraint here is that every token requires exactly the same amount of compute regardless of whether it contains a simple space or complex semantic meaning. If an architect wants to optimize for speed, they must shrink the model; if they want power, they must expand it. There is no mechanism within this architecture to say, "This part of the input is easy; I'll skip ahead," versus "That part is hard; I need heavy lifting." Consequently, when researchers attempt fixed-inference scaling studies using tokenizers like Llama 3's BPE, performance plateaus quickly because any effort spent shrinking steps inevitably reduces model capacity elsewhere.

### Dynamic Allocation via Entropy Patching

BLT changes this equation by replacing static tokens with **dynamic patches**. Instead of forcing every byte into a fixed-sized unit defined by a vocabulary, BLT learns where to draw boundaries based on the entropy—the uncertainty—of the next byte prediction in the sequence.

Imagine reading a book. Predicting the next word after seeing "The cat sat on..." is trivial because the pattern is established and highly predictable (low entropy). Conversely, predicting the first word of a novel or solving a complex math problem requires significant cognitive load (high entropy). Standard tokenizers treat these moments equally computationally because they cannot distinguish them without breaking words apart unpredictably or merging unrelated fragments together inefficiently.

BLT solves this through **entropy patching**. It trains a lightweight auxiliary language model specifically on byte sequences to estimate next-byte probabilities for every position in the text. Based on these estimates, it calculates an entropy score for each potential boundary:
$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$
If the entropy drops significantly below a learned threshold, indicating high predictability, BLT merges multiple bytes into a single patch. This reduces the number of transformer steps required for that portion of the text without losing information fidelity because patches act as containers rather than atomic dictionary entries. Conversely, when entropy rises and prediction difficulty spikes, boundaries open up naturally, ensuring high-compute regions receive dedicated attention resources immediately.

### Unlocking Simultaneous Growth

The true innovation lies in how BLT leverages reduced step counts during inference. In traditional models, fewer steps mean fewer parameters per step can be afforded within a fixed budget—a classic compromise between width and depth. In BLT's architecture, reducing step count frees up computational resources that can be reallocated directly toward growing model width and depth without penalty.

Consider an inference scenario constrained by $N$ total flops. A tokenized model might execute $M$ steps with small embeddings ($E$), yielding $M \times E$ total capacity under constraints that force inefficiencies at hard spots. An BLT model executing $M'$ steps ($M' < M$) due to successful patch merging retains those saved flops $(N - M'E)$ and invests them into larger global transformer layers and richer local encoders/decoders ($E'$). Mathematically represented as:
$$\text{Total Cost} = N_{steps} \times (\text{Cost per Step})$$
By minimizing $N_{steps}$ through intelligent patching while maximizing $(\text{Cost per Step})$ through architectural upgrades like n-gram hash embeddings and multi-head cross-attention mechanisms, BLT achieves higher effective capacity per dollar spent than its tokenized peers allow at identical parameter counts or flop budgets up to 8 Billion parameters and 4 Tera-bytes of training data analyzed in recent studies up until August 2025 today!
