\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### How does the 'Monotonically non-decreasing constraint' specifically alter the search space of RoPE dimensions?
Question:
In Section \ref{sec:method}, under 'Evolution-based search', the paper proposes a constraint where $\lambda_i \leq \lambda_{i+1}$ for all RoPE dimensions. The text explains this is based on NTK theory suggesting lower dimensions (higher frequency) require less interpolation (smaller $\lambda_i$).

However, I am struggling to connect this constraint to the raw search space defined in Table \ref{tbl:searchspace}. The table allows searching for $\lambda_i$ from 1.0 up to $s \times 1.25$. Without these monotonicity constraints, an evolutionary algorithm would randomly pick factors where dimension 0 might have a factor of 2.0 while dimension 64 has a factor of 1.5.

Does this constraint force ALL dimensions to have at least $\lambda=1.0$? Or does it just prune invalid candidates after they are generated? Furthermore, since NTK suggests intercepting frequencies differently (where some should be <1 and some >1), how exactly does this global ordering assumption ($0 	o d/2-1$) account for cases where certain high-frequency components inside that range might theoretically need *more* extrapolation than their immediate neighbors?
Answer:
The monotonically non-decreasing constraint $\lambda_i \leq \lambda_{i+1}$ does not force all dimensions to have a value of 1.0, nor does it prune individuals after they are generated; rather, it acts as a structural filter applied immediately after the probabilistic mutation and crossover operators of the evolutionary algorithm. In the population generation phase, the algorithm generates random rescale factors $\lambda_i$ within the broad range $[1.0, s \times 1.25]$. If a mutation or crossover creates an individual where a lower-dimension factor is larger than its neighbor (e.g., $\lambda_0 > \lambda_1$), that specific individual is discarded before it is ever evaluated for perplexity. This occurs because the constraint is hard-coded into the mutation and crossover functions (`Mutation_with_mono_constraint` and `Crossover_with_mono_constraint`), ensuring that only valid search trajectories enter the evaluation loop.

While $1.0$ is indeed the minimum value allowed by Table \ref{tbl:searchspace}, representing direct extrapolation where no interpolation weight is applied, the monotonicity assumption implies that any dimension with $\lambda < 1$ must be smaller than all subsequent dimensions, which propagate this "high frequency, less interpolation" behavior to increasing degrees in dimension space. Consequently, while theoretically one might expect certain frequency bands distinct from Room frequency to require more extrapolation (closer to 1) than others, or vice versa, this global ordering forces a smooth transition of interpolation magnitude. If an NTK theory suggests that dimension $i$ needs slightly less interpolation than $i+1$ further inside the high-frequency range (which would technically violate local monotonicity if we reversed the bound), the search landscape effectively treats such complex local oscillations as noise or sub-optimal. The constraint trades off potentially optimal non-monotonic patterns in favor of a simplified manifold where frequency simply decays smoothly as dimension increases from 0 to $d/2-1$. This reduces the combinatorial explosion ($O(s^{d/2})$) of the search space significantly by restricting candidates to a simplex rather than a hypercube, aligning with NTK's broad heuristic that high-frequency components generally require less positional stretching than low-frequency ones across the spectrum of observable dimensions.
