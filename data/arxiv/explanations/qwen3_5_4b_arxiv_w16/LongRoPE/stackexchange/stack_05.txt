\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Can you explain why optimizing for perplexity alone isn't sufficient when using cross-entropy loss during fine-tuning?
Question:
**A critical confusion regarding loss function mismatch in LongRoPE**
The paper consistently refers to optimizing based on per-predicted perplexity using random sampling from PG-19 development sets. This makes sense for evaluating whether our positional embedding successfully preserves language structure during inference-style exposure within text.

However, actual model training occurs via cross-entropy loss on parallel training data. Training models with cross-entropy loss but searches done with Perplexity as objective without considering gradient directions could introduce distribution shifts that hinder convergence.
The paper notes that experiments were conducted after seeing common practice issues like random initialization of activation vectors causing gradient explosion during optimization applying new positional embeddings across layers.
Answer:
The optimization of perplexity alone during the search phase for LongRoPE is a deliberate and mathematically sound strategy designed to minimize the specific error introduced by the positional embedding, rather than attempting to learn general task-specific distributions. By minimizing cross-entropy on sampled data (which corresponds to minimizing perplexity), the search algorithm effectively optimizes for language modeling capability—the ability of the model to predict the next token accurately—without requiring full pre-training on massive datasets.

When a positional embedding is modified, it fundamentally alters the internal representations of tokens, effectively moving the model's output distribution away from its pre-trained optimum. The gap between these distributions is quantified by the cross-entropy loss (or negative log-likelihood), which is $-\frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^{L} \log P(y_i | x_{<i}; \theta)$. Minimizing this average loss per token across a diverse set of random samples from a development set like PG-19 ensures that the positional parameters $\theta$ (specifically the rescale factors $\hat{\lambda}_i$ and threshold $\hat{n}$) best preserve the pre-trained language model's predictive statistics. Because these statistical properties of natural language are highly consistent across large volumes of text, optimizing over random samples provides a robust signal that the new embeddings are "closer" to the original pre-trained space than alternatives.

This approach avoids consideration of specific gradient directions triggered by irrelevant gradient directions:

The concern raised is whether optimizing with random sampled cross-entropy loss during search (a stochastic evaluation loop) differs significantly from using gradients on parallel training data during final fine-tuning. In this architecture, these two phases serve distinct purposes and do not harmonize their objectives in a naive way, which prevents the distribution shifts you fear:  
The search phase is an \textit{evaluation} loop, not an optimization loop in terms of gradient back-propagation. It iteratively computes perplexity scores on several test samples and selects configurations that minimize these scores without attaching gradients to update weights or queries for convergence stability. The "objective function" here is purely scalar—a best-in-class score in terms of language modeling ability—not a vector field guiding weights via back-propagation. Consequently, there are no "gradient directions" being aligned with or misaligned from training because no gradients are flowing during this search. The search simply tests candidate parameter sets against a metric that approximates predictive quality.

There can be perceived "distribution shifts," but they are actually mitigated design features:  
Training data for fine-tuning (e.g., RedPajama or Together computer collections) may differ statistically from the PG-19 development set used for evaluation; however, because LongRoPE targets preserving the generalized language modeling capability ($P(y|x) \approx P_{pretrain}(y|x)$), it inherently performs well across diverse datasets where this statistic holds consistent patterns. Furthermore, any adversarial initialization risks causing gradient explosion during fine-tuning are prevented by nature of LongRoPE's progressive extension strategy rather than lack thereof; by first extending to 128k and then 256k before reaching 2048k, and eventually recovering short context lengths via re-searching parameters, we avoid pushing parameters into regions where gradients would blow up due to extreme length-over disentanglement issues reported in similar works like streamingllm or lminfinite.
