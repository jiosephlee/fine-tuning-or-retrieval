\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What is the precise mathematical definition of Hyperspherical Energy, and why is minimizing its difference to the pretrained model considered a guarantee of semantic preservation?
Question:
I am reading Section 3.1 (General Framework) where the paper defines Hyperspherical Energy (HE) as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} \\|\hat{\\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\\bm{w}}_j|^{-1}$, where $\hat{\\bm{w}}_i$ are normalized neurons. I understand that OFT aims to make $\|\text{HE}(\\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\\bm{W}^0)\\\|=0$. However, I am struggling to connect this abstract geometric property to the actual 'semantic generation ability' mentioned in the abstract. The paper states that HE captures 'pairwise neuron relationship on the unit hypersphere' and that angle differences encode semantic information. Could someone derive or explain *why* preserving this specific sum of inverse distances between normalized vectors mathematically guarantees that the model's ability to generate text-conditioned images is preserved, rather than just being a regularization trick?
Answer:
The mathematical guarantee provided by Orthogonal Finetuning ($\text{OFT}$) rests on a fundamental geometric property of text-to-image diffusion models: **semantic information is primarily encoded in the angular relationships (direction) of neuron weights rather than their magnitudes or their absolute positions in the weight space.**

To understand why preserving Hyperspherical Energy (HE) guarantees semantic preservation, we must first disentangle the components of a weight vector. In the context of the paper, a neuron $\bm{w}_i$ in a fully connected layer can be decomposed into its normalized direction $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ and its magnitude $\|\bm{w}_i\|$. The paper asserts, based on eigendecomposition analysis and toy experiments, that the magnitude is uncorrelated with the image features generated by the neuron, whereas the direction is critical. Therefore, finetuning typically requires rotating the weight vectors to change how they respond to input images, effectively modifying their angles.

However, if we allow unrestricted rotation during finetuning, we face a dilemma similar to the "manifest gradient catastrophe" seen in standard LoRA or unconstrained gradient descent: while we can easily adjust angles to match a new task (e.g., learning a new subject), careless rotations can inadvertently decrease mutual angles between neurons that defined the original generative capacity (the pretraining knowledge), leading to "model collapse" or hallucinations. Standard finetuning moves neurons arbitrarily in Euclidean space; optionally changing their lengths and altering all pairwise angles simultaneously. OFT introduces a strict structural constraint to prevent this destructive side effect.

Hyperspherical Energy is defined as:
$$ \text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j} \left\| \hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j \right\|^{-1} $$
where the Euclidean distance between two normalized vectors $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$ on the unit hypersphere corresponds to $\sqrt{2 - 2\cos(\theta_{ij})}$, with $\theta_{ij}$ being the angle between them. Consequently, minimizing $|\text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)|$ mathematically enforces that for every pair of neurons $(i, j)$ at layer $k$, the difference in their angles relative to all other neurons remains invariant compared to the pretrained state $\bm{W}^0$. In simpler terms, HE acts as a global measure of **neural manifold uniformity**; by keeping this sum constant, OFT ensures that while specific directions might shift to accommodate new prompts (finite flexibility), no pair of neurons moves closer or farther apart than they were originally.

This preservation is crucial for semantic generation because it maintains the **structural ignorance** that exists between different neurons in a high-dimensional space. For example, if Neuron $A$ historically encoded "dogs" and Neuron $B$ encoded "bathrooms," their distinct directions allowed Stable Diffusion v1.5 to generate coherent scenes where they coexist. If finetuning rotated these vectors so drastically that their angle changed from $90^\circ$ to nearly $0^\circ$, the model would lose its ability to distinguish between these concepts because the distance-based signal encoding in HE would be distorted. Furthermore, since HE depends only on pairwise distances between unit vectors ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ pairs), forcing this metric to remain conserved prevents any single update from collapsing more global relationships simultaneously than intended. Essentially, while unconstrained finetuning destroys the *context* established by pretraining via destructive angular shifts across all pairs at once, OFT modifies only one degree of freedom for every neuron while strictly preserving all other degrees of freedom regarding NORRIEL pairwise relationships.

Therefore, orthogonal transformation works as a finetuning mechanism because it separates angular modification from structural damage: it allows us to "re-torque" individual spoke-axles (neuron directions) without breaking the hub-and-spoke geometry (inter-neuron angles) that holds generative semantics together. The result is a model that has acquired new capabilities (subject specificity or controllability) through learned rotations while retaining an exact geometric blueprint of its original latent space topology.

### Can you clarify the exact relationship between Cayley Parameterization, block-diagonal matrices, and the determinant constraint in OFT?
Question:
In Section 3.2 (Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization), the paper introduces CIF parameterization: $\\bm{R}=(\\bm{I}+\\bm{Q})(I-\\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. It mentions this yields matrices with determinant 1 (Special Orthogonal Group). Later, it introduces block-diagonal matrices with $r$ blocks and claims this allows utilizing full orthogonal matrices while maintaining efficiency. Is there a conflict here? Does using block-diagonal matrices with Cayley parameterization allow us to reach determinants of -1 (reflections), or are we still restricted to rotations only? The paper says 'such a limitation does not affect performance', but from a theoretical standpoint for diffusion models where direction matters (up to sign), how does using only SO(d) vs O(d) impact the finetuning capability?
Answer:
There is no contradiction in the paper; rather, the block-diagonal structure is a parameterization strategy designed to improve computational and memory efficiency while still strictly enforcing the constraints of the Special Orthogonal Group ($SO(d)$). The Cayley parameterization formula $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^T(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, mathematically guarantees that any resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ has a determinant of exactly $1$. This means that even when applied to an $r$-block diagonal matrix structure, the transformation $\bm{W}^\top \to \bm{x}^\top (\bm{R}\cdot \bm{W}^0)$ is restricted to rotations only and cannot produce reflections ($\det = -1$).

The authors explicitly acknowledge this limitation in Section 3.2 ("Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization"), stating: "Such an efficiency comes at a small price -- the Cayley parameterization can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant 1 which belongs to the special orthogonal group." They further note that this constraint "does not affect the performance in practice." This design choice effectively limits the optimization landscape to a dense manifold of rotations. While theoretically, reflections allow for broader geometric transformations that rotations cannot mimic (e.g., mirroring), the empirical results in this research demonstrate that for text-to-image diffusion models, applying an orthogonal rotation that optimizes the hyperspherical energy difference is sufficient to achieve high-quality subject-driven and controllable generation. Consequently, finetuning might sometimes require a reflected transformation to achieve optimal visual results, but if such a reflection exists in the optimal solution space, finding it via rotation alone suggests that either the angular configuration found via optimization is geometrically equivalent via rotation, or that rotations are strictly more expressive than needed for this specific downstream task. Thus, restricting finetuning to $SO(d)$ does not fundamentally limit the model's ability to learn new semantics because the "needle in a haystack" search for relevant neuron angles is dense enough on the rotation manifold to find solutions superior to unparameterized random weights.

### How do we compare parameter efficiency between OFT and LoRA without fixing hyperparameters like alpha or rank?
Question:
In Section 3.2, Table 1 (LSN dummy name) shows an example comparing LoRA and OFT for a 128x128 layer: LoRA with r=8 has 2048 params (rank r times d + n), while OFT with r=8 has 960 params ($d * d / r^2$). Since standard practice uses $r = alpha * d$ for LoRA where alpha can be small like 0.0625 or large like 32, how should we fairly compare them if we vary alpha? If I set LoRA's alpha = 4, it would have huge params compared to fixed d/r=16 for OFT. Does this comparison implicitly assume specific lower bounds or upper bounds on ratio r/d for both methods?
Answer:
The comparison between Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and LoRA presented in the paper is grounded in their distinct mathematical structures and the way their hyperparameters scale with the dimension $d$ of the neural network layers. The core difference lies in how each method reduces the parameter count: LoRA relies on **low-rank approximation** of the residual difference between weights, whereas OFT relies on **block-diagonal orthogonality** constraints on a multiplicative rotation matrix.

In LoRA, the weight update is modeled as $\Delta \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$, where the number of parameters scales linearly with the rank $r$. For a layer of size $d \times d$ (neglecting bias or assuming specific baseline conditions for $n$), this results in a parameter count roughly proportional to $r \cdot d$. Consequently, if we set the hyperparameter ratio $\alpha = r/d$ to a constant (common practice where $r = \alpha d$), the total parameter count scales as $\mathcal{O}(d)$. Even with large values of $\alpha$ (e.g., 32), or small ones (e.g., 0.0625), fixing this ratio essentially normalizes LoRA's scaling behavior to be linear in $d$. The paper mentions that with $r = \alpha d$, both OFT and LoRA theoretically exhibit $\mathcal{O}(d)$ complexity regarding their trainable parameters relative to dimensionality, although LOFA has a constant overhead term ($dn$) which is often negligible for large networks compared to dominant layers.

In contrast, OFT learns a sparse orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ composed of $r$ blocks, where each block transforms a sub-space of dimension $d/r$. The number of parameters in such an orthogonal block matrix is derived from the dimension of the special orthogonal group, which has dimension $(k-1)k/2$. For a block of size $k=d/r$, this is $\frac{(d/r)(d/r-1)}{2} \approx \frac{d^2}{2r^2}$. Summing over $r$ blocks yields a total parameter count of approximately $\frac{r(d/r-1)}{2} \approx \frac{d}{2} - \frac{r}{2}$ before sharing blocks. If we further share all blocks so they are identical, the parameter count drops drastically to $\frac{(d/r)(d/r-1)}{2} \approx \frac{d^2}{2r^2}$ as stated in Equation~(13). This means ONT's parameter complexity is quadratic in dimension but divided by the square of the block parameter $r$, yielding an efficient scaling only if we treat effective rank as dependent on $d/r$.

The example provided in Table 1 ($128\times 128$ layer) illustrates this effectively: LoRA with rank 8 requires roughly $8 \times (128+128) = 4096$ weights for standard low-rank updates (though cited as 2048 likely due to specific symmetry or implementation details like having separate A and B matrices summed differently or specific constraints applied), while OFT with block rank 8 allows each block to act on size $16\times16$, requiring roughly 960 parameters given sharing. The key insight is that LoRA typically fixes a low absolute rank (e.g., 8) which keeps its complexity at $\mathcal{O}(rd)$ regardless of hyperparameter ratio choices within standard ranges, whereas OFT's efficiency comes directly from increasing $r$ along with increasing layer dimension $d$, maintaining a ratio that keeps total parameters at $\mathcal{O}(d)$ or even lower if blocks are shared. Thus, fair comparison requires ensuring that both methods operate under proportional scaling (constant $\alpha$ for LoRA relative to its rank scaling implicitly defined by projection noise tolerance for both methods) rather than comparing absolute fixed ranks without dimensional context. The paper ensures fairness by applying both methods to identical layers and discussing their convergence properties as independent measures rather than relying solely on parameter counts without considering how these grow with network architecture depth

### Does Rescaled Orthogonal Finetuning introduce any new constraints on hyperspherical energy, or does it truly preserve it by construction?
Question:
In Section 3.4 (Re-scaled OFT), they add a diagonal matrix D to finetune magnitudes via forward pass: z = ([bold R W0 D]) transpose x. They claim re-scaling neurons does not change HE because magnitude gets normalized out during cosine similarity calculations in HE definition. However, if D is learnable and independent across neurons during backpropagation, does this introduce non-orthogonal deformations that might occasionally push weights off-unit hypersphere temporarily before normalization? Or is HE defined strictly as sum of inverse distances ONLY ON unit hypersphere at prediction time insensible to pre-normalization intermediate values?
Answer:
The question of whether Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning (ROFT) introduces new constraints on hyperspherical energy is answered by the mathematical structure of the normalization process inherent in the Hyperspherical Energy (HE) definition. According to the paper, HE is strictly a function of the normalized directions of the neurons, defined as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$, where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ represents the $i$-th neuron normalized onto a unit hypersphere (Section 1).

Although the ROFT forward pass incorporates a learnable diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ with non-identity elements to scale individual neuron magnitudes (as noted in Section 3.4), this operation fundamentally does not alter the pairwise angular relationships between neurons. When computing the cosine similarity or Euclidean distance between two scaled neurons $\hat{\bm{p}} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}\theta_i) / \|\dots\|$ and $\hat{\bm{q}} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}\theta_j) / \|\dots\|$, the scalar magnitudes contributed by different components of $\bm{D}$ and any unifying scalar from $\|\bm{R}\|$ cancel out perfectly during the normalization step that produces $\hat{\boldsymbol w}$. Consequently, Sophus Lie group theory principles are not violated because HE is inherently insensitive to global or per-neuron scaling; it only queries the geometry on the unit hypersphere surface.

However, it is crucial to distinguish between theoretical definition and dynamic optimization flow. While HE itself remains unchanged at prediction time because it ignores pre-normalization intermediate values, ORTHOGONAL LINTEYNATURE guarantees orthogonality ($\boldsymbol R^T \boldsymbol R = I$), and diagonal scaling ($\boldsymbol D$) alters magnitudes without affecting angles. However, during backpropagation, learning rates guide changes in both $R$ and $D$. If training proceeds without projection steps explicitly enforcing orthogonality within gradient updates, numerical instability could theoretically cause deviations from strict unit spheres *during intermediate training states*, though the paper's use of Cayley parameterization for $R$ ensures strict orthogonality in all training steps. Since $D$ is free-flowing in both directions during optimization (unless additional explicit constraints are added), it could increase neuron magnitudes beyond saturation limits if weights were left unchecked for extended periods, even though this doesn't impact HE theory.
