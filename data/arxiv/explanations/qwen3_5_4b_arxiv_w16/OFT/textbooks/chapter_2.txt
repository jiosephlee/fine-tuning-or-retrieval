Chapter 2: # Why Neuron Angles Matter: The Physics of Deep Semantics

## Hyperspherical Similarity and Hyperuniformity in Neural Networks

To understand why the orientation of neurons—and not merely their magnitude—is critical for semantic representation, one must first examine the geometric distribution of neuron weights in high-dimensional spaces. A foundational insight in modern deep learning is that the direction of a neuron vector, often referred to as its angle, carries the vast majority of the stored semantic information, while the magnitude often serves as a less critical scaling factor. This observation suggests that training neural networks by constraining or optimizing the angular relationships between neurons can preserve or enhance generative capabilities more effectively than arbitrary weight updates.

A powerful theoretical framework to visualize this angular relationship is hyperspherical geometry. In a neural network layer, a set of neurons can be represented as vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$. The "hyperspherical similarity" between two neurons $i$ and $j$ is defined by the cosine similarity of their vectors: $\cos(\theta_{ij}) = \frac{\bm{w}_i^\top \bm{w}_j}{\|\bm{w}_i\| \|\bm{w}_j\|}$, where $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between them. When we project all neurons onto the unit hypersphere (normalizing them to have unit length), their pairwise similarities correspond exactly to their geodesic distances on the sphere's surface. The paper argues that an ideal, robust neural network representation exhibits "hyperuniformity," meaning the neurons are distributed in a way that minimizes redundancy while maintaining rich relational structure necessary for generalization.

The significance of this property becomes evident when considering how knowledge is encoded. If we assume that angles encode semantics, then any operation that preserves these angles should theoretically preserve the underlying knowledge of the network. This leads to the concept of hyperspherical energy (~HE~), a metric that quantifies how uniformly distributed a set of vectors are on the unit hypersphere. Specifically, hyperspherical energy is defined as the sum of inverse cosines (or other divergences) over all unique pairs of normalized directions:
$$ \text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \cos^{-1}\left( \frac{\hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j}{\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i\|_\infty} \right) $$
However, a more intuitive interpretation from the paper focuses on minimizing the deviation from an optimal hyperuniform state or maintaining consistency with it. A key finding presented is that if we apply an orthogonal transformation—a linear transformation where $\myR^\top\myR = \myR\myR^\top = \myI^\prime$—to all vectors in a layer simultaneously, every pairwise angle remains invariant. Mathematically, if $\myv_i' = \myR \cdot \myv_i^\prime$, then:
$$ (\yvec_i')^\top (\yvec_j') = (\myR\myv_i)^\top (\myR\yvec_j) = (\yvec_i)^\top (\yvec_j) $$
This implies that no matter how much we globally rotate or reflect all neurons at once via an orthogonal matrix ~$\vasive~$, their relative angular positions on the hypersphere remain identical. This invariance forms the bedrock argument for why Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is effective: it allows us to adapt neurons to new tasks (changing their absolute orientation in space) without destroying their relative structural integrity (preserving their angles), thus theoretically guaranteeing that semantic capacity associated with those angles is intact.

## Experimental Evidence: Angular Information vs. Magnitude in Autoencoders

To ground this theoretical framework in observable reality, the authors present empirical evidence demonstrating a stark contrast between ignoring and utilizing angular information for reconstruction tasks. The study employs a toy experiment using a standard convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images to isolate variables regarding feature extraction and reconstruction fidelity. The experiment creates three distinct scenarios for generating feature maps during inference by manipulating how inner products between learned kernels ($\ymap$) and input patches ($\mapx$) are computed: (a) computing both magnitude and direction information (ground truth style), (b) ignoring direction information and focusing solely on magnitudes, and (c) ignoring magnitude information and focusing solely on angular (directional) information.

The results are visually striking and definitive. When reconstructing flower images using only magnitude information—essentially discarding all directionality from the learned activations—the model fails completely to recover any recognizable visual features; what emerges are noise patterns or distorted blobs devoid of semantic content like petals or centers. Conversely, when extracting purely directional information—effectively normalizing all features onto a unit sphere regardless of their original strength—the model achieves near-perfect reconstruction quality of the original input flowers without requiring any cosine activation functions during training or inference phase adjustments previously applied elsewhere in similar literature.
