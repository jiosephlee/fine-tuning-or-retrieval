\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# ControlNet vs. The New Guard: Controlling Images Without Extra Code

Text-to-image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion have revolutionized creative workflows, allowing users to generate photorealistic images from simple text prompts. However, relying solely on text guidance often lacks the precision required for specific artistic directives. Enter **ControlNet**, a dominant solution that enables fine-grained control through additional inputs like segmentation maps or skeletal drawings. While ControlNet has been widely adopted, it comes with a significant drawback: it requires injecting extra neural adapters into the model architecture, increasing computational load and complicating deployment pipelines. A promising alternative emerging from recent research is **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, a method that promises to deliver comparable or superior control without altering the model's inference pipeline at all.

At the heart of OFT lies a fundamental insight regarding how neural networks store information. Rather than focusing on the magnitude of neuron weights, which tends to vary arbitrarily across training runs, researchers discovered that the *angles* between neurons—specifically their directional relationships on a unit hypersphere—are critical for encoding semantic meaning. In this view, changing the direction of neurons allows a model to adapt its behavior while maintaining its foundational knowledge base. Traditional finetuning methods often struggle to strike a balance between making necessary adjustments and preserving this core structure, frequently leading to degraded generation quality or unstable convergence. OFT addresses this by applying an orthogonal transformation to the pretrained weights. Mathematically, this ensures that the pairwise angles between all neurons remain invariant during training, effectively acting as a rigid rotation of the model's internal coordinate system rather than a distortion of it.

This structural preservation distinguishes OFT from established techniques like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) and ControlNet in several key ways. Methods such as LoRA rely on adding low-rank matrices to pretrained weights to inject new functionality. While parameter-efficient, this additive approach can sometimes shift the pretrained distribution too drastically, limiting the ceiling of achievable performance or causing instability during training cycles. Conversely, ControlNet operates by freezing the main diffusion model and training separate lightweight networks conditioned on user-provided signals; these signals are then concatenated before passing them through the frozen backbone. This design necessitates additional memory usage and inference steps every time an image is generated—a bottleneck for real-world applications requiring low latency or high throughput.

OFT sidesteps these limitations entirely by operating multiplicatively within the existing weight matrix itself. Instead of appending new layers or modules, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ that rotates the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ into new weights $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because this transformation happens internally within the standard feed-forward pass, there is absolutely no increase in computational overhead during inference time. The model functions identically to its pretrained counterpart in terms of hardware requirements and execution speed, yet it possesses enhanced controllability tailored to specific downstream tasks such as subject-driven generation or sketch-to-image translation.

Empirical evaluations suggest that OFT not only matches but exceeds current state-of-the-art methods in terms of stability and convergence speed. In experiments involving controllable generation tasks like converting Canny edge maps or segmentation masks into detailed images, OFT demonstrated robust alignment with input controls while maintaining high-fidelity image synthesis qualities previously lost in aggressive finetuning attempts. Furthermore, unlike some adaptive methods that degrade rapidly after extensive training epochs due to overfitting or catastrophic forgetting of general priors, OFT exhibits smooth convergence dynamics governed by its inherent orthogonality constraints.
