\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Can you clarify the exact relationship between Cayley Parameterization, block-diagonal matrices, and the determinant constraint in OFT?
Question:
In Section 3.2 (Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization), the paper introduces CIF parameterization: $\\bm{R}=(\\bm{I}+\\bm{Q})(I-\\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. It mentions this yields matrices with determinant 1 (Special Orthogonal Group). Later, it introduces block-diagonal matrices with $r$ blocks and claims this allows utilizing full orthogonal matrices while maintaining efficiency. Is there a conflict here? Does using block-diagonal matrices with Cayley parameterization allow us to reach determinants of -1 (reflections), or are we still restricted to rotations only? The paper says 'such a limitation does not affect performance', but from a theoretical standpoint for diffusion models where direction matters (up to sign), how does using only SO(d) vs O(d) impact the finetuning capability?
Answer:
There is no contradiction in the paper; rather, the block-diagonal structure is a parameterization strategy designed to improve computational and memory efficiency while still strictly enforcing the constraints of the Special Orthogonal Group ($SO(d)$). The Cayley parameterization formula $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^T(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, mathematically guarantees that any resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ has a determinant of exactly $1$. This means that even when applied to an $r$-block diagonal matrix structure, the transformation $\bm{W}^\top \to \bm{x}^\top (\bm{R}\cdot \bm{W}^0)$ is restricted to rotations only and cannot produce reflections ($\det = -1$).

The authors explicitly acknowledge this limitation in Section 3.2 ("Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization"), stating: "Such an efficiency comes at a small price -- the Cayley parameterization can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant 1 which belongs to the special orthogonal group." They further note that this constraint "does not affect the performance in practice." This design choice effectively limits the optimization landscape to a dense manifold of rotations. While theoretically, reflections allow for broader geometric transformations that rotations cannot mimic (e.g., mirroring), the empirical results in this research demonstrate that for text-to-image diffusion models, applying an orthogonal rotation that optimizes the hyperspherical energy difference is sufficient to achieve high-quality subject-driven and controllable generation. Consequently, finetuning might sometimes require a reflected transformation to achieve optimal visual results, but if such a reflection exists in the optimal solution space, finding it via rotation alone suggests that either the angular configuration found via optimization is geometrically equivalent via rotation, or that rotations are strictly more expressive than needed for this specific downstream task. Thus, restricting finetuning to $SO(d)$ does not fundamentally limit the model's ability to learn new semantics because the "needle in a haystack" search for relevant neuron angles is dense enough on the rotation manifold to find solutions superior to unparameterized random weights.
