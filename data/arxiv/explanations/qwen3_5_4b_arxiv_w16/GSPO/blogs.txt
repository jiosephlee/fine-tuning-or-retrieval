# Why LLM RL Training Hits a Wall: The GRPO Instability Explained

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the primary engine for pushing language models beyond simple pattern matching into sophisticated reasoning and coding capabilities. However, as we scale these models to compete at human and expert levels, a recurring bottleneck emerges: model collapse. This phenomenon, where the model's loss function diverges and it forgets learned skills, disproportionately affects current state-of-the-art algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). While GRPO was designed to solve the computational cost issues of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) by eliminating the need for a separate value network, it introduces a fundamental mathematical instability that prevents it from scaling indefinitely.

To understand why GRPO fails at scale, one must look beyond hyperparameter tuning and examine the core principle of importance sampling. Importance sampling is a statistical technique used to estimate the expectation of a function under a target distribution by re-weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution. Theoretically, this works reliably only when averaging over a massive number of independent samples to correct for the distributional mismatch between the old and new policies. In standard PPO, this averaging happens naturally over sequences sampled from an autoregressive process. However, recent failures in MoE (Mixture-of-Experts) models suggest that reliance on single-token re-weighting is insufficient to stabilize training dynamics in massive architectures.

The heart of the problem lies in how GRPO defines its gradient. Unlike PPO, which updates based on per-token importance ratios, GRPO computes relative advantages based on group comparisons but applies token-level importance weights. This creates a critical misalignment: the unit of reward is often assigned to the complete sequence rather than individual tokens. Consequently, when applying importance sampling at the token level without an equivalent amount of behavioral data to smooth out noise, high-variance gradients accumulate rapidly as generations lengthen. This noise amplifies with each step, eventually invalidating the estimated advantage function and leading to catastrophic divergence—a situation described as model collapse where resuming training becomes impossible even with perfect hyperparameter settings.

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) was proposed specifically to address this theoretical flaw by aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. Instead of weighting each token individually based on its local likelihood ratio, GSPO defines a sequence-level importance ratio derived from the overall likelihood of the entire response generated by both policies. By performing clipping, scoring, and optimization on these sequence-level weights rather than individual logits or tokens, GSPO significantly reduces variance in gradients $ \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} $. This shift ensures that the re-weighting effect is robust against the sheer volume of tokens generated during long reasoning chains and mitigates the noise accumulation that plagues GRPO in deep models.

Moreover, GSPO demonstrates remarkable stability during Mixture-of-Experts training, an environment notoriously difficult for standard RL algorithms due in part to "expert-activation volatility." In traditional approaches like Routing Replay used with GRPO for MoE models, experts activated during generation must be cached and replayed to maintain consistent importance ratios across updates; if not handled artificially, random expert activations cause token-level weights to fluctuate wildly. Indeed GSPO obviates this need entirely because sequence likelihood focuses on overall generation quality rather than specific token probabilities influenced by temporary routing changes. By removing this dependency on complex stabilization tricks and avoiding precision mismatches common between training inference engines like Megatron versus SGLang tools GSPO allows for direct use of inference-generated likelihoods for optimization which makes partial rollouts or multi-turn fine-tuning substantially more feasible by using only sequence-level likelihoods which are inherently more tolerant of precision discrepancies we typically encounter when scaling LLMs through RL



# GSPO: The New Game Changer in LLM Alignment

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the engine driving the rapid scaling of large language models (LLMs), enabling them to tackle complex reasoning tasks. However, this scaling has hit a hard wall. Current state-of-the-art algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) are plagued by severe instability. When training massive models, these algorithms often suffer from "catastrophic and irreversible model collapse," a phenomenon where the model's capabilities disappear permanently. Researchers have identified that this instability stems not from poor engineering, but from a fundamental flaw in how importance sampling is applied. This gap is now being filled by Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), an algorithm that stabilizes training with remarkable efficiency.

The core problem with existing methods like GRPO lies in the granularity of its optimization objective. In RL training, we generate responses from an old policy to evaluate and improve them against a new policy. To measure success, we typically compare the probability of specific tokens within those responses. However, GRPO applies importance ratios at the token level. It calculates how much more likely each individual token was under the new policy compared to the old one and weighs the gradient updates based on these micro-level probabilities. The paper argues that this approach is theoretically misapplied because importance sampling should average over many samples to correct for distributional mismatch, yet GRPO relies on single-token probabilities which introduce high-variance noise. This noise accumulates over long sequences, destabilizing training and leading to collapse.

GSPO addresses this by shifting the unit of optimization from the token to the sequence. Instead of weighing individual tokens, GSPO defines an importance ratio based on the likelihood of the entire response $y$. It uses a geometric mean to calculate this sequence-level ratio:

$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$

By aligning the unit of optimization (the sequence) with the unit of reward (the entire response), GSPO eliminates the accumulation of unstable noise found in token-level approaches. The graphical equivalent might be struggling with a flickering screen caused by pixel-level interference; GSPO essentially replaces that cracked filter with one that smooths out visual artifacts across an entire image at once, resulting in a sharp and stable presentation. This shift makesGradient estimation rewards highly converged even when dealing with long-form generation tasks where token probabilities can be notoriously elusive.

The practical impact of this theoretical shift is profound, particularly regarding training stability and efficiency. Experiments conducted using GSPO demonstrate drastically improved performance compared to GRPO across standard benchmarks like AIME'24 and LiveCodeBench. Crucially, GSPO achieves superior sample efficiency; it reaches higher accuracy and benchmark scores while consuming fewer compute resources than GRPO for equivalent tasks. In another significant finding observed in the research, GSPO allows for continuous performance improvement as training time progresses without hitting diminishing returns or hitting ceiling limits associated with catastrophic collapse seen in previous algorithms.

Perhaps most notably, GSPO offers a breakthrough for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, which integrate different specialized layers within a single network architecture but only activate subsets during inference. Training MoE models under RL is notoriously difficult due to "expert-activation volatility," where different experts are activated across gradient updates even for identical prompts, rendering token-level metrics meaningless or useless guidance signals. To fix this volatility under GRPO, researchers had to employ a complex stabilization strategy called Routing Replay, which caches and reuses routing modes during training to artificially stabilize importance ratios but slows down training speeds due to increased memory overheads and communication costs. GSPO completely obviates this need by focusing only on sequence likelihood rather than individual token likelihoods, making it inherently insensitive to expert activation volatility. # MoE Models Finally Can Scale Without Complex Workarounds

The road to superintelligent AI has increasingly relied on the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture, where a single model dynamically activates a subset of specialized sub-networks to handle different tasks. This approach allows us to achieve massive parameter counts without the prohibitive memory and compute costs associated with storing every parameter in flight during training. However, this efficiency comes with a razor-thin stability margin. Improving an MoE model via Reinforcement Learning (RL) is notoriously difficult, often because minute fluctuations in expert activation patterns can invalidate the learning signals required for scalability.

Historically, training MoE models in RL required elaborate stabilization techniques known as "Routing Replay." When using algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), researchers had to cache the specific set of experts activated for a sample and force the model to reuse that exact routing configuration when recalculating importance ratios. This effectively created a "frozen" routing layer that prevented the dynamic routing mechanism from changing between updates. While this hack prevented catastrophic training collapse, it came at a steep price: significant extra memory overhead for storing cached routes and communication costs to persist these states across devices. Furthermore, artificially freezing the routing logic limited the model's ability to fully explore its capacity, potentially capping its final performance ceiling.

The solution lies in Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), a new algorithm that fundamentally changes how stability is achieved in RL training. Unlike previous methods that rely on token-level importance sampling—which amplifies noise when small changes in expert activation alter the probability distribution of a single token—GSPO operates entirely at the sequence level. It calculates importance ratios based on the likelihood of the entire response $y$ given a query $x$, rather than summing up weighted tokens. Mathematically, this means GSPO uses an importance weight defined as $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$.

This shift is critical for MoE models because it decouples optimization from volatile routing decisions. As long as an MoE model maintains its core language modeling capability and generates syntactically valid sequences, the aggregate sequence likelihood does not fluctuate drastically due to internal routing shuffling within each step of inference. By focusing on sequence-likelihood and applying clipping constraints to whole responses rather than individual tokens, GSPO renders it insensitive to the expert-activation volatility that plagued GRPO-based training. Consequently, GSPO eliminates the need for complex infrastructure hacks like Routing Replay entirely.

The empirical results support this theoretical advantage unequivocally. In benchmarks involving large MoE models, GSPO demonstrated notably superior training stability compared to GRPO equipped with Routing Replay strategies. Most significantly, it achieved continuous performance improvement across scaling dimensions without collapsing or requiring periodic resets of hyperparameters like clipping ranges. Moreover, GSPO offers additional benefits beyond stability for general RL infrastructure; by relying on sequence-level likelihoods rather than token-level probabilities, it becomes much more tolerant of precision discrepancies between training and inference engines. This allows practitioners to leverage likelihood estimates directly from inference engines without re-computing them within expensive training loops, simplifying distributed training frameworks significantly.

In essence, GSPO provides a robust algorithmic foundation that removes artificial constraints from MoE training. It proves that we no longer need to freeze our models' most dynamic components just to learn how to use them better. With GSPO as its new cornerstone for large-scale reinforcement learning, we are finally approaching an era where highly efficient architectures can be safely pushed toward their theoretical limits without fragile workarounds holding them back.



# From Token-Clipping to Sequence-Clipping: A Revolution in Hill Climbing

Training large language models using reinforcement learning (RL) is akin to navigating a vast, rugged landscape in search of the highest peak. For years, researchers have relied on algorithms designed to explore this terrain with a specific method of "climbing": modifying the AI's predictions one token at a time. This approach, exemplified by Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), introduced a mechanism to filter out poor decisions by clipping the importance of individual tokens. While this helped manage unstable training dynamics, it introduced a fundamental inefficiency: the algorithm attacked noise at the wrong level of granularity, leading to high-variance gradients and frequent model collapse. The arrival of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) marks a paradigm shift, moving the constraints and adjustments from individual tokens to entire sequences. By aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward, GSPO achieves unprecedented stability and efficiency, particularly for large and complex models like Mixture-of-Experts architectures.

To understand why shifting from token-level clipping to sequence-level clipping matters, we must first look at how importance sampling works in this context. In RL, we want to estimate what would have happened if our model had made different choices. Costly samples are those that deviate significantly from what we already know (the old policy). Originally, GRPO calculated these deviations for every single token in a generated response. If a model generated a unique token that its previous self would rarely produce, that token received a low importance score. While intuitive, this method suffers from high variance. When optimizing across long sequences or models with sparse structure, small errors in these per-token weights accumulate rapidly. The result is training noise that can eventually drown out valuable learning signals, causing catastrophic failures where the model forgets what it learned before collapse becomes irreversible. GSPO resolves this by recognizing that rewards are given for the entire sequence $y$. Therefore, it is more logical to calculate an importance weight based on the likelihood of the whole sequence $y$, denoted as $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x)}{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. This sequence-level metric acts as a robust indicator of how far a specific response deviates from our baseline policy without introducing the erratic fluctuations seen at the token level.

The operational difference between GSPO and its predecessor GRPO lies in their clipping strategies and their resulting impact on hardware utilization. GRPO clips individual tokens within groups to prevent extreme updates on unlikely next-token choices. In practice, this meant that even if most of a sequence was reasonable because it led to a good final outcome but contained one glitched token early on, that single glitch could theoretically degrade gradient estimates if it wasn't clipped severely enough or if it caused downstream instability. Conversely, GSPO applies clipping thresholds directly to entire sequences $s_i(\theta)$ before applying them during optimization via Equation 13 in the original paper ($\text{clip}\left(s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon}\right)$). This effectively discards any entire response whose aggregate deviation is too large before viewing any part of it further during training. Surprisingly, this behavior leads to higher training efficiency; because GSPO filters out "bad" responses entirely rather than wasting computation trying to correct noisy gradients on poorly sampled tokens within acceptable responses, it maximizes hardware utilization during division-consumption budgeted batches. The paper notes that while GRPO might clip fewer overall tokens due to its per-token logic often allowing partial convergence within groups, GSPO clips significantly more whole responses yet produces cleaner gradients and faster convergence rates. This suggests that token-level fixes are noisier than sequence-level rejections for long-response tasks parsed into multiple mini-batches for updates.

This architectural advantage proves particularly critical when training advanced sparse models known as Mixture-of-Experts (MoE). In MoE models, not all layers are active simultaneously; instead, some "experts" are routed based on input features. Training such models via standard RL pipelines has historically been unstable because changes in parameters can drastically alter routing decisions between steps—a phenomenon termed "expert-activation volatility."
