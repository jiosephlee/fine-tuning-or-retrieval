\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# MoE Models Finally Can Scale Without Complex Workarounds

The road to superintelligent AI has increasingly relied on the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture, where a single model dynamically activates a subset of specialized sub-networks to handle different tasks. This approach allows us to achieve massive parameter counts without the prohibitive memory and compute costs associated with storing every parameter in flight during training. However, this efficiency comes with a razor-thin stability margin. Improving an MoE model via Reinforcement Learning (RL) is notoriously difficult, often because minute fluctuations in expert activation patterns can invalidate the learning signals required for scalability.

Historically, training MoE models in RL required elaborate stabilization techniques known as "Routing Replay." When using algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), researchers had to cache the specific set of experts activated for a sample and force the model to reuse that exact routing configuration when recalculating importance ratios. This effectively created a "frozen" routing layer that prevented the dynamic routing mechanism from changing between updates. While this hack prevented catastrophic training collapse, it came at a steep price: significant extra memory overhead for storing cached routes and communication costs to persist these states across devices. Furthermore, artificially freezing the routing logic limited the model's ability to fully explore its capacity, potentially capping its final performance ceiling.

The solution lies in Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), a new algorithm that fundamentally changes how stability is achieved in RL training. Unlike previous methods that rely on token-level importance sampling—which amplifies noise when small changes in expert activation alter the probability distribution of a single token—GSPO operates entirely at the sequence level. It calculates importance ratios based on the likelihood of the entire response $y$ given a query $x$, rather than summing up weighted tokens. Mathematically, this means GSPO uses an importance weight defined as $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$.

This shift is critical for MoE models because it decouples optimization from volatile routing decisions. As long as an MoE model maintains its core language modeling capability and generates syntactically valid sequences, the aggregate sequence likelihood does not fluctuate drastically due to internal routing shuffling within each step of inference. By focusing on sequence-likelihood and applying clipping constraints to whole responses rather than individual tokens, GSPO renders it insensitive to the expert-activation volatility that plagued GRPO-based training. Consequently, GSPO eliminates the need for complex infrastructure hacks like Routing Replay entirely.

The empirical results support this theoretical advantage unequivocally. In benchmarks involving large MoE models, GSPO demonstrated notably superior training stability compared to GRPO equipped with Routing Replay strategies. Most significantly, it achieved continuous performance improvement across scaling dimensions without collapsing or requiring periodic resets of hyperparameters like clipping ranges. Moreover, GSPO offers additional benefits beyond stability for general RL infrastructure; by relying on sequence-level likelihoods rather than token-level probabilities, it becomes much more tolerant of precision discrepancies between training and inference engines. This allows practitioners to leverage likelihood estimates directly from inference engines without re-computing them within expensive training loops, simplifying distributed training frameworks significantly.

In essence, GSPO provides a robust algorithmic foundation that removes artificial constraints from MoE training. It proves that we no longer need to freeze our models' most dynamic components just to learn how to use them better. With GSPO as its new cornerstone for large-scale reinforcement learning, we are finally approaching an era where highly efficient architectures can be safely pushed toward their theoretical limits without fragile workarounds holding them back.
