# Chapter 1: The Current Landscape of RLHF in Large Language Models

## Introduction to RLHF and the Shift from Token-Level to Sequence-Level Optimization

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has become the cornerstone paradigm for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human intent, enabling these systems to tackle sophisticated tasks such as competitive mathematics, complex code generation, and multi-step logical reasoning. The journey toward these capabilities began with traditional Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), where models learned high-quality instruction-following behaviors by mimicking expert responses. However, open-ended tasks often require exploration and iterative improvement that SFT alone cannot provide. This is where Reinforcement Learning takes center stage. Modern RL algorithms typically employ Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a clipped-reinforcement-learning objective that seeks to maximize rewards while constraining the policy update to remain within a proximal region of the previous policy. This constraint prevents drastic shifts in behavior that could destabilize training or degrade performance on previously learned skills.

However, as models scale toward trillions of parameters, extending the context window, and reasoning over longer sequences, the standard approach faces a critical bottleneck: training stability. In this chapter, we establish the necessity for robust training dynamics not just as a desire for efficiency, but as a fundamental prerequisite for scaling intelligence. We will review how standard RLHF architectures—specifically Dense models and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)—are structured to handle these demands. We will discuss the role of the human verifier in assigning rewards and calculating advantages within group settings. Crucially, we will examine Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which eliminated the need for an external value model by computing relative advantages among multiple responses generated per query.

Yet, despite GRPO's architectural elegance, it has proven insufficiently stable when applied to gigantic models, long-context tasks, or sparse MoE structures. This chapter will delve into the fundamental causes of this instability: the misapplication of token-level importance sampling weights that fail to account for distributional shifts in off-policy learning scenarios. By understanding why token-level corrections introduce high-variance noise that accumulates catastrophically over sequence length, we lay the theoretical groundwork for next-generation algorithms like Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). Finally, we will explore the unique challenges posed by MoE models, where sparsity-induced expert volatility renders standard token-level importance ratios increasingly invalid after mere gradient updates, creating a complex environment where simplified aggregation techniques offer a path back to convergence.

## The Human Verifier: Scoring Responses and Preference Learning

Unlike early alignment methods that relied on synthetic filters like Reinforcement Learning with Model-as-Judge (RLM) or static reward functions, current state-of-the-art approaches utilize proxy reward functions trained via supervised fine-tuning on datasets containing human judgments. A response $y$ generated by the policy $\pi_\theta$ for a given query $x$ is passed through this verifier $r$, which outputs a scalar reward $r(x,y) \in [0, 1]$.

The verifier acts as an oracle within a black-box feedback loop that is inaccessible—or barely intelligible—to itself during training iterations after it converges. # Chapter 2: Theoretical Foundations of GSPO

Modern reinforcement learning for large language models (LLMs) stands at a critical crossroads between capability and stability. While the artificial intelligence boom has been fueled by scaling laws, the trajectory of this boom relies fundamentally on the robustness of the training dynamics. Previous state-of-the-art algorithms, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), have paved the way for sophisticated reasoning capabilities yet have suffered from severe instability mechanisms that can lead to irreversible model collapse. These failures are deeply rooted in the mathematical structure of policy optimization, specifically in how importance sampling weights are constructed and applied in autoregressive settings. This chapter provides a rigorous theoretical derivation of our Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) framework. We begin by establishing the mathematical notation for autoregressive language modeling and defining the score functions used to evaluate response quality. We then deconstruct the theoretical limitations of token-level importance sampling, demonstrating why applying off-policy corrections at the granularity of individual tokens violates the principles of expected value estimation in long-horizon tasks. Finally, we derive the optimal objective function that aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward, introducing the sequence-likelihood ratio and proving its stability compared to its token-level predecessor.

## Probabilistic Language Modeling: Likelihoods and Autoregressive Generation

To understand GSPO, one must first grasp the precise mechanical definition of how an autoregressive language model generates text and assigns probabilities to sequences. In this paper, we denote an autoregressive language model with parameters $\theta$ as a policy $\pi_\theta$. The domain of interest consists of queries $x$ drawn from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ and their corresponding responses $y$. We adopt standard notation where $x$ represents a prompt or query string, while $y$ represents a sequence of tokens generated in response to that query. The length of a sequence $y$, denoted as $|y|$, corresponds to the number of tokens contained within it.

The core mechanism of language modeling is sequential dependency; each token's probability is conditioned not only on its immediate antecedent text but also on all previous tokens in the current generation path. Specifically, if $y_{<t}$ denotes the substring preceding token $y_t$, then $\pi_\theta(y | x)$ is defined as the product of individual conditional probabilities at every time step $t$ from 1 to $|y|$. Formally, we define:
$$ \pi_\theta(y | x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t}) $$
This equation captures the fundamental nature of deterministic generation risks under stochastic policies; if any single token prediction has low probability, its direct contribution renders the entire sequence score exponentially lower. This multiplicative structure poses a specific challenge for reinforcement learning: a small error or high variance in any single conditional probability term $p(y_t | x, y_{<t})$ can disproportionately skew the aggregate likelihood $\pi_\theta(y | x)$. Understanding how these individual likelihoods combine into a global sequence score is essential because GSPO fundamentally relies on treating this global aggregate—the sequence likelihood—as the sole metric for distributional correction during optimization. Thus, our attention shifts from solving problems at step size one (per token) to solving problems over an entire horizon (per generated block), necessitating a re-evaluation of how we weight historical data against current parameters.

## Principles of Importance Sampling: Why Token-Level Weights Fail

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or similar reward-maximization paradigms often falls within an off-policy learning setting because actions (tokens) are sampled from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, while gradients are computed with respect to an updated policy $\pi_\theta$. To compensate for this distributional mismatch without memorizing all past data, algorithms rely on importance sampling (IS). The theoretical bedrock of IS is that we can estimate an expectation under a target distribution by re-weighting samples taken from a behavior distribution
$$ \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}}\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z)\right] $$
Here, $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ acts as an importance weight that corrects for drawing samples with undesirable densities. For this principle to hold rigorously in RL algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), two conditions are typically required: sufficient sample size ($N \gg 1$) averaging out rare events where weights explode or vanish near zero or infinity before clipping intervention occurs; and precise mathematical alignment where every element being optimized contributes directly to correcting exactly one element being rewarded
$$ \text{unit}(reward) \approx \text{unit}(objective). $$
However, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) systematically violates these principles when applied to long-form generation tasks involving mixture-of-experts architectures
$$ w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}. $$
By defining weights at token level ($t=1...|y|$), GRPO attempts to adjust expectations one token at a time without averaging over multiple samples drawn from equivalent conditional distributions at each step; instead it adjusts based on just one sample per step leading to higher variance noise accumulation . Furthermore most importantly GRPO creates a catastrophic misalignment between reward units and objective units since rewards typically measure performance across entire outputs not individual steps leading to unreliable gradient estimates especially pronounced when models become increasingly deterministic or exhibit extreme sparsity like MoE models Finally this sequential adjustment creates additional noise that propogates forward through time exacerbating whatever issues exist at each step thereby creating unpredictable consequences as training progresses which is why we need GSPO which directly uses sequence likelihoods instead

# Chapter 3: The GSPO Algorithm Architecture

## The GSPO Objective Function: Equation Review and Interpretation

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) represents a fundamental architectural shift from previous reinforcement learning algorithms, most notably Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), by redefining the unit of optimization to match the unit of reward. In standard language generation tasks, rewards are assigned to completed sequences based on their utility, correctness, or alignment quality. However, prior algorithms like GRPO attempted to apply importance sampling corrections at the token level. This approach was fundamentally flawed because it treated the distributional mismatch between an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and a new policy $\pi_\theta$ using data points that were insufficient to estimate the correction. Specifically, GRPO utilized a token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ calculated for a single token $y_{i,t}$ conditional on its prefix. As discussed in theoretical frameworks for importance sampling, estimating the expectation under a target distribution requires averaging over multiple samples from the behavior distribution to stabilize the variance of the weights. By relying on a single sample trajectory at each token position, GRPO introduced high-variance noise into the gradient estimates. This noise accumulates with increasing sequence length and is further exacerbated by optimization dynamics, frequently leading to catastrophic model collapse where training becomes unstable and performance degrades irreversibly.

The core insight driving GSPO is that the unit of optimization must align with the unit of reward to ensure stable learning dynamics. Since rewards are defined for entire responses $y_i$ given a query $x$, GSPO shifts its focus to sequence-level importance ratios. Instead of correcting off-policy discrepancies token-by-token, GSPO computes how much the probability of generating an entire response $y_i$ has changed when moving from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ to $\pi_\theta$. This alignment ensures that the correction applied to the gradients accurately reflects the deviation that led to or prevented a successful outcome. The objective function formalizes this philosophy:

$$
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min \left( s_{i}(\theta)  \widehat{A}_{i},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i} \right) 
\right]
$$

In this equation, $G$ denotes the group size, representing a batch of responses generated by consulting $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ in response to individual queries $x$ drawn from a query distribution $\mathcal{D}$. The term inside the summation applies a clipped probability ratio scaled by an estimated advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. Unlike traditional PPO which relies on absolute advantage estimates requiring value networks, GSPO leverages relative advantages computed within each group. Crucially, this backbone structure remains agnostic to architecture type; it does not require a separate critic network or external value estimator. The algorithm operates entirely on generated outputs and reward scores derived from verifiers or feedback mechanisms.

The definition of the importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ is where GSPO diverges significantly from its predecessors. Rather than computing separate ratios for every token in a sequence, GSPO defines a unified weight for the entire response:

$$
s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}
$$

It is essential to understand the intuition behind taking the geometric mean—the log-sum-exp term exponentiated back—by dividing by $|y_i|$. Without this normalization, sequences that are only one token long would exert orders of magnitude more influence on gradients than sequences consisting of hundreds or thousands of tokens, assuming identical raw likelihood ratios per token. By normalizing over sequence length ($|y_i|$), GSPO ensures that all groups contribute equally regardless of how long their responses are varied across different tasks or model phases. Furthermore, while one might consider raising this ratio to some power other than $1/|y_i|$ for robustness against degenerate cases where different tokens have vastly different probabilities under new policies compared to old policies (e.g., $\prod p_t^\alpha$), empirical analysis suggests that exponentiating via geometric mean alone provides stable performance without significant loss in efficacy for scaling language models effectively.

# Chapter 4: Stability, Efficiency, and Inference Integration

## Counter-Intuitive Clipping Efficiency: Larger Failsures Mean Better Signals

One of the most profound insights from Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is a counter-intuitive phenomenon regarding its clipping mechanism and training efficiency. Unlike the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm, which traditionally clips individual tokens to manage off-policy variance, GSPO operates on a sequence-level basis. By applying the clipping functor $\mathrm{clip}(s_{i}(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)$ to the entire importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ rather than the gradients of individual tokens, GSPO inherently triggers a much higher fraction of token-level clipping. Empirical analyses observed in large-scale training benchmarks reveal that GSPO can result in two orders of magnitude fewer effective training tokens compared to GRPO under equivalent hyperparameter configurations. Specifically, while GRPO aims to preserve high-variance per-token gradients by clipping only outliers per token position, GSPO's sequence-level aggregation prepares a "filter" that rejects entire responses deemed sufficiently off-policy before the gradient is ever computed with respect to those tokens.

At first glance, this massive reduction in contributing samples appears detrimental to sample efficiency; intuitively one would expect that learning from fewer tokens would inevitably degrade performance or train slower. However, the data demonstrates that GSPO consistently achieves superior training accuracy and benchmark performance precisely because these fewer tokens are effectively *all* good quality. The act of discarding responses via sequence-level clipping removes noise that propagates across every single token within the response simultaneously. In contrast, GRPO's noise accumulation is granular: even a single highly determined (over-weighted) or under-weighted token contributes a skewed gradient signal that slowly corrupts the update vector of every subsequent layer until the corruption becomes catastrophic. Because GSPO identifies and eliminates the source of this corruption—the entire invalid response trajectory—it ensures that every single token utilized for gradient computation resides within the stable cluster near the optimal likelihood surface. Thus, despite processing fewer total tokens per step (due to longer generation times potentially increasing generation counts before loss), the *signal-to-noise ratio* per training example is vastly superior in GSPO. The "failsures" are larger in scale but cleaner in nature, allowing for aggressive sampling rates without instability, which paradoxically translates to better overall hardware utilization and faster empirical convergence when considering runtime spent on valid updates versus wasted computation on noisy signals.

## Noise Reduction in Long-Response Training Scenarios

The stability issues inherent in training large language models often stem from extending response lengths beyond standard dialogue constraints into complex multi-step reasoning tasks. Mathematical analysis reveals that noise variance in reinforcement learning objectives typically scales with response length if key components are additive or multiplicative across unaligned positions. In GRPO, the importance ratio for each token $y_{i,t}$ is defined as $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$. Because these ratios are multiplied by their respective advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to form the gradient contribution for a specific token $t$, any errorEstimation in $w_{i,t}$ directly translates into error accumulation as more tokens are processed linearly through the forward pass and backward propagation. When innovations occur deep within a long sequence—such as unexpected model collapse at step 500 out of 5000—the locally determined importance weight at token 500 might fluctuate wildly due to distributional shifts at earlier steps (steps 1 through 499). Since GRPO computes an independent importance weight for each position based solely on local conditions at that step without referencing global behavior consistency, these local fluctuations compound over time, creating high-variance gradients that destabilize microbbakas or update parameters erratically in directions detrimental to long-term coherence.

GSPO resolves this structural vulnerability by shifting the unit of optimization from masked temporal positions ($t$) to global events ($y$). The importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ aggregates all historical observations into a logarithmic average across the entire sequence length $|y_i|$, normalized by this length itself as $s_i(\theta) = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log w_{i,t} \right)$. This mathematical formulation forces an averaging mechanism over all temporal steps for each response sample before any gradient weighting occurs. By averaging $\log w_t$ uniformly over $t=1$ to $|y|$ and then exponentiating back to obtain a scalar weight assigned equally to all $\nabla \log \pi_t$, GSPO effectively dilutes local stochastic anomalies into smooth global signals. A significant spike in likelihood decay at one point cannot dominate the weight calculation for subsequent neurons as severely as it does in token-sequence multiplication models like GRPO where corrections factorize locally. This structural smoothing mitigates variance growth as sequence length increases linearly rather than multiplicatively or exponentially relative to task complexity.
