\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does GSPO define the importance ratio as a geometric mean rather than a simple product?
Question:
In Section 5.1, the paper defines the importance ratio for GSPO as $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. The term inside the parenthesis is the ratio of sequence likelihoods. 

However, earlier in the paper (Section 3 and Equation ~1), the standard importance weight for token-level PPO is defined as $w_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t | x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t | x, y_{<t})}$. If we simply applied maximum likelihood estimation to a sequence, would we expect the unnormalized objective to be proportional to $\prod_t w_t$? 

By taking the $|y_i|$-th root, GSPO effectively treats every token's contribution to the importance weight equally. Is this transformation necessary to prevent numerical instability caused by very long sequences where standard products might underflow to zero? Or does it fundamentally change the interpretation of how 'off-policy' we are for each token?
Answer:
The geometric mean, defined as $s_i(\theta) = |y_i|^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log r(y_{i,t})$, is used in GSPO to fundamentally resolve two distinct problems: severe numerical instability (catastrophic gradient growth) and theoretical misalignment with the reward signal. While a standard product (equivalent to the raw exponent before normalization) technically preserves the logarithmic likelihood ratio correctly, its magnitude explodes or vanishes too quickly for long sequences, breaking floating-point arithmetic. More critically, taking the $|y_i|$-th root ensures that the importance weight remains bounded regardless of sequence length, which aligns with the paper's core thesis.

In Section 2, PPO uses token-level weights $w_t(\theta)$ which can deviate significantly from unity if a single token is generated with vastly different probability under $\pi_\theta$ versus $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. When these weights are multiplied across $|y_i|$ tokens in a standard unnormalized product (the geometric mean without the root), the variance of the estimator grows drastically as sequence length increases. The paper explicitly states in Section 3 that this misapplication leads to "high-variance training noise that progressively accumulates with increased response length." For long responses common in language models, a simple product of many such weights will almost certainly underflow to zero or overflow to infinity due to finite-precision arithmetic. The $|y_i|$-th root effectively normalizes this product by raising it to a power that scales with length ($|y_i|^0 = 1$ per token), keeping the weight's magnitude comparable across short and long sequences. As noted in Equation ~4, this step is essential "to reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range."

Furthermore, this transformation is theoretically necessary because GSPO operates at the sequence level rather than the token level. In GRPO (Equation ~5), each token carries an advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$, allowing fine-grained adjustments based on which specific tokens are advantageous or disadvantageous within a group of responses. In GSPO (Equation ~6), all tokens in a response share a single advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ derived from the total sequence reward. Consequently, weighting each token differently by its individual probability ratio contradicts the assumption that all tokens contribute equally to the sequence-level signal; it would introduce arbitrary noise into tokens whose objectives are not individually tracked by the advantage estimate. By using the geometric mean, GSPO treats every token's contribution to corrective scaling equally, ensuring that the importance correction applied to gradients reflects only how well the *entire* sequence was generated according to policy consistency relative to its group reward. This aligns with Equation ~8 and ~10, where the gradient for GSPO features a constant scalar multiplier $s_i(\theta)$ preceding an average of equal weights over tokens, whereas GRPO's gradient uses varying importance weights directly attached to individual gradients.

Thus, while applying maximum likelihood estimation would indeed expect an objective proportional to $\prod_t w_t$, converting this product into its geometric mean via normalization is not merely a numerical trick but a theoretical requirement imposed by shifting from localized token constraints (GRPO) to global sequence constraints (GSPO). It prevents numerical collapse caused by extreme weights and ensures that the clipping mechanism remains effective across diverse response lengths without requiring per-token advantage estimates.
