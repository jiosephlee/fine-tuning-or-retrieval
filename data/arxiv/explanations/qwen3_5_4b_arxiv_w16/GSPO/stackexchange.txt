\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does GSPO define the importance ratio as a geometric mean rather than a simple product?
Question:
In Section 5.1, the paper defines the importance ratio for GSPO as $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. The term inside the parenthesis is the ratio of sequence likelihoods. 

However, earlier in the paper (Section 3 and Equation ~1), the standard importance weight for token-level PPO is defined as $w_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t | x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t | x, y_{<t})}$. If we simply applied maximum likelihood estimation to a sequence, would we expect the unnormalized objective to be proportional to $\prod_t w_t$? 

By taking the $|y_i|$-th root, GSPO effectively treats every token's contribution to the importance weight equally. Is this transformation necessary to prevent numerical instability caused by very long sequences where standard products might underflow to zero? Or does it fundamentally change the interpretation of how 'off-policy' we are for each token?
Answer:
The geometric mean, defined as $s_i(\theta) = |y_i|^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log r(y_{i,t})$, is used in GSPO to fundamentally resolve two distinct problems: severe numerical instability (catastrophic gradient growth) and theoretical misalignment with the reward signal. While a standard product (equivalent to the raw exponent before normalization) technically preserves the logarithmic likelihood ratio correctly, its magnitude explodes or vanishes too quickly for long sequences, breaking floating-point arithmetic. More critically, taking the $|y_i|$-th root ensures that the importance weight remains bounded regardless of sequence length, which aligns with the paper's core thesis.

In Section 2, PPO uses token-level weights $w_t(\theta)$ which can deviate significantly from unity if a single token is generated with vastly different probability under $\pi_\theta$ versus $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. When these weights are multiplied across $|y_i|$ tokens in a standard unnormalized product (the geometric mean without the root), the variance of the estimator grows drastically as sequence length increases. The paper explicitly states in Section 3 that this misapplication leads to "high-variance training noise that progressively accumulates with increased response length." For long responses common in language models, a simple product of many such weights will almost certainly underflow to zero or overflow to infinity due to finite-precision arithmetic. The $|y_i|$-th root effectively normalizes this product by raising it to a power that scales with length ($|y_i|^0 = 1$ per token), keeping the weight's magnitude comparable across short and long sequences. As noted in Equation ~4, this step is essential "to reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range."

Furthermore, this transformation is theoretically necessary because GSPO operates at the sequence level rather than the token level. In GRPO (Equation ~5), each token carries an advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$, allowing fine-grained adjustments based on which specific tokens are advantageous or disadvantageous within a group of responses. In GSPO (Equation ~6), all tokens in a response share a single advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ derived from the total sequence reward. Consequently, weighting each token differently by its individual probability ratio contradicts the assumption that all tokens contribute equally to the sequence-level signal; it would introduce arbitrary noise into tokens whose objectives are not individually tracked by the advantage estimate. By using the geometric mean, GSPO treats every token's contribution to corrective scaling equally, ensuring that the importance correction applied to gradients reflects only how well the *entire* sequence was generated according to policy consistency relative to its group reward. This aligns with Equation ~8 and ~10, where the gradient for GSPO features a constant scalar multiplier $s_i(\theta)$ preceding an average of equal weights over tokens, whereas GRPO's gradient uses varying importance weights directly attached to individual gradients.

Thus, while applying maximum likelihood estimation would indeed expect an objective proportional to $\prod_t w_t$, converting this product into its geometric mean via normalization is not merely a numerical trick but a theoretical requirement imposed by shifting from localized token constraints (GRPO) to global sequence constraints (GSPO). It prevents numerical collapse caused by extreme weights and ensures that the clipping mechanism remains effective across diverse response lengths without requiring per-token advantage estimates.

### How does shifting from token-level clipping to sequence-level clipping address model collapse in GRPO?
Question:
The paper argues that GRPO suffers from model collapse due to high-variance noise in token-level importance weights which accumulates over response length. It claims that GSPO stabilizes this by 'performing sequence-level clipping.'

Specifically, in Equation ~10, GSPO clips the scalar $s_i(\theta)$ derived from the entire response's likelihood ratio. In contrast, GRPO clips $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ for every single token.

Can you explain mathematically why clipping at the token level is inherently unstable for long sequences while sequence-level clipping remains stable? Is it merely that noise averages out over a sequence in GSPO but not in GRPO, or does the geometric mean property of $s_i$ drastically reduce the magnitude of fluctuations compared to a direct product of potentially clipped tokens?
Answer:
The shift from token-level clipping in GRPO to sequence-level clipping in GSPO addresses model collapse by fundamentally changing how the variance of importance weights is propagated through long sequences and how it interacts with the clipping mechanism. In GRPO, the importance weight at token $t$ is a direct ratio of conditional probabilities, $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$. Because these probabilities are conditioned on increasingly specific histories as the sequence grows, the denominator can become extremely small for rare tokens in long responses, causing $w_{i,t}$ to explode. When such an exploding weight is subsequently clipped (e.g., bounded by $1+\varepsilon$), the gradient update relies on the extreme tail of the unclipped distribution. Over a sequence of length $|y|$, this leads to the accumulation of high-variance noise because each token's weight represents a distinct probabilistic event that is poorly estimated from a single sample in an off-policy setting.

Mathematically, GSPO stabilizes this by defining the importance ratio as the geometric mean of the per-token weights:
$$ s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log w_{i,t}(\theta) \right). $$
The stability arises from two key properties inherent in this geometric mean formulation. First, exponentiation converts multiplicative fluctuations into additive ones in their logarithms; averaging over the log-likelihoods across all tokens smooths out outlier noise that would otherwise dominate if multiplied directly. Second, and crucially for stability, clipping at the geometric mean level provides a sharp threshold that acts as an early gatekeeper. If any single token in a sequence deviates sufficiently from the previous policy to cause $s_i(\theta)$ to exceed $1+\varepsilon$, the entire response is discarded before its per-token components are individually evaluated and clipped. This eliminates the "noise averaging" fallacy often assumed with multiplication; instead of hoping random noise cancels out via multiplication (which is statistically ineffective for rare events), GSPO ensures that only responses whose *entire* trajectory remains consistent with the behavior policy contribute to gradients.

By performing sequence-level clipping on $s_i(\theta)$ rather than on each $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, GSPO alters the optimization landscape: gradients are now weighted uniformly across all tokens within a valid response (as seen in Equation ~4 where all terms receive equal weighting despite differing log-likelihoods), preventing unstable large weights from disproportionately influencing learning dynamics specific to certain positions. This aligns with the principle that clamping deviating samples reduces variance more effectively than clamping underlying unstable components after they have drifted far from their expected distributions. In MoE models specifically, where expert routing changes drastically between policies lead to invalidating conditional probabilities for individual tokens (as highlighted in Section ~6), GSPO's reliance on aggregate sequence likelihood insulates training from these routing instabilities without requiring auxiliary strategies like routing replay. Thus, GSPO replaces gradient noise accumulation with geometric smoothing and strict global consistency checks to prevent irreversible model collapse during long-sequence RL training.

### What is the precise role of 'Routing Replay' in GRPO-MoE, and why does GSPO make it obsolete?
Question:
Section 5.4 discusses MoE training challenges. It states that in GRPO trained with MoE models, experts become 'activated' differently at each gradient update step because their selection depends on tokens generated by an old policy versus a new policy. To fix this noise in importance ratios ($w_{i,t}$), they use 'Routing Replay,' which caches expert activations from one step to force consistency.

The paper claims GSPO eliminates this need because it only relies on sequence likelihood ($\pi(y|x)$). I understand series species usually depend on all previous tokens.

My confusion lies in: Since MoE routing typically rejects paths if activation conditions (like gating scores) aren't met early on, wouldn't a new policy with different expert weights produce a different routing trajectory anyway? If so, how does measuring just $P(y|x)$ avoid capturing these routing fluctuations that vary between steps? isn't there still a hidden dependency on intermediate states?
Answer:
The precise role of 'Routing Replay' in GRPO-MoE is to stabilize training by artificially coupling the routing trances of the old and new policies, thereby rendering token-level importance ratios consistent. In Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, expert activation is stochastic and depends on specific routing layers; as model parameters update after a gradient step, the gating mechanism produces different expert selection patterns for identical tokens. In GRPO, this mismatch between the expert configuration used to generate the reference response (old policy) and the one used to compute the likelihood ratio (new policy) introduces massive variance into the importance weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. Routing Replay addresses this by caching the expert activation set from the old policy step and replaying it during the computation of gradients for the new policy, forcing $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | \dots)$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | \dots)$ to share the same active network.

GSPO obviates this strategy because it eliminates sensitivity to per-token or expert-level routing fluctuations entirely. While MoE models do exhibit volatility in which experts are activated for a given token, these internal routing variations do not necessarily alter the aggregate sequence likelihood $s_i(\theta) = (\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)})^{1/|y_i|}$ significantly enough to cause catastrophic instability. Routing Replay serves no functional purpose in GSPO because GSPO aggregates token probabilities over the entire sequence history before applying importance weighting; rather than relying on noisy, token-by-token consistency of expert paths, GSPO relies on macro-level sequence coherence where such fine-grained routing noise averages out. Consequently, GSPO computes importance ratios directly without caching activation states, avoiding both the computational overhead of replay and potential capacity constraints imposed by artificially locking onto outdated routing modes.

Answer:
The GSPO-token variant is numerically equivalent to the original GRPO algorithm **only under the specific condition that all advantages within a single response are equalized**, i.e., $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$ for every token $t$ in sequence $i$.

The equivalence arises from the specific construction of the importance ratio in GSPO-token. The formula provided in the paper,
$$
s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg} \left[ s_{i}(\theta) \right] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] },
$$
divides the per-token policy probability by a "stopped gradient" version of itself ($\mathrm{sg}$ or `detach`). This operation isolates the numerical value of the ratio from its gradient flow during optimization. Consequently, since the denominator perfectly mirrors the numerator for optimization purposes but acts as a constant scalar (equal to 1.0) during backpropagation, $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ extracts to the exact numerical value of $s_i(\theta)$.

When this mechanism is combined with per-token importance ratios in the objective function (as GRPO does), and then aggregated over tokens using a uniform advantage term ($\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$), the resulting theoretical gradient matches that of GSPO exactly. However, this numerical identity does not imply functional equivalence if advantages are not equalized. If distinct advantages ($\widehat{A}_{i,t}$) are assigned to specific tokens—as intended in scenarios like multi-turn RL or custom token weighting—GSPO-token reverts to being distinct from standard GSPO and differs from GRPO's behavior because GSPO-token applies sequence-level likelihood normalization ($s_i(\theta)$) rather than token-dependent normalization ($w_{i,t}(\theta)$). Even if GSPO-token and Standard GSPO coincide mathematically under equalized advantages, neither is functionally identical to GRPO because GSPO utilizes a geometric mean-based sequence importance ratio, whereas GRPO utilizes a direct product of individual token likelihood ratios; these two mathematical formulations yield different weighting for longer sequences despite converging to the same scalar when applied uniformly across tokens. Thus, GSPO-token serves as a flexible interface that can theoretically replicate GRPO's constraints and numerics but alters them whenever granular advantage adjustments are required.
