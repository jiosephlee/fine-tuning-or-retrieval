# Chapter 1: The Challenge of Scaling Foundation Models

## The Rise of Large Language and Vision Models (LLMs/VLMs)

The landscape of artificial intelligence has undergone a seismic shift in recent years, driven by the advent of massive foundation models capable of mastering a vast array of cognitive skills simultaneously. Pioneering architectures such as GPT-3, developed by OpenAI, demonstrated that scaling language models to hundreds of billions of parameters could yield unprecedented capabilities in reasoning, coding, and creative writing. Similarly, in the realm of multimodal learning, models like DALL-E and Stable Diffusion revolutionized image synthesis and understanding by integrating deep generative priors with conditional control mechanisms. These systems serve as the bedrock for modern AI applications, acting as versatile engines that can be adapted to solve diverse problems ranging from medical diagnosis to autonomous navigation.

However, the sheer magnitude of these models presents a formidable barrier to adoption and deployment. Take GPT-3 as a benchmark; with approximately 175 billion parameters, it represents an immense computational asset requiring petabytes of memory during training phases lasting months. The cost implications extend far beyond mere electricity consumption; they encompass the acquisition of high-end hardware clusters, specialized cooling infrastructure, and substantial human capital dedicated to orchestration and maintenance. Furthermore, the environmental footprint associated with training such colossal networks is staggering, raising ethical concerns regarding sustainability. Consequently, while these foundational models exhibit remarkable zero-shot transfer capabilities—performing well on unseen tasks without explicit instruction—their static nature limits their utility in dynamic environments where specific domain expertise or fine-grained task requirements are needed.

For researchers and practitioners alike, the era has shifted from building general-purpose giants from scratch to efficiently deploying them across myriad downstream applications. This transition necessitates a rigorous examination of how best to leverage these powerful pre-trained assets without repeating the exorbitant expenses of initial training. The challenge lies not merely in adapting these models but doing so in a manner that respects their architectural integrity while maximizing performance gains on target tasks. Understanding this tension between scalability and adaptability forms the critical foundation for exploring advanced finetuning methodologies that balance efficiency with effectiveness.

## Cost-Benefit Analysis: Training vs. Adaptation

The decision between training a foundation model from scratch versus adapting an existing one involves a complex interplay of financial constraints, temporal resources, and risk management strategies. From a purely economic standpoint, training a new large-scale model is prohibitively expensive. Recent benchmarks suggest that reaching comparable performance levels through full-parameter finetuning can incur costs measured in millions or even tens of millions of dollars per model variant depending on dataset complexity and compute availability. In contrast, adapting an existing foundation model offers a pathway to near-equivalent performance at a fraction of the cost—a phenomenon often referred to as "parametric efficiency."

When considering resource allocation timelines, the disparity becomes even more pronounced. Full training campaigns typically span weeks or months due to iterative optimization cycles required across trillions of operations. # Chapter 2: Principles and Limitations of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

## Introduction to Efficient Adaptation and the Role of Orthogonal Finetuning

As foundation models grow in scale, reaching billions or even trillions of parameters, the computational cost associated with training them from scratch becomes prohibitive. Consequently, the field has shifted toward efficient adaptation paradigms that allow these massive pre-trained models to acquire new capabilities for downstream tasks without retraining the entire architecture. Among the various strategies—such as adapter tuning and prompt tuning—model finetuning stands out for its simplicity and lack of inference latency. However, standard finetuning methods often struggle to balance the need for updating weights with the imperative to preserve the rich semantic knowledge encoded during pre-training. To address this tension, a more principled approach was developed known as Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). Unlike traditional methods that treat weight updates heuristically or through additive low-rank perturbations, OFT leverages the geometric properties of linear algebra to ensure that the relationship between neurons is maintained rigorously throughout the optimization process. This chapter delves into the theoretical foundations of OFT, exploring how it preserves pairwise angles between neurons through orthogonal transformations and detailing its mathematical implementation via Cayley parameterization. Furthermore, we critically examine the limitations inherent in OFT's reliance on block-diagonal structures, highlighting issues related to parameter efficiency and expressiveness that motivate subsequent research into more advanced parameterizations like Orthogonal Butterfly.

## Fundamental Principle: Preserving Pairwise Angles Between Neurons

The core philosophy driving Orthogonal Finetuning stems from a deep understanding of how neural networks encode information during their initial training phase. Large-scale models learn complex representations where neurons function as features or filters; crucially, the interaction between pairs of neurons defines the model's capacity to distinguish different classes and handle diverse inputs. In a linear layer $\bm{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $d$ is the hidden dimension and $n$ is the input dimension (or vice versa depending on convention), each row vector represents a neuron's projection capability onto the input space. When two rows are updated arbitrarily during standard gradient descent, they may rotate relative to each other unpredictably. This rotation alters the cosine similarity—the angle—between neurons, potentially destroying subtle correlations learned during pre-training that are essential for generalization.

OFT posits that if we constrain all row vectors within a layer to undergo an orthogonal transformation together, their pairwise angles remain invariant regardless of how far they move in parameter space. Mathematically, let $\bm{W}^0$ be a pretrained weight matrix consisting of rows $\bm{w}_1^0, \dots, \bm{w}_n^0$. If we apply an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in O(d)$ such that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ to these rows simultaneously (effectively transforming $\bm{W}^0$ to $\tilde{\bm{W}} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top$), then for any pair of indices $i$ and $j$:
$$
\cos(\theta_{ij}') = \frac{\langle r_i\bm{w}_i^0, r_j\bm{w}_j^0\rangle}{||r_i\bm{w}_i^0|| ||r_j\bm{w}_j^0||} = \frac{\langle r_i^\top r_j^\top \rangle}{1/2...}
$$
Actually simpler: The dot product between any two transformed rows satisfies $\langle (\bm{R}\boldsymbol{\alpha}), (\bm{R}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \rangle = \boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top (\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}) \boldsymbol{\beta} = \boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}$ because $\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta}$ are column vectors here? Wait careful with dimensions. Let's stick to rows being vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$. If we premultiply by $R$, then $(RW)_i = R w_i$. Then inner product is $(RW)_i . (RW)_j = w_i.R^\top R w_j = w_i.w_j$. Yes.
Thus:
$$
(R\mathbf{x})^\top(R\mathbf{x}) = \|x\|^2
$$
And cross terms vanish similarly? No wait cross term: $(Rx)^T(Ry) = x^T R^T R y = x^T I y = x^T y$. Correct.
So yes: **Pairwise Inner Products Are Preserved.**
By maintaining these inner products constant throughout training, OFT ensures that no new correlations are created artificially and none are destroyed accidentally. This acts as a strong regularization mechanism: it forces any changes in behavior to arise solely from scaling magnitudes or rotations aligned with global constraints rather than arbitrary drifts between feature detectors. Empirical studies have shown that this strict adherence to angular consistency leads to more stable training dynamics and superior generalization compared to unconstrained finetuning or even low-rank adapters in certain domains like image generation control.

# Chapter 3: An Information Transmission Framework

### Reframing Matrix Multiplication as Data Flow

In the realm of deep learning, particularly within the context of orthogonal finetuning, standard approaches often treat matrix operations through purely algebraic lenses. One might simply observe that a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters to store explicitly. However, the fundamental challenge in parameter-efficient finetuning is not merely finding *any* orthogonal matrix, but constructing one that is dense (capable of expressing complex transformations) while minimizing the number of trainable parameters. Traditional strategies like Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) attempt to solve this by imposing rigid structural assumptions, such as block-diagonal sparsity, which inevitably sacrifice expressivity. The core difficulty lies in reconciling the requirement for density with the desire for sparsity without violating the mathematical constraint of orthogonality.

To resolve this tension, we introduce a novel conceptual lens: the **Information Transmission View**. This framework reframes the problem of generating a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of sparse matrices as an information flow problem on a grid-structured graph. Instead of viewing $\bm{R}$ solely as a collection of numbers satisfying geometric constraints, we interpret it as a system where information travels from source nodes to destination nodes across intermediate stages. Consider the factorization $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$, where each $\bm{B}_i$ is a sparse square matrix. We can visualize this sequence as a pipeline connecting $d$ input dimensions (nodes at level 0) to $d$ output dimensions (nodes at level $m$). Each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ acts as a switchboard or router operating between adjacent levels of this pipeline—specifically, it defines how information flows from the nodes at level $i$ to the nodes at level $i+1$.

Under this interpretation, every non-zero element in a matrix $\bm{B}_i$ represents an active communication channel or edge allowing data transfer between two specific dimensions at consecutive steps. Conversely, every zero element signifies a blocked link where information cannot propagate directly from one node to another during that specific transformation step. Consequently, constructing a dense final matrix $\bm{R}$ is equivalent to designing a series of interconnected routers such that *every* piece of initial information originating from any source node eventually reaches *every* destination node after passing through all layers. If even a single path exists between two nodes that never activates regardless of how many times we multiply by subsequent matrices (due to structural zeros blocking all routes), the resulting product will remain sparse rather than dense. Thus, the algebraic property of density emerges naturally from topological completeness: for $\bm{R}$ to be dense ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$), the underlying communication graph must be fully connected in terms of reachability from start to finish.

This shift in perspective offers profound advantages for analyzing parameter efficiency. By mapping matrices to graphs, we can decompose complex optimization problems into manageable sub-problems regarding network topology and edge selection. The "information transmission" metaphor allows researchers and practitioners to reason about what constitutes an efficient representation not just by counting parameters statically, but by evaluating dynamic connectivity requirements. It clarifies why arbitrary random sparse products fail to produce dense matrices—they likely lack sufficient paths for complete information propagation—and guides us toward structured designs that guarantee reachability while keeping edge counts minimal. Furthermore, this view bridges fields outside pure mathematics; concepts derived from computer networking and signal processing regarding optimal routing and broadcasting become directly applicable tools for designing neural network architectures that balance computational cost with functional capacity.

### Nodes as Dimensions, Edges as Connections

To rigorously operationalize this framework, we must precisely define how neurons correspond to nodes and how weight updates correspond to edges within our grid-structured graph model. Let us consider a linear layer in a neural network with an input dimension $n$ and an internal feature dimension $d$. In orthogonal finetuning paradigms like OFT or BOFT, we aim to learn an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ applied post-multiplication (or pre-multiplication depending on convention) to preserve angular relationships between features while adapting them for downstream tasks. Within our information transmission view, let us assume without loss of generality that we are interested in transforming signals residing in $d$ distinct dimensions into another set of $d$ dimensions via a chain of transformations involving $m$ sparse matrices: $\bm{R} = \prod_{j=1}^m \bm{B}_j$.

Here, each dimension $k \in \{1, 2, ..., d\}$ serves as both an origin and a destination point in our communication network. Imagine arranging these dimensions vertically across time steps or processing stages labeled by integers $0$ through $m$. At stage 0 (input), there are $d$ active nodes representing the initial state vectors; similarly, at stage $m+1$ (output), there are corresponding target nodes waiting for incoming signals. Between any two adjacent stages $i$ and $i+1$, exactly one transition occurs governed entirely by the specific sparse matrix $\bm{B}_{i+1}$. The entries within this transition matrix dictate which nodes communicate with which others during this hop. Specifically, if we examine the element located at row index $(u,v)$ within matrix $\bm{B}_{i+1}$—denoted hereafter as $(\bm{B}_{i+1})_{uv}$—this scalar value represents whether there is an active directed edge connecting node $v$ at stage $i$ to node $u$ at stage $i+1$. If $(\bm{B}_{i+1})_{uv} > 0$, it implies that whatever signal resides at node $v$ entering stage $i$ will successfully traverse into node $u$ emerging from stage $i+1$. If $(\bm{B}_{i+1})_{uv} = 0$, then no direct connection exists; signal flow between these specific pairings is severed for this particular step in the sequence.

It is crucial to distinguish between "direct connections" provided by individual matrices and "indirect paths" formed by sequences thereof. A single non-zero entry guarantees immediate transmission capability between two adjacent levels' nodes but says nothing about long-range dependencies unless combined with other matrices in the product chain. For instance, suppose node 1 communicates with node 3 via multiple intermediate hops enabled by different factors in our product expansion; although no single factor creates a direct link between 1
