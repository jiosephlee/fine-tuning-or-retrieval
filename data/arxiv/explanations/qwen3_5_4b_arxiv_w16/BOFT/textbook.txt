# Chapter 1: The Landscape of Foundation Model Adaptation

## The Exorbitant Cost of Training Large Foundation Models from Scratch

In the rapidly evolving landscape of artificial intelligence, large foundation models have reshaped the boundaries of what machines can achieve. Architectures such as GPT-3, with approximately 175 billion parameters, and Stable Diffusion represent the pinnacle of generalization capabilities, performing exceptionally well across diverse tasks in language processing, image generation, and beyond. However, this exponential leap in model scale brings with it a corresponding explosion in computational resources. Training such massive models from scratch is no longer a feasible endeavor for most research institutions and industry labs; it is prohibitively expensive, requiring terabytes of data, massive clusters of GPUs or TPUs, and months of continuous computing time. The financial and environmental costs are immense, creating a significant barrier to entry that stifles innovation and hinders the democratization of these powerful tools.

As a result, the field has shifted its focus toward efficient methods that allow researchers to adapt these pre-trained giants to specific downstream tasks without retraining them from their initial parameters. This paradigm shift is crucial because these foundation models have mastered a vast array of phenomena during their massive pre-training phase. When faced with a new task—whether it involves classifying objects in an image or understanding the sentiment of a news article—a "best" finetuning approach must preserve as much of this pre-training knowledge as possible while nudging the model toward the new objective. Retraining from scratch essentially discards all that hard-earned knowledge, creating redundancy; therefore, preservation becomes the fundamental principle guiding effective adaptation strategies. It is in this context that the concept of parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) emerged not merely as an optimization trick, but as a necessary requirement for sustainable research progress.

The challenge lies in finding a balance between two competing demands: introducing enough flexibility to perform well on specific tasks while maintaining enough regularity to prevent catastrophic forgetting of general-purpose knowledge. Traditional full finetuning often strikes too hard against the internal structure learned during pretraining, leading to unstable training dynamics and poor generalization on common or generalizable tasks. This dilemma has driven significant theoretical and practical research into how we can modify a few billion parameters—or extremely few—in ways that are structurally compatible with the frozen backbone. Understanding this landscape is vital for any researcher entering this field; one must first appreciate why efficiency matters so deeply before exploring the specific mathematical structures used to achieve it.

## Defining Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT)

Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) represents a methodological family designed to solve the problem of large-scale adaptation by constraining which parts of the neural network are allowed to change during training. The core philosophy behind PEFT is that if we can intelligently select or structure the subset of parameters responsible for task-specific adaptation, we can minimize computational cost while maximizing performance gains over baselines like random initialization or standard full finetuning with low learning rates. While there is no single universal definition for PEFT methods given their diversity in architecture and training objective, they all share a fundamental characteristic: they introduce additional trainable parameters to an existing pre-trained model but restrict these changes significantly compared to updating every weight and bias matrix simultaneously. These methods generally operate under one of two assumptions: either by modifying only a small subset of existing weights (approximating them with simple structures), or by adding entirely new layers with zero initialization containing low-rank or sparse structures.

The distinction between PEFT methods and other adaptive strategies often hinges on where they insert their learnable components relative to the frozen backbone weights $\mathbf{W}^{0}$. In approaches like full finetuning adapted via small learning rates (often referred to as gradient-based PEFT), the update rule is simple additive decrement: $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}^{0} - \eta \nabla_{\mathbf{W}} L$, where $\eta$ is tiny. While this preserves stability practically speaking without explicitly enforcing it theoretically, it relies heavily on *ad hoc* tuning rather than principled constraints on weight updates that often lead this specific aditional subtopics into developments such as Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). In contrast, structured PEFT methods impose algebraic constraints on how weights evolve. ## Chapter Description

This chapter shifts from the engineering intuition of efficient parameterization to a rigorous mathematical theory governing the structure of weight updates. By reframing the construction of dense orthogonal matrices as an 'information transmission' task over a grid graph, we establish the fundamental constraints required for parameter efficiency. We begin by examining the theoretical underpinnings of re-parameterization, explaining how multiplying weights by an orthogonal matrix serves as a principled method to preserve pre-training semantics while enabling task-specific adaptation. We then rigorously define the mathematical constraints of orthogonality, focusing on the preservation of pairwise angles between latent representations. Finally, we deconstruct the high-dimensional complexity of dense orthogonal matrices by introducing a factorization approach and visualizing these structures as directed acyclic graphs to quantify the flow of information across node levels.

## Re-parameterization Theory: Changing Weight Matrices without Forgetting Pre-training Knowledge

In the context of fine-tuning large foundation models, the primary objective is to adapt a pretrained model to a specific downstream task without discarding the vast amount of knowledge acquired during its initial pretraining phase. From an optimization perspective, simply adjusting weights via standard gradient descent (as in standard fine-tuning) or adding low-rank updates (as in LoRA) treats all parameters as equally significant, potentially leading to catastrophic forgetting where critical pretraining semantics are overwritten by task-specific noise. The core theoretical insight driving Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its extension, Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), is that pretraining knowledge encodes relational information between neurons—the semantic features carried by one dimension are often coupled with others orthogonal to it. When we update weight matrices arbitrarily, we risk misaligning these relationships.

To address this, OFT introduces a multiplicative re-parameterization strategy that fundamentally alters how weights are updated compared to additive methods. Let $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ represent the frozen pretrained weight matrix for a linear layer, where $d$ is the feature dimension and $n$ is the output dimension (or number of input tokens). Instead of learning new weights directly or adding small delta matrices $\Delta \bm{W} \approx \bm{W}_{new} - \bm{W}^0$, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ such that the new weight matrix is defined as the product:
$$
\bm{W}_{new} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0
$$
Here, $\bm{R}$ acts as a rotation operator in the input feature space $\mathbb{R}^d$. By treating $\bm{W}_{new}$ as a composition rather than an independent entity introduced into existence through addition or subtraction, we impose a structural constraint on all possible updates. Specifically, any transformation applied to one neuron's direction via learning also applies in a consistent geometric manner to its entire space orthogonal basis defined by $\bm{R}$. This ensures that if two neurons in $\bm{W}^0$ were associated with specific features (say, color and shape) such that their inner product was zero (indicating orthogonality in feature space), their corresponding representations in $\bm{W}_{new}$ will maintain zero inner product because orthogonal transformations preserve angles and thus orthogonality. This property allows for configuration-independent learning; having access to an angle-preserving mapping encodes all topological properties of weights shared across layers into each layer automatically without needing explicit attention mechanisms at every step. This theoretical advantage provides powerful regularization effects naturally while enabling potentially more stable training dynamics for complex tasks requiring precise semantic manipulation within neural networks' latent spaces.

# From Block-Diagonal to Butterfly Structures

# Sparsity Patterns: Perils of Independent Block Processing

In the landscape of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), the seminal work on Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) introduced a paradigm shift by preserving the angular relationships between neurons rather than merely adding low-rank perturbations. To handle the prohibitive cost of training a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix—which scales quadratically with dimension, requiring $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters—early implementations relied on decomposing this high-dimensional task into $r$ smaller, independent sub-tasks. This approach introduced a block-diagonal structure to the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, defined as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each block $\bm{R}_i$ has dimensions $b \times b$ and the total dimension $d$ is partitioned such that $br=d$.

The intuition behind this block-diagonal strategy is straightforward: if we can learn a locally invertible transformation within small blocks, we can stitch these together to form a global permutation-like update. This drastically reduces the trainable parameters to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, effectively trading memory for parameter count. However, this pinching of the high-dimensional space into rigid sub-problems creates immediate theoretical and practical hurdles. The most intrinsic issue lies in the arbitrariness of this partitioning. In standard linear algebra and data representation theory, there is no inherent property that justifies dividing the $d$ dimensions of a neuron into $r$ specific groups based solely on their index. The indices themselves are arbitrary labels assigned during model initialization and pretraining; therefore, enforcing a separation where dimensions $1$ through $b/2$ interact only with other dimensions in that same range, while ignoring all cross-group interactions (e.g., between index 1 and index $b+1$), imposes an artificial constraint on the model's representational capacity.

From an information theory perspective, this limitation becomes starkly obvious when visualizing the flow of data through these matrices. Consider a vector input that needs to be fully transformed across its entire dimensionality to capture downstream task nuances. In a dense orthogonal matrix, information flows freely from every source dimension to every destination dimension according to the optimal transformation learned for the specific task. In contrast, the block-diagonal structure creates "silos." If an information unit resides in dimension ten and needs to be rotated into direction twenty-five by finetuning parameters residing in dimension five's block, it cannot happen independently; however, if step 8 falls in dim 5's block and step 2 falls into dim 2's block (assuming sorted indices for simplicity), they still cannot mix effectively without inter-block coupling. This structural decoupling means that while the model maintains sparsity in terms of non-zero entries relative to a full matrix ($O(d)$ vs $O(d^2)$), it fails to capture true correlations between distant dimensions because those correlations are physically impossible under the fixed sparsity pattern unless they happen by chance within a specific block alignment.

Consequently, while OFT with block-diagonal structures provides a baseline for stability and parameter efficiency compared to full finetuning, it suffers from restricted expressivity. The model is forced to approximate complex transformations using only local moves within predetermined slots. This rigidity suggests that simply scaling up these small blocks does not necessarily improve performance quadratically; rather, it raises the threshold difficulty for approximation if those small blocks remain disconnected from parts of the space they should influence but do not inhabit due to their fixed boundaries. To overcome this barrier without sacrificing parameter efficiency legally (i.e., truly minimizing parameters), one must abandon static partitioning altogether and instead seek factorizations where sparsity arises dynamically from graph topologies that naturally allow dense connectivity while keeping edge counts minimal. The solution to this puzzle lies not in rearranging indices into blocks, but in discovering a communication protocol—a graph topology—that allows every node to talk to every other node using far fewer edges than a direct link would suggest. This leads us directly to studying how random sparse connections fail and how specific structured connections succeed.

In deep learning models like vision transformers or large language models (LLMs), architecture choices often imbue these models with biases that constrain what kinds of data they can learn most easily without explicit regularization costs. # Chapter 4: The Orthogonal Butterfly Architecture (BOFT)

### Algorithm Derivation: The Recursive Decomposition Formula

In the quest to parameterize a dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix without incurring the prohibitive $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameter cost associated with naive dense weights, we introduce the Orthogonal Butterfly architecture (BOFT). This architecture is rooted in the mathematical elegance of the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm, which utilizes a recursive structure to decompose complex global transformations into local operations. To understand the derivation of BOFT, we must first formally define the primitive building blocks that constitute this butterfly structure: the butterfly factor and the butterfly component.

Consider a dimension $k \geq 2$ that is a power of 2. We begin by defining the $k \times k$ butterfly factor, denoted as $\bm{B}^F(k)$. This matrix is constructed as a block matrix composed of four diagonal blocks, each containing a vector $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ for $i=1, 2, 3, 4$. Specifically, $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Here, $\text{diag}(\cdot)$ denotes a matrix where the input vector forms the main diagonal and zero otherwise. Geometrically and computationally, this specific pattern represents a permutation of non-zero entries typical in butterfly networks; specifically, it relates nodes from the lower half of the range to nodes in the upper half and vice-versa. However, as an arbitrary independent product of constants would not necessarily yield an orthogonal matrix, we introduce learnable parameters only within specific sub-blocks to ensure that every resulting operator $\tilde{\bm{B}}$ remains orthogonal throughout training.

To handle dimensions larger than $k$, we define a recursive construction for the $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ where $d=2^N$. This matrix is assembled by multiplying a scaling layer $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)$ with two halved butterfly matrices:
\begin{equation}
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2}) & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This recursive definition implies that to construct a dense orthogonal transformation of dimension $d$, one simply needs to concatenate the finest-level differences ($k=2$), next-coarsest ($k=4$), up to the largest scale ($k=d$). Each scale corresponds to a specific "level" in the recursion tree.

We formalize these intermediate operations as **butterfly components**. Let us define $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ as a block-diagonal matrix consisting of $d/k$ copies of $\bm{B}^F(k)$ stacked vertically and horizontally on an extended grid if necessary to match dimensions. Mathematically:
