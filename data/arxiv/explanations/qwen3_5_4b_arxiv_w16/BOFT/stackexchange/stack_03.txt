\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How should I correctly initialize the trainable parameters in BOFT compared to LoRA or standard OFT?
Question:
In LoRA, weights are initialized at zero ($W = W_0 + B \cdot C$). In OFT, weights follow $W = R W_0$ where $R=I$. In BOFT, $W = R(m,b) W_0$.

The paper states: 'In BOFT, we initialize all the butterfly components with identity matrices (i.e., the skew-symmetric matrix is initialized as zeros in Cayley transform).' 

Given that each component $\tilde{{\bm B}}^b_{(d,j)}$ contains many free parameters (entries corresponding to edges), why can't I initialize them differently? If I set most entries of an orthogonal factor to small random values instead of exactly zero/skew-symmetric form, wouldn't it deviate from $R=W_0$ immediately without any gradients acting on them? How does this initialization strategy specifically maintain training stability or ensure that $X=Y$ initially?
Answer:
Regarding the initialization of trainable parameters in Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) versus LoRA or standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the paper's recommendation to initialize all butterfly components with identity matrices ($\mathrm{equivalent}$ to initializing the underlying skew-symmetric matrices to zero) is critical for maintaining both mathematical consistency during training and numerical stability.

In LoRA, weights are updated additively as $W = W_0 + BC$, allowing direct initialization of the low-rank components $B$ and $C$ to arbitrary small values without disrupting the base weights. In standard block-diagonal OFT, where weights follow $W = R W_0$ with $R$ being a permutation-based block-diagonal matrix (using only sign flips: $\pm 1$), an identity initialization naturally represents a discrete permutation that maps inputs directly to themselves ($X=Y$ at initialization). This works because the constraints are so rigid that any deviation immediately implies either a permutation or a substitution of vector values rather than a smooth rotation.

The scenario in BOFT is more complex due to its parametric nature. The final weight transformation is defined by the product $R(m,b) = \prod_{j=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,j)}$, where each component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,j)}$ is learned via a Cayley transform from a skew-symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$: $R = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$. If one were to initialize components with random small values or non-skew-symmetric deviations immediately, the model would not satisfy the orthogonality constraint $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(j)\top} = \mathbf{I}$. Unlike LoRA, where parameter deviations simply result in different linear transformations, violating orthogonality in BOFT causes the fine-tuned matrices to drift out of the orthogonal manifold during early steps. This violates the core principle of Boise-based training, which aims to preserve pairwise angles and spectral properties by ensuring fine-tuning stays within the special orthogonal group.

Furthermore, because BOFT uses a factorized sparsity structure derived from an information transmission view (circuitry on a grid of nodes representing matrices), these factors act like sequential gates or rotors transforming data through layers. Initializing them randomly or with non-orthogonal small values introduces asynchronous inconsistencies across these layers rather than a coherent rotational bias field (like Fourier transforms or Hadamard matrices). While gradients flow through these parameters, initializing them as identity ensures that at step zero, every vector $x$ remains transformed by an identity matrix ($Rx=x$), guaranteeing perfect alignment with pretraining immediately. The backpropagation then proceeds by smoothly rotating away from this identity baseline. Initializing arbitrary entries would effectively introduce "$\mathrm{noise}$" into this geometric trajectory before training begins, potentially destabilizing convergence and preventing the model from realizing its structured prior knowledge about how classic linear transforms should smoothly transition from identity to general orthogonal operations.
