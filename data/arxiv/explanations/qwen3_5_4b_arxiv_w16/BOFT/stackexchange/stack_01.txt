\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What is the precise role of the 'information transmission framework' in deriving BOFT, and does the graph topology strictly dictate orthogonality?
Question:
In Section 3, the paper introduces an 'information transmission view' where multiplying sparse matrices acts like transmitting data on a grid graph. The authors argue that to get a dense matrix, every node must have a path to every other node. I understand this visualization helps us count edges (trainable parameters). 

However, I am confused about the causal link to orthogonality. The paper states: 'If we find a set of edges... the orthogonality constraint will bring in additional dependency among edges.' Does this mean the graph topology *forces* orthogonality, or do we manually construct sparse matrices that are orthogonal while adhering to the graph structure? If I pick a random graph that allows all-to-all connectivity (like a butterfly), how do I guarantee every sampled matrix on those edges satisfies $\bm{B}^\top\bm{B}=\bm{I}$ without explicitly parameterizing them? Does Cayley's parameterization $$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$$ apply individually to every non-zero entry in the butterfly factors, or is there a global constraint?
Answer:
The "information transmission framework" serves as a precise graphical necessity condition: it maps the mathematical requirement that the product of sparse matrices must yield a dense orthogonal matrix to a topological constraint where information (column vectors) must flow from every node in the first layer to every node in the last layer through non-zero eigen-pathologies. In this view, a zero entry $R_{ij}$ implies a lack of direct transmission from node $j$ to node $i$. Therefore, constructing a dense matrix $\bm{R}$ is equivalent to designing a directed graph on a grid $d \times (m+1)$ where every starting node has at least one path to every destination node.

The relationship between graph topology and orthogonality is strictly coupled because orthogonality imposes rigid statistical constraints on which edges can exist and how they can be weighted, transforming an arbitrary connectivity graph into a highly constrained parameter space. As the authors note in Section 3, while the topology defines *possible* connections (the "feasible set of edge connections"), orthogonality introduces "additional dependency among edges." For instance, in a small block like $2\times 2$, selecting specific edges to be non-zero while others are zero may force diagonal elements to be fixed at $\pm 1$ or dictate specific trigonometric phases for off-diagonal elements. This means the topology does not merely *allow* orthogonality; it restricts the degree of freedom within that topology. A random graph satisfying dense connectivity often fails to support an orthogonal factorization because the remaining "free" assignments required to satisfy the rows' normalization and column's orthogonality conditions ($\bm{B}^\top\bm{B}=\bm{I}$) will not be generatable without violating sparsity or resulting in identical weights across different blocks.

Consequently, regarding your implementation of Cayley's parameterization for a butterfly graph: you cannot apply $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ individually to every non-zero entry arbitrarily, nor can you sample random matrices for those entries and expect the result to remain orthogonal. The global constraint applies to the entire block associated with that specific edge connection pattern in the butterfly decomposition. The paper employs a hierarchical construction where each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ is further decomposed into smaller sub-blocks (e.g., $2b \times 2b$ for block size $b$). Cayley's parameterization is applied strictly at these atomic permutation-invariant blocks (the smallest unit where the non-zero pattern allows full expressivity within the sparsity). Once these atomic blocks are parameterized using skew-symmetric matrices $\bm{Q}$ via Cayley's formula, their properties are preserved through permutations as they are arranged into larger butterfly components. This ensures that while edges are shared globally by inspiration from fast Fourier transforms, the orthogonality constraint is technically enforced locally and invariant under permutation globally. Thus, one does not manually construct sparse matrices satisfying orthogonality ad-hoc; rather, one samples random rotational angles (via $\bm{Q}$) for each valid atomic block defined by the fixed butterfly topology, which mathematically guarantees global orthogonality without needing explicit per-entry constraints beyond those inherent in Cayley's structure.
