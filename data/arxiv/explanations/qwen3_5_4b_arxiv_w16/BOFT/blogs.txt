# The Butterfly Secret: How a Signal Processing Trick Makes AI Finetuning Faster

In the world of artificial intelligence, respecting the power of large foundation models often conflicts with the practical need to adapt them to new tasks without expending a fortune in compute. Methods like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) have become the standard for this, updating networks by adding small, low-rank matrices. However, orthogonal finetuning has emerged as a more powerful alternative for complex models like diffusion systems, ensuring that the underlying mathematical relationships between neurons remain stable. The problem? Standard orthogonal finetuning requires updating a massive number of weights because an orthogonal matrix has quadratic dimensions relative to the network layer size.

Recently, a cutting-edge approach called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) has addressed this parameter inefficiency by borrowing a concept from signal processing: the butterfly structure used in the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform algorithm. BOFT achieves significant reductions in trainable parameters by treating dense weight updates as information transmission problems solved through sparse matrix multiplications.

## Repurposing the Butterfly Structure

To understand why this works, we must look at how we represent mathematical transformations in deep learning. When we update a layer's weight matrix $\bm{W}$, we can decompose it into an original pretrained part and an update part. The core innovation of BOFT is using specific sparse patterns that allow us to synthesize a full-dense transformation while storing only a fraction of its components in memory.

Imagine you want to construct a dense grid of connections where every input line connects to every output line. A naive approach would require $d^2$ parameters for a dimension $d$, which scales poorly. Instead, BOFT utilizes mulitiplication rules found in fast linear transforms like Fourier and Hadamard transforms. These algorithms use what are known as *butterfly graphs*, where information moves between nodes in a hierarchical structure with very few active connections at each step.

Mathematically, a butterfly factorization decomposes an efficient orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ into a product of many sparse matrices $\tilde{\bm{B}}_j$:
$$ \bm{R} = \tilde{\bm{B}}_1 \cdot \tilde{\bm{B}}_2 \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}_m $$
By choosing these blocks according to the butterfly topology (often powers of 2), researchers can generate $m = \log_d N$ sparse layers where each layer connects its inputs via only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ parameters rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. The total parameter count drops from quadratic to linear-logarithmic: $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. This structure allows the model to learn complex transformations that span across all dimensions without needing to store the overwhelming number of non-zero values directly.

## Information Transmission as Graph Theory

The brilliance of this method lies in reframing weight updates as an information transmission problem on a grid graph. If we visualize the matrix multiplication process as stages along an axis, each stage represents a "hop" where data moves from one set of coordinates to another. In this graph view, setting an entry in the butterfly matrix to zero means severing that specific path; keeping it non-zero means allowing information flow there passantly through computation rather than memory storage.

A key insight derived from this viewpoint is that orthogonality imposes strict constraints on these edges, ensuring they remain valid rotations or reflections after multiplication. For instance, because orthogonal matrices form groups closed under inversion, certain zero patterns imply other necessary zeros or fixed values (like $+1$ or $-1$). By adhering to the butterfly sparsity pattern inherent to Cooley-Tukey algorithms, BOFT guarantees that these global constraints are satisfied automatically at every step of the construction process. This removes much of the manual work usually required by sparse matrices and ensures that even though the factorization is sparse, its product $\bm{R}$ is guaranteed to be dense and orthogonal where needed for downstream tasks like language modeling or image generation.



# Beyond Low-Rank Updates: The Rise of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

The landscape of parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) has been dominated for some time by methods like LoRA, which rely on adding low-rank matrices to pretrained weights. While these additive updates are effective, they fundamentally alter the geometric structure of the weight matrix. In contrast, a newer approach called Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) takes a multiplicative route that preserves the intrinsic geometry of the original model, leading to superior performance in complex downstream tasks such as high-quality image generation.

### The Geometric Intuition: Preserving Angles

The foundational concept behind OFT stems from an observation that pretrained models often store knowledge in the differential relationships between neurons rather than their absolute values. In a standard linear layer, inputs are projected via a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. If we redefine this projection as the product of an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and the pretrained weights—that is, $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$—we ensure that $\bm{R}$ only rotates the feature space without stretching or compressing it.

In mathematics, two vectors are orthogonal if their angle is $90^\circ$, but OFT ensures something more general: it preserves the pairwise angles between all neurons in a layer throughout the finetuning process. By maintaining these angular relationships, OFT effectively keeps the "semantic map" established during pretraining intact while learning new task-specific directions. This geometric constraint mandates that $\bm{R}$ must remain an orthogonal matrix at every step of training.

### The Pristic Challenge and Block-Diagonality

A major hurdle arises when trying to construct a dense orthogonal matrix, which would perhaps perfectly capture all possible rotations in an $d$-dimensional space. A naive full-rank orthogonal matrix requires scaling parameters proportional to $d^2$, which is computationally expensive for large foundation models used in fields like natural language processing or computer vision. To address this dimensionality issue without sacrificing orthogonality, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure. Instead of one giant square matrix, the total transformation is represented as a diagonal stack of smaller independent orthogonal matrices: $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$ where $br=d$. This block-diagonal parameterization reduces the trainable parameters from $O(d^2)$ to $O(bd)$, making it practical for deployment while still guaranteeing angle preservation within each block.

### Why It Works Better Than LoRA

Unlike LoRA's additive updates which can shift singular values and potentially destabilize training, OFT acts multiplicatively to preserve the spectral norm (or distribution of singular values) of the original weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. In linear algebra terms, if $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ represents its singular value decomposition (SVD), applying an orthogonal rotation $\bm{R}$ results in $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Since multiplying by an orthogonal matrix does not change its singular values, OFT maintains the stability guaranteed by powerful regularization techniques like Spectral Normalization. This preservation leads to tighter loss landscapes and smoother training dynamics compared to methods whose hypotheses deform at every iteration.



# From Block-Diagonal to Butterfly Factorization: Solving the Parameter Efficiency Paradox

Large foundation models have revolutionized fields ranging from natural language processing to computer vision, but their sheer scale—often comprising hundreds of billions of parameters—makes training them from scratch prohibitively expensive. To bridge the gap between pre-training and downstream task adaptation, researchers have turned to parameter-efficient finetuning methods. Among these, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) has gained attention for its ability to preserve the semantic knowledge of pretrained models by maintaining the angles between neurons through orthogonal transformations. However, a significant bottleneck remains: OFT typically requires a large number of trainable parameters due to the high dimensionality of orthogonal matrices, limiting its practicality compared to methods like LoRA.

The core issue with standard OFT lies in how it represents these orthogonal matrices. To achieve computational efficiency without sacrificing performance, the original approach relies on a block-diagonal structure. This structure divides the dimensions of a neuron into groups, applying different orthogonal transformations independently within each block. While this reduces the parameter count from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ (where $d$ is the dimensionality) to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ with $b \ll d$, it introduces a rigid assumption that treats neuron dimensions as independent silos. Because the sparsity pattern is fixed based on index groups rather than learned data dynamics, this approach sacrifices the model's expressivity and fails to approximate classic linear transforms effectively. The fundamental question becomes: can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix that supports complex interactions between neuron layers while still being parameter-efficient?

To answer this, we can reframe matrix factorization as an information transmission problem visualized through graph theory. In this view, a dense orthogonal matrix represents a grid-structured graph where every node in the source set must be able to transmit information to every node in the destination set via directed edges generated by sequential matrix multiplications. A naive single dense matrix creates $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ connections, which is both computationally heavy and parameter-inefficient. The block-diagonal method creates fewer edges by design, but these connections are strictly localized, preventing global flow between certain nodes. We need a topology that connects all nodes efficiently using far fewer edges than a full dense connection but more connectivity than fixed blocks.

This is where butterfly factorization offers an elegant solution. Inspired by the Cooley-Tukey algorithm for fast Fourier transforms, butterfly structures provide a graph topology capable of densely connecting $d$ source nodes and $d$ receiver nodes with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges. By constructing an orthogonal matrix as a product of $m = \log d$ sparse orthogonal butterfly factors, we drastically reduce the number of trainable parameters with minimal loss in expressivity. This approach allows us to treat the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix not as a static algebraic constraint but as a dynamic process of information exchange across levels in our graph.

The mathematical formulation leverages this structural insight by decomposing a large orthogonal matrix into multiple smaller blocks followed by permutations that coordinate their interactions across the grid levels. Specifically, if we define $m = \log(d/b)$ for some block size $b$, we can form an efficient parameterization known as Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). Contrary to what one might expect with repeated sparse operations potentially degrading accuracy or convergence properties during finetuning, theoretical analysis confirms that BOFT maintains strong expressivity and training stability within the orthogonal group $O(d)$. More importantly, this structured approach naturally facilitates interpolation between general orthogonal transforms and identity matrices, offering better generalization guarantees than unstructured methods without relying on rigid block assumptions that hinder architectural flexibility.

The shift from block-diagonal decomposition to butterfly factorization represents more than just a reduction in variable count; it signifies a change in how we constrain hypotheses for downstream learning tasks. While various techniques reduce parameters—for example, by freezing entire layers or using low-rank updates—they often struggle with preserving complex relational structures necessary for high-performance task adaptation. BOFT addresses this by introducing structural priors derived from efficient network topologies found in signal processing and computer science. By leveraging permutations derived from butterfly factors at each level of recursion, we ensure that information flows are maximally connected despite sparsity constraints imposed at construction time ($\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}$). This generalized framework effectively optimizes over all possible sparsity patterns rather than being limited by predetermined blocks—an essential capability when moving beyond specific tasks like controllable text-to-image generation to broader adaptation scenarios in language and vision domains
