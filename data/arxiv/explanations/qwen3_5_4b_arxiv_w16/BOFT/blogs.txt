\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# Why Orthogonal Finetuning Matters: Preserving Knowledge Without Relearning

Adapting massive foundation models like GPT-3 or Stable Diffusion to specific downstream tasks is a critical challenge in modern machine learning. Training these colossal architectures from scratch requires prohibitive computational resources and time. Consequently, the industry has shifted toward parameter-efficient techniques that allow models to acquire new skills while retaining their foundational knowledge. One of the most prominent approaches involves **fine-tuning**, but traditional fine-tuning often struggles with a phenomenon known as *catastrophic forgetting*. When a model learns new information, it frequently overwrites the weights associated with old capabilities, causing it to lose what it previously knew.

This is where **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** offers a principled alternative. Instead of arbitrarily updating weights, OFT treats them as rigid geometric objects that must maintain their relative positions during training. To understand why this matters, we need to look at how deep neural networks represent information internally. Inside a layer of a neural network, thousands of neurons act together as a feature extractor. If we visualize these neurons as vectors in a high-dimensional space, they are arranged at specific angles relative to one another. These angles encode the semantic relationships between features—for instance, distinguishing between "king" and "queen" might rely on a specific angular relationship preserved across many layers.

Standard fine-tuning methods often disrupt these delicate balances because they treat weights as independent variables subject to gradient descent noise. This can cause the angles between neurons to drift apart, leading to instability and loss of pre-training knowledge. OFT addresses this by enforcing an **orthogonality constraint**. Mathematically, an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies the condition $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, meaning its columns remain perpendicular to each other throughout the optimization process. In practical terms, this guarantees that the angle between any pair of neurons remains exactly constant before and after finetuning ($\theta_{new} = \theta_{old}$). By preserving these pairwise angles provably throughout training, OFT ensures that the internal geometry—the relational knowledge—of the pretrained model is locked in place while allowing the overall orientation of the feature space to shift towards solving new tasks.

While OFT successfully stabilizes training and improves generalization compared to standard methods like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), it faces its own hurdle regarding efficiency. Implementing an orthogonal transformation on a large matrix $\bm{W}^0$ requires storing and updating roughly $d^2$ parameters per layer (where $d$ is the hidden dimension). This defeats part of the purpose of efficient finetuning if we cannot afford such memory usage or compute costs during backpropagation. To overcome this bottleneck without sacrificing stability, recent research has proposed enhancing OFT through advanced factorization techniques inspired by signal processing algorithms like the Fast Fourier Transform. These innovations aim to decompose complex orthogonal operations into sequences of simpler sparse transformations, drastically reducing the number of trainable parameters while maintaining the rigorous orthogonality constraints that protect against catastrophic forgetting. Ultimately, moving toward such sophisticated frameworks allows us to leverage powerful pretrained models for diverse applications—from natural language processing to image generation—without paying the steep tax on resources required for full retraining or risking the erosion of essential prior knowledge.



# From Block-Diagonal to Butterflies: The Quest for Smarter Parameter Efficiency

Large foundation models like GPT-3 and Stable Diffusion have revolutionized artificial intelligence, but their sheer scale—often comprising hundreds of billions of parameters—makes training them from scratch economically prohibitive. Consequently, the field has shifted toward parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) techniques that allow these massive models to adapt to downstream tasks without retraining everything. One promising approach is Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT), which preserves the internal geometric relationships of a model's neurons by applying an orthogonal matrix during the update process. While OFT offers strong generalization capabilities, its traditional reliance on a block-diagonal structure creates a bottleneck: it drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters but simultaneously limits the model's expressiveness and fails to capture complex dependencies between different parts of a feature map.

To solve this paradox, researchers have turned to a novel mathematical framework that views matrix factorization through the lens of information transmission. Imagine trying to send data from a set of source nodes to destination nodes across a network; if every source needs to reach every destination, the connections must form a fully connected web. In standard matrix algebra, constructing such a dense connection requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters for a dimension $d$. However, by decomposing this task into sequential steps where information hops through intermediate layers, we can achieve full connectivity with significantly fewer resources. This insight led to the development of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), a method inspired by the butterfly structures found in the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform algorithm.

The core innovation lies in replacing rigid block-diagonal constraints with flexible butterfly graphs. Just as signal processing algorithms use butterfly motifs to efficiently compute transforms by swapping values between pairs of nodes at each step, BOFT constructs dense orthogonal matrices as products of sparse matrices arranged in a hierarchical tree-like structure. Mathematically, instead of learning one large orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ which would require roughly $d^2$ degrees of freedom, BOFT approximates $\bm{R}$ as $\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, where each component $\tilde{\bm{B}}$ is an orthogonal matrix with only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries per layer and $m = \log_2 d$ layers. This results in a total parameter count scaling as $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ rather than quadratic complexity.

This architectural shift brings substantial practical advantages beyond mere parameter savings. The block-diagonal structure imposes an artificial assumption that features must be grouped into independent buckets based on their indices, effectively ignoring cross-feature correlations unless they fall within the same small block. In contrast, the butterfly structure facilitates smooth interpolation between identity transformations and arbitrary orthogonal ones. By allowing information from any initial dimension to propagate to any final dimension through multiple stages of interaction, BOFT recovers many classic linear transforms—including Discrete Fourier Transforms and Hadamard transforms—that were previously inaccessible under strict block constraints. Furthermore, this structured hypothesis class acts as an implicit regularizer; by restricting the search space to highly structured subsets of the orthogonal group, BOFT helps prevent overfitting while maintaining sufficient capacity for complex reasoning tasks.

Empirical studies confirm that BOFT outperforms both standard fine-tuning methods and earlier versions of OFT across diverse domains including natural language processing and computer vision experiments such as ImageNet classification and text-to-image generation like Stable Diffusion control tasks. Notably, while training times may see slight increases due to repeated matrix multiplications during optimization compared to static block assignments, inference latency remains identical once trained because the learned components simply merge back into the original weight matrices seamlessly. Ultimately, BOFT demonstrates that adhering to rigorous mathematical principles regarding information flow can yield smarter representations that balance efficiency and expressivity more effectively than heuristic approaches ever could.
