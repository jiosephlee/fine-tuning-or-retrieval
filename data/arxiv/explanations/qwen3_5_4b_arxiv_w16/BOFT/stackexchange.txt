\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What is the precise role of the 'information transmission framework' in deriving BOFT, and does the graph topology strictly dictate orthogonality?
Question:
In Section 3, the paper introduces an 'information transmission view' where multiplying sparse matrices acts like transmitting data on a grid graph. The authors argue that to get a dense matrix, every node must have a path to every other node. I understand this visualization helps us count edges (trainable parameters). 

However, I am confused about the causal link to orthogonality. The paper states: 'If we find a set of edges... the orthogonality constraint will bring in additional dependency among edges.' Does this mean the graph topology *forces* orthogonality, or do we manually construct sparse matrices that are orthogonal while adhering to the graph structure? If I pick a random graph that allows all-to-all connectivity (like a butterfly), how do I guarantee every sampled matrix on those edges satisfies $\bm{B}^\top\bm{B}=\bm{I}$ without explicitly parameterizing them? Does Cayley's parameterization $$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$$ apply individually to every non-zero entry in the butterfly factors, or is there a global constraint?
Answer:
The "information transmission framework" serves as a precise graphical necessity condition: it maps the mathematical requirement that the product of sparse matrices must yield a dense orthogonal matrix to a topological constraint where information (column vectors) must flow from every node in the first layer to every node in the last layer through non-zero eigen-pathologies. In this view, a zero entry $R_{ij}$ implies a lack of direct transmission from node $j$ to node $i$. Therefore, constructing a dense matrix $\bm{R}$ is equivalent to designing a directed graph on a grid $d \times (m+1)$ where every starting node has at least one path to every destination node.

The relationship between graph topology and orthogonality is strictly coupled because orthogonality imposes rigid statistical constraints on which edges can exist and how they can be weighted, transforming an arbitrary connectivity graph into a highly constrained parameter space. As the authors note in Section 3, while the topology defines *possible* connections (the "feasible set of edge connections"), orthogonality introduces "additional dependency among edges." For instance, in a small block like $2\times 2$, selecting specific edges to be non-zero while others are zero may force diagonal elements to be fixed at $\pm 1$ or dictate specific trigonometric phases for off-diagonal elements. This means the topology does not merely *allow* orthogonality; it restricts the degree of freedom within that topology. A random graph satisfying dense connectivity often fails to support an orthogonal factorization because the remaining "free" assignments required to satisfy the rows' normalization and column's orthogonality conditions ($\bm{B}^\top\bm{B}=\bm{I}$) will not be generatable without violating sparsity or resulting in identical weights across different blocks.

Consequently, regarding your implementation of Cayley's parameterization for a butterfly graph: you cannot apply $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ individually to every non-zero entry arbitrarily, nor can you sample random matrices for those entries and expect the result to remain orthogonal. The global constraint applies to the entire block associated with that specific edge connection pattern in the butterfly decomposition. The paper employs a hierarchical construction where each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ is further decomposed into smaller sub-blocks (e.g., $2b \times 2b$ for block size $b$). Cayley's parameterization is applied strictly at these atomic permutation-invariant blocks (the smallest unit where the non-zero pattern allows full expressivity within the sparsity). Once these atomic blocks are parameterized using skew-symmetric matrices $\bm{Q}$ via Cayley's formula, their properties are preserved through permutations as they are arranged into larger butterfly components. This ensures that while edges are shared globally by inspiration from fast Fourier transforms, the orthogonality constraint is technically enforced locally and invariant under permutation globally. Thus, one does not manually construct sparse matrices satisfying orthogonality ad-hoc; rather, one samples random rotational angles (via $\bm{Q}$) for each valid atomic block defined by the fixed butterfly topology, which mathematically guarantees global orthogonality without needing explicit per-entry constraints beyond those inherent in Cayley's structure.

### Why does specifying $$O(d \log d)$$ parameters for BOFT not inherently limit expressivity to only specific transforms like FFT?
Question:
The paper claims BOFT generates a $d$-dimensional dense matrix with $$\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$$ parameters by composing butterfly factors. It also mentions that butterfly structures inherently induce ways to perform specific transforms like Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT).

My confusion lies in the trade-off between parameter count and expressivity. In standard linear algebra, specifying a dense matrix requires $$O(d^2)$$ free parameters. If we fix the parameter count to $$O(d \log d)$$ by enforcing a butterfly pattern, doesn't this severely restrict the subspace of reachable matrices? Theorem 1 claims BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT with the same block size, but can it actually approximate *any* arbitrary orthogonal matrix in $$O(d \log d)$$ parameters? Or does 'expressive' here just mean better than OFT/same sparsity pattern? Does this paper rule out using other network topologies (not butterflies) if we are careful enough?
Answer:
The perception that an $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameterization severely restricts expressivity to specific transforms like the FFT arises from conflating the *structural shape* of the butterfly matrix with its *algebraic properties* under composition. While it is true that a single "standard" butterfly factor (like those in the Cooley-Tukey FFT) implements a very specific unitary transform, the paper explicitly argues that BOFT's expressivity comes from the ability to **permute and aggregate** these factors to construct a dense orthogonal matrix.

The paper addresses this tension directly in **Section 1**, where it explains that while block-diagonal OFT enforces independent transformations on disjoint subsets of neurons, performing a service like Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) requires every neuron to contribute to and receive contributions from every other neuron globally. BOFT resolves this by treating the matrix generation as an **information transmission problem** on a grid graph (Section 2). In this framework, the sparse factors are not static filters; they are dynamic layers in a product decomposition $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_i$ where each sparse layer $\tilde{\bm{B}}_i$ is required to be orthogonal and invertible. By ensuring dense connectivity in the combined product, BOFT can synthesize any permutation of information flow required to approximate general functions, not just pre-defined frequency-domain operations.

Furthermore, **Theorem 1** (presented in Section 5) proves that purely composing standard butterfly matrices in a linear stack ($\bm{B}_n \cdots \bm{B}_1$) might be insufficient to cover the entire orthogonal group $\mathcal{O}(d)$. The theorem provides a constructive extension involving both forward butterfly matrices ($\bm{B}$) and their transposes ($\bm{B}^\top$), forming a hierarchical composition often referred to as a "kaleidoscope hierarchy." This generalized structure allows the model to traverse different subspaces of the orthogonal group, effectively allowing it to approximate *any* arbitrary orthogonal matrix within the hypothesis space defined by those parameters.

Therefore, specifying $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters does not restrict B0FT to only recovering FFT; rather, it defines a highly constrained yet sufficiently rich hypothesis class. The limited parameter count forces the model to learn smooth interpolations along specific geodesics on the manifold of orthogonal matrices defined by the butterfly topology, which induces an inductive bias beneficial for generalization. As stated in **Section 5**, this structured approach acts as "tuning knobs:" one can trade off between regularity (constraining parameters further) and expressivity by adjusting block sizes $b$ and matrix depth $m$, with Theorem 1 guaranteeing that sufficiently complex compositions can theoretically reach anywhere on the orthogonality manifold.

### How should I correctly initialize the trainable parameters in BOFT compared to LoRA or standard OFT?
Question:
In LoRA, weights are initialized at zero ($W = W_0 + B \cdot C$). In OFT, weights follow $W = R W_0$ where $R=I$. In BOFT, $W = R(m,b) W_0$.

The paper states: 'In BOFT, we initialize all the butterfly components with identity matrices (i.e., the skew-symmetric matrix is initialized as zeros in Cayley transform).' 

Given that each component $\tilde{{\bm B}}^b_{(d,j)}$ contains many free parameters (entries corresponding to edges), why can't I initialize them differently? If I set most entries of an orthogonal factor to small random values instead of exactly zero/skew-symmetric form, wouldn't it deviate from $R=W_0$ immediately without any gradients acting on them? How does this initialization strategy specifically maintain training stability or ensure that $X=Y$ initially?
Answer:
Regarding the initialization of trainable parameters in Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) versus LoRA or standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the paper's recommendation to initialize all butterfly components with identity matrices ($\mathrm{equivalent}$ to initializing the underlying skew-symmetric matrices to zero) is critical for maintaining both mathematical consistency during training and numerical stability.

In LoRA, weights are updated additively as $W = W_0 + BC$, allowing direct initialization of the low-rank components $B$ and $C$ to arbitrary small values without disrupting the base weights. In standard block-diagonal OFT, where weights follow $W = R W_0$ with $R$ being a permutation-based block-diagonal matrix (using only sign flips: $\pm 1$), an identity initialization naturally represents a discrete permutation that maps inputs directly to themselves ($X=Y$ at initialization). This works because the constraints are so rigid that any deviation immediately implies either a permutation or a substitution of vector values rather than a smooth rotation.

The scenario in BOFT is more complex due to its parametric nature. The final weight transformation is defined by the product $R(m,b) = \prod_{j=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,j)}$, where each component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,j)}$ is learned via a Cayley transform from a skew-symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$: $R = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$. If one were to initialize components with random small values or non-skew-symmetric deviations immediately, the model would not satisfy the orthogonality constraint $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(j)\top} = \mathbf{I}$. Unlike LoRA, where parameter deviations simply result in different linear transformations, violating orthogonality in BOFT causes the fine-tuned matrices to drift out of the orthogonal manifold during early steps. This violates the core principle of Boise-based training, which aims to preserve pairwise angles and spectral properties by ensuring fine-tuning stays within the special orthogonal group.

Furthermore, because BOFT uses a factorized sparsity structure derived from an information transmission view (circuitry on a grid of nodes representing matrices), these factors act like sequential gates or rotors transforming data through layers. Initializing them randomly or with non-orthogonal small values introduces asynchronous inconsistencies across these layers rather than a coherent rotational bias field (like Fourier transforms or Hadamard matrices). While gradients flow through these parameters, initializing them as identity ensures that at step zero, every vector $x$ remains transformed by an identity matrix ($Rx=x$), guaranteeing perfect alignment with pretraining immediately. The backpropagation then proceeds by smoothly rotating away from this identity baseline. Initializing arbitrary entries would effectively introduce "$\mathrm{noise}$" into this geometric trajectory before training begins, potentially destabilizing convergence and preventing the model from realizing its structured prior knowledge about how classic linear transforms should smoothly transition from identity to general orthogonal operations.

### What is the computational complexity difference between applying OFT and BOFT during inference versus training?
Question:
The abstract and introduction highlight that 'model finetuning introduces no inference latency.' This implies applying $R(W_0)^\top x$ is cheap.

Since OFT subsumes BYOT as a special case ($m=1$, single block diagonal), how much overhead does expanding from one large orthogonal matrix decomposition into $m=\log d$ smaller sparse matrices introduce?

Specifically:
1. Is there extra multiplication cost due to having more layers of sparse matrices?
2. Can we cache intermediate results across batches?
3. The paper mentions 'training runtime overhead'. Does this imply we cannot run mini-batches efficiently because data dependencies across layers prevent parallelism? How does this compare to LoRA's negligible runtime?
Answer:
### Computational Complexity of OFT vs. BOFT: Training and Inference

The computational complexity differences between Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) arise primarily from how the weight transformation matrix $R(W_0)$ is parameterized and computed, rather than the fundamental linear algebra of matrix-vector multiplication. Crucially, regarding your observation in the abstract and introduction, applying the learned transformation $R(W_0)^\top x$ at inference time is indeed computationally cheap for both methods with negligible overhead; the latency constraint applies strictly to the training phase where $R$ is optimized.

**Training Overhead: The Multiplicative Cascade**
The primary difference in training runtime stems from the presence of additional multiplicative layers introduced by BOFT's butterfly structure. OFT computes $O(R)W$ using a single orthogonal matrix factorization, whereas BOFT decomposes this dense update into $m = \log d$ sparse butterfly components $\tilde{B}_{(d,i)}$. Mathematically, during forward passes for training gradient computation, BOFT must perform $m-1$ intermediate matrix-vector multiplications ($\tilde{B}_1^T (W_0^T x)$, $\tilde{B}_2^T (\dots)$, etc.) instead of one. Since each sparse butterfly component has $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ (like a dense matrix), the cost of these multiplications scales linearly with $m$ but not quadratically. Consequently, while BOFT increases the number of forward/backward passes per iteration by a logarithmic factor ($\log d$), it avoids the quadratic explosion often associated with dense OU transformations if implemented efficiently via sparse attention mechanisms or CSR/CSC formats.

However, as noted in the conclusion section of the paper ("the training runtime overhead is slightly larger than OFT"), this implies that mini-batch efficiency is less trivial than in LoRA. In LoRA, updates are additive, allowing standard zero-initialization and straightforward batching without re-composing structures across layers. In BOFT, because data dependencies permeate these sequential sparse layers to generate the final dense effect (as described in the "Information Transmission View"), standard batch processing requires careful scheduling to ensure correct accumulation of weighted updates across all intermediate butterfly stages before computing gradients for a single sample. This prevents the naive parallelism seen in pure LoRA where weights are fully decoupled across rank matrices. Nevertheless, compared to full fine-tuning which re-parametrizes thousands of matrices for every layer change, BOFT's overhead remains bounded by $\mathcal{O}(d \log d \cdot m_{layers})$.

**Inference Cost and Caching**
During inference, both methods decohere to their final frozen weight matrices $\hat{W} = R_{learned} W_0$. At this stage, no reversal of complex sparse multiplications is needed; one simply performs standard linear operations with $\hat{W}$ just like any other parameter-efficient method like adapters or LoRA. However, if inference requires "warm-start" deviation from identity or explicit application of full rotation updates during dynamic selection, caching these pre-computed dense approximations or exploiting the sparsity of the factor components immediately after finetuning can accelerate processing. Unlike LoRA where weights are small dense matrices that fit entirely in registers for fast addition/subtraction, BOFT's factor components are very large ($\mathcal{O}(d)$ entries each). While they cannot be summed for fast addition like LoRA ranks (due to orthogonality constraining their interaction order), they can be pre-multiplied by $W_0$ *before* deployment to form efficient dense weights $\hat{W} = R_{final} W_0$, essentially rendering them indistinguishable from LoRA or adapters in terms of inference latency once trained.

**Comparison with LoRA**
Unlike LoRA's truly negligible runtime (up to constant factors), BOFT introduces a modestly higher training overhead due to these necessary cascades ($m = \log d$ layers). However, this is often an acceptable trade-off given that LOFA's negative modes are constrained by orthogonality preservation which improves generalization stability more effectively than low-rank approximations alone for large-scale fine-tuning tasks on diffusion models and transformers. This aligns with your query about data dependencies: while these dependencies increase gradient computation time slightly compared to fully independent rank updates in LoRA or additive mask modifications in prompt tuning, they do not hinder scalability in practice because $\log d$ grows very slowly (e.g., 2 steps for 8 dim blocks relative to full rank logic implies only 3-4 extra passes vs potentially 1 step). Evaluating all layers forward and backward takes more time but remains feasible within standard hyperparameter configurations due to existing optimization techniques such as mixed precision zeroshell optimization or gradient checkpointing which can reduce memory footprint associated with storing intermediate activations across multiple butterfly layers during training steps without significantly impacting overall cost efficiency significantly when compared to full finetuning
