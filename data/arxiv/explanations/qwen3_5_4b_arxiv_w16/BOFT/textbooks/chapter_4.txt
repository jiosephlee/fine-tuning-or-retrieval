### Algorithm Derivation: The Recursive Decomposition Formula

In the quest to parameterize a dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix without incurring the prohibitive $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameter cost associated with naive dense weights, we introduce the Orthogonal Butterfly architecture (BOFT). This architecture is rooted in the mathematical elegance of the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm, which utilizes a recursive structure to decompose complex global transformations into local operations. To understand the derivation of BOFT, we must first formally define the primitive building blocks that constitute this butterfly structure: the butterfly factor and the butterfly component.

Consider a dimension $k \geq 2$ that is a power of 2. We begin by defining the $k \times k$ butterfly factor, denoted as $\bm{B}^F(k)$. This matrix is constructed as a block matrix composed of four diagonal blocks, each containing a vector $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ for $i=1, 2, 3, 4$. Specifically, $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Here, $\text{diag}(\cdot)$ denotes a matrix where the input vector forms the main diagonal and zero otherwise. Geometrically and computationally, this specific pattern represents a permutation of non-zero entries typical in butterfly networks; specifically, it relates nodes from the lower half of the range to nodes in the upper half and vice-versa. However, as an arbitrary independent product of constants would not necessarily yield an orthogonal matrix, we introduce learnable parameters only within specific sub-blocks to ensure that every resulting operator $\tilde{\bm{B}}$ remains orthogonal throughout training.

To handle dimensions larger than $k$, we define a recursive construction for the $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ where $d=2^N$. This matrix is assembled by multiplying a scaling layer $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)$ with two halved butterfly matrices:
\begin{equation}
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2}) & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This recursive definition implies that to construct a dense orthogonal transformation of dimension $d$, one simply needs to concatenate the finest-level differences ($k=2$), next-coarsest ($k=4$), up to the largest scale ($k=d$). Each scale corresponds to a specific "level" in the recursion tree.

We formalize these intermediate operations as **butterfly components**. Let us define $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ as a block-diagonal matrix consisting of $d/k$ copies of $\bm{B}^F(k)$ stacked vertically and horizontally on an extended grid if necessary to match dimensions. Mathematically:
