Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Principles and Limitations of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

## Introduction to Efficient Adaptation and the Role of Orthogonal Finetuning

As foundation models grow in scale, reaching billions or even trillions of parameters, the computational cost associated with training them from scratch becomes prohibitive. Consequently, the field has shifted toward efficient adaptation paradigms that allow these massive pre-trained models to acquire new capabilities for downstream tasks without retraining the entire architecture. Among the various strategies—such as adapter tuning and prompt tuning—model finetuning stands out for its simplicity and lack of inference latency. However, standard finetuning methods often struggle to balance the need for updating weights with the imperative to preserve the rich semantic knowledge encoded during pre-training. To address this tension, a more principled approach was developed known as Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). Unlike traditional methods that treat weight updates heuristically or through additive low-rank perturbations, OFT leverages the geometric properties of linear algebra to ensure that the relationship between neurons is maintained rigorously throughout the optimization process. This chapter delves into the theoretical foundations of OFT, exploring how it preserves pairwise angles between neurons through orthogonal transformations and detailing its mathematical implementation via Cayley parameterization. Furthermore, we critically examine the limitations inherent in OFT's reliance on block-diagonal structures, highlighting issues related to parameter efficiency and expressiveness that motivate subsequent research into more advanced parameterizations like Orthogonal Butterfly.

## Fundamental Principle: Preserving Pairwise Angles Between Neurons

The core philosophy driving Orthogonal Finetuning stems from a deep understanding of how neural networks encode information during their initial training phase. Large-scale models learn complex representations where neurons function as features or filters; crucially, the interaction between pairs of neurons defines the model's capacity to distinguish different classes and handle diverse inputs. In a linear layer $\bm{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $d$ is the hidden dimension and $n$ is the input dimension (or vice versa depending on convention), each row vector represents a neuron's projection capability onto the input space. When two rows are updated arbitrarily during standard gradient descent, they may rotate relative to each other unpredictably. This rotation alters the cosine similarity—the angle—between neurons, potentially destroying subtle correlations learned during pre-training that are essential for generalization.

OFT posits that if we constrain all row vectors within a layer to undergo an orthogonal transformation together, their pairwise angles remain invariant regardless of how far they move in parameter space. Mathematically, let $\bm{W}^0$ be a pretrained weight matrix consisting of rows $\bm{w}_1^0, \dots, \bm{w}_n^0$. If we apply an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in O(d)$ such that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ to these rows simultaneously (effectively transforming $\bm{W}^0$ to $\tilde{\bm{W}} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top$), then for any pair of indices $i$ and $j$:
$$
\cos(\theta_{ij}') = \frac{\langle r_i\bm{w}_i^0, r_j\bm{w}_j^0\rangle}{||r_i\bm{w}_i^0|| ||r_j\bm{w}_j^0||} = \frac{\langle r_i^\top r_j^\top \rangle}{1/2...}
$$
Actually simpler: The dot product between any two transformed rows satisfies $\langle (\bm{R}\boldsymbol{\alpha}), (\bm{R}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \rangle = \boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top (\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}) \boldsymbol{\beta} = \boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}$ because $\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta}$ are column vectors here? Wait careful with dimensions. Let's stick to rows being vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$. If we premultiply by $R$, then $(RW)_i = R w_i$. Then inner product is $(RW)_i . (RW)_j = w_i.R^\top R w_j = w_i.w_j$. Yes.
Thus:
$$
(R\mathbf{x})^\top(R\mathbf{x}) = \|x\|^2
$$
And cross terms vanish similarly? No wait cross term: $(Rx)^T(Ry) = x^T R^T R y = x^T I y = x^T y$. Correct.
So yes: **Pairwise Inner Products Are Preserved.**
By maintaining these inner products constant throughout training, OFT ensures that no new correlations are created artificially and none are destroyed accidentally. This acts as a strong regularization mechanism: it forces any changes in behavior to arise solely from scaling magnitudes or rotations aligned with global constraints rather than arbitrary drifts between feature detectors. Empirical studies have shown that this strict adherence to angular consistency leads to more stable training dynamics and superior generalization compared to unconstrained finetuning or even low-rank adapters in certain domains like image generation control.
