# The 1.58-Bit Revolution: How Ternary Logic Can Compete with Full-Precision AI

For the majority of the last decade, the landscape of Large Language Models (LLMs) has been defined by a single trajectory: bigger models, trained on more data, using higher precision floating-point numbers. We have moved comfortingly from 8-bit training to the industry standard of 16-bit Floating Point (FP16) and its derivative BFloat16. FP16 offers a sweet spot between dynamic range and storage efficiency, but it comes with an unsustainable cost. As these models scale into the hundreds of billions of parameters, the energy required for their operation becomes staggering, while their memory footprint makes deployment on anything less than massive cloud clusters prohibitively expensive.

A new paper, titled "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits," challenges this paradigm by introducing a model variant called **BitNet b1.58**. While 1-bit models have existed in theoretical forms for a long time, this work introduces a specific configuration where every single parameter in the model is restricted to three discrete values: -1, 0, or +1. This ternary representation effectively uses **1.58 bits** per parameter in the binary system without rounding up to full 2 bits. The central claim of this research is that if we engineer the architecture and hardware correctly to exploit this constraint, we can match the performance of full-precision models while achieving massive gains in speed, memory efficiency, and energy consumption.

This idea rests on a fundamental observation about how LLMs compute information. The dominant cost in any LLM inference pipeline is matrix multiplication operations known as $\text{nn.Linear}$ layers (often referred to as linear attention). In standard FP16 implementations, these layers require high-precision additions and multiplications ($FP32$ or $FP16$ arithmetic). However, if a weight matrix is quantized to ternary values, the mathematical operation changes drastically. The multiplication by a +1 or -1 value becomes a simple sign flip (bitwise XOR), and multiplying by zero nullifies the output entirely. Consequently, the computation reduces almost entirely to additions of integers ($INT8$). Since integer addition requires significantly fewer transistors and roughly an order of magnitude less energy than floating-point multiplication on modern chips, this shift alone promises dramatic throughput improvements.

However, reducing precision often leads to catastrophic performance drops due to information loss during quantization. For example, many text descriptions are not black and white; they contain shades of gray that traditional integer rounding struggles to capture systematically. BitNet b1.58 counters this intuition through this specific architecture: **BitLinear** layers combined with 8-bit activations allow the model to retain specific nuances that make it effectively "Bloom." Unlike naive quantization which compresses values into intervals like $\{-24740000 \dots -24733499\}$ mapped to $\{-1 \dots +1\}$ or $[0 \dots 3]$ mapped to $\{0 \dots +3\}$, BitNet b1.58 preserves meaningful weight magnitudes within its supported variable set $\{-1 \dots +3\}$. This allows for explicit feature filtering via zero weights while maintaining expressive power through non-zero weights scaled by magnitudes $[0 \dots Q_b]$.

The innovation introduced here is **absmean quantization**, which ensures that every learned weight adheres strictly to the set $\{-1 \times n_A-\gamma \setminus n_A+\gamma+-\gamma+-\gamma+ \dots n_A+\gamma\}$.
