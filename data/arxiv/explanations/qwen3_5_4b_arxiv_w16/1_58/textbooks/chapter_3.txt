# Forcing Standardized Backbones: Why RMSNorm was Chosen over LayerNorm

The widespread adoption of large language models (LLMs) is inextricably linked to their interoperability within the open-source ecosystem. For the introductory student, it is crucial to understand that "standardization" in deep learning architecture does not merely mean using common functions; it often involves deliberate trade-offs between performance tailoring and community adoption. $LLaMA$ models, which have become the de-facto standard for open-source transformers, rely heavily on the Layer Normalization (LN) block. This standard relies on calculating a batch norm mean and variance for each token $x$ in a sequence matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, defined as $\mu_B = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i x_i$ and $\sigma_B^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i (x_i - \mu_B)^2$. The normalized output is then computed as $\tilde{x}_i = \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}}$. However, when the domain shifts to extreme low-bit quantization schemes like the ternary approach used in $\text{BitNet b1.58}$, such standard operations present significant implementation challenges.

In a full-precision 16-bit environment, the values of $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ are explicit floating-point numbers that can be precisely represented. In contrast, when weights are constrained to ternary values $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and activations to integers (typically 8-bit), maintaining a precise global or batch normalization statistic across thousands of discrete value samples can introduce errors due to rounding during forward propagation. These errors can accumulate non-linearly as they pass through subsequent layers in the network. Furthermore, standard LN requires division operations to normalize the activations by their standard deviation. In a computation paradigm dominated by integer arithmetic—as seen in BitNet's optimization where floating-point multiplication is decoupled from matrix multiplication—performing high-precision division at every layer is computationally expensive and difficult to optimize on specialized hardware designed for adders rather than dividers.

To resolve these issues while maintaining compatibility with the vast repository of tools built around $LLaMA$, the authors of $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ opted for Root Mean Square Layer Normalization (RMSNorm> rather than the standard Layer Norm used in $LLaMA$. RMSNorm simplifies the normalization process by dropping both the mean ($\mu_B$) and the zero-mean subscale bias ($\epsilon_{mean}$). The mathematical formulation of RMSNorm transforms an input $h$ with values of dimension $d$ into a normalized representation $h'$ as follows:
$$ h'_i = \frac{h_i}{\sqrt{\text{rms}(h)^2 + \epsilon}} + m $$
where $\text{rms}(h)^2 = \frac{1}{d} \sum_h h_i^2$, $m$ is usually set to zero for stability during training without any explicit bias term learning within this specific context, and $\epsilon$ is a small scalar added for numerical stability before division or square root operation. The key advantage here is that RMSNorm only relies on squaring the inputs and taking their root mean square. Since our activations are restricted to integer ranges (specifically scaled integers), squaring an activation value is an operation that maps perfectly into integer arithmetic logic without loss of precision relative to floating-point norms. This aligns with BitNet's broader philosophy of minimizing floating-point operations and maximizing utilization of integer adders and multipliers found in custom accelerators or highly optimized software backends like vLLM.

Moreover, changing from Batch Norm (used in original $LLaMA_equivalent concepts though effectively replaced by RMSNorm) to RMSNorm eliminates the dependency on batch statistics across varying sequence lengths during inference batches of different sizes without requiring mini-batch statistics adaptation strategies typically found in Batch Norm implementations using statistics generated over multiple acceleration units per token as proposed by BitNet's design guidelines> Excluding Mean Elimination> By removing statistical dependencies on dynamic input data (\emph{i.e.,} eliminating means), we prevent gradient perturbation caused by numerical noise introduced by quantization noise propagating through complex normalization layers with batch statistics calculations which are then sampled per token< .

By adopting RMSNorm, BitNet b1.58 forces a standardized backbone that respects both computational constraints and software ecosystem compatibility, serving as an analogy for how modern embedded AI systems prune unnecessary statistical computations when hardware resources are scarce but output fidelity must remain high despite aggressive quantization strategies applied during model construction phases involving quantization functions like absmean quantization shown earlier in bitnet papers> . Instead of being shackled by rigid standards that assume full precision dynamics, BitNet evolves the standard into a form specifically tuned for low-bit regimes, thereby ensuring that academic research can seamlessly transition from notebook experiments to production-grade deployable systems used in edge devices< .
