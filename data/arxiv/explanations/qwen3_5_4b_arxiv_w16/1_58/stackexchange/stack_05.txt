Answer:
The question regarding why non-linear processing is constrained during write-back (training) rather than handled entirely at inference time stems from a fundamental misunderstanding of what post-training quantization aims to achieve and how the provided paper, "The Era of 1-bit LLMs," defines its own architecture.

First, it is crucial to clarify that the paper describes **BitNet b1.58** as a model trained from scratch with specific low-precision constraints, not merely a post-training quantized version of a full-precision model like SmoothQuant. The paper states in Section 2 that the model has "1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations" and explicitly mentions using an `absmean` quantization function during training to constrain weights to $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. Because the weights are hard-constrained to ternary values $\{-1, 0, +1\}$ throughout training, the model's property function does not have access to finer-grained gradients or continuous values; it must learn representations directly in this discrete manifold. Consequently, the "non-linear processing" (specifically activations passed through functions like SwiGLU or RMSNorm) cannot be arbitrarily constrained later because the underlying weight space that shapes those activations was already frozen into discrete steps during training. If one were to rely solely on inference-time clipping for non-linearities without adjusting the training process to align with such constraints, the model would fail because its learned parameters do not interact correctly with cutoff activation values.

Secondly, regarding your mention of SMOOTQUANT: SMOOTQUANT is a technique designed for **post-training** quantization where the model is originally trained with full precision (FP32/FP16), and rounding errors introduced during weight scaling are mitigated by smoothing the non-linear activation functions *during* the quantization phase (often involving dynamic gradients or specific loss terms) so that gradients can still flow effectively through the smoothed operations. However, BitNet b1.58 operates under a different paradigm; it is an architecture designed where weights are permanently ternary $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. In this specific design, the inclusion of a $0$ value allows for explicit feature filtering, which changes how information flows compared to standard binary networks without zero nodes.

The paper explicitly details that \text{BitNet b1.58} reduces activations from 16 bits down to **8 bits** during both training and inference. This choice is strategic: reducing activations after they leave a layer (via write-back) allows them to be quantized anywhere in the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ without needing zero-point adjustment complexities often found when clamping activations *within* non-linear layers at epoch end or test time. By quantizing activations outside specific non-linear functions (i.e., not strictly inside every individual activation-dependent layer operation in a way that would destroy gradient flow), the model avoids performance degradation caused by biases in flattened functions or specific thresholds that would arise if every single non-linearity were tied directly to an extreme quantization floor/ceiling.

Finally, your concern about handling constraints entirely at inference time ignores two key factors highlighted in this work:
1.  **Hardware Trade-offs**: The goal of BitNet b1.58 is drastic reduction in energy consumption and memory usage by replacing FP32/BF16 operations with simple integer addition and negligible multiplication logic ($O(1)$). If complex smoothing operations or adaptive thresholds were required at inference time based on activation magnitudes (as might be necessary if trainable smoothing were omitted), it would undo the computational efficiency gains promised by removing multiplications from \textit{nn.Linear}.
2.  **Training Paradigm Shift**: As noted in Section 2 ("All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"), this work posits that we can train LLMs fundamentally differently—using ternary weights—to match full-precision performance on standard tasks while offering massive energy savings over billions of tokens trained on datasets like RedPajama. Relying on heuristic smoothing at inference only could match performance for small models but fails at scale because it does not change how data actually flows through the network structure during its development cycle or deployment efficiency compared to native support for long sequences via reduced activation precision ($8$ vs $16$ bits).

In summary, non-linearity handling is intrinsically linked to how BitNet b1.58 was trained with ternary weights; attempting to decouple these into simple post-training inference clips would break the equivalence between 1.58-bit and full-precision tasks observed in their experiments because the signal propagation path depends on those specific structural constraints being present from training onwards rather than being retrofitted later for computational shortcuts only achievable at much higher energy costs or batch sizes not supported by typical deployment scenarios described in Section 3 ("Native Support of Long Sequence").
