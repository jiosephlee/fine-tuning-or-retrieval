### CHAPTER 1 — PRIOR KNOWLEDGE AND FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES

Welcome to the foundational concepts that underpin modern language model alignment. Before we dive into the specifics of Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO), we need to build a toolkit. This chapter will walk you through the essential mathematical and conceptual building blocks: how we model language with probabilities, how we can mathematically represent human preferences, and how we can change a model's behaviour without breaking it. Think of this as gathering the ingredients and tools before we start cooking.

#### 1.1 The Language of Models: Probability and Autoregressive Generation

At its core, a language model is a machine for calculating probabilities. Given a prompt or context, which we'll call \(x\), and a potential response, \(y\), the model's fundamental job is to compute the conditional probability of generating \(y\) given \(x\):
\[
p(y \mid x).
\]
This simple expression reads: "the probability of response \(y\) given prompt \(x\)." A higher probability means the model thinks the response is a more likely or fitting continuation of the prompt.

Because these probabilities are often tiny numbers, multiplying them can lead to numerical errors. It's much more stable and convenient to work with their logarithms. The logarithm turns multiplication into addition and division into subtraction, which simplifies many calculations. We'll frequently use the identity:
\[
\log \frac{p_1}{p_2} \;=\; \log p_1 \;-\; \log p_2.
\]

How does a model actually compute the probability of an entire sequence of words? Modern language models are **autoregressive**, meaning they generate a response one token (a word or part of a word) at a time. If a response \(y\) is composed of tokens \((y_1, y_2, \dots, y_T)\), the model calculates the total probability as a product of step-by-step predictions:
\[
p(y \mid x) \;=\; p(y_1 \mid x) \cdot p(y_2 \mid x, y_1) \cdot \dots \cdot p(y_T \mid x, y_{<T}) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{T} p\!\big(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}\big).
\]
In plain English, the probability of the whole sentence is the probability of the first word, times the probability of the second word given the first, times the probability of the third word given the first two, and so on.

The initial training of these models, whether from scratch (pre-training) or on a curated dataset (supervised fine-tuning, or SFT), typically uses **maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)**. This means we adjust the model's parameters, \(\theta\), to maximise the log-probability of the correct responses in our training dataset \(\mathcal{D}\):
\[
\max_{\theta} \sum_{(x,y)\in \mathcal{D}} \log p_{\theta}(y \mid x).
\]
The model that results from this SFT process is our starting point. We call it the **reference policy**, and we'll denote it by \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\). It’s a capable model, but it’s not yet aligned with the nuanced preferences we want to instil.

#### 1.2 Mathematically Modeling Preferences

To improve upon \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), we need a way to teach it what humans prefer. This usually involves comparing two different responses to the same prompt and judging which one is better. This is a binary choice, and we can model it with simple, powerful tools.

The **logistic** or **sigmoid function**, \(\sigma(z)\), is perfect for this. It takes any real number \(z\) and squashes it into a probability between 0 and 1:
\[
\sigma(z) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}.
\]
If \(z\) is very large and positive, \(\sigma(z)\) is close to 1. If \(z\) is very large and negative, \(\sigma(z)\) is close to 0. If \(z\) is 0, \(\sigma(z)\) is exactly 0.5.

This function is the heart of the **Bradley-Terry model**, a classic framework for describing pairwise preferences. The model assumes that every candidate response (let's say, \(a\) and \(b\)) has a latent, unobserved "quality score," which we'll call \(r(a)\) and \(r(b)\). The probability that a human prefers response \(a\) over response \(b\) (denoted \(a \succ b\)) is modelled as the sigmoid of the *difference* in their scores:
\[
\Pr(a \succ b) \;=\; \sigma\!\big( r(a) - r(b) \big).
\]
This is intuitive: if \(a\) has a much higher score than \(b\), the difference is large and positive, and the probability of \(a\) being preferred approaches 1. If their scores are equal, the difference is zero, and the probability is 0.5—a coin flip. Notice that only the score difference matters; the absolute scores are irrelevant.

*The Plackett-Luce model extends this idea from comparing pairs to creating a full ranking of multiple items, but the core concept of scores translating to probabilities remains the same.*

#### 1.3 The Need for Regularisation: Don't Stray Too Far

Once we have preference data, we can start training our model to produce responses with higher "quality scores." However, if we only focus on maximising this new objective, the model might learn to exploit it in undesirable ways. For example, it might discover that simply writing longer, more verbose answers tends to get higher scores, leading to unhelpful, rambling responses. This is a form of "overfitting" to the preference data.

To prevent this, we need **regularisation**. We want the model to learn from preferences, but we also want it to *stay close* to the trusted, known-good distribution of our reference model, \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). The tool for measuring the "distance" between two probability distributions is the **Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence**. For two discrete distributions \(p\) and \(q\), it is defined as:
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) \;=\; \sum_{y\in \mathcal{Y}} p(y)\,\log \frac{p(y)}{q(y)}.
\]
A KL divergence of 0 means the distributions are identical. The larger the KL divergence, the more different they are. By adding a KL penalty to our training objective, we can incentivise the model to satisfy the new preference objective while discouraging it from drifting too far from the sensible language patterns learned by \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\).

#### 1.4 The Ideal Policy: A Theoretical Solution

A powerful idea from reinforcement learning frames this trade-off between reward and regularisation as a single, elegant optimisation problem. Let's imagine we have a reward function \(r(x,y)\) that assigns a quality score to each response \(y\) for a prompt \(x\). Our goal is to find an optimal policy, \(\pi_r\), that maximises this reward while staying close to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), as measured by the KL divergence.

The theoretical solution to this problem is surprisingly neat. The optimal policy takes the form:
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(r(x,y)/\beta\big)}{Z(x)}.
\]
Here, \(\beta\) is a parameter that controls the strength of the KL regularisation (a smaller \(\beta\) means we care more about the reward, and a larger \(\beta\) means we care more about staying close to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\)). The policy is our reference policy, re-weighted by the exponentiated reward.

However, there's a major practical problem: the term \(Z(x)\), known as the **partition function**:
\[
Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(r(x,y)/\beta\big).
\]
To calculate \(Z(x)\), we would have to sum over *every possible response sequence y*, an astronomically large number. This makes computing the optimal policy directly intractable. As we will see, the magic of DPO is that it finds a way to optimise this objective *without ever needing to compute* \(Z(x)\).

#### 1.5 The Key Quantities for DPO

To pave the way for DPO, let's define two crucial quantities. First, we define the **log-probability ratio**, \(\Delta\), which measures how much our new, trainable policy (\(\pi\)) has changed its opinion on a response compared to the reference policy (\(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\)):
\[
\Delta(x,y) \;=\; \log \pi(y\mid x) \;-\; \log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x).
\]
A positive \(\Delta(x,y)\) means our new policy \(\pi\) likes the response \(y\) more than the reference policy did.

Next, consider a preference pair from our dataset, consisting of a winning response \(y_w\) and a losing response \(y_l\) for the same prompt \(x\). We define the **margin** as the difference in their log-probability ratios:
\[
m(x;y_w,y_l) \;=\; \Delta(x,y_w) \;-\; \Delta(x,y_l).
\]
This single number captures everything we need to know: how much more our policy has learned to favour the winner over the loser, relative to its starting point. A large, positive margin is exactly what we want our training to encourage.

#### 1.6 Evaluation and Practical Considerations

How do we know if our efforts are successful?
1.  **Reward-KL Frontier:** When we can approximate a reward function (e.g., using a sentiment classifier), we can plot the model's average reward against its KL divergence from \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). A better alignment method will push this curve up (higher reward) and to the left (lower KL divergence).
2.  **Win Rate:** For open-ended tasks, we compare the outputs of our new model against a baseline model and have humans or a strong AI judge which is better. The percentage of times our model's outputs are preferred is its **win rate**.

Finally, theory is nothing without robust practice. The quality of preference data is paramount. Labels can be noisy, and models can learn to exploit superficial patterns, like rewarding verbosity. Careful data collection, clear instructions for human labellers, and rigorous validation are essential. On the engineering side, disciplined logging of key metrics during training—like the average margin, the KL divergence, and win rates on a held-out test set—is critical for diagnosing problems and understanding what the model is learning.

With these tools in hand—conditional probabilities, preference models, KL regularisation, and the key log-ratio definitions—we are now ready to tackle the main subject: Direct Preference Optimisation.
