\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### Why does multiplying by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ help with FP8 quantization error?
Question:
The paper mentions 'incoherent processing' where $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ are multiplied by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ before being quantized to FP8. The authors claim this 'spreads out' outliers. I understand the math $(\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K} \mathbf{M})^{\top} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^{\top}$, but I don't understand the intuition: how does rotating the coordinate system of the vectors actually make the quantization to 8-bits more accurate?
Answer:
The intuition behind "incoherent processing" lies in how quantization handles the dynamic range of values. In large language models, activations often exhibit "outlier features"—a small number of dimensions that have magnitudes significantly larger than the rest. When you quantize a tensor to FP8 using per-tensor scaling, the scaling factor must be large enough to accommodate these outliers to avoid clipping. Because FP8 has a very limited mantissa (only 3 bits in the e4m3 format), a scaling factor driven by a massive outlier forces the majority of the other, smaller values to be rounded to zero or represented with very poor precision. This creates a high quantization error for the bulk of the data.

Multiplying by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ acts as a change of basis that "spreads" the energy of these outliers across all dimensions. Technically, each element in the transformed matrix $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}$ is a weighted sum of the original elements in $\mathbf{Q}$. Because $\mathbf{M}$ is random and orthogonal, it effectively rotates the coordinate system so that no single axis is aligned with the outlier. Instead of one dimension having a value of $100$ and others having $1$, the rotation distributes that magnitude, resulting in many dimensions having moderate values (e.g., all dimensions moving toward a value of $10$).

By smoothing out these spikes, the dynamic range within each dimension is significantly reduced. This allows the FP8 quantization scaling factor to be much tighter and more representative of the overall data distribution, rather than being dictated by a few extreme values. This reduction in the relative difference between the maximum and minimum values in the tensor minimizes the precision loss during rounding. As the paper notes, this technique, combined with block quantization, allows $\text{FlashAttention-3}$ to achieve up to $2.6\times$ lower numerical error than standard per-tensor FP8 quantization in the presence of outlier features, all while preserving the final result because the orthogonality ensures that $(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^{\top} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{\top} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$.
