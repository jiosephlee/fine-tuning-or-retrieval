\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Introduction to Controlled Text-to-Image Generation

# Overview of Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

Large-scale text-to-image diffusion models have fundamentally transformed the landscape of generative artificial intelligence, demonstrating an impressive ability to synthesize photorealistic images from natural language descriptions. These models leverage the diffusion process to iteratively refine noise into high-fidelity visual content guided by text embeddings. Depending on their architectural choices, these models typically operate in one of two primary domains: the pixel space or the latent space. For instance, models such as GLIDE and Imagen perform the diffusion process directly within the pixel space. In these architectures, the way the text encoder is handled varies; GLIDE trains the text encoder jointly with a diffusion prior using paired text-image data, whereas Imagen utilizes a frozen pretrained text encoder to maintain stability.

Alternatively, models like Stable Diffusion and DALL-E 2 operate within a latent space to reduce computational complexity while maintaining image quality. Stable Diffusion utilizes a VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook that serves as its latent space, while DALL-E 2 employs CLIP to construct a joint latent embedding space that represents both images and text. Despite these different architectural approaches, the common goal remains the same: to translate the semantic meaning of a text prompt into a coherent and visually accurate image. These models are often considered "model-agnostic" in the context of certain finetuning strategies, meaning that the fundamental principles of adaptation can be applied across various diffusion-based frameworks.

# The Ambiguity of Text Guidance

While text-to-image diffusion models are capable of generating stunning visuals, relying solely on text prompts presents a significant challenge: ambiguity. Text is a discrete and often imprecise medium for describing complex visual scenes. A user might provide a prompt that is conceptually clear but lacks the specificity required for fine-grained control over the spatial arrangement, precise geometry, or exact identity of objects within the generated image. This inherent limitation means that text guidance is often insufficient to provide the accurate, detailed control necessary for specialized downstream tasks.

Because text prompts cannot explicitly define every pixel or coordinate, the model must make a series of probabilistic guesses about the intended layout and appearance. When a user requires a specific output—such as a precise pose for a human face or the exact boundaries of a building—textual descriptions like "a person looking left" or "a city skyline" are too coarse. This gap between the user's intent and the model's interpretation creates a demand for more robust control mechanisms that can supplement text with additional, more precise signals to guide the generative process.

# Subject-Driven Generation: Concept and Goals

Subject-driven generation is a specialized task designed to overcome the limitations of general text-to-image models when a user wants to generate images of a specific, unique subject. In a standard text-to-image setup, a prompt for "a dog" will generate a generic dog. However, in subject-driven generation, the goal is to provide the model with a few images of a specific subject—such as a pet dog or a specific piece of art—and then generate new images of that same subject in entirely different contexts.

The primary goal of this task is to maintain the identity-related details of the subject while allowing the context to be modified via a text prompt. For example, if a user provides three images of their specific dog, they should be able to prompt the model to generate "that dog wearing a space suit on Mars" without the dog's unique features being lost or replaced by a generic dog. This requires the model to learn the specific visual characteristics of the subject from a very small dataset (often just a few images) and integrate that knowledge into the existing weights of the diffusion model without destroying the model's ability to generate diverse and high-quality backgrounds.

# Controllable Generation: Concept and Goals

Controllable generation extends the capabilities of diffusion models by introducing additional control signals beyond the text prompt. While subject-driven generation focuses on "who" or "what" is in the image, controllable generation focuses on "where" and "how" elements are structured. The goal is to enable the model to take in auxiliary signals—such as Canny edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks—and ensure the generated image strictly adheres to these constraints while still following the overall theme of the text prompt.

There are several key applications of this approach. In Canny edge-to-image (C2I) tasks, the model uses a rough outline of an image to determine the structure of the output. In segmentation map-to-image (S2I) tasks, a map designating which areas of the image belong to specific classes (e.g., "sky," "road," "building") dictates the layout. In landmark-to-face (L2F) tasks, specific points on a human face are used to control the exact pose and expression of the generated person. The ultimate objective of controllable generation is to achieve high-fidelity images that exhibit a strong "control consistency," meaning that if one were to extract the control signal from the generated image, it should closely match the original input signal.

# The Fundamental Trade-off: Training Efficiency vs. Generalizability Preservation

A central challenge in adapting large-scale diffusion models to these tasks is the tension between training efficiency and the preservation of generalizability. When finetuning a model for a specific subject or control signal, there is a risk of "model collapse" or the loss of the pretrained generative performance. The ideal finetuning method must satisfy two primary desiderata. First, it must be training-efficient, meaning it should require a small number of trainable parameters and a low number of training epochs to reach convergence. Second, it must preserve generalizability, ensuring that the model retains the high-fidelity, diverse, and realistic generation capabilities it acquired during its massive pretraining phase.

Traditionally, practitioners have attempted to balance this by updating neuron weights using a very small learning rate or by adding small, re-parameterized components to the model. These methods implicitly assume that a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights $\bm{M}$ and the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$, denoted as $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0 \|$, corresponds to better preservation of the pretrained abilities. However, this assumption is often insufficient. Simply minimizing the Euclidean difference does not fully capture the degree of semantic preservation. To truly preserve the "knowledge" of the network, a more structural measure is needed—one that looks beyond raw weight values to the relationships between neurons, such as the angles and directions they form on a hypersphere. This realization drives the need for more principled finetuning strategies that can modify the model's behavior without destroying its underlying semantic structure.

Foundations of Model Finetuning

# Standard Gradient Descent Finetuning

In the context of large text-to-image diffusion models, finetuning is the process of adapting a pretrained model to a specific downstream task. A common objective for these tasks is subject-driven generation, where a model is taught to generate a specific subject given a few reference images, or controllable generation, where the model is adapted to follow additional signals like segmentation maps or Canny edges. The most basic approach to achieving this is through standard gradient descent finetuning. In this paradigm, the neuron weights of the pretrained model are updated using a small learning rate to minimize a specific loss function, such as a reconstruction loss.

The intuition behind using a small learning rate is to ensure that the model does not deviate too far from its original state. From a mathematical perspective, standard finetuning can be viewed as an implicit attempt to minimize the Euclidean distance between the finetuned model weights $\bm{M}$ and the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$, represented as $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$. By limiting the magnitude of the updates, practitioners hope to steer the model toward the new task while keeping the broad generative capabilities acquired during the massive pretraining phase.

However, this approach is often too flexible. Because standard gradient descent allows weights to move in any direction in the high-dimensional parameter space, it lacks a principled constraint to guarantee that the pretrained generative performance is preserved. This lack of structural guidance often makes the training process unstable and highly sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the number of training epochs and the specific learning rate.

# Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA): Principles and Constraints

To address the inefficiencies and instabilities of full-weight finetuning, researchers introduced parameter-efficient methods, most notably Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). Rather than updating all the neurons in a layer, LoRA introduces a small additive component to the pretrained weights. This method is based on the hypothesis that the update to the weight matrix can be represented by a low-rank structure. Specifically, LoRA assumes that the difference between the finetuned weights $\bm{M}$ and the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$ has a rank $r'$, where $r'$ is a small integer.

The primary advantage of LoRA is its extreme parameter efficiency. For a weight matrix of size $d \times n$, a full update would require modifying $d \times n$ parameters. In contrast, by exploiting the low-rank constraint, LoRA only requires the optimization of $r'(d+n)$ parameters. This significantly reduces the memory footprint and computational requirements during the finetuning process.

Intuitively, LoRA prevents the pretrained weight matrix from shifting dramatically by restricting the degrees of freedom available for the update. By forcing the change to occur within a low-rank subspace, the method aims to maintain a level of stability that standard gradient descent lacks. Despite this, LoRA still operates on the assumption that the additive weight update is the best way to adapt the model, and it does not provide a provable guarantee that the intrinsic semantic structures of the pretrained model remain intact.

# The Problem of Semantic Drift and Model Collapse

A recurring challenge in finetuning diffusion models is the tension between flexibility and regularity. When a model is too flexible, it is prone to semantic drift and model collapse. Semantic drift occurs when the model begins to lose its ability to follow text prompts or maintain the identity of a subject because the finetuning process has "overwritten" the pretrained knowledge. Model collapse is a more severe form of this failure, where the model ceases to generate high-fidelity or diverse images, instead producing distorted or repetitive outputs.

These phenomena are clearly observable in subject-driven generation tasks. For instance, using a method like DreamBooth, a model might successfully learn the identity of a specific subject but lose its "prompt fidelity," meaning it can no longer accurately place that subject in a new context described by a text prompt. Conversely, LoRA might preserve the general image quality but fail to preserve the specific identity of the subject, or it might misinterpret the text prompt entirely—such as generating "yellow fur" when the prompt specifies a "yellow shirt."

The root cause of these failures is that existing finetuning strategies do not have a mechanism to quantify or preserve the structural relationship between neurons. When the weights are updated—even with a small learning rate or a low-rank constraint—the relative orientations of the neurons can change. If these orientations shift too drastically, the semantic knowledge embedded in the pretrained model is destroyed, leading to a collapse in generative performance.

# Measuring Preservation: Euclidean Distance vs. Structural Measures

The fundamental disagreement between traditional finetuning and more advanced structural approaches lies in how they measure the "distance" between a pretrained model and its finetuned version. Most existing methods rely on the Euclidean distance $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$ as a proxy for semantic preservation. The underlying assumption is that a smaller Euclidean difference implies that the finetuned model will behave more like the pretrained model. However, this assumption is often insufficient because Euclidean distance does not capture the geometric structure of the neurons.

A more effective way to characterize the difference between models is through structural measures, specifically hyperspherical energy. This approach is based on the observation that semantic information in neural networks is primarily encoded in the angles (or directions) of the neurons rather than their absolute magnitudes. To quantify this, we can project neurons onto a unit hypersphere. If $\bm{w}_i$ is the $i$-th neuron, its normalized version is $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$.

The hyperspherical energy $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ of a layer is defined as the sum of the hyperspherical similarities—such as the inverse of the distance—between all pairwise neurons in that layer:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

This measure captures the level of neuron uniformity and the pairwise relational structure on the unit hypersphere. While Euclidean distance focuses on the absolute position of weights in space, hyperspherical energy focuses on the relative angles between neurons. The hypothesis is that a finetuned model preserves the pretrained generative ability most effectively when the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned model and the pretrained model is minimized:

$$\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\|$$

By focusing on the preservation of these pairwise angles, the model can adapt to new tasks (by changing the overall direction of the neurons) without destroying the internal structural relationships that allow it to generate high-fidelity, photorealistic images.

The Geometry of Neurons: Hyperspherical Energy

# The Unit Hypersphere and Neuron Normalization

To understand how a neural network stores and processes information, it is helpful to view the weights of a layer not just as a collection of numbers, but as a set of vectors in a high-dimensional space. In a fully connected layer, the weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ consists of $n$ individual neurons, where each neuron $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a vector in $d$-dimensional space. One of the most powerful ways to analyze these neurons is to focus on their direction rather than their magnitude. This is achieved through neuron normalization, where each neuron $\bm{w}_i$ is projected onto a unit hypersphere. The normalized neuron is defined as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$.

By normalizing neurons in this way, we essentially treat every neuron as a point on the surface of a unit hypersphere in $\mathbb{R}^d$. This perspective is grounded in the observation that the direction of neurons can capture the most critical information from data. Research into architectures like SphereNet has demonstrated that training a neural network with all neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere yields a capacity comparable to standard networks and can even result in better generalizability. This suggests that the angular relationship between neurons is a primary driver of the network's ability to represent complex patterns, making the unit hypersphere a natural geometric setting for studying semantic information.

# Defining Hyperspherical Energy (HE)

Once we have mapped neurons to the unit hypersphere, we need a way to quantify the structural relationship between them. This is where the concept of hyperspherical energy (HE) becomes essential. Hyperspherical energy is a measure of the uniformity of neurons on the unit hypersphere. It is formally defined as the sum of the hyperspherical similarity between all pairwise neurons within the same layer. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}$, the hyperspherical energy is calculated as:

$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$

In this equation, the term $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ represents the similarity between two normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$. When neurons are spaced far apart and uniformly across the hypersphere, the energy is minimized. Conversely, when neurons are clustered together, the energy increases. This metric allows us to characterize the "relational structure" of a layer.

A critical property of hyperspherical energy is its invariance under orthogonal transformations. If we apply the same orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ to all neurons in a layer—such that $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$—the pairwise angles between the neurons are preserved. Consequently, the hyperspherical energy of the finetuned model remains identical to that of the pretrained model. This invariance is the theoretical foundation for Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), as it provides a mathematical guarantee that the intrinsic structural relationship of the pretrained neurons will not be destroyed during adaptation to a downstream task.

# Angular Similarity vs. Magnitude: Which stores semantics?

A fundamental question in the design of finetuning strategies is whether the semantic knowledge of a model is stored in the magnitude of the weights or in their directions. Based on empirical observations, it appears that angular feature differences are far more effective at characterizing the semantic gap than magnitude differences. This implies that the direction of the neurons—their orientation on the hypersphere—is where the most important semantic information resides.

This intuition is supported by toy experiments using a standard convolutional autoencoder trained on images. When comparing three different ways of generating feature maps—using the standard inner product, using only magnitude information, and using only angular information—the results are striking. The angular information of the neurons is capable of almost perfectly recovering the original input images, whereas the magnitude information contains virtually no useful information for reconstruction. 

This discovery reveals that to semantically modify a generated image, such as in subject-driven or controllable generation tasks, the most effective approach is to change the direction of the neurons. Because the angles between neurons are crucial for representing the knowledge of the network, finetuning should prioritize adjusting these directions while maintaining the structural integrity of the overall neuron distribution.

# Case Study: The 2D Fourier Transform Analogy (Phase vs. Magnitude)

The importance of neuron direction can be further understood through an analogy with the 2D Fourier transform. In image processing, a 2D Fourier transform decomposes an image into a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. The magnitude spectrum describes the strength of various frequency components, while the phase spectrum contains the angular information between the input and the basis.

Crucially, the phase spectrum preserves the vast majority of the image's semantics. For instance, if one reconstructs an image using the original phase spectrum but replaces the original magnitude spectrum with a random one, the resulting image can still be recognized as the original subject. The semantic structure remains intact despite the total loss of magnitude information.

This phenomenon directly mirrors the behavior of neurons in deep networks. Just as the phase spectrum preserves the "where" and "what" of an image, the angles (directions) of neurons preserve the semantic knowledge of a model. Changing the neuron directions is equivalent to modifying the phase of the network's internal representations. This is why the goal of Orthogonal Finetuning is to rotate the neurons using an orthogonal matrix, as this operation adjusts the "phase" or direction of the neurons to suit a new task without collapsing the underlying semantic structure.

# The Relationship Between Hyperspherical Energy and Generalization

The relationship between hyperspherical energy and a model's performance varies depending on whether the model is being trained from scratch or finetuned. In the context of training classification networks from scratch, researchers have found that a small hyperspherical energy leads to better generalization. A low energy state means that neurons are uniformly distributed across the hypersphere, reducing redundancy and allowing the network to capture a more diverse set of features.

However, the objectives for finetuning a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model are different. In finetuning, the goal is not to maximize general diversity from scratch, but to adapt the model to a specific task (like a specific subject or control signal) without destroying the high-fidelity generative abilities learned during pretraining. If one were to simply minimize the hyperspherical energy during finetuning, the process would likely destroy the original semantic structures and the intrinsic pretrained knowledge.

Therefore, while classification training seeks to minimize HE, Orthogonal Finetuning seeks to preserve it. By ensuring that the difference in hyperspherical energy between the pretrained model $\bm{W}^0$ and the finetuned model $\bm{W}$ is zero, the model maintains the same level of neuron uniformity and relational structure it had during its original training. This balance allows the model to gain the flexibility needed for a new task while maintaining the stability and generative quality of the pretrained weights.

# Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) Framework

# The Principle of Orthogonal Transformation

To understand why orthogonal transformations are effective for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models, one must first understand the relationship between the geometry of neurons and the semantic information they encode. In deep learning, the weights of a layer can be viewed as a collection of neurons. The authors hypothesize that the direction or angle of these neurons is far more critical for representing semantic knowledge than their magnitude. This intuition is supported by the observation that angular feature differences are strong characterizations of the semantic gap.

A helpful analogy for this phenomenon can be found in the 2D Fourier transform, where an image is decomposed into a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. The phase spectrum represents the angular information between the input and the basis and preserves the vast majority of the image's semantics. In fact, an image can often be reconstructed using its original phase spectrum combined with a random magnitude spectrum without losing its primary semantic meaning. This suggests that while magnitudes provide scale, the directions of the vectors are what actually define the content.

This principle was empirically validated through a toy experiment using a standard convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images. When comparing different ways to generate a feature map during the testing stage—using the full inner product, using only magnitude information, or using only angular information—it was discovered that angular information alone could almost perfectly recover the input images. Conversely, the magnitude of the neurons contained no useful information for reconstruction. Therefore, to modify the semantic output of a generative model—such as adapting it to a specific subject or a new control signal—the most effective strategy is to finetune the directions of the neurons.

However, allowing these directions to change with total freedom often leads to model collapse or the destruction of the pretrained generative capabilities. To find a balance between the flexibility needed to learn new tasks and the regularity needed to preserve pretrained knowledge, orthogonal transformations are employed. By rotating or reflecting all neurons in a layer using an orthogonal matrix, the model can adjust its semantic focus while maintaining the structural relationships between neurons.

# The OFT Forward Pass: Mathematically Transforming Weights

In conventional finetuning, weights are typically updated using gradient descent with a small learning rate. This process implicitly attempts to minimize the Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights $\bm{M}$ and the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$, expressed as $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0 \|$. While this approach aims to prevent the model from deviating too far from its starting point, it often lacks the structural constraints necessary to guarantee the preservation of generative performance.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) replaces this additive update strategy with a multiplicative transformation. Consider a fully connected layer with pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents the $i$-th neuron. In a standard forward pass, the output vector $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ for an input $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is computed as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. OFT modifies this process by introducing a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. The modified forward pass becomes:

$$\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$$

Here, $\bm{W}$ denotes the finetuned weight matrix and $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. To implement this efficiently, the authors utilize Cayley parameterization. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is constructed as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix such that $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. This ensures that $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout the training process.

Because a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix can be parameter-heavy, OFT employs a block-diagonal structure. The matrix $\bm{R}$ is composed of $r$ blocks: $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i \in \text{O}(d/r)$. This reduces the parameter complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. If these blocks are further shared across the diagonal, the complexity drops to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. This makes OFT highly parameter-efficient, often requiring fewer parameters than Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) while maintaining the full-rank nature of the transformation.

# Proof of Hyperspherical Energy Invariance

The core theoretical motivation behind OFT is the preservation of hyperspherical energy (HE), which characterizes the pairwise relational structure of neurons on a unit hypersphere. To define this, let $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ be the $i$-th normalized neuron. The hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix $\bm{W}$ is defined as:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

Hyperspherical energy captures the level of neuron uniformity; a high energy indicates that neurons are more evenly distributed across the hypersphere. The authors hypothesize that the generative semantic ability of a pretrained model is tied to this energy. Therefore, a successful finetuning method should minimize the difference in hyperspherical energy between the pretrained model $\bm{W}^0$ and the finetuned model $\bm{W}$:

$$\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\|$$

The key mathematical property utilized by OFT is that pairwise hyperspherical similarity—and consequently the hyperspherical energy—is provably invariant under the same orthogonal transformation applied to all neurons. Because an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ preserves the inner product between vectors, the angle between any two neurons $\bm{w}_i$ and $\bm{w}_j$ remains identical after the transformation $\bm{R} \bm{w}_i$ and $\bm{R} \bm{w}_j$. 

Since the distances $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ are preserved, the sum that defines the hyperspherical energy remains unchanged. By restricting the weight update to $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$, OFT ensures that the internal geometric structure of the pretrained model is not destroyed, which prevents the semantic collapse often seen in naive finetuning.

# OFT as a Coordinate System Adjustment

Conceptually, OFT can be viewed as an adjustment of the canonical coordinate system for the neurons within a layer. Rather than shifting the weights in a random direction in the weight space, OFT rotates the entire space of neurons. This differs significantly from methods like LoRA, which adds a low-rank matrix to the weights to prevent drastic shifts. While LoRA exploits a low-rank structure for parameter efficiency, OFT exploits a sparsity structure via block-diagonal orthogonality.

The relationship between OFT and LoRA can be expressed by rewriting the OFT forward pass:

$$\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I}) \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$$

In this formulation, the term $(\bm{R} - \bm{I}) \bm{W}^0$ acts as the weight update, analogous to the low-rank update in LoRA. If the matrix $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is low-rank, OFT essentially performs a low-rank update. However, because the transformation is orthogonal, it is fundamentally a full-rank operation that preserves the intrinsic information of $\bm{W}^0$ while allowing for a global re-orientation of the neurons.

One of the most significant practical advantages of viewing OFT as a coordinate adjustment is that it introduces no additional computational overhead during inference. Since $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{W}^0$ are both matrices, the product $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ can be pre-computed after training. The resulting matrix $\bm{W}$ is then used as a standard weight matrix, meaning the inference speed and memory usage are identical to those of the original pretrained model.

# Initialization Strategies for Stable Start

To ensure that the finetuning process begins exactly from the state of the pretrained model, OFT requires a specific initialization strategy. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is initialized as an identity matrix $\bm{I}$. In the context of the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, this is achieved by initializing the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as an all-zero matrix. This ensures that at the start of training, $\bm{W} = \bm{I} \cdot \bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0$.

To further improve stability, the authors introduce Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT). While OFT preserves the hyperspherical energy, it still allows the neurons to rotate any distance away from their original positions. COFT imposes an additional constraint to keep the finetuned model within a small hyperspherical neighborhood of the pretrained model, defined by a radius $\epsilon$:

$$\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$$

Implementing this constraint directly on the Cayley-parameterized matrix $\bm{R}$ is non-trivial. However, by applying the Neumann series, the authors approximate the transformation as $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q}$. This allows the constraint to be moved inside the Cayley transform, resulting in an equivalent constraint on the skew-symmetric matrix: $\|\bm{Q} - \bm{0}\| \leq \epsilon'$. This constraint is easily enforced using projected gradient descent.

By combining the requirement for minimal hyperspherical energy difference with this explicit deviation constraint, COFT provides even greater stability than standard OFT. The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ acts as a control for the flexibility of the finetuning; a larger $\epsilon$ allows the model to behave like standard OFT, while a smaller $\epsilon$ forces the finetuned model to remain closer to the original pretrained diffusion model.

Efficient Implementation of Orthogonal Matrices

# Challenges of Standard Orthogonalization (Gram-Schmidt)

In the context of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the goal is to learn an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ that transforms the neurons of a pretrained model. Mathematically, an orthogonal matrix must satisfy the property $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. While there are established numerical methods to produce such matrices, implementing them within a deep learning framework presents significant challenges.

One common approach to orthogonalization is the Gram-Schmidt method. This process takes a set of vectors and generates an orthogonal set by iteratively subtracting the projections of a vector onto the subspace spanned by the previously processed vectors. Although the Gram-Schmidt process is differentiable, which is a prerequisite for training via backpropagation, it is often too computationally expensive to be practical for the large-scale weight matrices found in text-to-image diffusion models. The overhead associated with these standard orthogonalization techniques makes them unsuitable for efficient finetuning, necessitating a more streamlined parameterization strategy.

# Cayley Parameterization: Generating Orthogonal Matrices via Skew-Symmetric Matrices

To overcome the efficiency bottlenecks of standard orthogonalization, OFT utilizes Cayley parameterization. This method provides a way to generate an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ using a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$. A matrix is considered skew-symmetric if it satisfies the condition $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. The relationship between the skew-symmetric matrix and the resulting orthogonal matrix is defined by the Cayley transform:

$$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$$

By optimizing the elements of $\bm{Q}$ instead of $\bm{R}$ directly, the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ is guaranteed by construction. One specific characteristic of the Cayley parameterization is that it can only produce orthogonal matrices with a determinant of $1$, meaning it generates matrices belonging to the special orthogonal group. However, empirical evidence suggests that this limitation does not negatively impact the performance of the finetuning process.

For the finetuning process to begin exactly from the pretrained model weights, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ must be initialized as an identity matrix. In the Cayley framework, this is achieved simply by initializing the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as an all-zero matrix. This ensures that the model starts its adaptation from the original pretrained state, providing a stable baseline for downstream tasks.

# Block-Diagonal Parameterization for Efficiency

While the Cayley transform ensures orthogonality, the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ can still be parameter-inefficient when the neuron dimension $d$ is very large. A full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix requires a significant number of trainable parameters, which can hinder the efficiency of the finetuning process. To address this, OFT introduces a block-diagonal parameterization.

Instead of learning a single dense orthogonal matrix, $\bm{R}$ is constructed as a block-diagonal matrix consisting of $r$ smaller orthogonal blocks:

$$\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r) = \begin{bmatrix} \bm{R}_1 \in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r}) & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \bm{R}_r \in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r}) \end{bmatrix} \in \text{O}(d)$$

In this structure, each block $\bm{R}_i$ is an orthogonal matrix of size $\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}$. This approach decomposes the high-dimensional transformation into several smaller, independent orthogonal transformations. Because the direct sum of orthogonal matrices is itself orthogonal, the overall matrix $\bm{R}$ remains a member of the orthogonal group $\text{O}(d)$. This ensures that the critical property of preserving hyperspherical energy is maintained while drastically reducing the number of parameters that need to be optimized.

# Complexity Analysis: OFT vs. LoRA

The efficiency of OFT can be understood by analyzing its parameter complexity relative to standard orthogonal matrices and other popular finetuning methods like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). For a standard unconstrained orthogonal matrix of size $d \times d$, the number of parameters is $d(d-1)/2$, resulting in a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. By using the $r$-block diagonal structure, the number of parameters is reduced to $d(d/r-1)/2$, which lowers the complexity to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$.

When compared to LoRA, which uses a low-rank update with rank $r'$, the number of trainable parameters in LoRA is $r'(d+n)$. To compare these methods fairly, if we assume that both the rank $r'$ in LoRA and the number of blocks $r$ in OFT are dependent on the neuron dimension $d$ (for example, $r = r' = \alpha d$ for some constant $0 < \alpha \leq 1$), the complexities diverge significantly. In this scenario, LoRA's complexity is $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$, whereas OFT's complexity is reduced to $\mathcal{O}(d)$.

To illustrate this with a concrete example, consider a weight matrix of size $128 \times 128$. If LoRA is used with a rank $r' = 8$, it requires $2,048$ trainable parameters. In contrast, OFT using $r = 8$ blocks (without block sharing) requires only $960$ trainable parameters. This demonstrates that OFT can achieve a more compact parameterization than LoRA while providing the structural benefits of orthogonality.

# Block Sharing Strategies to Reduce Trainable Parameters

Beyond block-diagonal parameterization, OFT can further reduce its parameter footprint through a block sharing strategy. In the standard block-diagonal approach, each block $\bm{R}_i$ is learned independently. However, the number of parameters can be further minimized by forcing all blocks to be identical, such that $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$.

When this sharing strategy is applied, the model only needs to learn a single orthogonal matrix of size $\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}$ and replicate it across the diagonal of the larger matrix $\bm{R}$. This reduces the overall parameter complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. This strategy allows for an even more extreme reduction in the number of trainable parameters without sacrificing the orthogonality of the global matrix $\bm{R}$, thereby continuing to preserve the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained neurons while maximizing parameter efficiency.

Advanced Variants: COFT and Re-scaled OFT

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): The Radius Constraint

While basic Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) preserves the hyperspherical energy of a pretrained model by utilizing orthogonal transformations, it can still be overly flexible for certain downstream tasks. In the context of finetuning large-scale models, there is often a need to ensure that the finetuned weights do not deviate too drastically from the original pretrained weights to prevent the loss of general generative capabilities. To address this, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) introduces a structural limitation on the orthogonal matrix, effectively forcing the finetuned model to remain within a small neighborhood of the pretrained model.

The core mechanism of COFT is the introduction of an $\epsilon$-deviation constraint. In a standard OFT forward pass, the output vector $\bm{z}$ is computed as $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix and $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weights. COFT maintains the requirement that $\bm{R}$ must be orthogonal, meaning $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$. However, it adds the explicit constraint that $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This constraint ensures that the distance between the learned transformation $\bm{R}$ and the identity matrix $\bm{I}$ is bounded by a hyperparameter $\epsilon$.

By imposing this radius constraint, COFT explicitly limits the angular deviation of the neurons from their original positions in the pretrained weight space. The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ serves as a dial to control the trade-off between flexibility and stability. When $\epsilon$ is set to a large value, the model behaves similarly to standard OFT, allowing for significant rotation of the neurons. Conversely, as $\epsilon$ decreases, the finetuned model is constrained to behave more like the original pretrained text-to-image diffusion model, providing a tighter safeguard against the degradation of pretrained semantic knowledge.

# Implementing the Epsilon-Deviation Constraint via Projected Gradient Descent

Implementing the $\epsilon$-deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ is non-trivial because $\bm{R}$ is typically parameterized using the Cayley transform to ensure orthogonality. The Cayley parameterization defines the orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix such that $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. Because the constraint is defined on $\bm{R}$ but the optimization happens on $\bm{Q}$, a direct application of the radius constraint is computationally difficult.

To overcome this, COFT utilizes the Neumann series to approximate the relationship between $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{Q}$. Under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm, the expression for $\bm{R}$ can be approximated as $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$. This linear approximation allows the constraint on $\bm{R}$ to be moved inside the Cayley transform. Specifically, the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ is translated into an equivalent constraint on the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, formulated as $\|\bm{Q} - \bm{0}\| \leq \epsilon'$, where $\bm{0}$ is an all-zero matrix and $\epsilon'$ is a corresponding error hyperparameter.

Once the constraint is reformulated in terms of $\bm{Q}$, it can be efficiently enforced using projected gradient descent. During the training process, if the update to $\bm{Q}$ results in a value that exceeds the norm $\epsilon'$, it is projected back onto the boundary of the $\epsilon'$-ball. To ensure the model begins exactly at the pretrained state, $\bm{Q}$ is initialized as an all-zero matrix, which corresponds to $\bm{R}$ being the identity matrix. This combination of Cayley parameterization and projected gradient descent allows COFT to maintain strict orthogonality while explicitly controlling the deviation from the pretrained weights.

# Re-scaled OFT: Integrating Magnitude Scaling

The basic OFT framework focuses exclusively on adjusting the direction of neurons via orthogonal transformations. While this is highly effective for preserving hyperspherical energy—since hyperspherical energy is calculated based on normalized neurons where magnitude is factored out—it may limit the model's ability to adapt to the specific scales required by a downstream task. To enhance flexibility without sacrificing the benefits of orthogonal transformation, the authors propose Re-scaled OFT.

Re-scaled OFT introduces a learnable magnitude scaling coefficient for each neuron. In this variant, the forward pass is modified to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$. Here, $\bm{R}$ continues to function as the layer-shared orthogonal matrix that preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, while $\bm{D}$ is introduced as a learnable diagonal matrix. This architecture allows the model to simultaneously tune the orientation of the neurons and their respective magnitudes.

The intuition behind this extension is that re-scaling the neurons does not change the hyperspherical energy of the layer. Because the hyperspherical energy is defined as the sum of similarities between normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$, any scalar multiplication of a neuron's weight is neutralized during the normalization process. Therefore, adding a scaling factor provides the model with more degrees of freedom to optimize for a task without destroying the intrinsic pretrained structure that the orthogonal transformation is designed to preserve.

# The Role of the Diagonal Matrix D in Enhancing Flexibility

The diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ in Re-scaled OFT is defined as $\bm{D} = \text{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_n) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, where each diagonal element $s_i$ must be greater than zero. Each $s_i$ corresponds to a scaling factor for the $i$-th neuron in the weight matrix. By allowing these scalars to be learnable parameters, the model can independently adjust the "strength" or importance of different neurons in the layer.

This addition significantly increases the flexibility of the finetuning process. In standard OFT, the only way to modify the output is to rotate the weight vectors. With the inclusion of $\bm{D}$, the model can now perform a more nuanced adaptation where it can amplify or attenuate specific feature detectors. This added flexibility is particularly useful for complex downstream tasks, such as subject-driven or controllable generation, where the model might need to emphasize certain pretrained features over others to better match the target subject or control signal.

Crucially, the introduction of the diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ comes with a negligible increase in the number of trainable parameters. Since $\bm{D}$ only adds $n$ parameters per layer (where $n$ is the number of neurons), it remains highly parameter-efficient. Empirical findings indicate that Re-scaled OFT generally outperforms the original OFT, demonstrating that combining angular transformation with magnitude scaling provides a more comprehensive toolkit for model adaptation.

# Comparing the Stability of OFT and COFT

The primary distinction between OFT and COFT lies in the nature of their constraints. OFT is designed to minimize the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned and pretrained models. By using an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, OFT ensures that the pairwise angles between neurons are perfectly preserved, which is a structural measure of semantic preservation. However, OFT does not explicitly limit how far the weights can move in Euclidean space, provided the orthogonality is maintained.

COFT enhances this stability by combining the minimal hyperspherical energy difference of OFT with an explicit deviation constraint. While OFT provides a "regularity" by preserving angles, COFT adds a second layer of "regularity" by restricting the model to a fixed radius around the pretrained weights. This makes COFT a more constrained version of OFT, further reducing the risk of model collapse. In standard finetuning, using a small learning rate is an implicit attempt to minimize $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$; COFT makes this an explicit, enforceable constraint through the $\epsilon$-deviation.

Experimental results indicate that COFT provides even better finetuning stability than standard OFT. Because COFT strictly limits the flexibility of the transformation, it is less sensitive to the choice of learning rate and the number of training iterations. While OFT is already more stable than naive finetuning or LoRA, COFT's explicit constraint allows users to set relatively large iteration numbers without carefully tuning the hyperparameters for every individual subject, as the radius constraint prevents the model from drifting into unstable regions of the weight space.

Empirical Evaluation and Results

# Experimental Setup: Stable Diffusion v1.5

To evaluate the efficacy of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), the researchers utilized Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the foundational pretrained text-to-image model. The primary objective of the experimental framework was to determine how well OFT could adapt this large-scale model to specific downstream tasks—namely subject-driven and controllable generation—without degrading the high-fidelity generative capabilities established during pretraining.

For a rigorous and fair comparison, the researchers selected a set of established baselines tailored to each task. In the context of subject-driven generation, the model was compared against DreamBooth and LoRA. For controllable generation, the baselines included ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. To ensure a level playing field when comparing OFT and COFT to LoRA, the researchers applied the orthogonal transformations only to the specific layers where LoRA was implemented. This ensured that any performance gains were attributable to the orthogonal constraint rather than a difference in the number of layers being modified.

# Evaluating Subject-Driven Generation: DINO, CLIP-I, and LPIPS

The evaluation of subject-driven generation focuses on a critical trade-off: the model must preserve the specific identity of a subject (subject fidelity) while remaining responsive to new text prompts (prompt fidelity) and maintaining a variety of outputs (sample diversity). To quantify these aspects, several embedding-based metrics were employed.

Subject fidelity is measured using DINO and CLIP-I. The CLIP-I metric calculates the average pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between the generated images and the real images of the subject. Similarly, the DINO metric performs a similar calculation but utilizes ViT S/16 DINO embeddings. High scores in these metrics indicate that the generated images are visually and semantically similar to the actual subject. To complement this, prompt fidelity is measured via CLIP-T, which computes the average cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between the input text prompt and the resulting generated image. This ensures that the model is not simply recreating the training images but is actually following the user's instructions.

Finally, the researchers evaluated sample diversity using LPIPS. Specifically, they calculated the average LPIPS cosine similarity between different images of the same subject generated using the same text prompt. To minimize the influence of random initialization and ensure the stability of these results, the researchers repeatedly finetuned the pretrained model using 30 different random seeds.

# Evaluating Controllable Generation: The Control Consistency Metric

Measuring the success of controllable generation is challenging because standard image quality metrics do not capture whether the model actually followed the provided control signal (such as a segmentation map or edge map). To address this, the authors introduced a novel control consistency metric. The intuition behind this metric is to treat the generated image as an input to an off-the-shelf detection or segmentation model, extract the resulting control signal, and then compare that extracted signal to the original input control signal.

This consistency metric is adapted based on the specific task. For Canny edge-to-image (C2I) generation, the researchers used the Intersection over Union (IoU) and the F1 score to compare the generated edges with the original edges. For segmentation map-to-image (S2I) generation, they utilized mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy. In the case of landmark-to-face (L2F) generation, the consistency was measured by calculating the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the original control landmarks and the predicted landmarks extracted from the generated face. This approach allows for an objective, quantitative assessment of how accurately the model adheres to spatial and geometric constraints.

# Analyzing Convergence Speed and Data Efficiency

One of the most significant findings in the empirical analysis is the superior convergence speed and data efficiency of the OFT framework. In the L2F task, quantitative results demonstrated that OFT and COFT reach a high level of performance much faster than baseline methods. For instance, it took LoRA 20 epochs to reach the level of landmark accuracy that OFT achieved in only 8 epochs.

The stability of this convergence is also a key differentiator. While some methods, such as ControlNet, exhibit a phenomenon where controllability "suddenly emerges" after a certain number of epochs, the training dynamics of OFT are smooth and predictable. This makes the hyperparameter tuning process—specifically the selection of learning rates and the number of training iterations—significantly easier for the user.

Furthermore, OFT demonstrates remarkable sample efficiency. The researchers observed that OFT could converge effectively using only $5\%$ of the ADE20K dataset for segmentation-to-image tasks, whereas other methods required significantly more data to achieve comparable levels of control. This suggests that the orthogonal constraint effectively regularizes the learning process, allowing the model to acquire new control signals without requiring massive amounts of training data.

# Qualitative Analysis: Subject Fidelity and Control Accuracy

Qualitative evaluations further validate the advantages of OFT and COFT across both target tasks. In subject-driven generation, the researchers found that LoRA often struggled to preserve the subject's identity, sometimes losing the subject entirely in complex scenes. Conversely, while DreamBooth preserved identity well, it often failed to follow the text prompts accurately. OFT and COFT successfully bridged this gap, delivering images that maintained high subject fidelity while remaining strictly adherent to the text prompts.

In controllable generation, the qualitative gap was equally evident. In S2I tasks, LoRA completely failed to follow the segmentation maps, while OFT, COFT, and ControlNet succeeded. When comparing OFT/COFT to ControlNet, the orthogonal methods produced images with higher fidelity, more vivid details, and more reasonable geometric structures, despite using far fewer parameters.

For C2I tasks, OFT and COFT were able to "hallucinate" semantically appropriate images based on rough Canny edges, outperforming T2I-Adapter and LoRA. In the L2F task, the orthogonal framework produced the most accurate pose control, even when faced with challenging face angles. These results suggest that by preserving the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model, OFT maintains the original semantic knowledge of the network while flexibly adapting its direction to accommodate new control signals.

Comparative Analysis and Future Directions

# OFT vs. LoRA: Low-Rank vs. Sparse Orthogonal Structures

When adapting large-scale pretrained models to downstream tasks, the primary objective is to find a balance between flexibility—the ability of the model to learn new information—and regularity—the ability to preserve the original generative capabilities. Two prominent approaches to achieving this are Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) and Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). While both are designed to be parameter-efficient, they rely on fundamentally different mathematical constraints to prevent the model from deviating too far from its pretrained state.

LoRA operates on the assumption that the update to the weights can be represented by a low-rank matrix. In this framework, the difference between the finetuned weight matrix $\bm{M}$ and the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{M}^0$ is constrained such that $\text{rank}(\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0) = r'$, where $r'$ is a small constant. This prevents the information in the pretrained weights from shifting dramatically by limiting the degrees of freedom available for the update. In essence, LoRA uses an additive low-rank structure to modify the model.

In contrast, OFT utilizes a multiplicative approach based on orthogonal transformations. Instead of adding a low-rank matrix, OFT transforms the pretrained weights using an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, where $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This approach is grounded in the principle of preserving hyperspherical energy, which characterizes the pairwise angles between neurons. Because an orthogonal transformation preserves these angles, OFT ensures that the intrinsic semantic structure of the pretrained model remains intact. While LoRA exploits the low-rank structure of the weight update, OFT exploits a sparsity structure through the use of block-diagonal orthogonal matrices.

Interestingly, these two methods are not entirely unrelated. The forward pass of OFT can be rewritten as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. In this formulation, the term $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ acts as the weight update, similar to the low-rank update in LoRA. Therefore, if the matrix $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is low-rank, OFT effectively performs a low-rank weight update. The key difference remains that while LoRA restricts the rank of the update directly, OFT restricts the transformation to be orthogonal, which provides a more structured way to preserve the pretrained knowledge.

# Architectural Agnosticism: Applying OFT to Transformers and Convolutions

A significant advantage of the OFT framework is that it is agnostic to the specific architecture of the neural network. Because it operates on the weight matrices of individual layers, it can be applied to any layer that can be represented as a matrix multiplication, including the attention weights in Transformers and the kernels in convolutional layers.

In Transformer architectures, OFT is typically applied to the attention weights. This allows for a fair comparison with methods like LoRA, which are also frequently applied to these specific layers. By learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix for the attention weights, OFT can modify the model's behavior for downstream tasks while ensuring that the attention mechanism's pretrained structural properties are not destroyed.

The application of OFT to convolutional layers provides further interesting interpretations. In a convolutional layer, the block-diagonal structure of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be tuned to control the transformation of neuron channels. For instance, if the number of blocks in the diagonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is set to be equal to the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel. This is conceptually similar to the operation of depth-wise convolution kernels. Alternatively, if the blocks in $\bm{R}$ are shared across the diagonal, the neurons are transformed by a single orthogonal matrix shared across all channels. This flexibility allows OFT to be adapted to the specific spatial and channel-wise requirements of different vision tasks.

# Zero Inference Overhead: The Weight-Merging Advantage

One of the most practical benefits of Orthogonal Finetuning is that it introduces zero additional computational overhead during the inference stage. This stands in stark contrast to other controllable generation frameworks, such as ControlNet, which introduce additional trainable components that must be computed during every forward pass of the model.

The efficiency of OFT stems from the fact that the orthogonal transformation is a linear operation applied to the weights. During the training phase, the model learns the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and uses it to transform the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ to produce the finetuned weights $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. However, once training is complete, the learned matrix $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ a single time.

The result of this multiplication is a standard weight matrix $\bm{W}$ that is identical in shape and function to the original pretrained weights. Consequently, the finetuned model can be deployed as a standard diffusion model without any modified architecture or additional parameters. This "weight-merging" advantage ensures that the inference speed and memory requirements of an OFT-finetuned model are exactly the same as those of the original pretrained model, making it highly suitable for efficient deployment in real-world applications.

# Open Problem: Scaling Matrix Inverses in Differentiable Frameworks

Despite its effectiveness, OFT faces a challenge regarding scalability due to the way the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized. To ensure that $\bm{R}$ remains strictly orthogonal throughout the training process, OFT employs Cayley parameterization. In this method, the orthogonal matrix is constructed as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix such that $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$.

The primary bottleneck of this approach is the requirement of a matrix inverse, $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. Computing the inverse of a large matrix is computationally expensive and can become a limiting factor as the dimension $d$ of the layers increases. To mitigate this, OFT utilizes a block-diagonal parameterization, which breaks the large $d \times d$ matrix into $r$ smaller blocks of size $d/r \times d/r$. This reduces the complexity of the inversion process significantly.

However, the need for a matrix inverse in a differentiable framework remains an open research problem. While block-diagonal structures provide a temporary solution by reducing the size of the matrices being inverted, they introduce their own biases and potentially limit the flexibility of the finetuning. Finding a way to speed up the matrix inverse or develop a different differentiable orthogonalization strategy that scales more efficiently without sacrificing the orthogonal property is a key direction for future improvement.

# The Potential for Compositionality through Orthogonal Matrix Multiplication

A compelling theoretical property of the OFT framework is its potential for compositionality. This potential arises from a fundamental property of the orthogonal group: the product of two orthogonal matrices is itself an orthogonal matrix.

In a typical finetuning scenario, a model might be adapted for several different downstream tasks, each resulting in a unique orthogonal matrix—for example, $\bm{R}_A$ for subject-driven generation and $\bm{R}_B$ for a specific controllable generation task. Because these matrices are orthogonal, they can be multiplied together, and the resulting matrix $\bm{R}_{combined} = \bm{R}_A \bm{R}_B$ will still be an orthogonal matrix.

This suggests a possible path toward creating a single model that possesses the capabilities of multiple downstream tasks without needing to train a separate model for every combination of tasks. If the orthogonal matrices can be composed through multiplication, it may be possible to combine the knowledge gained from different finetuning tasks into one unified transformation. Whether this compositionality preserves the specific semantic knowledge of all the individual tasks remains an open question, but it presents an intriguing direction for studying how to modularly combine different learned behaviors in large-scale generative models.
