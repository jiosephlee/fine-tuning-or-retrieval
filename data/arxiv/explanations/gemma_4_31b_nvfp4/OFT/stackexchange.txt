\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is 'hyperspherical energy' and why does an orthogonal matrix preserve it?
Question:
I'm reading the paper 'Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning' and I'm struggling with the core concept of hyperspherical energy. The authors define it in Equation (1) as the sum of the inverse of the distances between normalized neurons: $\text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} {\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}}$. 

I understand that they want to keep this value constant to preserve semantic information, but I don't follow the mathematical jump to why applying an orthogonal transformation $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ preserves this energy. If $\bm{R}$ rotates the neurons, why doesn't that change the distances between them?
Answer:
To understand hyperspherical energy (HE) and why orthogonal transformations preserve it, it is helpful to think of the neurons in a weight matrix not as arbitrary numbers, but as vectors pointing in a high-dimensional space.

Hyperspherical energy, as defined in the paper via $\text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} {\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}}$, is essentially a measure of how "spread out" the normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}$ are on the surface of a unit hypersphere. The formula is mathematically analogous to the electrostatic potential energy of a system of particles (like electrons) on a sphere, where the energy is inversely proportional to the distance between particles. If neurons are clumped together, the distance $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ is small, making the inverse large and the total energy high. Conversely, if neurons are uniformly distributed and far apart, the energy is minimized. In the context of this paper, the authors hypothesize that this spatial arrangement (the pairwise relational structure) encodes the core semantic knowledge of the pretrained model.

The reason an orthogonal transformation $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ preserves this energy lies in the fundamental property of orthogonal matrices: they are isometry operators. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, which means that multiplying a vector by $\bm{R}$ preserves its length and the angle between any two vectors. Intuitively, applying $\bm{R}$ to the weight matrix is equivalent to rotating or reflecting the entire "cloud" of neurons on the hypersphere without changing the relative positions of the neurons to one another.

Technically, since $\bm{R}$ preserves the Euclidean norm, the distance between any two transformed normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$ is exactly the same as the distance between the original neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j$. This can be shown by noting that $\|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\| = \|\bm{R}(\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j)\|$. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, $\|\bm{R}\bm{v}\| = \|\bm{v}\|$ for any vector $\bm{v}$, meaning $\|\bm{R}(\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j)\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|$. Since every single pairwise distance in the summation remains unchanged, the total sum—the hyperspherical energy—remains constant.

By restricting the finetuning process to learning an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, the authors ensure that they are only changing the "coordinate system" (the global orientation) of the neurons while strictly preserving the internal semantic structure (the pairwise angles) that was learned during pretraining. This prevents the model from "collapsing" or losing its original generative capabilities, which often happens during standard finetuning when neurons are allowed to move independently and destroy these relational distances.

### How does the block-diagonal structure of $\bm{R}$ reduce parameters without breaking orthogonality?
Question:
In Section 3.3, the authors describe representing the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal matrix with $r$ blocks, where each block $\bm{R}_i$ is itself an orthogonal matrix of size $\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}$. 

I'm confused about how this works. If we only have these small blocks on the diagonal and zeros everywhere else, does the resulting large $d \times d$ matrix $\bm{R}$ still satisfy the condition $\bm{R}^T\bm{R} = \bm{I}$? Also, does restricting $\bm{R}$ to this block-diagonal form limit the 'rotation' capabilities compared to a full orthogonal matrix?
Answer:
The block-diagonal structure of $\bm{R}$ preserves orthogonality because the product of two block-diagonal matrices is simply the block-wise product of their corresponding components. Specifically, if $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$, then the transpose $\bm{R}^T$ is also block-diagonal with blocks $\bm{R}_1^T, \bm{R}_2^T, \dots, \bm{R}_r^T$. When computing the product $\bm{R}^T\bm{R}$, the resulting matrix is a block-diagonal matrix where each block is the product $\bm{R}_i^T\bm{R}_i$. Since the authors ensure that each individual block $\bm{R}_i$ is itself an orthogonal matrix of size $\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}$, it follows that $\bm{R}_i^T\bm{R}_i = \bm{I}_{d/r}$ for all $i$. Consequently, the full $d \times d$ matrix $\bm{R}^T\bm{R}$ becomes a diagonal of identity matrices, which is exactly the identity matrix $\bm{I}_d$. This allows the model to satisfy the global orthogonality constraint while only needing to maintain orthogonality within smaller, independent subspaces.

This architecture significantly reduces the number of parameters by introducing sparsity. A full orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ has a parameter complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ because almost every element must be learned to maintain the constraint. In contrast, the block-diagonal form restricts the learnable parameters to the blocks on the diagonal. Since there are $r$ blocks, each with a complexity of $\mathcal{O}((\frac{d}{r})^2)$, the total complexity becomes $r \cdot \mathcal{O}(\frac{d^2}{r^2})$, which simplifies to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. If the authors choose to share the blocks (setting $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$), the complexity drops even further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$.

Regarding the "rotation" capabilities, you are correct that this restriction limits the flexibility of the transformation. A full orthogonal matrix can rotate any vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$ toward any other direction on the unit hypersphere. A block-diagonal matrix, however, can only rotate vectors within their respective $\frac{d}{r}$-dimensional subspaces; it cannot "mix" dimensions across different blocks. While this reduces the degrees of freedom, the authors argue that this is actually a desirable trade-off. In the context of finetuning, too much flexibility can lead to model collapse or the destruction of the pretrained semantic structure. By using the block-diagonal structure, OFT imposes a stronger regularization that balances the need for semantic modification (changing neuron directions) with the need for stability (preserving the pretraining hyperspherical energy).

### Is the $\epsilon$ constraint in COFT just a way to simulate a small learning rate?
Question:
The paper introduces Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which adds the constraint $\norm{\bm{R}-\bm{I}} \leq \epsilon$. The authors mention that standard finetuning uses a small learning rate to implicitly keep the model close to the pretrained weights. 

It seems to me that $\epsilon$ is doing the exact same thing as a small learning rate—preventing the model from drifting too far from $\bm{W}^0$. If that's the case, what is the actual advantage of making this an explicit constraint via projected gradient descent on the matrix $\bm{Q}$ instead of just using a small $\eta$ in SGD?
Answer:
While the $\epsilon$ constraint in Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) and a small learning rate $\eta$ in SGD both aim to prevent the model from drifting too far from the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$, they operate on fundamentally different principles. A small learning rate controls the *speed* and *step size* of the updates, but it does not impose a hard limit on how far the weights can eventually travel. Over many iterations, even with a tiny $\eta$, the weights can drift arbitrarily far from their starting point, which can lead to the "model collapse" or loss of generative performance mentioned in the paper. In contrast, the constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ defines a *hard boundary* (a neighborhood) around the identity matrix. Regardless of the number of training iterations or the size of the learning rate, the orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ is strictly forbidden from leaving this region.

The actual advantage of this explicit constraint via projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$ is twofold: stability and the decoupling of hyperparameters. First, by enforcing a hard radius, COFT provides a guarantee of stability that an implicit learning rate cannot. The authors note that COFT yields better stability than standard OFT because it explicitly limits the angular deviation from the pretrained model. Second, this structural constraint makes the model significantly less sensitive to the choice of learning rate and the total number of training epochs. In standard finetuning, tuning the number of iterations is critical to prevent overfitting or collapse; however, in COFT, the $\epsilon$ parameter controls the "flexibility" of the finetuning independently of the optimization speed. This allows the user to set a relatively large number of iterations without worrying about the model drifting into an unstable region.

Furthermore, it is important to remember that the $\epsilon$ constraint does not act alone. COFT combines this distance constraint with an orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. While a small $\eta$ in standard finetuning simply limits Euclidean distance, it does nothing to preserve the "hyperspherical energy" (the pairwise angles between neurons). COFT ensures that the intrinsic semantic structure—characterized by these angles—is provably preserved via the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, while the $\epsilon$ constraint ensures the resulting transformation remains close to the original coordinate system. By mapping the constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ to $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$ via the Cayley transform approximation $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q}$, the authors can efficiently maintain this stability using projected gradient descent, which simply clips the values of $\bm{Q}$ back to the $\epsilon'$-ball if they exceed it.

### Connection between 2D Fourier phase spectrum and orthogonal weight transformations
Question:
In the introduction, the authors provide an intuition for why orthogonal transformations work by citing 2D Fourier transforms, specifically that the phase spectrum (angular information) preserves semantics while the magnitude spectrum is less critical. 

I'm trying to connect this to the actual mechanism of OFT. In a Fourier transform, the phase refers to the shift/position of sinusoids. In OFT, they are rotating neuron weights in a weight matrix $\bm{W}$. How does the concept of 'phase spectrum' in image processing mathematically map to the 'neuron directions' in a neural network layer? Is this a literal mathematical analogy or just a conceptual heuristic?
Answer:
The connection between the 2D Fourier phase spectrum and Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a conceptual heuristic rather than a literal mathematical identity. In the context of the paper, the authors use this analogy to motivate why focusing on the *direction* of neurons is more important for preserving and modifying semantics than focusing on their *magnitude*.

In a 2D Fourier Transform, an image is decomposed into magnitude and phase spectra. The magnitude spectrum tells you "how much" of a certain frequency is present, while the phase spectrum tells you "where" those frequencies are located. A well-known property in image processing is that the phase spectrum encodes the structural and semantic layout of the image; if you reconstruct an image using the original phase but a random magnitude, the image remains recognizable. The authors draw a parallel here: they hypothesize that the semantic knowledge of a neural network is similarly encoded in the relative angular relationships (the directions) of the neurons rather than their absolute magnitudes.

Mathematically, this maps to the weight matrix $\bm{W}$ through the concept of hyperspherical energy. The authors define the semantic structure of a layer by the pairwise angles between normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$. An orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ (where $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$) is specifically chosen because it preserves the inner product between vectors. Since the cosine similarity between two neurons $\bm{w}_i$ and $\bm{w}_j$ is defined as $\frac{\bm{w}_i^\top \bm{w}_j}{\|\bm{w}_i\| \|\bm{w}_j\|}$, applying the same $\bm{R}$ to all neurons in a layer ensures that the angle between any two neurons remains invariant: $(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i)^\top (\bm{R}\bm{w}_j) = \bm{w}_i^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} \bm{w}_j = \bm{w}_i^\top \bm{w}_j$. 

Therefore, the "phase spectrum" in the analogy corresponds to the relative geometry of the neurons on the unit hypersphere. By learning $\bm{R}$, OFT effectively rotates the entire "coordinate system" of the neurons. This allows the model to adapt to new semantic targets (like a specific subject or a control signal) by shifting the global direction of the neurons while strictly preserving the internal relative angles that characterize the pretrained generative ability. In short, the Fourier analogy justifies the focus on angular transformations as the primary vehicle for semantic modification without destroying the model's fundamental structural knowledge.

### Does OFT effectively perform a low-rank update similar to LoRA?
Question:
The paper claims that OFT can be rewritten as $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0+(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^T\bm{x}$, and suggests that if $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is low-rank, it's analogous to LoRA. 

However, LoRA explicitly learns a low-rank matrix $\bm{B}\bm{A}$ to add to the weights. In OFT, $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix. If $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix, can $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ actually be low-rank? I thought orthogonal matrices were by definition full-rank. I'm confused about how OFT can be viewed as a 'sparsity' alternative to the 'low-rank' approach of LoRA.
Answer:
To understand how Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) relates to LoRA, it is first necessary to distinguish between the properties of the transformation matrix $\bm{R}$ and the properties of the update matrix $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})$.

You are correct that an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ must be full-rank by definition; if it weren't, it couldn't satisfy $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$. However, the "low-rank" claim does not refer to $\bm{R}$ itself, but to the difference $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$. While $\bm{R}$ is full-rank, $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ can indeed be low-rank. A classic example is a Householder reflection, where $\bm{R} = \bm{I} - 2\bm{v}\bm{v}^\top$ for some unit vector $\bm{v}$. In this case, $\bm{R}$ is a full-rank orthogonal matrix, but $\bm{R}-\bm{I} = -2\bm{v}\bm{v}^\top$, which is a rank-1 matrix.

In the context of the paper, the analogy to LoRA comes from the way the weights are modified. LoRA approximates a weight update as $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{B}\bm{A}$, where $\bm{B}\bm{A}$ is a low-rank matrix. OFT defines the weights as $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. By rewriting this as $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$, the term $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ acts as the additive update $\Delta \bm{W}$. If $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is low-rank, then the resulting update to the weights is also low-rank, mirroring the mechanism of LoRA.

The paper describes OFT as a "sparsity" alternative to the "low-rank" approach of LoRA because of how they achieve parameter efficiency. LoRA reduces the number of trainable parameters by explicitly constraining the rank of the update matrix. OFT, however, achieves parameter efficiency by imposing a structural constraint on $\bm{R}$—specifically, using a block-diagonal structure. Instead of learning a full $d \times d$ matrix, they learn $r$ smaller blocks. This is a form of structural sparsity (many elements of the matrix are forced to be zero), which reduces the parameter complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ or even $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ with block sharing.

Intuitively, while LoRA restricts the *expressive dimension* of the update, OFT restricts the *degree of freedom* of the rotation while ensuring the "energy" (the pairwise angles between neurons) is perfectly preserved. This is further reinforced in Constrained OFT (COFT), where the constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ ensures that the orthogonal transformation remains a small perturbation of the identity, keeping the finetuned model close to the pretrained weights to maintain stability.
