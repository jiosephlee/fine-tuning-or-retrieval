\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 3: The Geometry of Neurons: Hyperspherical Energy

# The Unit Hypersphere and Neuron Normalization

To understand how a neural network stores and processes information, it is helpful to view the weights of a layer not just as a collection of numbers, but as a set of vectors in a high-dimensional space. In a fully connected layer, the weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ consists of $n$ individual neurons, where each neuron $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a vector in $d$-dimensional space. One of the most powerful ways to analyze these neurons is to focus on their direction rather than their magnitude. This is achieved through neuron normalization, where each neuron $\bm{w}_i$ is projected onto a unit hypersphere. The normalized neuron is defined as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$.

By normalizing neurons in this way, we essentially treat every neuron as a point on the surface of a unit hypersphere in $\mathbb{R}^d$. This perspective is grounded in the observation that the direction of neurons can capture the most critical information from data. Research into architectures like SphereNet has demonstrated that training a neural network with all neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere yields a capacity comparable to standard networks and can even result in better generalizability. This suggests that the angular relationship between neurons is a primary driver of the network's ability to represent complex patterns, making the unit hypersphere a natural geometric setting for studying semantic information.

# Defining Hyperspherical Energy (HE)

Once we have mapped neurons to the unit hypersphere, we need a way to quantify the structural relationship between them. This is where the concept of hyperspherical energy (HE) becomes essential. Hyperspherical energy is a measure of the uniformity of neurons on the unit hypersphere. It is formally defined as the sum of the hyperspherical similarity between all pairwise neurons within the same layer. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}$, the hyperspherical energy is calculated as:

$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$

In this equation, the term $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ represents the similarity between two normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$. When neurons are spaced far apart and uniformly across the hypersphere, the energy is minimized. Conversely, when neurons are clustered together, the energy increases. This metric allows us to characterize the "relational structure" of a layer.

A critical property of hyperspherical energy is its invariance under orthogonal transformations. If we apply the same orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ to all neurons in a layer—such that $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$—the pairwise angles between the neurons are preserved. Consequently, the hyperspherical energy of the finetuned model remains identical to that of the pretrained model. This invariance is the theoretical foundation for Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), as it provides a mathematical guarantee that the intrinsic structural relationship of the pretrained neurons will not be destroyed during adaptation to a downstream task.

# Angular Similarity vs. Magnitude: Which stores semantics?

A fundamental question in the design of finetuning strategies is whether the semantic knowledge of a model is stored in the magnitude of the weights or in their directions. Based on empirical observations, it appears that angular feature differences are far more effective at characterizing the semantic gap than magnitude differences. This implies that the direction of the neurons—their orientation on the hypersphere—is where the most important semantic information resides.

This intuition is supported by toy experiments using a standard convolutional autoencoder trained on images. When comparing three different ways of generating feature maps—using the standard inner product, using only magnitude information, and using only angular information—the results are striking. The angular information of the neurons is capable of almost perfectly recovering the original input images, whereas the magnitude information contains virtually no useful information for reconstruction. 

This discovery reveals that to semantically modify a generated image, such as in subject-driven or controllable generation tasks, the most effective approach is to change the direction of the neurons. Because the angles between neurons are crucial for representing the knowledge of the network, finetuning should prioritize adjusting these directions while maintaining the structural integrity of the overall neuron distribution.

# Case Study: The 2D Fourier Transform Analogy (Phase vs. Magnitude)

The importance of neuron direction can be further understood through an analogy with the 2D Fourier transform. In image processing, a 2D Fourier transform decomposes an image into a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. The magnitude spectrum describes the strength of various frequency components, while the phase spectrum contains the angular information between the input and the basis.

Crucially, the phase spectrum preserves the vast majority of the image's semantics. For instance, if one reconstructs an image using the original phase spectrum but replaces the original magnitude spectrum with a random one, the resulting image can still be recognized as the original subject. The semantic structure remains intact despite the total loss of magnitude information.

This phenomenon directly mirrors the behavior of neurons in deep networks. Just as the phase spectrum preserves the "where" and "what" of an image, the angles (directions) of neurons preserve the semantic knowledge of a model. Changing the neuron directions is equivalent to modifying the phase of the network's internal representations. This is why the goal of Orthogonal Finetuning is to rotate the neurons using an orthogonal matrix, as this operation adjusts the "phase" or direction of the neurons to suit a new task without collapsing the underlying semantic structure.

# The Relationship Between Hyperspherical Energy and Generalization

The relationship between hyperspherical energy and a model's performance varies depending on whether the model is being trained from scratch or finetuned. In the context of training classification networks from scratch, researchers have found that a small hyperspherical energy leads to better generalization. A low energy state means that neurons are uniformly distributed across the hypersphere, reducing redundancy and allowing the network to capture a more diverse set of features.

However, the objectives for finetuning a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model are different. In finetuning, the goal is not to maximize general diversity from scratch, but to adapt the model to a specific task (like a specific subject or control signal) without destroying the high-fidelity generative abilities learned during pretraining. If one were to simply minimize the hyperspherical energy during finetuning, the process would likely destroy the original semantic structures and the intrinsic pretrained knowledge.

Therefore, while classification training seeks to minimize HE, Orthogonal Finetuning seeks to preserve it. By ensuring that the difference in hyperspherical energy between the pretrained model $\bm{W}^0$ and the finetuned model $\bm{W}$ is zero, the model maintains the same level of neuron uniformity and relational structure it had during its original training. This balance allows the model to gain the flexibility needed for a new task while maintaining the stability and generative quality of the pretrained weights.
