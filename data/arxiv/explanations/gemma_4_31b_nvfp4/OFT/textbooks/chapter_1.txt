Chapter 1: Introduction to Controlled Text-to-Image Generation

# Overview of Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

Large-scale text-to-image diffusion models have fundamentally transformed the landscape of generative artificial intelligence, demonstrating an impressive ability to synthesize photorealistic images from natural language descriptions. These models leverage the diffusion process to iteratively refine noise into high-fidelity visual content guided by text embeddings. Depending on their architectural choices, these models typically operate in one of two primary domains: the pixel space or the latent space. For instance, models such as GLIDE and Imagen perform the diffusion process directly within the pixel space. In these architectures, the way the text encoder is handled varies; GLIDE trains the text encoder jointly with a diffusion prior using paired text-image data, whereas Imagen utilizes a frozen pretrained text encoder to maintain stability.

Alternatively, models like Stable Diffusion and DALL-E 2 operate within a latent space to reduce computational complexity while maintaining image quality. Stable Diffusion utilizes a VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook that serves as its latent space, while DALL-E 2 employs CLIP to construct a joint latent embedding space that represents both images and text. Despite these different architectural approaches, the common goal remains the same: to translate the semantic meaning of a text prompt into a coherent and visually accurate image. These models are often considered "model-agnostic" in the context of certain finetuning strategies, meaning that the fundamental principles of adaptation can be applied across various diffusion-based frameworks.

# The Ambiguity of Text Guidance

While text-to-image diffusion models are capable of generating stunning visuals, relying solely on text prompts presents a significant challenge: ambiguity. Text is a discrete and often imprecise medium for describing complex visual scenes. A user might provide a prompt that is conceptually clear but lacks the specificity required for fine-grained control over the spatial arrangement, precise geometry, or exact identity of objects within the generated image. This inherent limitation means that text guidance is often insufficient to provide the accurate, detailed control necessary for specialized downstream tasks.

Because text prompts cannot explicitly define every pixel or coordinate, the model must make a series of probabilistic guesses about the intended layout and appearance. When a user requires a specific output—such as a precise pose for a human face or the exact boundaries of a building—textual descriptions like "a person looking left" or "a city skyline" are too coarse. This gap between the user's intent and the model's interpretation creates a demand for more robust control mechanisms that can supplement text with additional, more precise signals to guide the generative process.

# Subject-Driven Generation: Concept and Goals

Subject-driven generation is a specialized task designed to overcome the limitations of general text-to-image models when a user wants to generate images of a specific, unique subject. In a standard text-to-image setup, a prompt for "a dog" will generate a generic dog. However, in subject-driven generation, the goal is to provide the model with a few images of a specific subject—such as a pet dog or a specific piece of art—and then generate new images of that same subject in entirely different contexts.

The primary goal of this task is to maintain the identity-related details of the subject while allowing the context to be modified via a text prompt. For example, if a user provides three images of their specific dog, they should be able to prompt the model to generate "that dog wearing a space suit on Mars" without the dog's unique features being lost or replaced by a generic dog. This requires the model to learn the specific visual characteristics of the subject from a very small dataset (often just a few images) and integrate that knowledge into the existing weights of the diffusion model without destroying the model's ability to generate diverse and high-quality backgrounds.

# Controllable Generation: Concept and Goals

Controllable generation extends the capabilities of diffusion models by introducing additional control signals beyond the text prompt. While subject-driven generation focuses on "who" or "what" is in the image, controllable generation focuses on "where" and "how" elements are structured. The goal is to enable the model to take in auxiliary signals—such as Canny edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks—and ensure the generated image strictly adheres to these constraints while still following the overall theme of the text prompt.

There are several key applications of this approach. In Canny edge-to-image (C2I) tasks, the model uses a rough outline of an image to determine the structure of the output. In segmentation map-to-image (S2I) tasks, a map designating which areas of the image belong to specific classes (e.g., "sky," "road," "building") dictates the layout. In landmark-to-face (L2F) tasks, specific points on a human face are used to control the exact pose and expression of the generated person. The ultimate objective of controllable generation is to achieve high-fidelity images that exhibit a strong "control consistency," meaning that if one were to extract the control signal from the generated image, it should closely match the original input signal.

# The Fundamental Trade-off: Training Efficiency vs. Generalizability Preservation

A central challenge in adapting large-scale diffusion models to these tasks is the tension between training efficiency and the preservation of generalizability. When finetuning a model for a specific subject or control signal, there is a risk of "model collapse" or the loss of the pretrained generative performance. The ideal finetuning method must satisfy two primary desiderata. First, it must be training-efficient, meaning it should require a small number of trainable parameters and a low number of training epochs to reach convergence. Second, it must preserve generalizability, ensuring that the model retains the high-fidelity, diverse, and realistic generation capabilities it acquired during its massive pretraining phase.

Traditionally, practitioners have attempted to balance this by updating neuron weights using a very small learning rate or by adding small, re-parameterized components to the model. These methods implicitly assume that a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights $\bm{M}$ and the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$, denoted as $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0 \|$, corresponds to better preservation of the pretrained abilities. However, this assumption is often insufficient. Simply minimizing the Euclidean difference does not fully capture the degree of semantic preservation. To truly preserve the "knowledge" of the network, a more structural measure is needed—one that looks beyond raw weight values to the relationships between neurons, such as the angles and directions they form on a hypersphere. This realization drives the need for more principled finetuning strategies that can modify the model's behavior without destroying its underlying semantic structure.
