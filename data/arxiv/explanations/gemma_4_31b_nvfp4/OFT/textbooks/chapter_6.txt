Chapter 6: Advanced Variants: COFT and Re-scaled OFT

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): The Radius Constraint

While basic Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) preserves the hyperspherical energy of a pretrained model by utilizing orthogonal transformations, it can still be overly flexible for certain downstream tasks. In the context of finetuning large-scale models, there is often a need to ensure that the finetuned weights do not deviate too drastically from the original pretrained weights to prevent the loss of general generative capabilities. To address this, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) introduces a structural limitation on the orthogonal matrix, effectively forcing the finetuned model to remain within a small neighborhood of the pretrained model.

The core mechanism of COFT is the introduction of an $\epsilon$-deviation constraint. In a standard OFT forward pass, the output vector $\bm{z}$ is computed as $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix and $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weights. COFT maintains the requirement that $\bm{R}$ must be orthogonal, meaning $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$. However, it adds the explicit constraint that $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This constraint ensures that the distance between the learned transformation $\bm{R}$ and the identity matrix $\bm{I}$ is bounded by a hyperparameter $\epsilon$.

By imposing this radius constraint, COFT explicitly limits the angular deviation of the neurons from their original positions in the pretrained weight space. The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ serves as a dial to control the trade-off between flexibility and stability. When $\epsilon$ is set to a large value, the model behaves similarly to standard OFT, allowing for significant rotation of the neurons. Conversely, as $\epsilon$ decreases, the finetuned model is constrained to behave more like the original pretrained text-to-image diffusion model, providing a tighter safeguard against the degradation of pretrained semantic knowledge.

# Implementing the Epsilon-Deviation Constraint via Projected Gradient Descent

Implementing the $\epsilon$-deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ is non-trivial because $\bm{R}$ is typically parameterized using the Cayley transform to ensure orthogonality. The Cayley parameterization defines the orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix such that $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. Because the constraint is defined on $\bm{R}$ but the optimization happens on $\bm{Q}$, a direct application of the radius constraint is computationally difficult.

To overcome this, COFT utilizes the Neumann series to approximate the relationship between $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{Q}$. Under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm, the expression for $\bm{R}$ can be approximated as $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$. This linear approximation allows the constraint on $\bm{R}$ to be moved inside the Cayley transform. Specifically, the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ is translated into an equivalent constraint on the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, formulated as $\|\bm{Q} - \bm{0}\| \leq \epsilon'$, where $\bm{0}$ is an all-zero matrix and $\epsilon'$ is a corresponding error hyperparameter.

Once the constraint is reformulated in terms of $\bm{Q}$, it can be efficiently enforced using projected gradient descent. During the training process, if the update to $\bm{Q}$ results in a value that exceeds the norm $\epsilon'$, it is projected back onto the boundary of the $\epsilon'$-ball. To ensure the model begins exactly at the pretrained state, $\bm{Q}$ is initialized as an all-zero matrix, which corresponds to $\bm{R}$ being the identity matrix. This combination of Cayley parameterization and projected gradient descent allows COFT to maintain strict orthogonality while explicitly controlling the deviation from the pretrained weights.

# Re-scaled OFT: Integrating Magnitude Scaling

The basic OFT framework focuses exclusively on adjusting the direction of neurons via orthogonal transformations. While this is highly effective for preserving hyperspherical energy—since hyperspherical energy is calculated based on normalized neurons where magnitude is factored out—it may limit the model's ability to adapt to the specific scales required by a downstream task. To enhance flexibility without sacrificing the benefits of orthogonal transformation, the authors propose Re-scaled OFT.

Re-scaled OFT introduces a learnable magnitude scaling coefficient for each neuron. In this variant, the forward pass is modified to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$. Here, $\bm{R}$ continues to function as the layer-shared orthogonal matrix that preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, while $\bm{D}$ is introduced as a learnable diagonal matrix. This architecture allows the model to simultaneously tune the orientation of the neurons and their respective magnitudes.

The intuition behind this extension is that re-scaling the neurons does not change the hyperspherical energy of the layer. Because the hyperspherical energy is defined as the sum of similarities between normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$, any scalar multiplication of a neuron's weight is neutralized during the normalization process. Therefore, adding a scaling factor provides the model with more degrees of freedom to optimize for a task without destroying the intrinsic pretrained structure that the orthogonal transformation is designed to preserve.

# The Role of the Diagonal Matrix D in Enhancing Flexibility

The diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ in Re-scaled OFT is defined as $\bm{D} = \text{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_n) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, where each diagonal element $s_i$ must be greater than zero. Each $s_i$ corresponds to a scaling factor for the $i$-th neuron in the weight matrix. By allowing these scalars to be learnable parameters, the model can independently adjust the "strength" or importance of different neurons in the layer.

This addition significantly increases the flexibility of the finetuning process. In standard OFT, the only way to modify the output is to rotate the weight vectors. With the inclusion of $\bm{D}$, the model can now perform a more nuanced adaptation where it can amplify or attenuate specific feature detectors. This added flexibility is particularly useful for complex downstream tasks, such as subject-driven or controllable generation, where the model might need to emphasize certain pretrained features over others to better match the target subject or control signal.

Crucially, the introduction of the diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ comes with a negligible increase in the number of trainable parameters. Since $\bm{D}$ only adds $n$ parameters per layer (where $n$ is the number of neurons), it remains highly parameter-efficient. Empirical findings indicate that Re-scaled OFT generally outperforms the original OFT, demonstrating that combining angular transformation with magnitude scaling provides a more comprehensive toolkit for model adaptation.

# Comparing the Stability of OFT and COFT

The primary distinction between OFT and COFT lies in the nature of their constraints. OFT is designed to minimize the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned and pretrained models. By using an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, OFT ensures that the pairwise angles between neurons are perfectly preserved, which is a structural measure of semantic preservation. However, OFT does not explicitly limit how far the weights can move in Euclidean space, provided the orthogonality is maintained.

COFT enhances this stability by combining the minimal hyperspherical energy difference of OFT with an explicit deviation constraint. While OFT provides a "regularity" by preserving angles, COFT adds a second layer of "regularity" by restricting the model to a fixed radius around the pretrained weights. This makes COFT a more constrained version of OFT, further reducing the risk of model collapse. In standard finetuning, using a small learning rate is an implicit attempt to minimize $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$; COFT makes this an explicit, enforceable constraint through the $\epsilon$-deviation.

Experimental results indicate that COFT provides even better finetuning stability than standard OFT. Because COFT strictly limits the flexibility of the transformation, it is less sensitive to the choice of learning rate and the number of training iterations. While OFT is already more stable than naive finetuning or LoRA, COFT's explicit constraint allows users to set relatively large iteration numbers without carefully tuning the hyperparameters for every individual subject, as the radius constraint prevents the model from drifting into unstable regions of the weight space.
