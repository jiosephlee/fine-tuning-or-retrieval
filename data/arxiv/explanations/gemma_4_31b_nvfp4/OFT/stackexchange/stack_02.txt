\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does the block-diagonal structure of $\bm{R}$ reduce parameters without breaking orthogonality?
Question:
In Section 3.3, the authors describe representing the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal matrix with $r$ blocks, where each block $\bm{R}_i$ is itself an orthogonal matrix of size $\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}$. 

I'm confused about how this works. If we only have these small blocks on the diagonal and zeros everywhere else, does the resulting large $d \times d$ matrix $\bm{R}$ still satisfy the condition $\bm{R}^T\bm{R} = \bm{I}$? Also, does restricting $\bm{R}$ to this block-diagonal form limit the 'rotation' capabilities compared to a full orthogonal matrix?
Answer:
The block-diagonal structure of $\bm{R}$ preserves orthogonality because the product of two block-diagonal matrices is simply the block-wise product of their corresponding components. Specifically, if $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$, then the transpose $\bm{R}^T$ is also block-diagonal with blocks $\bm{R}_1^T, \bm{R}_2^T, \dots, \bm{R}_r^T$. When computing the product $\bm{R}^T\bm{R}$, the resulting matrix is a block-diagonal matrix where each block is the product $\bm{R}_i^T\bm{R}_i$. Since the authors ensure that each individual block $\bm{R}_i$ is itself an orthogonal matrix of size $\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}$, it follows that $\bm{R}_i^T\bm{R}_i = \bm{I}_{d/r}$ for all $i$. Consequently, the full $d \times d$ matrix $\bm{R}^T\bm{R}$ becomes a diagonal of identity matrices, which is exactly the identity matrix $\bm{I}_d$. This allows the model to satisfy the global orthogonality constraint while only needing to maintain orthogonality within smaller, independent subspaces.

This architecture significantly reduces the number of parameters by introducing sparsity. A full orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ has a parameter complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ because almost every element must be learned to maintain the constraint. In contrast, the block-diagonal form restricts the learnable parameters to the blocks on the diagonal. Since there are $r$ blocks, each with a complexity of $\mathcal{O}((\frac{d}{r})^2)$, the total complexity becomes $r \cdot \mathcal{O}(\frac{d^2}{r^2})$, which simplifies to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. If the authors choose to share the blocks (setting $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$), the complexity drops even further to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$.

Regarding the "rotation" capabilities, you are correct that this restriction limits the flexibility of the transformation. A full orthogonal matrix can rotate any vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$ toward any other direction on the unit hypersphere. A block-diagonal matrix, however, can only rotate vectors within their respective $\frac{d}{r}$-dimensional subspaces; it cannot "mix" dimensions across different blocks. While this reduces the degrees of freedom, the authors argue that this is actually a desirable trade-off. In the context of finetuning, too much flexibility can lead to model collapse or the destruction of the pretrained semantic structure. By using the block-diagonal structure, OFT imposes a stronger regularization that balances the need for semantic modification (changing neuron directions) with the need for stability (preserving the pretraining hyperspherical energy).
