\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Connection between 2D Fourier phase spectrum and orthogonal weight transformations
Question:
In the introduction, the authors provide an intuition for why orthogonal transformations work by citing 2D Fourier transforms, specifically that the phase spectrum (angular information) preserves semantics while the magnitude spectrum is less critical. 

I'm trying to connect this to the actual mechanism of OFT. In a Fourier transform, the phase refers to the shift/position of sinusoids. In OFT, they are rotating neuron weights in a weight matrix $\bm{W}$. How does the concept of 'phase spectrum' in image processing mathematically map to the 'neuron directions' in a neural network layer? Is this a literal mathematical analogy or just a conceptual heuristic?
Answer:
The connection between the 2D Fourier phase spectrum and Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a conceptual heuristic rather than a literal mathematical identity. In the context of the paper, the authors use this analogy to motivate why focusing on the *direction* of neurons is more important for preserving and modifying semantics than focusing on their *magnitude*.

In a 2D Fourier Transform, an image is decomposed into magnitude and phase spectra. The magnitude spectrum tells you "how much" of a certain frequency is present, while the phase spectrum tells you "where" those frequencies are located. A well-known property in image processing is that the phase spectrum encodes the structural and semantic layout of the image; if you reconstruct an image using the original phase but a random magnitude, the image remains recognizable. The authors draw a parallel here: they hypothesize that the semantic knowledge of a neural network is similarly encoded in the relative angular relationships (the directions) of the neurons rather than their absolute magnitudes.

Mathematically, this maps to the weight matrix $\bm{W}$ through the concept of hyperspherical energy. The authors define the semantic structure of a layer by the pairwise angles between normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$. An orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ (where $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$) is specifically chosen because it preserves the inner product between vectors. Since the cosine similarity between two neurons $\bm{w}_i$ and $\bm{w}_j$ is defined as $\frac{\bm{w}_i^\top \bm{w}_j}{\|\bm{w}_i\| \|\bm{w}_j\|}$, applying the same $\bm{R}$ to all neurons in a layer ensures that the angle between any two neurons remains invariant: $(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i)^\top (\bm{R}\bm{w}_j) = \bm{w}_i^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} \bm{w}_j = \bm{w}_i^\top \bm{w}_j$. 

Therefore, the "phase spectrum" in the analogy corresponds to the relative geometry of the neurons on the unit hypersphere. By learning $\bm{R}$, OFT effectively rotates the entire "coordinate system" of the neurons. This allows the model to adapt to new semantic targets (like a specific subject or a control signal) by shifting the global direction of the neurons while strictly preserving the internal relative angles that characterize the pretrained generative ability. In short, the Fourier analogy justifies the focus on angular transformations as the primary vehicle for semantic modification without destroying the model's fundamental structural knowledge.
