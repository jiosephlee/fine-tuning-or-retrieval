Chapter 5: # BLT Architecture: The Local Decoder

The Byte Latent Transformer ({\textsc BLT}}) is designed to move away from the rigid constraints of fixed-vocabulary tokenization by operating directly on raw bytes. To achieve this while maintaining computational efficiency, the architecture employs a hierarchical structure. While the Latent Global Transformer handles high-level reasoning across patches, the process must eventually return to the byte level to produce readable text. This is the primary responsibility of the Local Decoder.

# Role of the Local Decoder Model

The Local Decoder, denoted as $\mathcal{D}$, is a lightweight transformer-based model designed to map the high-level output of the global model back into the granular sequence of raw bytes. In the broader {\textsc BLT} pipeline, the global model produces a sequence of output patch representations, $o_j$. These representations are abstract and latent; they contain the necessary semantic and syntactic information to predict the next segment of text, but they are not bytes themselves. The Local Decoder acts as the final translation layer, decoding these global patch representations $o_j$ into a sequence of raw bytes, $y_i$.

Because the Local Decoder is tasked with generating bytes, it must operate autoregressively. It predicts each raw byte as a function of the bytes that have already been decoded. To ensure that the decoding process is grounded in the original input context, the Local Decoder does not work in isolation. It takes as input the hidden representations produced by the Local Encoder for the byte-sequence. By combining these local byte-level features with the global patch-level insights, the decoder can accurately reconstruct the intended byte sequence.

# Symmetry and Differences Between Encoder and Decoder

The Local Decoder is designed as a structural counterpart to the Local Encoder. Both are "local" models, meaning they are significantly smaller than the Latent Global Transformer, with far fewer layers ($l_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$). Both models utilize a sequence of alternating layers consisting of cross-attention and transformer blocks. This symmetry allows the architecture to effectively compress bytes into patches at the start and expand patches back into bytes at the end.

Despite these similarities, there are critical differences in how these two modules function. First, the order of operations is shifted: in the Local Decoder, the cross-attention layer is applied before the transformer layer. This ensures that the model first incorporates the global patch information before the local transformer layer refines the byte-level representations. Second, the roles of the queries, keys, and values in the cross-attention mechanism are interchanged. While the encoder uses a patch-based query to pool byte information, the decoder uses byte-based queries to extract information from the patches.

# Decoder Multi-Headed Cross-Attention: Patch-to-Byte Mapping

The core of the Local Decoder's ability to map patches back to bytes lies in its multi-headed cross-attention mechanism. In this stage, the goal is to determine which parts of the global patch representation $o_j$ are relevant for predicting the current byte $y_i$. This is achieved by treating the byte-representations as queries and the patch representations as the keys and values.

The process begins with the initialization of byte-representations, where $D_0$ is set to the output of the last encoder layer, $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$. For each subsequent layer $l$, the model computes a new set of byte-representations $B_l$ through a cross-attention operation. The queries $Q_i$ are derived from the byte-representations of the previous layer, $d_{l-1,i}$, via a projection matrix $W_q$. Simultaneously, the patch representations $o_j$ are processed by a linear transformation and split operation, denoted as $\mathcal{D}_C$, and then projected into keys $K_i$ and values $V_i$ using $W_k$ and $W_v$.

The resulting byte-representations are calculated as:
\begin{align}
B_l &= D_{l-1} + W_o\left(\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right)
\end{align}
This mechanism allows the model to "attend" to the global latent state. Because the queries are at the byte level and the keys/values are at the patch level, the model can precisely map the high-level intent of the Global Transformer to the specific byte being generated. This module employs multi-headed attention, pre-LayerNorm, and a residual connection around the cross-attention block, and it does not use positional embeddings.

# The Decoder Transformer Layer and Byte Prediction

Once the cross-attention layer has integrated the global patch information into the byte stream, the resulting representations $B_l$ are passed into a decoder transformer layer. The purpose of this layer is to perform local processing on the byte sequence, ensuring that the predicted bytes are coherent and follow the local patterns of the language.

The output of the decoder transformer layer, $D_l$, represents the final refined state of the byte sequence for that layer. After passing through $l_{\mathcal{D}}$ such alternating layers of cross-attention and transformer processing, the model is capable of predicting the actual raw byte $y_i$. By operating on the refined representations, the Local Decoder ensures that the final output is not just a product of the global model's high-level logic, but is also linguistically sound at the character and byte level.

# End-to-End Information Flow: Byte $\rightarrow$ Patch $\rightarrow$ Patch $\rightarrow$ Byte

The Local Decoder represents the final segment of a sophisticated information pipeline that allows {\textsc BLT} to operate without a fixed vocabulary. To understand the decoder's role, one must look at the entire flow of data. The process begins with a sequence of raw bytes. These bytes are first processed by the Local Encoder, which uses its own cross-attention mechanism to pool these bytes into latent patch representations, $p_j$. This step effectively compresses the data, reducing the sequence length and allowing the model to allocate compute more efficiently.

These patch representations $p_j$ are then fed into the Latent Global Transformer. This is the most computationally expensive part of the model, where the transformer performs complex autoregressive reasoning on the patches. The output of this stage is a new sequence of output patch representations, $o_j$, which contain the "answers" or the predicted next steps of the sequence in a latent form.

Finally, the Local Decoder takes these output patches $o_j$ and reverses the initial compression. By using the byte-level queries in its cross-attention layers, the decoder expands the latent patches back into a sequence of raw bytes $y_i$. This end-to-end flow—from bytes to patches, from patches to patches, and finally from patches back to bytes—enables {\textsc BLT} to maintain the efficiency of patch-based computation while retaining the robustness and flexibility of raw byte modeling.
