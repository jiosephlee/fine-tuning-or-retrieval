\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 6: Scaling Laws and Computational Efficiency

# Estimating FLOPs for Byte-Level Models

To understand the computational cost of the Byte Latent Transformer ($\text{BLT}$), one must first understand how Floating Point Operations (FLOPs) are calculated in a transformer-based architecture. The $\text{BLT}$ model follows the general principles established by the Chinchilla scaling laws, accounting for the operations required by feed-forward layers, the $QKVO$ projections within self-attention layers, the computation of the attention matrix itself, and the final output projection. A key simplification in the $\text{BLT}$ framework is the treatment of the input embedding layer as an efficient lookup table rather than a dense matrix multiplication, which effectively reduces its computational cost to zero FLOPs. Additionally, it is standard practice in these estimations to assume that the backwards pass during training requires twice as many FLOPs as the forward pass.

The total computational cost per byte in a $\text{BLT}$ model is an aggregate of several distinct components: the local encoder transformer, the global latent transformer, the local decoder transformer, and the cross-attention blocks found in both the encoder and decoder. Because $\text{BLT}$ operates on two different levels of granularity—individual bytes and grouped patches—the FLOPs must be calculated relative to the sequence length and the patch size. Specifically, the cost of the global latent transformer is divided by the patch size $n_p$ because it operates on patches rather than bytes, meaning it is invoked fewer times than the local models.

The formal estimation of $\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}}$ involves summing the transformer FLOPs of the three main models and the cross-attention FLOPs. For the local encoder, the cost is tied to the encoder window $w_{\mathcal{E}}$, while for the local decoder, it is tied to the decoder window $w_{\mathcal{D}}$ and the byte vocabulary size $V=256$. The cross-attention calculations also depend on the ratio of queries to keys/values and the ratio $k$, which represents the number of local model splits that are concatenated to form a global model representation. By summing these disparate components, researchers can determine the exact compute budget required to process a specific number of bytes.

# Compute-Optimal Scaling Trends

Compute-optimal scaling refers to a training regime where the ratio of model parameters to training data is optimized to achieve the best possible performance within a given budget of total training FLOPs. In traditional Large Language Models (LLMs), this is usually determined by a specific balance between the size of the model and the amount of tokens it sees. The $\text{BLT}$ research seeks to determine if these same "compute-optimal" principles apply to models that operate directly on raw bytes without a fixed vocabulary.

When comparing $\text{BLT}$ to Byte Pair Encoding ($\text{BPE}$) models—such as Llama 3—the results indicate that $\text{BLT}$ either matches or outperforms $\text{BPE}$ counterparts across various sizes, ranging from 1B to 8B parameters. This finding is significant because it validates the assumption that the optimal parameter-to-compute ratio identified for token-based models is largely applicable to byte-level architectures. It suggests that moving away from tokenization does not fundamentally break the scaling laws that govern how model performance improves with more data and larger parameters.

Crucially, achieving this parity requires more than just using bytes; it requires the specific architectural innovations of $\text{BLT}$, particularly dynamic patching and cross-attention. When compared to simpler byte-level approaches, such as "SpaceByte" or strided patching (where bytes are grouped every $k$ bytes), $\text{BLT}$ shows a marked advantage. Static patching schemes fail to allocate compute where it is most needed, often wasting transformer steps on predictable sequences or under-allocating compute for complex data. Dynamic patching allows $\text{BLT}$ to maintain the high performance of $\text{BPE}$ models while operating on the more robust raw byte stream.

# Training FLOP-Controlled Parity with Llama 3

One of the primary achievements of the $\text{BLT}$ architecture is demonstrating training FLOP-controlled parity with Llama 3 up to an 8B parameter scale. In a FLOP-controlled experiment, both the $\text{BPE}$-based model and the $\text{BLT}$ model are trained using the same total number of floating-point operations. This ensures that any difference in performance is a result of the architecture and the patching strategy rather than one model simply having more "compute" or more training time than the other.

In evaluations using the $\text{BLT}$-1T dataset, the $\text{BLT}$-Entropy model—which uses next-byte entropy to determine patch boundaries—outperformed the Llama 3 model on four out of seven standard tasks, including common sense reasoning and world knowledge. This is particularly impressive given that both models were trained with the same budget. The performance gain is attributed to two factors: the more efficient allocation of training compute via dynamic patching and the model's ability to directly capture byte-level information, which $\text{BPE}$ models lose during the tokenization process.

While a model using "space-patching" (grouping bytes by whitespace) was also tested, it generally underperformed compared to the Llama 3 tokenizer. However, the $\text{BLT}$-Entropy variant proves that when compute is allocated dynamically based on the complexity of the data, a tokenizer-free model can not only match but exceed the performance of state-of-the-art tokenized models. This establishes that the "bottleneck" in previous byte-level models was not the use of bytes themselves, but the lack of a dynamic mechanism to group those bytes into computationally efficient units.

# Inference Efficiency: Trading Patch Size for Model Size

A fundamental limitation of tokenization-based models is that they allocate the same amount of compute to every token, regardless of whether that token is a common word or a complex technical term. In contrast, $\text{BLT}$ introduces a mechanism to trade patch size for model size to optimize inference efficiency. Since the global latent transformer is the most computationally expensive part of the model, the number of times it is invoked during inference determines the total inference cost.

By increasing the average patch size, $\text{BLT}$ reduces the number of steps the global transformer must execute. For example, moving from a patch size of 4.5 bytes to 8 bytes can lead to nearly 50% savings in inference FLOPs. This is because larger patches mean the model spends more time in the lightweight local encoder and decoder and less time in the heavy global transformer. This creates a unique opportunity: the FLOPs saved by using larger patches can be reallocated to increase the actual size (number of parameters) of the global latent transformer.

This trade-off allows $\text{BLT}$ to maintain a fixed inference budget while simultaneously growing the model's capacity. In practical terms, a user can choose a version of the model that is physically larger (more parameters) but executes fewer steps (larger patches), maintaining the same speed (inference cost) while potentially achieving higher accuracy. This flexibility is impossible in $\text{BPE}$ models, where the vocabulary size and token length are fixed prior to training and cannot be dynamically adjusted during inference to save compute.

# Fixed Inference Budget Scaling: Why Patches Scale Better Than Tokens

The concept of "fixed inference budget scaling" explores how model performance evolves when the cost per inference step is held constant. In this regime, $\text{BLT}$ exhibits significantly better scaling trends than $\text{BPE}$ architectures. While $\text{BPE}$ models may perform better at very small training budgets, $\text{BLT}$ quickly surpasses them as the training budget increases beyond the compute-optimal point.

The reason patches scale better than tokens lies in the ability to scale the global transformer and the patch size in tandem. As the total parameter count of the model grows—for instance, from 400M to 8B parameters—the share of total FLOPs consumed by the local encoder and decoder becomes smaller relative to the global transformer. Because the local models scale more slowly than the global transformer, the efficiency gains from larger patch sizes become even more pronounced at larger scales.

Experimental data shows that $\text{BLT}$ models with a patch size of 8 bytes have a steeper scaling trend in larger flop classes, overtaking $\text{BPE}$ models sooner. This suggests that as we push toward even larger model scales, the advantage of using dynamic patches over fixed tokens increases. Essentially, $\text{BLT}$ unlocks a new dimension of scaling: instead of just increasing parameters or data, researchers can optimize the "granularity" of the computation (the patch size) to maximize the performance gained for every single FLOP spent during inference.

# Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) as a Tokenizer-Independent Metric

In standard language modeling, "perplexity" is the primary metric used to measure a model's uncertainty. However, perplexity is intrinsically tied to the tokenizer; it measures uncertainty per token. Because different tokenizers produce different numbers of tokens for the same string, perplexity cannot be used to fairly compare a $\text{BPE}$-based model with a byte-level model. To solve this, $\text{BLT}$ utilizes Bits-Per-Byte ($\text{BPB}$), a tokenizer-independent version of perplexity.

The $\text{BPB}$ is calculated by taking the sum of the cross-entropy loss $\mathcal{L}_{CE}$ over a sequence $\pmb{x}$ and normalizing it by the total number of bytes $n_{\text{bytes}}$ and a constant $\ln(2)$. The formula is expressed as:

$$\text{BPB}(x)= \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}$$

By normalizing the loss by the number of bytes rather than the number of tokens, $\text{BPB}$ provides a "universal currency" for measuring language modeling performance. This allows researchers to compare the efficiency of the $\text{BLT}$ architecture directly against $\text{BPE}$ models, as well as different patching strategies (such as space-patching vs. entropy-patching), without the results being biased by how the input text was segmented. This metric is essential for validating that $\text{BLT}$ achieves parity with Llama 3, as it removes the inherent advantage that $\text{BPE}$ models have by processing "chunks" of text as single units.
