\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 4: BLT Architecture: The Global Latent Transformer

# The Latent Global Transformer Model

The Latent Global Transformer, denoted as $\mathcal{G}$, serves as the central computational engine of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) architecture. It is designed as an autoregressive transformer model consisting of $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers. The primary objective of this model is to map a sequence of latent input patch representations, $p_j$, into a corresponding sequence of output patch representations, $o_j$. In this notation, the subscript $j$ is used specifically to denote indices relating to patches, while the subscript $i$ is reserved for indices relating to individual bytes.

To ensure high performance, the global model follows the general architectural hyperparameters of Llama 3. This includes the use of the SwiGLU activation function within the feed-forward layers, RMSNorm for layer normalization, and rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) with $\theta=500000$ specifically within the self-attention layers. Because the global transformer is significantly larger and more computationally intensive than the local models, it consumes the vast majority of the floating point operations (flops) during both the pre-training phase and the inference phase. By controlling when and how often this model is invoked, the BLT architecture can precisely manage the amount of compute expended relative to the complexity of the data.

# Block-Causal Attention Masks

A critical component of the Latent Global Transformer is its use of a block-causal attention mask. In a standard causal transformer, each token attends to all preceding tokens in a sequence. However, the global model in BLT operates on patches rather than individual bytes or fixed tokens. The block-causal attention mask restricts the attention mechanism such that it can only attend to information up to and including the current patch within the current document.

This masking strategy is essential for maintaining the autoregressive property of the model while operating at the patch level. By ensuring that the model cannot "look ahead" into future patches, the architecture preserves the integrity of the next-byte prediction task. This structure allows the global model to process high-level latent representations of byte groups, treating each patch as a single unit of information while maintaining the necessary constraints for generative language modeling.

# Processing Latent Patch Representations

The global model does not interact with raw bytes directly. Instead, it processes latent patch representations. These patches are the primary units of computation in the BLT system. Unlike traditional Large Language Models (LLMs) that use a fixed vocabulary to map tokens to embeddings, BLT utilizes a dynamic grouping mechanism. The latent representations $p_j$ are produced by a lightweight local encoder and serve as the input to the global transformer $\mathcal{G}$.

The transition from $p_j$ to $o_j$ allows the model to perform complex reasoning and semantic processing in a latent space. Because the patches are dynamically sized based on the entropy of the data, the global model is essentially operating on a compressed version of the input stream. The output $o_j$ is then passed to a local decoder, which is responsible for expanding these latent representations back into a sequence of raw bytes. This separation of concerns—where the global model handles the "latent" semantic heavy lifting and the local models handle the "surface" byte-level mapping—is what enables BLT to match the performance of tokenization-based models.

# Dynamic Compute Allocation: Why Patches Reduce FLOPs

The core intuition behind the BLT architecture is that not all parts of a data sequence require the same amount of computational effort. In traditional tokenization-based LLMs, the model allocates the same amount of compute to every token, regardless of whether that token is a common piece of whitespace or a complex technical term. BLT replaces this static allocation with dynamic compute allocation based on the entropy of the next-byte prediction.

The model recognizes that predicting the ending of most words is relatively easy and involves low entropy, whereas choosing the first word of a new sentence is a high-entropy, more difficult decision. By segmenting bytes into patches based on this entropy, BLT creates contextualized groupings with uniform information density. When the data is predictable (low entropy), the system creates longer patches. Because the Latent Global Transformer is invoked once per patch, longer patches result in fewer total steps for the global model.

This mechanism allows for a unique scaling advantage. For a fixed inference flop budget, one can simultaneously increase both the patch size and the model size. Specifically, by increasing the average patch size, the global model is run less frequently, which saves compute. This saved compute can then be reallocated to grow the size of the global latent transformer (increasing $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ or the hidden dimension). This means that BLT can achieve better scaling trends than tokenization-based models because it can employ a larger, more powerful global model without increasing the total computational cost per byte.

# Interactions Between the Local and Global Models

The BLT architecture is structured as a tripartite system: a local encoder, a global latent transformer, and a local decoder. The local encoder $\mathcal{E}$ is a lightweight transformer-based model with $l_{\mathcal{E}}$ layers, where $l_{\mathcal{E}}$ is significantly smaller than $l_{\mathcal{G}}$. Its role is to map a sequence of input bytes $b_i$ into the expressive patch representations $p_j$ that the global model requires.

The global model $\mathcal{G}$ then processes these patches to produce $o_j$. Finally, the local decoder $\mathcal{D}$—also a lightweight model with $l_{\mathcal{D}}$ layers—takes these output patch representations and decodes them back into raw bytes $y_i$. The local decoder functions by predicting a sequence of raw bytes based on the hidden representations produced by the local encoder.

The interaction is characterized by a significant disparity in scale and resolution. The local models operate at the byte level, dealing with the high-resolution details of the text, including orthographic and character-level information. The global model operates at the patch level, focusing on the high-level semantic and structural components of the language. By utilizing a lightweight local infrastructure to "bridge" the gap between raw bytes and the massive global transformer, BLT achieves the efficiency of a token-based model while retaining the robustness and flexibility of a byte-level model.
