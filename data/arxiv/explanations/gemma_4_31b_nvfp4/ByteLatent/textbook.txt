\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Introduction to Byte-Level Language Modeling

# The Role and Limitations of Tokenization (BPE)

In the architecture of traditional Large Language Models (LLMs), tokenization serves as a critical heuristic pre-processing step. The most common approach is Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), which groups raw bytes into a static set of tokens. This process effectively compresses the input string, allowing the model to process a sequence of tokens rather than a much longer sequence of individual bytes. By operating on tokens, the model can handle longer contexts with fewer computational steps, as each token typically represents multiple characters or bytes.

However, this reliance on a fixed vocabulary introduces several fundamental limitations. Because the tokens are determined by a static vocabulary created before the training process begins, the model is essentially viewing the data through a biased lens. The way a string is compressed is dictated by these pre-defined heuristics rather than the actual complexity or information density of the data. This means the model's understanding of the input is coupled to the specific way the tokenizer was constructed, rather than a direct understanding of the underlying bytes.

Furthermore, the trade-off between vocabulary size and compute in tokenization-based models is rigid. To increase the average number of bytes per token, one must typically increase the size of the embedding table, which increases the output dimension of the final projection layer. This creates a ceiling on how much efficiency can be gained through tokenization, as the benefits of larger tokens are eventually offset by the increased memory and computational requirements of a massive embedding matrix.

# Issues with Static Vocabularies: Domain Sensitivity and Multilingual Inequity

The use of a static vocabulary leads to significant issues regarding how models generalize across different domains and languages. Because the tokenizer is trained on a specific corpus, it may not be optimized for all types of data. This results in domain and modality sensitivity, where the model performs inconsistently when encountering text from a specialized field or a different medium that was not well-represented in the tokenizer's training set.

This limitation is particularly evident in the context of multilingual inequity. Different languages possess different orthographic and structural properties; a tokenizer optimized for English may be highly efficient for English text but extremely inefficient for other languages. In such cases, the model may require significantly more tokens to represent a sentence in a low-resource language than it would for a similar sentence in English. This disparity means that the model must expend more computational effort to process the same amount of semantic information in some languages compared to others, leading to an inherent imbalance in performance and efficiency.

Beyond linguistic issues, static vocabularies hinder the model's ability to acquire orthographic knowledge. Since the model processes tokens as atomic units, it often lacks a direct understanding of how those tokens are spelled or how characters relate to one another. This lack of granular awareness makes it difficult for tokenizer-based models to perform tasks that require a precise understanding of the internal structure of a word, such as spelling or phonological analysis.

# The Challenge of Raw Byte Modeling: Sequence Length and Computational Cost

If tokenization is so limiting, a natural question is why models are not simply trained on raw bytes. The primary obstacle to byte-level modeling is the massive increase in sequence length. Because a single word can be composed of many bytes, the number of steps required to process a document increases dramatically when operating at the byte level. For a standard Transformer, this increase in sequence length would be prohibitively costly in terms of both memory and time.

While previous research attempted to mitigate this through more efficient self-attention mechanisms or attention-free architectures, these solutions primarily helped only for small models. At a larger scale, the computational bottleneck of a Transformer is not actually the attention mechanism, but the large feed-forward network (FFN) layers. These layers must be executed for every single unit in the sequence. When the unit is a byte, the FFNs must run many more times than they would for a token, leading to a vast increase in the total number of floating-point operations (flops) required.

Consequently, raw byte modeling creates a conflict between granularity and efficiency. To achieve the granularity of bytes, the model must sacrifice the efficiency of tokens, making the training and inference of large-scale byte-level models computationally exhausting. The challenge, therefore, is to find a way to operate on bytes without forcing the most expensive parts of the model to run on every single byte.

# Orthographic Knowledge and Sensitivity to Input Noise

One of the most significant disadvantages of tokenization is the resulting sensitivity to input noise. Because tokenizers are rigid, a small change at the byte level—such as a typo, a random capitalization change, or a repeated character—can completely change the tokens produced. For example, a word like "Apple" and "apple" might be mapped to different tokens, or a misspelled word might be broken into several rare, meaningless tokens. This makes tokenizer-based models fragile when faced with "noisy" data, as the model cannot easily see that the underlying byte sequence is nearly identical to a known word.

This fragility extends to tasks requiring character-level understanding. Tokenizer-based models often struggle with orthographic similarity or the manipulation of character sequences. They may "know" a word's meaning but fail to manipulate its spelling effectively because they do not have direct access to the byte-level features. This is observed in tasks involving phonology, such as mapping graphemes to phonemes, where the internal structure of the word is essential.

In contrast, a model that operates directly on bytes possesses an inherent awareness of the constituents of the text. By processing the raw data, the model can recognize that "A-p-p-l-e" and "a-p-p-l-e" are the same sequence of characters regardless of the case, and it can maintain robustness even when characters are dropped or repeated. This allows for a deeper and more flexible understanding of the long-tail of data, where standard tokenization often fails.

# Overview of the BLT Proposal: Dynamic Compute Allocation

The Byte Latent Transformer ($\text{BLT}$) is proposed as a solution that combines the robustness of byte-level modeling with the efficiency of token-level processing. The core intuition behind $\text{BLT}$ is that not all parts of a byte sequence require the same amount of computational effort. For instance, predicting the first letter of a word is often a high-complexity task (high entropy), whereas predicting the remaining letters of a common word is relatively simple (low entropy). Tokenization-based models waste compute by applying the same large Transformer to every token, regardless of its complexity.

$\text{BLT}$ addresses this by replacing static tokens with "patches." Patches are dynamically sized groups of bytes that serve as the primary units of computation for the model's most expensive component. Instead of using a fixed vocabulary, $\text{BLT}$ uses a data-driven approach to determine where a patch should begin and end. This is achieved by using a small, lightweight byte-level language model to estimate the entropy of the next-byte prediction. When the entropy is high, the model identifies a boundary and starts a new patch, effectively allocating more compute to complex segments of the data.

The $\text{BLT}$ architecture consists of three main parts: a Local Encoder, a Global Latent Transformer, and a Local Decoder. The Local Encoder maps the bytes into latent patch representations, which are then processed by the large Global Latent Transformer. Finally, the Local Decoder converts these patch representations back into raw bytes. By running the expensive Global Transformer only on these patches—rather than on every individual byte—$\text{BLT}$ dynamically allocates its computational resources, matching the performance of tokenization-based models while significantly increasing robustness and inference efficiency.

# The Mechanism of Patching

In traditional large language models, the process of converting raw text into a format the model can process is handled by tokenization. This is typically a heuristic pre-processing step that groups bytes into a static set of tokens based on a fixed vocabulary. However, this approach introduces several biases and inefficiencies, as the model must allocate the same amount of computational resources to every token, regardless of whether that token is a common piece of punctuation or a complex technical term. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) addresses these shortcomings by replacing fixed tokenization with a dynamic process called patching.

Patching is the mechanism by which BLT groups individual bytes into variable-sized "patches," which then serve as the primary units of computation for the model's main transformer. The fundamental intuition behind patching is that the computational cost of a transformer is primarily determined by the number of steps it must execute. By grouping bytes into patches, BLT can significantly reduce the sequence length that the main transformer needs to process. This allows the model to dynamically allocate more compute to complex, high-information data and less compute to predictable, low-information data.

# Defining the Patching Function

To understand how BLT manages this transition from raw bytes to computational units, we must first define the patching function. Formally, a patching function $f_p$ takes a sequence of bytes $\pmb{x}=\{x_i,|i=1,\ldots n\}$ of length $n$ and segments it into a sequence of $m$ patches, $\pmb{p}=\{p_j|j=1,\ldots,m\}$, where $m$ is necessarily smaller than $n$. The function achieves this by mapping each byte $x_i$ to a binary value in the set $\{0, 1\}$, where a value of 1 indicates the start of a new patch and 0 indicates the continuation of the current patch.

The efficiency of this system is governed by the average patch size. Because the main transformer—the global latent transformer—operates on these patches rather than individual bytes, the number of patches determines the total number of floating point operations (flops) required during both training and inference. By altering the patching function to change the average patch size, researchers can directly control the computational budget of the model. This flexibility allows BLT to move away from the rigid constraints of a fixed vocabulary, instead treating the grouping of bytes as a dynamic process that can be optimized for efficiency.

# Static Patching: Strided Fixed-Size Groups

The most basic approach to grouping bytes is strided patching, where bytes are grouped into patches of a fixed size $k$. In this scheme, every $k$-th byte marks the start of a new patch, creating a uniform stride across the entire sequence. From an implementation standpoint, strided patching is highly attractive because it is straightforward to deploy for both training and inference. It provides a predictable mechanism for controlling the total flop cost, as the number of transformer steps is always exactly $n/k$.

Despite its simplicity, static strided patching suffers from significant theoretical and practical downsides. The primary issue is that it fails to allocate compute based on the actual complexity of the data. For instance, the model might waste a full transformer step processing a sequence of whitespace characters in a block of code, while simultaneously failing to allocate sufficient compute to a dense sequence of mathematical symbols that carry high information density. Furthermore, this method is non-contextual; the same word may be split across different patches depending on its position in the sequence, preventing the model from consistently representing similar linguistic units.

# Heuristic Patching: Space-Based Segmentation

To improve upon the rigidity of strided patching, one can employ heuristic patching, specifically space patching. This method utilizes the observation that in many languages, space-like bytes serve as natural boundaries for linguistic units. A byte is defined as space-like if it is not a Latin character, a digit, or a UTF-8 continuation byte. Under this scheme, the patching function creates a new patch boundary after any space-like byte, provided that the patch contains at least one non space-like byte.

The intuition behind space patching is that the most difficult part of predicting a word is typically predicting its first byte. Once the first character of a word like "Mozart" is determined, the remaining bytes are comparatively easy to predict. By allocating a new latent transformer step to every word, space patching ensures that the model expends more effort on the "hard" predictions that follow spaces. While this is more effective than strided patching and ensures that words are patched consistently across different sequences, it still relies on a rule-based heuristic. This means it cannot gracefully handle all languages or domains, and it lacks a mechanism to vary the patch size based on the actual unpredictability of the data.

# Entropy-Based Patching: Using a Small Byte LM for Boundary Detection

The most sophisticated method implemented in BLT is entropy patching. Rather than relying on fixed strides or whitespace heuristics, entropy patching takes a data-driven approach to identify where compute is most needed. This is achieved by using a small, separate byte-level auto-regressive language model (LM) to estimate the entropy of the next-byte prediction. The entropy $H(x_i)$ for a byte $x_i$ is calculated using the LM distribution $p_e$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ as follows:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$

In this context, entropy serves as a proxy for uncertainty. When the entropy is high, the next byte is difficult to predict, suggesting that the model should trigger a new patch boundary to invoke the full power of the global latent transformer. Conversely, when entropy is low, the data is predictable, and the model can group more bytes into a single patch, thereby saving computational resources. This process is performed during a lightweight preprocessing step during dataloading, ensuring that the main model's compute is focused on the most complex segments of the input.

# Global vs. Approximate Monotonic Constraints for Entropy

To translate entropy values into actual patch boundaries, BLT experiments with two distinct constraints. The first is the Global Constraint, where a patch boundary is created whenever the entropy $H(x_t)$ exceeds a predefined global threshold $\theta_g$:

$$\text{Global Constraint: } H(x_t) > \theta_g$$

The second approach is the Approximate Monotonic Constraint. Instead of looking at an absolute value, this method identifies points where the entropy increases relative to the previous byte:

$$\text{Approx. Monotonic Constraint: } H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$$

The intuition here is that within a single patch, entropy should ideally be monotonically decreasing as the model gathers more information about the current unit. When the entropy jumps—meaning it breaks this approximate monotonic decrease—it signals the start of a new, high-uncertainty segment. In large-scale runs, the approximate monotonicity constraint is often preferred because it is less susceptible to "entropy drift," a phenomenon where changes in context length can cause the entropy model to produce inconsistent values.

# The Property of Incremental Patching and its Difference from BPE

A critical requirement for any patching scheme used during text generation is the property of incremental patching. Because the model generates text one byte at a time, it must decide whether the current byte marks a patch boundary without knowing what the future bytes will be. Formally, a patching scheme $f_p$ is incremental if:

$$f_p(\pmb{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\pmb{x})_{<i}$$

This means the segmentation of the prefix of a sequence must remain identical regardless of what follows it. This property is what distinguishes BLT patches from Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) tokens. In BPE, the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on the continuation sequence; therefore, BPE is not an incremental patching scheme.

Furthermore, BLT fundamentally changes the trade-off between vocabulary size and computation. In standard BPE-based models, increasing the vocabulary size to get larger tokens (and thus fewer steps) requires a larger embedding table, which increases the output dimension of the final projection layer. BLT breaks this link because it has no fixed vocabulary. It can increase the average patch size to save inference flops without needing to expand a vocabulary table. This allows BLT to simultaneously scale the model size and the patch size while maintaining a fixed inference budget, a dimensionality of scaling that is unavailable to traditional tokenization-based architectures.

# BLT Architecture: The Local Encoder

The Byte Latent Transformer ($\text{BLT}$) architecture departs from traditional large language models by removing the need for a fixed-vocabulary tokenizer. Instead, it processes raw bytes. To manage the computational overhead that typically accompanies byte-level modeling, $\text{BLT}$ utilizes a hierarchical structure. The first stage of this pipeline is the Local Encoder, a specialized module designed to transform a raw sequence of bytes into a set of compressed, expressive latent representations known as patches.

# Role of the Local Encoder Model

The Local Encoder, denoted as $\mathcal{E}$, serves as the bridge between the raw input bytes and the high-capacity global transformer. Its primary objective is to map a sequence of individual input bytes $b_i$ into patch representations $p_j$. These patches act as the fundamental units of computation for the rest of the model, allowing the global transformer to operate on a much shorter sequence than the original byte stream.

A defining characteristic of the Local Encoder is that it is designed to be extremely lightweight. In terms of depth, the number of layers in the Local Encoder, $l_{\mathcal{E}}$, is significantly smaller than the number of layers in the global latent transformer, $l_{\mathcal{G}}$. By keeping the local model small, $\text{BLT}$ ensures that the bulk of the model's parameters and floating-point operations ($\text{flops}$) are concentrated in the global model, where complex reasoning and long-range dependencies are handled.

Unlike a standard transformer, which typically outputs a representation for every single input token, the Local Encoder incorporates a specialized mechanism to pool information. This is achieved through the addition of cross-attention layers after each transformer layer, which effectively condense multiple byte-level representations into a single patch representation. This architecture allows $\text{BLT}$ to dynamically allocate compute, as the encoder can group varying numbers of bytes into a single patch based on the data's complexity.

# Hash n-gram Embeddings: Capturing Local Context with RollPolyHash

To ensure that the Local Encoder produces robust and expressive representations, $\text{BLT}$ does not rely solely on individual byte embeddings. Because bytes themselves carry very little information in isolation, the model incorporates local context by treating each byte as part of a byte n-gram. For every position $i$ in the sequence, the model constructs byte-grams $g_{i,n}$ consisting of the current byte and its preceding bytes, specifically $\{b_{i-n+1}, \dots, b_i\}$ for n-gram sizes $n$ ranging from 3 to 8. If the current position $i$ is smaller than $n$, that specific n-gram is omitted.

To handle these n-grams efficiently without requiring a massive, fixed vocabulary for every possible combination of bytes, $\text{BLT}$ employs hash n-gram embeddings. Each n-gram is processed through a hash function, specifically $\text{RollPolyHash}$, which maps the n-gram to an index within an embedding table $E_n^{hash}$ of a fixed size. The hash index is determined by calculating the $\text{RollPolyHash}$ of the n-gram and taking the modulo of the embedding table size: $\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \% |E_n^{hash}|$.

The final embedding $e_i$ for a byte at position $i$ is the sum of the individual byte embedding $x_i$ and the embeddings retrieved from the hash tables for all n-gram sizes from 3 to 8. This is expressed mathematically as:
$e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3, \dots, 8} E_n^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))$
To maintain stability, this augmented embedding is normalized by the number of n-gram sizes plus one. This mechanism allows the model to capture frequent sub-word patterns and orthographic information effectively, which is critical for matching the performance of traditional tokenizers.

# Local Block Causal Attention Masks

The transformer layers within the Local Encoder do not use global attention, as attending to every byte in a long sequence would be computationally prohibitive. Instead, they employ a local block causal attention mask. This means that each byte representation only attends to a fixed window of $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ preceding bytes.

This masking strategy is designed to capture immediate local dependencies while remaining efficient. A key detail of this implementation is that the attention window is allowed to cross the dynamic boundaries of the patches. This ensures that the encoder can maintain a continuous flow of information across the sequence, regardless of where a patch begins or ends. However, the mask strictly prohibits attention from crossing document boundaries, ensuring that the model does not leak information between unrelated texts.

# Multi-Headed Cross-Attention: Pooling Bytes into Patches

The most critical departure from standard transformer architectures in the Local Encoder is the use of multi-headed cross-attention to pool byte representations into patch representations. This module is inspired by the Perceiver architecture but is adapted for the variable-sized patches used in $\text{BLT}$. In this setup, the latent representations correspond to the dynamic patches, and each patch representation only attends to the specific bytes that compose that patch.

The process begins with the initialization of a query vector $Q_j$ for each patch $p_j$. This initialization is performed by pooling the byte representations that belong to that patch. These queries then pass through a linear projection $\mathcal{E}_C \in \mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathcal{E}} \times (h_{\mathcal{E}} \times U_{\mathcal{E}})}$, where $U_{\mathcal{E}}$ represents the number of encoder cross-attention heads. The patch representations are then updated iteratively across layers. If $P_{l-1}$ is the patch representation from the previous layer and $h_{l-1}$ are the byte representations, the update is:
$P_l = P_{l-1} + W_o\left(\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right)$
where $Q_j = W_q(P_{l-1,j})$, $K_i = W_k(h_{l-1,i})$, and $V_i = W_v(h_{l-1,i})$.

Because the global model's hidden dimension $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ is typically larger than the local model's dimension $h_{\mathcal{E}}$, the cross-attention module maintains $P_l$ as multiple heads of dimension $h_{\mathcal{E}}$. These are concatenated later to form the final $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ dimensions. The module utilizes pre-LayerNorm on the queries, keys, and values, employs no positional embeddings, and includes a residual connection around the cross-attention block to facilitate training.

# Architecture of the Encoder Transformer Layers

The overall structure of the Local Encoder consists of a series of alternating transformer layers and cross-attention layers. The transformer layers process the sequence of bytes using the local block causal attention described previously, refining the byte-level representations $h_i$ based on their immediate neighborhood. Following these, the cross-attention layers pool these refined byte representations into the latent patch representations $p_j$.

The efficiency of this design is highlighted in ablation studies. When the model is equipped with hash n-gram embeddings, the Local Encoder can be remarkably lightweight. Experiments indicate that the encoder can function effectively with as few as one transformer layer, while the local decoder typically requires a more substantial number of layers to map patches back into bytes. This asymmetric design emphasizes the encoder's role as a lightweight compressor that prepares raw data for the high-capacity global latent transformer.

BLT Architecture: The Global Latent Transformer

# The Latent Global Transformer Model

The Latent Global Transformer, denoted as $\mathcal{G}$, serves as the central computational engine of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) architecture. It is designed as an autoregressive transformer model consisting of $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers. The primary objective of this model is to map a sequence of latent input patch representations, $p_j$, into a corresponding sequence of output patch representations, $o_j$. In this notation, the subscript $j$ is used specifically to denote indices relating to patches, while the subscript $i$ is reserved for indices relating to individual bytes.

To ensure high performance, the global model follows the general architectural hyperparameters of Llama 3. This includes the use of the SwiGLU activation function within the feed-forward layers, RMSNorm for layer normalization, and rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) with $\theta=500000$ specifically within the self-attention layers. Because the global transformer is significantly larger and more computationally intensive than the local models, it consumes the vast majority of the floating point operations (flops) during both the pre-training phase and the inference phase. By controlling when and how often this model is invoked, the BLT architecture can precisely manage the amount of compute expended relative to the complexity of the data.

# Block-Causal Attention Masks

A critical component of the Latent Global Transformer is its use of a block-causal attention mask. In a standard causal transformer, each token attends to all preceding tokens in a sequence. However, the global model in BLT operates on patches rather than individual bytes or fixed tokens. The block-causal attention mask restricts the attention mechanism such that it can only attend to information up to and including the current patch within the current document.

This masking strategy is essential for maintaining the autoregressive property of the model while operating at the patch level. By ensuring that the model cannot "look ahead" into future patches, the architecture preserves the integrity of the next-byte prediction task. This structure allows the global model to process high-level latent representations of byte groups, treating each patch as a single unit of information while maintaining the necessary constraints for generative language modeling.

# Processing Latent Patch Representations

The global model does not interact with raw bytes directly. Instead, it processes latent patch representations. These patches are the primary units of computation in the BLT system. Unlike traditional Large Language Models (LLMs) that use a fixed vocabulary to map tokens to embeddings, BLT utilizes a dynamic grouping mechanism. The latent representations $p_j$ are produced by a lightweight local encoder and serve as the input to the global transformer $\mathcal{G}$.

The transition from $p_j$ to $o_j$ allows the model to perform complex reasoning and semantic processing in a latent space. Because the patches are dynamically sized based on the entropy of the data, the global model is essentially operating on a compressed version of the input stream. The output $o_j$ is then passed to a local decoder, which is responsible for expanding these latent representations back into a sequence of raw bytes. This separation of concerns—where the global model handles the "latent" semantic heavy lifting and the local models handle the "surface" byte-level mapping—is what enables BLT to match the performance of tokenization-based models.

# Dynamic Compute Allocation: Why Patches Reduce FLOPs

The core intuition behind the BLT architecture is that not all parts of a data sequence require the same amount of computational effort. In traditional tokenization-based LLMs, the model allocates the same amount of compute to every token, regardless of whether that token is a common piece of whitespace or a complex technical term. BLT replaces this static allocation with dynamic compute allocation based on the entropy of the next-byte prediction.

The model recognizes that predicting the ending of most words is relatively easy and involves low entropy, whereas choosing the first word of a new sentence is a high-entropy, more difficult decision. By segmenting bytes into patches based on this entropy, BLT creates contextualized groupings with uniform information density. When the data is predictable (low entropy), the system creates longer patches. Because the Latent Global Transformer is invoked once per patch, longer patches result in fewer total steps for the global model.

This mechanism allows for a unique scaling advantage. For a fixed inference flop budget, one can simultaneously increase both the patch size and the model size. Specifically, by increasing the average patch size, the global model is run less frequently, which saves compute. This saved compute can then be reallocated to grow the size of the global latent transformer (increasing $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ or the hidden dimension). This means that BLT can achieve better scaling trends than tokenization-based models because it can employ a larger, more powerful global model without increasing the total computational cost per byte.

# Interactions Between the Local and Global Models

The BLT architecture is structured as a tripartite system: a local encoder, a global latent transformer, and a local decoder. The local encoder $\mathcal{E}$ is a lightweight transformer-based model with $l_{\mathcal{E}}$ layers, where $l_{\mathcal{E}}$ is significantly smaller than $l_{\mathcal{G}}$. Its role is to map a sequence of input bytes $b_i$ into the expressive patch representations $p_j$ that the global model requires.

The global model $\mathcal{G}$ then processes these patches to produce $o_j$. Finally, the local decoder $\mathcal{D}$—also a lightweight model with $l_{\mathcal{D}}$ layers—takes these output patch representations and decodes them back into raw bytes $y_i$. The local decoder functions by predicting a sequence of raw bytes based on the hidden representations produced by the local encoder.

The interaction is characterized by a significant disparity in scale and resolution. The local models operate at the byte level, dealing with the high-resolution details of the text, including orthographic and character-level information. The global model operates at the patch level, focusing on the high-level semantic and structural components of the language. By utilizing a lightweight local infrastructure to "bridge" the gap between raw bytes and the massive global transformer, BLT achieves the efficiency of a token-based model while retaining the robustness and flexibility of a byte-level model.

# BLT Architecture: The Local Decoder

The Byte Latent Transformer ({\textsc BLT}}) is designed to move away from the rigid constraints of fixed-vocabulary tokenization by operating directly on raw bytes. To achieve this while maintaining computational efficiency, the architecture employs a hierarchical structure. While the Latent Global Transformer handles high-level reasoning across patches, the process must eventually return to the byte level to produce readable text. This is the primary responsibility of the Local Decoder.

# Role of the Local Decoder Model

The Local Decoder, denoted as $\mathcal{D}$, is a lightweight transformer-based model designed to map the high-level output of the global model back into the granular sequence of raw bytes. In the broader {\textsc BLT} pipeline, the global model produces a sequence of output patch representations, $o_j$. These representations are abstract and latent; they contain the necessary semantic and syntactic information to predict the next segment of text, but they are not bytes themselves. The Local Decoder acts as the final translation layer, decoding these global patch representations $o_j$ into a sequence of raw bytes, $y_i$.

Because the Local Decoder is tasked with generating bytes, it must operate autoregressively. It predicts each raw byte as a function of the bytes that have already been decoded. To ensure that the decoding process is grounded in the original input context, the Local Decoder does not work in isolation. It takes as input the hidden representations produced by the Local Encoder for the byte-sequence. By combining these local byte-level features with the global patch-level insights, the decoder can accurately reconstruct the intended byte sequence.

# Symmetry and Differences Between Encoder and Decoder

The Local Decoder is designed as a structural counterpart to the Local Encoder. Both are "local" models, meaning they are significantly smaller than the Latent Global Transformer, with far fewer layers ($l_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$). Both models utilize a sequence of alternating layers consisting of cross-attention and transformer blocks. This symmetry allows the architecture to effectively compress bytes into patches at the start and expand patches back into bytes at the end.

Despite these similarities, there are critical differences in how these two modules function. First, the order of operations is shifted: in the Local Decoder, the cross-attention layer is applied before the transformer layer. This ensures that the model first incorporates the global patch information before the local transformer layer refines the byte-level representations. Second, the roles of the queries, keys, and values in the cross-attention mechanism are interchanged. While the encoder uses a patch-based query to pool byte information, the decoder uses byte-based queries to extract information from the patches.

# Decoder Multi-Headed Cross-Attention: Patch-to-Byte Mapping

The core of the Local Decoder's ability to map patches back to bytes lies in its multi-headed cross-attention mechanism. In this stage, the goal is to determine which parts of the global patch representation $o_j$ are relevant for predicting the current byte $y_i$. This is achieved by treating the byte-representations as queries and the patch representations as the keys and values.

The process begins with the initialization of byte-representations, where $D_0$ is set to the output of the last encoder layer, $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$. For each subsequent layer $l$, the model computes a new set of byte-representations $B_l$ through a cross-attention operation. The queries $Q_i$ are derived from the byte-representations of the previous layer, $d_{l-1,i}$, via a projection matrix $W_q$. Simultaneously, the patch representations $o_j$ are processed by a linear transformation and split operation, denoted as $\mathcal{D}_C$, and then projected into keys $K_i$ and values $V_i$ using $W_k$ and $W_v$.

The resulting byte-representations are calculated as:
\begin{align}
B_l &= D_{l-1} + W_o\left(\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right)
\end{align}
This mechanism allows the model to "attend" to the global latent state. Because the queries are at the byte level and the keys/values are at the patch level, the model can precisely map the high-level intent of the Global Transformer to the specific byte being generated. This module employs multi-headed attention, pre-LayerNorm, and a residual connection around the cross-attention block, and it does not use positional embeddings.

# The Decoder Transformer Layer and Byte Prediction

Once the cross-attention layer has integrated the global patch information into the byte stream, the resulting representations $B_l$ are passed into a decoder transformer layer. The purpose of this layer is to perform local processing on the byte sequence, ensuring that the predicted bytes are coherent and follow the local patterns of the language.

The output of the decoder transformer layer, $D_l$, represents the final refined state of the byte sequence for that layer. After passing through $l_{\mathcal{D}}$ such alternating layers of cross-attention and transformer processing, the model is capable of predicting the actual raw byte $y_i$. By operating on the refined representations, the Local Decoder ensures that the final output is not just a product of the global model's high-level logic, but is also linguistically sound at the character and byte level.

# End-to-End Information Flow: Byte $\rightarrow$ Patch $\rightarrow$ Patch $\rightarrow$ Byte

The Local Decoder represents the final segment of a sophisticated information pipeline that allows {\textsc BLT} to operate without a fixed vocabulary. To understand the decoder's role, one must look at the entire flow of data. The process begins with a sequence of raw bytes. These bytes are first processed by the Local Encoder, which uses its own cross-attention mechanism to pool these bytes into latent patch representations, $p_j$. This step effectively compresses the data, reducing the sequence length and allowing the model to allocate compute more efficiently.

These patch representations $p_j$ are then fed into the Latent Global Transformer. This is the most computationally expensive part of the model, where the transformer performs complex autoregressive reasoning on the patches. The output of this stage is a new sequence of output patch representations, $o_j$, which contain the "answers" or the predicted next steps of the sequence in a latent form.

Finally, the Local Decoder takes these output patches $o_j$ and reverses the initial compression. By using the byte-level queries in its cross-attention layers, the decoder expands the latent patches back into a sequence of raw bytes $y_i$. This end-to-end flow—from bytes to patches, from patches to patches, and finally from patches back to bytes—enables {\textsc BLT} to maintain the efficiency of patch-based computation while retaining the robustness and flexibility of raw byte modeling.

Scaling Laws and Computational Efficiency

# Estimating FLOPs for Byte-Level Models

To understand the computational cost of the Byte Latent Transformer ($\text{BLT}$), one must first understand how Floating Point Operations (FLOPs) are calculated in a transformer-based architecture. The $\text{BLT}$ model follows the general principles established by the Chinchilla scaling laws, accounting for the operations required by feed-forward layers, the $QKVO$ projections within self-attention layers, the computation of the attention matrix itself, and the final output projection. A key simplification in the $\text{BLT}$ framework is the treatment of the input embedding layer as an efficient lookup table rather than a dense matrix multiplication, which effectively reduces its computational cost to zero FLOPs. Additionally, it is standard practice in these estimations to assume that the backwards pass during training requires twice as many FLOPs as the forward pass.

The total computational cost per byte in a $\text{BLT}$ model is an aggregate of several distinct components: the local encoder transformer, the global latent transformer, the local decoder transformer, and the cross-attention blocks found in both the encoder and decoder. Because $\text{BLT}$ operates on two different levels of granularity—individual bytes and grouped patches—the FLOPs must be calculated relative to the sequence length and the patch size. Specifically, the cost of the global latent transformer is divided by the patch size $n_p$ because it operates on patches rather than bytes, meaning it is invoked fewer times than the local models.

The formal estimation of $\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}}$ involves summing the transformer FLOPs of the three main models and the cross-attention FLOPs. For the local encoder, the cost is tied to the encoder window $w_{\mathcal{E}}$, while for the local decoder, it is tied to the decoder window $w_{\mathcal{D}}$ and the byte vocabulary size $V=256$. The cross-attention calculations also depend on the ratio of queries to keys/values and the ratio $k$, which represents the number of local model splits that are concatenated to form a global model representation. By summing these disparate components, researchers can determine the exact compute budget required to process a specific number of bytes.

# Compute-Optimal Scaling Trends

Compute-optimal scaling refers to a training regime where the ratio of model parameters to training data is optimized to achieve the best possible performance within a given budget of total training FLOPs. In traditional Large Language Models (LLMs), this is usually determined by a specific balance between the size of the model and the amount of tokens it sees. The $\text{BLT}$ research seeks to determine if these same "compute-optimal" principles apply to models that operate directly on raw bytes without a fixed vocabulary.

When comparing $\text{BLT}$ to Byte Pair Encoding ($\text{BPE}$) models—such as Llama 3—the results indicate that $\text{BLT}$ either matches or outperforms $\text{BPE}$ counterparts across various sizes, ranging from 1B to 8B parameters. This finding is significant because it validates the assumption that the optimal parameter-to-compute ratio identified for token-based models is largely applicable to byte-level architectures. It suggests that moving away from tokenization does not fundamentally break the scaling laws that govern how model performance improves with more data and larger parameters.

Crucially, achieving this parity requires more than just using bytes; it requires the specific architectural innovations of $\text{BLT}$, particularly dynamic patching and cross-attention. When compared to simpler byte-level approaches, such as "SpaceByte" or strided patching (where bytes are grouped every $k$ bytes), $\text{BLT}$ shows a marked advantage. Static patching schemes fail to allocate compute where it is most needed, often wasting transformer steps on predictable sequences or under-allocating compute for complex data. Dynamic patching allows $\text{BLT}$ to maintain the high performance of $\text{BPE}$ models while operating on the more robust raw byte stream.

# Training FLOP-Controlled Parity with Llama 3

One of the primary achievements of the $\text{BLT}$ architecture is demonstrating training FLOP-controlled parity with Llama 3 up to an 8B parameter scale. In a FLOP-controlled experiment, both the $\text{BPE}$-based model and the $\text{BLT}$ model are trained using the same total number of floating-point operations. This ensures that any difference in performance is a result of the architecture and the patching strategy rather than one model simply having more "compute" or more training time than the other.

In evaluations using the $\text{BLT}$-1T dataset, the $\text{BLT}$-Entropy model—which uses next-byte entropy to determine patch boundaries—outperformed the Llama 3 model on four out of seven standard tasks, including common sense reasoning and world knowledge. This is particularly impressive given that both models were trained with the same budget. The performance gain is attributed to two factors: the more efficient allocation of training compute via dynamic patching and the model's ability to directly capture byte-level information, which $\text{BPE}$ models lose during the tokenization process.

While a model using "space-patching" (grouping bytes by whitespace) was also tested, it generally underperformed compared to the Llama 3 tokenizer. However, the $\text{BLT}$-Entropy variant proves that when compute is allocated dynamically based on the complexity of the data, a tokenizer-free model can not only match but exceed the performance of state-of-the-art tokenized models. This establishes that the "bottleneck" in previous byte-level models was not the use of bytes themselves, but the lack of a dynamic mechanism to group those bytes into computationally efficient units.

# Inference Efficiency: Trading Patch Size for Model Size

A fundamental limitation of tokenization-based models is that they allocate the same amount of compute to every token, regardless of whether that token is a common word or a complex technical term. In contrast, $\text{BLT}$ introduces a mechanism to trade patch size for model size to optimize inference efficiency. Since the global latent transformer is the most computationally expensive part of the model, the number of times it is invoked during inference determines the total inference cost.

By increasing the average patch size, $\text{BLT}$ reduces the number of steps the global transformer must execute. For example, moving from a patch size of 4.5 bytes to 8 bytes can lead to nearly 50% savings in inference FLOPs. This is because larger patches mean the model spends more time in the lightweight local encoder and decoder and less time in the heavy global transformer. This creates a unique opportunity: the FLOPs saved by using larger patches can be reallocated to increase the actual size (number of parameters) of the global latent transformer.

This trade-off allows $\text{BLT}$ to maintain a fixed inference budget while simultaneously growing the model's capacity. In practical terms, a user can choose a version of the model that is physically larger (more parameters) but executes fewer steps (larger patches), maintaining the same speed (inference cost) while potentially achieving higher accuracy. This flexibility is impossible in $\text{BPE}$ models, where the vocabulary size and token length are fixed prior to training and cannot be dynamically adjusted during inference to save compute.

# Fixed Inference Budget Scaling: Why Patches Scale Better Than Tokens

The concept of "fixed inference budget scaling" explores how model performance evolves when the cost per inference step is held constant. In this regime, $\text{BLT}$ exhibits significantly better scaling trends than $\text{BPE}$ architectures. While $\text{BPE}$ models may perform better at very small training budgets, $\text{BLT}$ quickly surpasses them as the training budget increases beyond the compute-optimal point.

The reason patches scale better than tokens lies in the ability to scale the global transformer and the patch size in tandem. As the total parameter count of the model grows—for instance, from 400M to 8B parameters—the share of total FLOPs consumed by the local encoder and decoder becomes smaller relative to the global transformer. Because the local models scale more slowly than the global transformer, the efficiency gains from larger patch sizes become even more pronounced at larger scales.

Experimental data shows that $\text{BLT}$ models with a patch size of 8 bytes have a steeper scaling trend in larger flop classes, overtaking $\text{BPE}$ models sooner. This suggests that as we push toward even larger model scales, the advantage of using dynamic patches over fixed tokens increases. Essentially, $\text{BLT}$ unlocks a new dimension of scaling: instead of just increasing parameters or data, researchers can optimize the "granularity" of the computation (the patch size) to maximize the performance gained for every single FLOP spent during inference.

# Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) as a Tokenizer-Independent Metric

In standard language modeling, "perplexity" is the primary metric used to measure a model's uncertainty. However, perplexity is intrinsically tied to the tokenizer; it measures uncertainty per token. Because different tokenizers produce different numbers of tokens for the same string, perplexity cannot be used to fairly compare a $\text{BPE}$-based model with a byte-level model. To solve this, $\text{BLT}$ utilizes Bits-Per-Byte ($\text{BPB}$), a tokenizer-independent version of perplexity.

The $\text{BPB}$ is calculated by taking the sum of the cross-entropy loss $\mathcal{L}_{CE}$ over a sequence $\pmb{x}$ and normalizing it by the total number of bytes $n_{\text{bytes}}$ and a constant $\ln(2)$. The formula is expressed as:

$$\text{BPB}(x)= \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}$$

By normalizing the loss by the number of bytes rather than the number of tokens, $\text{BPB}$ provides a "universal currency" for measuring language modeling performance. This allows researchers to compare the efficiency of the $\text{BLT}$ architecture directly against $\text{BPE}$ models, as well as different patching strategies (such as space-patching vs. entropy-patching), without the results being biased by how the input text was segmented. This metric is essential for validating that $\text{BLT}$ achieves parity with Llama 3, as it removes the inherent advantage that $\text{BPE}$ models have by processing "chunks" of text as single units.

Robustness and Character-Level Capabilities

# Evaluating Robustness to Input Noise

A fundamental limitation of tokenization-based large language models is their reliance on a fixed vocabulary, which creates a fragile dependency between the input string and the resulting tokens. When a model is trained on a specific set of tokens, any deviation in the input—such as a typo or unconventional formatting—can lead to the sequence being tokenized in a way the model has never encountered, causing performance to degrade. The Byte Latent Transformer ($\text{BLT}$) addresses this by operating directly on raw bytes, allowing it to maintain a consistent understanding of the input regardless of superficial noise.

To quantify this advantage, $\text{BLT}$ was evaluated against tokenizer-based models using five distinct character-level noising strategies designed to disrupt standard tokenization. The first strategy, known as AntSpeak, converts the entire text into uppercase letters separated by spaces. The second, Drop, randomly removes $10\%$ of the characters. The third, RandomCase, randomly assigns uppercase or lowercase to $50\%$ of the characters. The fourth, Repeat, duplicates $20\%$ of the characters up to four times. Finally, UpperCase transforms all characters to uppercase. These perturbations are applied to the prompt, the completion, or both.

The results demonstrate that $\text{BLT}$ is significantly more robust than its token-based counterparts. On the noised HellaSwag benchmark, $\text{BLT}$ outperformed the model trained on the same data by an average of $8$ points. Remarkably, $\text{BLT}$ even surpassed Llama 3.1 in certain instances, despite Llama 3.1 being trained on a much larger dataset. This indicates that the ability to process raw bytes provides a structural robustness that cannot be easily overcome by simply increasing the amount of training data in a token-based architecture.

# Phonology and Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) Tasks

Linguistic precision often requires an understanding of the relationship between the written form of a word and its spoken form. This is the core of Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) tasks, where a model must map a sequence of graphemes—the individual characters representing a word—into a transcription of that word's pronunciation, known as phonemes. For token-based models, this is a challenging task because the tokenizer groups characters into higher-level units, effectively hiding the granular orthographic information necessary to predict pronunciation.

$\text{BLT}$ is naturally suited for this task because it does not discard the character-level information through a tokenizer. By maintaining access to the raw byte sequence, the model can learn the subtle patterns and rules that govern how individual characters map to sounds. In a 5-shot evaluation using the Phonology Bench, $\text{BLT}$ outperformed the baseline Llama 3 1T tokenizer-based model. This result highlights that $\text{BLT}$ possesses a superior internal representation of the constituents of words, allowing it to handle phonological mappings that are often invisible or distorted in the latent space of a BPE-based model.

# The CUTE Benchmark: Testing Character-Level Manipulation

The CUTE benchmark is specifically designed to test a model's character-level understanding across three primary categories: understanding composition, understanding orthographic similarity, and the ability to manipulate sequences. Most tokenizer-based models struggle with these tasks because, while they may have an implicit knowledge of how a token is spelled, they cannot effectively utilize that information to perform precise manipulations of the text.

$\text{BLT}$ demonstrates a profound advantage in this area, with the $\text{BLT}$-Entropy variant outperforming BPE Llama 3 models by more than $25$ points. The model showed exceptional proficiency in spelling tasks, achieving an accuracy of $99.9\%$. The stark contrast in performance is particularly telling given that $\text{BLT}$ was trained on $16\times$ less data than Llama 3.1. This suggests that character-level manipulation is not merely a matter of data scale, but a fundamental architectural limitation of BPE models.

However, the evaluation also reveals a nuanced trade-off. In tasks involving word deletion and insertion, BPE models performed better than $\text{BLT}$. This suggests that while $\text{BLT}$ is far superior at manipulating the internal structure of a word (character-to-character), the BPE models' grouping of bytes into word-like tokens gives them a slight advantage in manipulating words as discrete units. Despite this, the gap is relatively small, and the evidence suggests that it is generally easier for a model to build an understanding of words from characters than to derive character-level precision from pre-defined tokens.

# Generalization to Low-Resource Machine Translation

The limitations of fixed vocabularies are most apparent in multilingual settings, particularly with low-resource languages. In traditional tokenization, languages that are under-represented in the training set often receive poor tokenization, where words are broken into overly small or meaningless fragments. This "multilingual inequity" hinders the model's ability to generalize to the long-tail of human languages.

$\text{BLT}$ avoids this issue by using a tokenizer-free approach. Since it learns from raw bytes, it can represent any language or script without needing a pre-defined vocabulary for that specific language. When evaluated on the FLORES-101 benchmark, which covers six language families and twenty-one low-resource languages, $\text{BLT}$ demonstrated a clear advantage. It achieved a $2$-point overall advantage when translating into English and a $0.5$-point advantage when translating from English compared to a model using the Llama 3 tokenizer.

While performance was comparable in popular, high-resource language pairs, $\text{BLT}$ significantly outperformed Llama 3 in numerous language pairs within low-resource families. This confirms that raw byte modeling is an effective strategy for generalizing to data that falls into the "long tail" of the distribution, as it provides a universal interface that does not bias the model toward the dominant languages present during the tokenizer's construction.

# The Long-Tail Advantage of Raw Byte Modeling

The cumulative results across noise robustness, phonology, character manipulation, and low-resource translation point to a broader conceptual advantage: the ability of $\text{BLT}$ to model the "long tail" of data. In the context of language modeling, the long tail consists of rare words, unconventional spellings, noisy inputs, and low-resource languages—all of which are typically mishandled by fixed-vocabulary tokenizers.

Tokenization-based models are designed for the "average" case, optimizing for the most frequent patterns in the training data. When they encounter an input that deviates from these patterns, the tokenizer often produces a sequence of tokens that does not accurately reflect the semantic or structural reality of the input. $\text{BLT}$, by contrast, treats all byte sequences with the same fundamental granularity. Whether a sequence is a common English word or a rare character combination from a low-resource language, $\text{BLT}$ processes it as a series of bytes.

This architecture allows $\text{BLT}$ to uncover sub-word aspects of data that token-based models simply miss. By dynamically grouping bytes into patches based on entropy rather than fixed rules, $\text{BLT}$ allocates compute where the data is most complex while maintaining a direct link to the raw input. This flexibility ensures that the model remains robust and precise, even when facing the most challenging and infrequent portions of the data distribution.

# Advanced Implementations and Ablations

Having established the core architecture of the Byte Latent Transformer ({\textsc BLT}}), it is essential to understand how the model can be optimized and how specific design choices influence its performance. This chapter explores the practicalities of implementing {\textsc BLT}}, including a method to leverage existing pre-trained models, an analysis of the components that drive its efficiency, and a discussion on the directions for future development.

# Distillation: Initializing BLT from Pre-trained Llama 3.1 Models

One of the most powerful ways to accelerate the training of a tokenizer-free model is to leverage the knowledge already captured by massive, tokenization-based models. The researchers explored a workflow known as "byte-ifying" pre-trained models, specifically using Llama 3.1 as a starting point. In this approach, the global transformer $\mathcal{G}$ of the {\textsc BLT}} architecture is not initialized randomly; instead, its parameters are initialized with the weights of a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model. This effectively transforms a tokenizer-based model into a tokenizer-free one.

The training process for this distilled version differs from training from scratch. Because the global transformer already possesses significant linguistic knowledge, the weights of $\mathcal{G}$ are updated using a learning rate that is only one-tenth of the learning rate used for the local encoder $\mathcal{E}$ and local decoder $\mathcal{D}$. The local models must be trained to map raw bytes into the latent space that the pre-trained global transformer understands and to decode those representations back into bytes.

This method of initialization leads to significantly faster and better convergence. Experiments demonstrate that the "BLT from Llama 3.1" model outperforms both the baseline {\textsc BLT}} and the original Llama 3 models when trained with the same number of flops. For instance, it achieves superior performance on the MMLU task compared to an {\textsc BLT}}-Entropy model trained on a much larger dataset of 1 trillion tokens. While there is some performance decline in specific areas like HumanEval and MMLU compared to the original Llama 3.1 model trained on 15 trillion tokens, the results suggest that this distillation process is a highly effective way to reduce the total training compute required to reach high performance.

# Ablation Study: The Impact of Entropy Model Size and Context Window

The efficacy of {\textsc BLT}} depends heavily on the patching function, which determines how bytes are grouped. In the entropy-based patching scheme, a small byte-level language model is used to estimate the entropy of the next-byte prediction. To understand the requirements of this entropy model, researchers conducted an ablation study varying its size from 1 million to 100 million parameters and its context window length from 64 to 512 bytes.

The results indicate a positive correlation between the size and context window of the entropy model and the overall language modeling performance of the final {\textsc BLT}} model. Intuitively, a more capable entropy model can more accurately identify high-uncertainty bytes, leading to more precise patch boundaries and a more efficient allocation of compute in the global transformer. However, this scaling is not linear; the study found diminishing returns once the entropy model exceeds 50 million parameters. This suggests that the entropy model does not need to be as powerful as the global model to be effective; it only needs enough capacity to identify basic structural patterns in the data to facilitate efficient patching.

# The Effectiveness of Hash Embeddings vs. Cross-Attention

The {\textsc BLT}} architecture employs two key mechanisms to ensure that the global transformer receives expressive representations: hash $n$-gram embeddings and multi-headed cross-attention. Through ablation studies, the researchers determined that these two components are largely complementary and provide improvements across different types of data.

Hash $n$-gram embeddings provide the model with direct orthographic knowledge by mapping byte sequences of lengths $n \in \{3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8\}$ to an embedding table. These embeddings are vital for {\textsc BLT}} to match the performance of tokenizer-based models. The study found that these embeddings are particularly beneficial for structured data, such as that found on Wikipedia and Github. In terms of scale, increasing the hash embedding vocabulary size up to 300,000 entries provides significant gains, but further increases lead to diminishing returns.

Cross-attention mechanisms, on the other hand, serve to pool byte representations into patch representations. The ablation results show that cross-attention is most effective within the local decoder. In the local encoder, cross-attention provides a slight improvement, but only when the queries are initialized by pooling the encoder hidden representations of the patch bytes. Furthermore, cross-attention was found to be especially helpful for data from Common-Crawl and for models using larger patch sizes. Because hash embeddings and cross-attention improve performance on different datasets, both are necessary to achieve a robust, general-purpose model.

# Comparing Local Model Hyperparameters

A critical design goal for {\textsc BLT}} is to keep the local encoder and decoder lightweight so that the bulk of the computational budget can be dedicated to the global latent transformer. The researchers ablated the number of layers in these local models to find the optimal balance of complexity.

The findings reveal an interesting asymmetry between the encoder and decoder. When paired with hash $n$-gram embeddings, the local encoder can be extremely lightweight—consisting of only a single layer—without sacrificing performance. This is likely because the hash embeddings already provide the model with significant low-level structural information about the byte sequence. In contrast, the local decoder requires more capacity to effectively map the high-dimensional global representations back into the specific raw bytes of the output sequence. Consequently, a heavier decoder is preferred to ensure that the final byte-level predictions are accurate.

# Future Directions: End-to-End Patching and Wall-Clock Optimization

While {\textsc BLT}} represents a significant step forward in byte-level modeling, several areas remain for future exploration. One primary direction is the transition toward end-to-end patching. Currently, {\textsc BLT}} relies on a separately trained entropy model to determine patch boundaries. A more integrated approach would involve learning the patching mechanism as part of the end-to-end training process, potentially through a learned boundary-predictor.

Another challenge is the optimization of "wall-clock" time. Most current deep learning libraries and hardware optimizations are tailored for the fixed-vocabulary structures of BPE-based transformers. Although {\textsc BLT}} is theoretically efficient in terms of floating-point operations (flops) and utilizes tools like FlexAttention to handle dynamic masks, it may not yet achieve the same real-world speed as highly optimized BPE models. Future work will likely focus on creating specialized kernels and libraries that can handle the dynamic, variable-length nature of patches as efficiently as static tokens. Finally, continuing to research the "byte-ification" of pre-trained models could unlock a way to transform existing large-scale models into tokenizer-free versions without the massive cost of training from scratch.
