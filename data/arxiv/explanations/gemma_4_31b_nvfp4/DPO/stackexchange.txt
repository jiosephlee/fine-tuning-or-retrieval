\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What is the role of the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ in the DPO objective?
Question:
I'm reading the DPO paper and I see that the objective function $\mathcal{L}_{DPO}$ relies heavily on a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. The paper says it is typically initialized as the SFT model. I'm confused as to why this is necessary. If the goal is simply to make the model $\pi_{\theta}$ prefer $y_w$ over $y_l$, why can't we just optimize the relative probabilities of the current model $\pi_{\theta}}$ without referring back to a base model?
Answer:
The reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ in Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) serves as a critical anchor that prevents the model from collapsing or deviating into unstable regions of the probability space during training. To understand why we cannot simply optimize the relative probabilities of the current model $\pi_{\theta}$, it is helpful to look at both the theoretical objective DPO is solving and the practical failure modes of "naive" preference learning.

Theoretically, DPO is not just a heuristic for preferring $y_w$ over $y_l$; it is a closed-form solution to a specific reinforcement learning problem: maximizing a reward function $r(x, y)$ while constrained by a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty against a base model. The original RLHF objective is $\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}[r(x, y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta} || \pi_{\text{ref}}]$. This $\beta$ parameter controls the trade-off between maximizing reward and staying close to the reference policy. In the DPO derivation, the authors show that the optimal policy $\pi^*$ for this objective can be reparameterized such that the reward function is implicitly defined as $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. By using the Bradley-Terry model for preferences, the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out, allowing us to express the preference loss entirely in terms of the ratio between the current policy and the reference policy.

Intuitively, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ provides a "baseline" for what a reasonable response looks like. If you were to optimize only $\pi_{\theta}(y_w \mid x) / \pi_{\theta}(y_l \mid x)$, the model could satisfy the objective by simply increasing the probability of $y_w$ to an extreme degree or driving the probability of $y_l$ to zero, regardless of whether the resulting distribution remains a coherent language model. The paper notes that a "naïve version" of this method without the reference-based weighting can cause the language model to degenerate. By measuring the change relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, DPO ensures that the model only moves away from the reference distribution in directions that specifically improve the preference alignment.

Furthermore, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ ensures that the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ remains grounded. This implicit reward tells the model: "how much more likely is this response under my current parameters than it was in the SFT model?" This relative measurement prevents the model from drifting too far from the distribution where the preference data was originally sampled, maintaining generation diversity and preventing "mode collapse" where the model generates a single, high-reward string repeatedly. In practice, initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ as the SFT model ensures that the training starts from a point of stability, leveraging the general linguistic capabilities learned during supervised fine-tuning while refining the behavior to match human preferences.

### Is DPO fundamentally different from supervised fine-tuning on preferred completions?
Question:
In the Experiments section, the authors compare DPO to 'Preferred-FT', which is just supervised learning on the chosen completion $y_w$. Looking at the DPO loss, it seems to be increasing the probability of $y_w$ and decreasing $y_l$. Is DPO just a more complex version of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) combined with unlikelihood training, or is there a theoretical reason why this specific formulation is superior to just training on the 'correct' answers?
Answer:
While DPO may superficially look like a combination of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and unlikelihood training, it is fundamentally different in both its theoretical objective and its mechanical update rule. SFT on preferred completions (referred to in the paper as Preferred-FT) only seeks to maximize the likelihood of the "correct" answer $y_w$, effectively ignoring the contrast provided by the dispreferred answer $y_l$. In contrast, DPO is derived as a closed-form solution to the standard RLHF objective: maximizing a reward function while maintaining a KL-divergence constraint relative to a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$.

The theoretical superiority of DPO stems from this connection to reinforcement learning. The authors show that there is an analytical mapping between a reward function $r(x, y)$ and its optimal policy $\pi_r(y|x)$. By substituting this mapping into the Bradley-Terry preference model, they transform a loss function over reward parameters into a loss function over policy parameters. This means that when you optimize the DPO loss, you are not simply "pushing" probabilities up and down; you are implicitly optimizing a reward model and its corresponding optimal policy simultaneously.

Mechanistically, the difference between DPO and a naive unlikelihood objective is most evident in the gradient. The gradient of the DPO loss is:
$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w)) \left[\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)\right]\right]$$
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ is the implicit reward. 

Unlike unlikelihood training, which typically applies a constant weight to the dispreferred sample, DPO incorporates a dynamic, per-example importance weight $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))$. This weight is high when the model is "wrong" (i.e., the implicit reward for the dispreferred completion is higher than for the preferred one) and low when the model already correctly distinguishes the two. This adaptive weighting prevents the model degeneration often seen in naive probability ratio objectives and ensures the policy stays grounded relative to $\pi_\text{ref}$, allowing DPO to achieve a more efficient reward-KL frontier than even PPO-based RLHF.

### How does the partition function $Z(x)$ actually cancel out in the DPO derivation?
Question:
The paper explains that the optimal policy is $\pi_r(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y))$. They then rearrange this to express the reward as $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. When substituting this into the Bradley-Terry model $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$, the authors state that the partition function cancels. I am struggling to follow the algebra—could someone show exactly how $Z(x)$ disappears during this step?
Answer:
The cancellation of the partition function $Z(x)$ is the core "trick" of the DPO derivation, and it stems from the fact that the Bradley-Terry model depends only on the relative difference between rewards rather than their absolute values.

To see this algebraically, we start with the expression for the reward $r(x, y)$ derived from the optimal policy. As stated in the paper, the optimal policy is $\pi_r(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y))$. By rearranging this to solve for the reward, we get $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. Here, $Z(x)$ is the partition function, which acts as a normalization constant to ensure that $\pi_r$ is a valid probability distribution that sums to $1$ over all possible completions $y$ for a given prompt $x$.

The Bradley-Terry model defines the probability that one completion $y_1$ is preferred over another $y_2$ as $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$. When we substitute the expression for $r(x, y)$ into this difference, we obtain:

$$r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2) = \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) \right) - \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) \right)$$

Because $Z(x)$ depends only on the prompt $x$ and not on the specific completion $y$, the term $\beta \log Z(x)$ is identical for both $y_1$ and $y_2$. When we subtract the two rewards, these terms exactly cancel each other out:

$$r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}$$

Intuitively, this means that the specific value of the normalization constant is irrelevant for ranking preferences. Since humans (under the Bradley-Terry assumption) only judge which of two responses is *better*, any constant offset added to all possible rewards for a specific prompt does not change the resulting preference probability. This is why DPO is so powerful: it allows us to bypass the computationally expensive task of estimating $Z(x)$—which would normally require summing over the entire vocabulary space of the language model—and instead optimize the policy $\pi_\theta$ directly using a simple classification loss.

### Does the DPO reparameterization limit the expressiveness of the learned reward?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that all reward classes consistent with the Bradley-Terry model can be represented by the reparameterization $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$. I'm confused by the mention of 'equivalence classes' where two rewards are equivalent if they differ by a function $f(x)$. Does this mean that DPO cannot learn a unique reward function, but only a class of functions? And if so, does this limitation affect the final quality of the policy $\pi_{\theta}$?
Answer:
The short answer is no; the DPO reparameterization does not limit the expressiveness of the learned reward in any way that affects the resulting policy. The mention of "equivalence classes" refers to a fundamental property of the Bradley-Terry (BT) model rather than a limitation of the DPO algorithm itself.

To understand this intuitively, consider that the BT model determines the probability of preferring one response over another based on the difference between their rewards: $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$. Because only the difference matters, adding any constant or any function $f(x)$ that depends only on the prompt $x$ to the reward of every possible completion $y$ will not change the outcome. For example, if you increase the reward of all possible answers for a specific prompt by $10$ points, the relative difference between any two answers remains exactly the same. Therefore, any two reward functions that differ only by a function of the prompt, $f(x)$, are "equivalent" because they induce the exact same preference distribution. This is the "under-specification" mentioned in Lemma 1.

The "limitation" you are worried about—that DPO cannot learn a "unique" reward function—is actually a feature of the preference modeling framework. Since any reward function within an equivalence class produces the same preference data and, as stated in Lemma 2, the same optimal policy $\pi^*$ under the KL-constrained objective, there is no need to identify a unique reward function. Any representative of that class is sufficient to derive the optimal behavior.

Theorem 1 proves that the DPO reparameterization $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ is expressive enough to represent at least one member of every possible reward equivalence class. The proof demonstrates that for any arbitrary reward function $r(x, y)$, we can apply a projection $f$ (effectively subtracting the log of the partition function $Z(x)$) to transform it into the DPO form. This projection creates a new reward function that is mathematically equivalent to the original one (it differs only by a function of $x$) but fits the specific structure required by DPO.

Consequently, this does not affect the final quality of the policy $\pi_{\theta}$. Since DPO can represent any reward class and all members of a class lead to the same optimal policy, DPO maintains the same theoretical expressiveness as traditional RLHF. It simply bypasses the instability of learning an explicit reward model by selecting one specific, analytically convenient representative from the equivalence class that allows the optimal policy to be solved in closed form.

### Why is the $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$ term in the DPO gradient critical for stability?
Question:
In the 'What does the DPO update do?' section, the authors derive the gradient and point out a weighting term $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$. They claim that a naive objective without this weighting causes the model to 'degenerate'. I don't understand the intuition here. Why does weighting the gradient by how 'wrong' the current implicit reward is prevent degeneration, and what does 'degeneration' actually look like in the context of language model outputs?
Answer:
The weighting term $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$ in the DPO gradient acts as a dynamic, per-example importance weight that modulates how aggressively the model updates its parameters based on the current state of its implicit reward. To understand its criticality, we first need to look at what the term represents. The implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ measures how much the current policy $\pi_\theta$ has increased the probability of a response $y$ relative to the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. The term $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)$ is the difference between the reward assigned to the dispreferred (losing) response and the preferred (winning) response.

Intuitively, this sigmoid term measures "how wrong" the model is. If the model already correctly assigns a much higher reward to $y_w$ than to $y_l$, the difference $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)$ will be a large negative number, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ will approach $0$. This effectively "turns off" the gradient for that example because the model has already learned the preference. Conversely, if the model incorrectly rates the losing response $y_l$ as much better than the winning response $y_w$, the difference is positive, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ approaches $1$, placing a high weight on the gradient to correct this error.

Without this weighting, a "naive" objective would simply push the likelihood of $y_w$ up and $y_l$ down with constant force regardless of the current reward gap. In the context of language models, this lack of modulation leads to "degeneration." Degeneration typically manifests as a form of mode collapse or the production of unnatural, repetitive, or gibberish text. Because the model is forced to maximize the gap between $y_w$ and $y_l$ indefinitely, it can over-optimize by finding "shortcuts"—mathematical anomalies in the probability space—that satisfy the objective but destroy the linguistic coherence of the output. By weighting the update by the current error, DPO ensures that once the model has "sufficiently" learned the preference for a specific pair, it stops pushing the probabilities to extremes. This allows the model to maintain a healthy balance between maximizing the reward and staying close to the reference distribution (the KL constraint), preventing the policy from drifting into the degenerate regions of the probability space.
