\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What is the role of the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ in the DPO objective?
Question:
I'm reading the DPO paper and I see that the objective function $\mathcal{L}_{DPO}$ relies heavily on a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. The paper says it is typically initialized as the SFT model. I'm confused as to why this is necessary. If the goal is simply to make the model $\pi_{\theta}$ prefer $y_w$ over $y_l$, why can't we just optimize the relative probabilities of the current model $\pi_{\theta}}$ without referring back to a base model?
Answer:
The reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ in Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) serves as a critical anchor that prevents the model from collapsing or deviating into unstable regions of the probability space during training. To understand why we cannot simply optimize the relative probabilities of the current model $\pi_{\theta}$, it is helpful to look at both the theoretical objective DPO is solving and the practical failure modes of "naive" preference learning.

Theoretically, DPO is not just a heuristic for preferring $y_w$ over $y_l$; it is a closed-form solution to a specific reinforcement learning problem: maximizing a reward function $r(x, y)$ while constrained by a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty against a base model. The original RLHF objective is $\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}[r(x, y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta} || \pi_{\text{ref}}]$. This $\beta$ parameter controls the trade-off between maximizing reward and staying close to the reference policy. In the DPO derivation, the authors show that the optimal policy $\pi^*$ for this objective can be reparameterized such that the reward function is implicitly defined as $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. By using the Bradley-Terry model for preferences, the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out, allowing us to express the preference loss entirely in terms of the ratio between the current policy and the reference policy.

Intuitively, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ provides a "baseline" for what a reasonable response looks like. If you were to optimize only $\pi_{\theta}(y_w \mid x) / \pi_{\theta}(y_l \mid x)$, the model could satisfy the objective by simply increasing the probability of $y_w$ to an extreme degree or driving the probability of $y_l$ to zero, regardless of whether the resulting distribution remains a coherent language model. The paper notes that a "naïve version" of this method without the reference-based weighting can cause the language model to degenerate. By measuring the change relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, DPO ensures that the model only moves away from the reference distribution in directions that specifically improve the preference alignment.

Furthermore, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ ensures that the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ remains grounded. This implicit reward tells the model: "how much more likely is this response under my current parameters than it was in the SFT model?" This relative measurement prevents the model from drifting too far from the distribution where the preference data was originally sampled, maintaining generation diversity and preventing "mode collapse" where the model generates a single, high-reward string repeatedly. In practice, initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ as the SFT model ensures that the training starts from a point of stability, leveraging the general linguistic capabilities learned during supervised fine-tuning while refining the behavior to match human preferences.
