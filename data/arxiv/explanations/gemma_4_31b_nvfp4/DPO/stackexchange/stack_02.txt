\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Is DPO fundamentally different from supervised fine-tuning on preferred completions?
Question:
In the Experiments section, the authors compare DPO to 'Preferred-FT', which is just supervised learning on the chosen completion $y_w$. Looking at the DPO loss, it seems to be increasing the probability of $y_w$ and decreasing $y_l$. Is DPO just a more complex version of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) combined with unlikelihood training, or is there a theoretical reason why this specific formulation is superior to just training on the 'correct' answers?
Answer:
While DPO may superficially look like a combination of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and unlikelihood training, it is fundamentally different in both its theoretical objective and its mechanical update rule. SFT on preferred completions (referred to in the paper as Preferred-FT) only seeks to maximize the likelihood of the "correct" answer $y_w$, effectively ignoring the contrast provided by the dispreferred answer $y_l$. In contrast, DPO is derived as a closed-form solution to the standard RLHF objective: maximizing a reward function while maintaining a KL-divergence constraint relative to a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$.

The theoretical superiority of DPO stems from this connection to reinforcement learning. The authors show that there is an analytical mapping between a reward function $r(x, y)$ and its optimal policy $\pi_r(y|x)$. By substituting this mapping into the Bradley-Terry preference model, they transform a loss function over reward parameters into a loss function over policy parameters. This means that when you optimize the DPO loss, you are not simply "pushing" probabilities up and down; you are implicitly optimizing a reward model and its corresponding optimal policy simultaneously.

Mechanistically, the difference between DPO and a naive unlikelihood objective is most evident in the gradient. The gradient of the DPO loss is:
$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w)) \left[\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)\right]\right]$$
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ is the implicit reward. 

Unlike unlikelihood training, which typically applies a constant weight to the dispreferred sample, DPO incorporates a dynamic, per-example importance weight $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))$. This weight is high when the model is "wrong" (i.e., the implicit reward for the dispreferred completion is higher than for the preferred one) and low when the model already correctly distinguishes the two. This adaptive weighting prevents the model degeneration often seen in naive probability ratio objectives and ensures the policy stays grounded relative to $\pi_\text{ref}$, allowing DPO to achieve a more efficient reward-KL frontier than even PPO-based RLHF.
