\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Does the DPO reparameterization limit the expressiveness of the learned reward?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that all reward classes consistent with the Bradley-Terry model can be represented by the reparameterization $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$. I'm confused by the mention of 'equivalence classes' where two rewards are equivalent if they differ by a function $f(x)$. Does this mean that DPO cannot learn a unique reward function, but only a class of functions? And if so, does this limitation affect the final quality of the policy $\pi_{\theta}$?
Answer:
The short answer is no; the DPO reparameterization does not limit the expressiveness of the learned reward in any way that affects the resulting policy. The mention of "equivalence classes" refers to a fundamental property of the Bradley-Terry (BT) model rather than a limitation of the DPO algorithm itself.

To understand this intuitively, consider that the BT model determines the probability of preferring one response over another based on the difference between their rewards: $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$. Because only the difference matters, adding any constant or any function $f(x)$ that depends only on the prompt $x$ to the reward of every possible completion $y$ will not change the outcome. For example, if you increase the reward of all possible answers for a specific prompt by $10$ points, the relative difference between any two answers remains exactly the same. Therefore, any two reward functions that differ only by a function of the prompt, $f(x)$, are "equivalent" because they induce the exact same preference distribution. This is the "under-specification" mentioned in Lemma 1.

The "limitation" you are worried about—that DPO cannot learn a "unique" reward function—is actually a feature of the preference modeling framework. Since any reward function within an equivalence class produces the same preference data and, as stated in Lemma 2, the same optimal policy $\pi^*$ under the KL-constrained objective, there is no need to identify a unique reward function. Any representative of that class is sufficient to derive the optimal behavior.

Theorem 1 proves that the DPO reparameterization $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ is expressive enough to represent at least one member of every possible reward equivalence class. The proof demonstrates that for any arbitrary reward function $r(x, y)$, we can apply a projection $f$ (effectively subtracting the log of the partition function $Z(x)$) to transform it into the DPO form. This projection creates a new reward function that is mathematically equivalent to the original one (it differs only by a function of $x$) but fits the specific structure required by DPO.

Consequently, this does not affect the final quality of the policy $\pi_{\theta}$. Since DPO can represent any reward class and all members of a class lead to the same optimal policy, DPO maintains the same theoretical expressiveness as traditional RLHF. It simply bypasses the instability of learning an explicit reward model by selecting one specific, analytically convenient representative from the equivalence class that allows the optimal policy to be solved in closed form.
