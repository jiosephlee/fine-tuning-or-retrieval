\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 2: The Traditional RLHF Pipeline

# Phase 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and the base reference model

The journey toward aligning a large language model (LM) with human preferences begins with the understanding that unsupervised pre-training, while powerful, is insufficient for precise behavioral control. Large-scale unsupervised LMs acquire vast world knowledge and reasoning skills by training on massive datasets. However, because this data is generated by humans with diverse goals and skillsets, the model may learn to imitate undesirable behaviors or misconceptions. To refine the model's behavior for specific downstream tasks—such as dialogue or summarization—the first phase of the pipeline is Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).

During SFT, a pre-trained LM is further trained using supervised learning on a curated dataset of high-quality demonstrations. These demonstrations typically consist of prompts and corresponding gold-standard completions written by experts. The goal of this phase is to produce a model, denoted as $\pi^\text{SFT}$, that is capable of generating responses that generally align with the desired task format and quality. This SFT model is crucial because it serves as the starting point for subsequent stages of alignment and acts as the base reference model, $\pi_\text{ref}$, throughout the RLHF process. By establishing a baseline of competence, the system ensures that the model possesses the basic ability to perform the task before the more complex process of preference optimization begins.

# Phase 2: Reward Modeling

Once a competent SFT model is established, the second phase focuses on quantifying "quality" through a reward model. While expert demonstrations are useful, relative human judgments—deciding which of two responses is better—are often easier for humans to provide than writing a perfect response from scratch. Therefore, the reward modeling phase aims to learn a scalar function that can predict which response a human would prefer given a specific prompt.

The process begins by sampling pairs of completions, $(y_1, y_2)$, from the SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}$ for a set of prompts $x$. Human labelers then examine these pairs and indicate their preference, denoted as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the preferred (winning) completion and $y_l$ is the dispreferred (losing) completion. These preferences are assumed to be governed by a latent, unknown reward function $r^*(y, x)$. To approximate this latent reward, researchers employ the Bradley-Terry (BT) model. The BT model provides a theoretical framework to map reward values to the probability of one response being preferred over another. Specifically, it stipulates that the probability of $y_1$ being preferred over $y_2$ is given by:
$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}$$

To implement this, a parametric reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is trained using a dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D}$. This model is typically initialized from the SFT model, with a linear layer added to the final transformer layer to output a single scalar value. The parameters $\phi$ are estimated via maximum likelihood, framing the task as a binary classification problem. The negative log-likelihood loss is defined as:
$$\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]$$
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. In this setup, the model is trained to maximize the difference between the reward assigned to the preferred completion and the reward assigned to the dispreferred completion. To maintain a lower variance in the reward signals, it is common practice to normalize the rewards such that the expected reward $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

# Phase 3: RL Optimization

The final phase of the traditional RLHF pipeline is the optimization of the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize the rewards predicted by the learned reward model $r_\phi$. However, simply maximizing reward can lead to "reward hacking," where the model finds adversarial shortcuts to get high scores from the reward model without actually producing high-quality text. To prevent this, the optimization is formulated as a constrained reward maximization problem:
$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]$$
In this objective, $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}$ represents the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the current policy $\pi_\theta$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (the SFT model). The parameter $\beta$ controls the strength of this constraint. The KL term is essential for several reasons: it prevents the model from drifting too far from the distribution where the reward model was trained and remains accurate, and it maintains generation diversity by preventing the model from collapsing into a single, high-reward answer (mode-collapse).

Because language generation involves discrete tokens, the objective is not directly differentiable, making standard gradient descent impossible. Consequently, reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms are used. The most common approach is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an actor-critic framework. In this framework, the system optimizes the policy by treating the reward as $r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x))$. The "actor" is the policy $\pi_\theta$ that generates text, while the "critic" helps estimate the value function to reduce the variance of the policy gradients.

# Challenges of RLHF: Instability, computational cost, and hyperparameter sensitivity

Despite its success, the traditional RLHF pipeline is fraught with practical difficulties. One of the primary issues is the extreme computational cost. Unlike supervised learning, RLHF requires sampling from the LM policy during the training loop. This means the model must generate full sequences of text, which are then evaluated by the reward model, creating a bottleneck in training throughput. Furthermore, the pipeline is complex, requiring the maintenance and training of multiple large models—the SFT model, the reward model, the policy model, and often a separate value function for the critic.

The process is also notoriously unstable. Policy gradient methods can exhibit high variance, making the training process sensitive to small changes in hyperparameters. The need for a learned value function in actor-critic algorithms like PPO adds another layer of complexity; if the value function is poorly optimized, the policy updates can become erratic. Even attempts to stabilize the process, such as normalizing rewards using human completion baselines, are essentially Monte-Carlo estimates that may not fully capture the underlying distribution. This combination of sampling overhead, multi-model coordination, and numerical instability makes RLHF a challenging and resource-intensive procedure to implement and tune.
