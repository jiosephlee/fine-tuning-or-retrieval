\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 1: Introduction to LLM Alignment

# Capabilities vs. Alignment: The gap between knowledge and behavior

Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) are capable of acquiring a vast breadth of world knowledge and sophisticated reasoning skills. This is achieved through training on massive datasets, allowing the models to develop surprising capabilities that can be leveraged for a variety of tasks. However, there is a fundamental distinction between the knowledge a model possesses and the behavior it exhibits when generating text. While a model may "know" a great deal about a topic, this does not automatically mean it will express that knowledge in a way that is helpful, accurate, or aligned with the user's intent.

The core challenge is that the unsupervised nature of pre-training provides very little precise control over the model's behavior. Because the training process involves predicting the next token from a diverse corpus of human text, the model learns to mirror the statistical properties of that data rather than adhering to a specific set of behavioral guidelines. Consequently, there is often a significant gap between the model's underlying abilities and its actual output, creating a need for a mechanism to steer the model toward desired responses.

# The risks of unsupervised pre-training: Imitating undesirable patterns

The primary risk of relying solely on unsupervised pre-training is that the datasets used to train these models are generated by humans with a wide array of conflicting goals, priorities, and skillsets. Since the model is trained to imitate this data, it may learn and subsequently replicate patterns that are not desirable. The goal of the model during pre-training is to be a general-purpose imitator, but in a production environment, we want the model to be a high-quality assistant, not a mirror of every human quirk or error found in the training set.

To illustrate this, consider an AI coding assistant. For such a model to be effective, it must be able to understand common programming mistakes so that it can identify and correct them. This is a capability—a piece of knowledge the model needs. However, we do not want the model to actually generate those mistakes when writing code for a user. If the model simply imitates the training data, it might bias its generations toward common but incorrect coding patterns.

A similar problem arises with factual misconceptions. A language model might be aware of a common misconception believed by a large percentage of the population because that misconception appears frequently in the training data. While it is useful for the model to be aware that such a belief exists, it is detrimental if the model claims the misconception is true in a percentage of its queries matching the frequency of the error in the training data. Thus, unsupervised training allows models to learn both correct and incorrect patterns, and without further intervention, the model cannot distinguish which patterns it should actually exhibit.

# Defining 'Alignment' in the context of LLMs

Alignment is the process of selecting the model's desired responses and behavior from its very wide range of knowledge and abilities. In the context of large language models, alignment is about steerability. It is the transition from a model that can generate any sequence of text that is statistically likely to the training data, to a model that generates text that is safe, performant, and controllable.

Achieving alignment means ensuring that the model's outputs adhere to human preferences. This involves biasing the model toward high-quality responses—such as those that are helpful, honest, or harmless—and away from low-quality or dangerous responses. By aligning the model, researchers can ensure that the model's vast capabilities are channeled into behaviors that are beneficial to the end-user and consistent with the developer's goals.

# Overview of the preference learning pipeline

The most common method for achieving this alignment is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). This is a multi-stage pipeline designed to transition a model from a general-purpose LM to an aligned assistant. The process typically begins with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), where a pre-trained LM is fine-tuned on a curated dataset of high-quality demonstrations of the target task, such as dialogue or summarization. This produces an initial model, $\pi^\text{SFT}$.

The second phase is reward modeling. In this stage, the SFT model is used to generate multiple responses to the same prompt. Human labelers then rank these responses, identifying a preferred completion $y_w$ and a dispreferred completion $y_l$. These preferences are used to train a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$, which is a neural network designed to predict the scalar reward of a given response. This is often modeled using the Bradley-Terry framework, where the probability that one response is preferred over another is represented as:
$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}$

The final phase is RL optimization. The learned reward model provides feedback to the language model policy $\pi_{\theta}$. The objective is to maximize the expected reward while ensuring the model does not drift too far from the original reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (usually the SFT model). This is formulated as:
$\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]$
Here, the $\text{KL}$ divergence term acts as a constraint to prevent the model from collapsing into a single high-reward answer or deviating into regions where the reward model is no longer accurate.

# The motivation for Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

While RLHF is effective, the pipeline is incredibly complex and often unstable. The requirement to train multiple different models—the policy model, the reward model, and the reference model—introduces significant computational overhead. Furthermore, the RL phase typically requires sampling from the language model in the training loop, which is computationally expensive and makes the process sensitive to hyperparameter tuning. Algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) are frequently used for this, but they can be difficult to optimize and prone to instability.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is motivated by the desire to eliminate this complexity. The core insight behind DPO is that the RLHF objective—maximizing reward with a KL-divergence constraint—can be solved analytically. By using a specific parameterization, the reward function can be expressed in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy. This allows the reward model to be implicitly represented by the policy network itself.

By bypassing the need for an explicit reward model and the subsequent RL training loop, DPO transforms the alignment problem into a simple classification task. Instead of using RL, DPO uses a binary cross-entropy objective to directly increase the relative log probability of preferred responses compared to dispreferred ones. This results in a training process that is stable, computationally lightweight, and significantly easier to implement, while still achieving alignment results that match or exceed those of traditional RLHF.
