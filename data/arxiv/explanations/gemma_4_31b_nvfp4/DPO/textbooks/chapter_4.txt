\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 4: # Mechanistic Understanding of the DPO Update

To understand how Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) actually modifies a language model during training, we must look beyond the high-level loss function and examine the gradients. While the DPO objective is framed as a binary cross-entropy loss, its effect on the model parameters $\theta$ is a sophisticated process of shifting probability mass between preferred and dispreferred responses. By analyzing the gradient of the loss function, we can uncover the "mechanics" of how the model learns to align with human preferences without an explicit reward model or reinforcement learning loop.

# Analyzing the DPO gradient: A step-by-step breakdown

The behavior of DPO is most clearly revealed by examining the gradient of its loss function, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}$. To understand this, we first define the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y)$, which is the reward defined by the current policy $\pi_\theta$ relative to the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. This is expressed as $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$. Here, $\beta$ is a scalar parameter that controls the strength of the constraint preventing the model from drifting too far from the reference policy.

The gradient of the DPO loss with respect to the model parameters $\theta$ is formulated as:
$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w)) \left[\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)\right]\right]$$

This expression can be decomposed into two primary components: a weighting term and a direction term. The weighting term, $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))$, acts as a dynamic importance weight for each individual training example. The direction term, $\left[\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)\right]$, determines how the model's probabilities should shift. Together, these components ensure that the model does not simply maximize the probability of the preferred response in a vacuum, but does so in a way that is sensitive to the model's current state of "correctness."

# Increasing likelihood of preferred responses ($y_w$)

The first part of the direction term in the gradient, $\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)$, is responsible for increasing the likelihood of the preferred completion $y_w$. In the context of gradient descent, minimizing the negative loss involves moving in the opposite direction of the gradient. Because of the negative sign at the front of the DPO gradient expression, the update effectively performs a gradient ascent step on the log-probability of the winning response.

By increasing the probability of $y_w$, the model is encouraged to generate responses that align with the preferences captured in the dataset $\mathcal{D}$. However, this increase is not absolute. The degree to which the model increases the likelihood of $y_w$ is modulated by the implicit reward difference between the preferred and dispreferred responses. If the model already assigns a significantly higher implicit reward to $y_w$ than to $y_l$, the need for a strong update to the probability of $y_w$ is diminished.

# Decreasing likelihood of dispreferred responses ($y_l$)

Simultaneously, the DPO update works to suppress the likelihood of the dispreferred response $y_l$. This is handled by the term $-\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)$ within the direction component of the gradient. While the preferred response is being pushed "up," the dispreferred response is being pushed "down."

This dual-action mechanism is critical for preference learning. Simply increasing the probability of "good" responses (as seen in standard supervised fine-tuning) is often insufficient because it does not explicitly penalize "bad" responses that the model might still find probable. By explicitly decreasing the log-probability of $y_l$, DPO forces the model to distinguish between two possible completions and actively move away from the one that humans found less desirable.

# The dynamic importance weight: How the model learns from its mistakes

The most distinctive feature of the DPO update is the inclusion of the dynamic importance weight: $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))$. This term uses the logistic function $\sigma$ to scale the gradient based on the current implicit reward values of the two completions.

Intuitively, this weight represents how "wrong" the model's current implicit reward model is for a specific example. If the model's implicit reward for the dispreferred response $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l)$ is much higher than the reward for the preferred response $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)$, the difference is positive and the sigmoid value is large. This signals that the model is making a significant mistake, and consequently, the gradient update is amplified to correct this error aggressively.

Conversely, if the model already correctly assigns a much higher implicit reward to $y_w$ than to $y_l$, the term $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w)$ becomes a large negative number. The sigmoid of a large negative number is close to zero, which effectively "turns off" or minimizes the gradient update for that example. This prevents the model from over-optimizing examples it has already mastered, allowing it to focus its learning capacity on the most challenging or incorrectly ordered pairs.

# Comparing DPO updates to naive probability ratio objectives

To appreciate the importance of the dynamic weight, it is helpful to compare DPO to a naive probability ratio objective. A naive approach to preference learning might simply attempt to increase the ratio of the probability of the preferred response to the dispreferred response without any weighting. Such an objective would look like a simple difference in log-probabilities without the $\sigma(\dots)$ term.

In a naive setup, the model would apply the same magnitude of update to every pair in the dataset, regardless of whether the model had already learned the preference or was completely wrong. This lacks the nuance of the DPO update, which recognizes that not all preference pairs are equally informative at different stages of training. The DPO update is not just about the ratio of probabilities, but about the *accuracy* of that ratio relative to the human labels, scaled by the parameter $\beta$ which accounts for the strength of the KL constraint.

# Preventing model degeneration through weighting

The inclusion of the dynamic importance weight is not merely a performance optimization; it is essential for the stability of the training process. The authors find that a naive version of the DPO method—specifically one that removes the weighting coefficient $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))$—can cause the language model to degenerate.

Model degeneration often manifests as the model losing its ability to generate coherent text or collapsing into repetitive patterns. This happens because, without the weighting term, the model may push the probabilities of $y_w$ and $y_l$ to extremes to satisfy the loss function, ignoring the underlying distribution of the language. The dynamic weight prevents this by tapering off the updates as the model's implicit reward begins to align with the human preferences. By ensuring that the updates vanish as the model becomes "correct," DPO maintains a healthier balance between maximizing preference reward and adhering to the reference policy, thereby preventing the catastrophic collapse of the model's generative capabilities.
