\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 6: # Practical Implementation and Empirical Evaluation

After establishing the theoretical foundation of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), it is essential to understand how this framework is applied in practice. Moving from a theoretical loss function to a deployed model requires a structured pipeline, a careful choice of reference policies, and a rigorous evaluation framework to ensure that the model is truly aligning with human preferences without collapsing or deviating too far from its original capabilities.

# The DPO pipeline: From preference dataset to aligned model

The DPO pipeline is designed to be significantly simpler than the traditional Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) pipeline. While traditional RLHF requires a multi-stage process—supervised fine-tuning, reward model training, and then a complex RL optimization loop like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)—DPO collapses these steps into a more direct approach. The goal is to optimize a language model policy $\pi_\theta$ such that it prefers completions that humans find helpful or safe over those they find undesirable.

The first stage of the pipeline is the construction of an offline preference dataset, denoted as $\mathcal{D}$. This dataset consists of triplets $\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, where $x$ is the prompt, $y_w$ is the preferred (winning) completion, and $y_l$ is the dispreferred (losing) completion. These pairs are typically generated by sampling two different completions from a reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ for each prompt and having human labelers indicate which completion is superior.

Once the dataset $\mathcal{D}$ is established, the second stage involves optimizing the policy $\pi_\theta$. Instead of using an RL algorithm to maximize a reward, DPO employs a simple binary cross-entropy objective. The model is trained to minimize the DPO loss:
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$
In this process, $\beta$ acts as a hyperparameter that controls the strength of the constraint relative to the preference optimization. By minimizing this loss, the model implicitly increases the likelihood of preferred responses while decreasing the likelihood of dispreferred ones, all while remaining anchored to the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

# Handling the absence of an SFT model: Initializing the reference policy

In an ideal RLHF scenario, the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) model—a model that has already been trained on high-quality demonstrations for the specific task. This ensures that the starting point for preference optimization is a model that already understands the basic format and requirements of the task. When $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ is available, it is the natural choice for $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and the starting weights for the policy $\pi_\theta$.

However, in many real-world research settings, one may wish to use existing public preference datasets where the original SFT model used to generate those samples is unknown or unavailable. This creates a distribution shift problem, as the DPO objective depends heavily on the relationship between the current policy and the reference policy. If the reference policy is too distant from the distribution of the preference data, the optimization may be unstable or suboptimal.

To mitigate this, DPO introduces a method to initialize $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ when a proper SFT model is missing. The reference model is initialized by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred completions $y_w$ present in the dataset $\mathcal{D}$. Mathematically, the reference policy is set as:
$$\pi_{\text{ref}} = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]$$
By training $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on the winning responses, the pipeline creates a "pseudo-SFT" model that aligns the reference distribution with the high-quality end of the preference data. This allows DPO to function effectively even when the researchers do not have access to the exact model that originally generated the preference pairs.

# Experimental Setup: Controlled sentiment, summarization, and dialogue tasks

To evaluate the effectiveness of DPO, the researchers tested it across three distinct natural language generation tasks, each posing different challenges in terms of alignment and complexity.

The first task was controlled sentiment generation using the IMDb movie review dataset. This served as a "controlled" environment because the ground-truth reward was known—a pre-trained sentiment classifier was used to determine if a generation was positive. GPT-2-large was first fine-tuned on IMDb reviews to create the SFT model. Preference pairs were then generated based on the sentiment classifier's scores, allowing the researchers to precisely measure how well DPO could steer the model toward positive sentiment.

The second task focused on summarization using the Reddit TL;DR dataset. In this setting, the model was prompted with a forum post and tasked with generating a concise summary. The researchers used human preferences gathered from previous work and a GPT-J model that had been fine-tuned on human-written summaries. This task is more complex than sentiment generation because "quality" in summarization is multifaceted, involving both accuracy and conciseness.

The final task was single-turn dialogue using the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless (HH) dataset. This dataset contains 170k dialogues where humans preferred one assistant response over another based on helpfulness and safety. Unlike the other tasks, no standard SFT model was available for this dataset. Consequently, the researchers used an off-the-shelf Pythia-2.8B model and initialized the reference policy using the "Preferred-FT" method (fine-tuning on the preferred completions $y_w$) before applying DPO.

# Evaluation Metrics: The Reward-KL Frontier and Win Rates

Evaluating alignment is challenging because there is often no single "correct" answer. The researchers used two primary metrics to assess DPO: the Reward-KL Frontier and Win Rates.

The Reward-KL Frontier is used primarily in controlled settings where a ground-truth reward function $r(x, y)$ exists. The goal of the RLHF objective is to maximize reward while minimizing the KL-divergence from the reference policy: $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta} || \pi_{\text{ref}}]$. A "frontier" is created by plotting the average reward achieved against the average KL-divergence across various hyperparameter settings. A more efficient algorithm is one that can achieve higher rewards for any given level of KL-divergence. This allows researchers to see if the model is "gaming" the reward (high reward but massive KL drift) or if it is efficiently learning the desired behavior.

In real-world tasks like dialogue and summarization, where no ground-truth reward function exists, the researchers used "Win Rates." This involves comparing the outputs of a model against a baseline policy and determining how often the model's output is preferred. Because human evaluation is expensive and slow, GPT-4 was used as a proxy for human judgment. To ensure this was valid, the researchers conducted a human study showing that GPT-4's judgments correlate strongly with human agreements. For summarization, the win rate was calculated against reference summaries in the test set; for dialogue, it was calculated against the preferred response in the test dataset.

# Analysis of results: DPO vs. PPO, SFT, and 'Best of N' sampling

The empirical results demonstrate that DPO is a powerful and stable alternative to traditional RLHF. In the controlled sentiment experiments, DPO's reward-KL frontier strictly dominated that of PPO. Remarkably, DPO performed better even than "PPO-GT," a version of PPO that had access to the actual ground-truth reward function, highlighting that DPO's direct optimization is more efficient than the actor-critic framework.

When compared to simpler supervised methods, DPO significantly outperformed both the base SFT model and "Preferred-FT" (supervised learning on winning responses). It also outperformed "Unlikelihood" training, which naively increases the probability of $y_w$ and decreases $y_l$ without the dynamic importance weighting provided by the DPO objective. This suggests that the specific weighting mechanism in DPO—which focuses on examples where the implicit reward model is most incorrect—is crucial for preventing model degeneration.

DPO was also compared against the "Best of $N$" sampling baseline. In this baseline, $N$ responses are sampled from the SFT model, and the one with the highest score from a learned reward model is selected. While "Best of $N$" can produce very high-quality responses, it is computationally impractical at test time because it requires generating $N$ samples for every single query. DPO was found to match or exceed the performance of "Best of 128" in dialogue tasks while remaining computationally efficient, as it only requires a single forward pass during inference.

# Generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) data

A critical concern for any alignment algorithm is whether the model overfits to the preference dataset or if it truly learns a generalizable preference. To test this, the researchers evaluated the models trained on Reddit TL;DR summarization on a completely different distribution: news articles from the CNN/DailyMail dataset.

The models were tested using the same sampling temperatures that performed best on the Reddit data. The evaluation was conducted using GPT-4 to determine the win rate of the models against ground-truth news summaries. The results indicated that DPO policies generalize significantly better than PPO policies when faced with this distribution shift.

This finding is particularly interesting because PPO typically utilizes additional unlabeled prompts from the training distribution to stabilize the policy, whereas DPO does not. The fact that DPO maintains a significant performance lead on OOD data suggests that the direct optimization of the policy relative to a reference model creates a more robust alignment that is less prone to overfitting the specific quirks of the preference training set.
