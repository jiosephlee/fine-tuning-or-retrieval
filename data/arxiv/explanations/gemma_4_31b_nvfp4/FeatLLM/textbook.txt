\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Introduction to Tabular Learning and Large Language Models

# What is Tabular Learning?

Tabular learning is a fundamental branch of machine learning focused on processing data organized in a structured format, typically consisting of rows and columns. In a formal sense, a tabular dataset can be represented as a collection of $N$ labeled samples, denoted as $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$. Within this structure, each sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ is a $d$-dimensional vector representing various input features, while $\mathbf{y}^i$ is the corresponding label. In a classification setup, the label $\mathbf{y}^i$ is selected from a predefined set of classes. To make this data interpretable, each feature is associated with a natural language name, represented by the set $F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^{d}$, along with a definition that describes the nature of the data it contains.

This type of learning is vital for a vast array of real-world applications across diverse domains. For instance, in healthcare, tabular learning is used to predict the occurrence of coronary artery disease or whether a patient's data indicates chronic heart failure. In finance, it can be applied to predict whether a customer will subscribe to a term deposit or whether an individual poses a good or bad credit risk. Other applications include predicting annual earnings, crime rates in specific regions, or even the quality of a car. Because tabular data often involves a mix of categorical variables and numerical values, the goal is to find a mathematical relationship between these heterogeneous features and the target class.

# The Challenge of Few-Shot Learning in Tabular Data

Few-shot learning occurs when a model must be trained using only a very small number of labeled samples, denoted as $k$, where $k$ is significantly smaller than the total number of available samples $N$. This scenario is common in industrial and medical domains where labeling data is expensive or difficult due to the high cost of human expertise. The primary challenge in few-shot tabular learning is the risk of learning spurious correlations. When a model is trained on a limited set of examples, it may identify patterns that appear to be predictive within that small sample but do not actually generalize to the broader population.

Unlike image or text data, where generalizable representations can be more easily extracted, the heterogeneous nature of tabular data makes it difficult to find universally applicable data augmentations. Traditional efforts to overcome this include semi-supervised learning using unlabeled datasets, or unsupervised pre-training techniques. These pre-training methods might involve masking or corrupting parts of the data and using a reconstruction objective, or employing contrastive learning. However, these strategies have not been notably effective in few-shot settings. Consequently, the main difficulty remains the lack of sufficient data to distinguish true causal relationships from random noise.

# The Role of Prior Knowledge in Machine Learning

To mitigate the risks associated with limited data, researchers incorporate prior knowledge to provide a helpful inductive bias. Prior knowledge consists of domain-specific information that informs the model about the likely relationship between features and targets, effectively substituting for the missing labeled data. For example, in a disease prediction task, the general medical knowledge that older individuals have an increased risk of certain conditions can be a powerful piece of prior knowledge that guides the model's predictions, even if the training set contains very few elderly patients.

One way to utilize this knowledge is through transfer learning, where models are trained on a broad range of tabular datasets to learn general semantic relationships before being applied to a specific target task. Some approaches involve learning the semantic relationships between columns through their natural language names, while others use synthetic data with diverse distributions to pre-train a model. By leveraging this pre-existing understanding of how different types of data behave, models can achieve higher accuracy in low-shot regimes than they would if they were relying solely on the small provided training set.

# Overview of LLMs: From Text Generation to Reasoning

Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as a powerful tool for incorporating prior knowledge into machine learning. Because they are trained on extensive and diverse text corpora, LLMs possess a vast amount of general knowledge that can act as a substitute for expert domain knowledge. While LLMs are well-known for text generation, they also demonstrate a remarkable ability to tackle unseen reasoning problems through in-context learning. By incorporating a task description and a few example demonstrations into a prompt, an LLM can "attend" to important features and understand the context of a specific problem.

Beyond simple prediction, LLMs can be used for structured reasoning. This involves a process where the model first infers the causal relationship or tendency between features and a target—relying on common sense or general knowledge—and then uses those insights along with specific examples to deduce decisive rules. For instance, an LLM can act as an expert feature engineer by determining which specific conditions of a feature (e.g., a certain value threshold) result in a specific class. This transforms the LLM from a black-box predictor into a tool for feature generation, where the "reasoning" process identifies the underlying criteria used to distinguish one class from another.

# Comparing Traditional Tabular Models with LLM-based Approaches

Traditional tabular learning models, such as Logistic Regression, XGBoost, and Random Forest, are computationally efficient and fast during inference. However, they often struggle in few-shot scenarios because they lack prior knowledge and are prone to overfitting when the number of training samples is small. These models rely entirely on the statistical patterns present in the provided data.

Recent LLM-based approaches attempt to bridge this gap by serializing tabular data into text and feeding it into the model. Some use in-context learning, while others employ parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques like LoRA or IA3. However, these end-to-end LLM approaches face several limitations. First, they are computationally expensive, requiring at least one LLM inference per sample at test time. Second, they are limited by prompt size; when a dataset has a large number of features, the serialized text can exceed the LLM's context window, leading to a decline in performance.

The \textsf{FeatLLM} framework proposes a hybrid approach: using the LLM as a feature engineer rather than a predictor. Instead of passing every sample through the LLM, \textsf{FeatLLM} uses the LLM to generate a set of binary rules. These rules are then converted into new binary features. A simple downstream machine learning model, such as a linear model without bias, is then trained to estimate class probabilities using these features. Specifically, if $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0, 1\}^R$ is the vector of binary features for class $k$ for sample $\mathbf{x}^i$, the logit is computed as $\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$, and the final probability $\mathbf{p}^i$ is determined via a Softmax function. This approach combines the high-level reasoning and prior knowledge of LLMs with the efficiency and robustness of traditional linear models.

# Limitations of End-to-End LLM Predictors

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into tabular learning has opened new avenues for utilizing prior knowledge to solve complex data problems. However, the most intuitive way of using these models—treating the LLM as an end-to-end predictor—presents significant hurdles when applied to real-world scenarios. In an end-to-end setup, the model is expected to take a raw data sample and directly output a prediction. While this may seem straightforward, the architectural and operational constraints of LLMs make this approach inefficient and often impractical.

# The End-to-End Prediction Pipeline

To understand the limitations, one must first understand how an LLM is typically used for end-to-end tabular prediction. Since LLMs are designed to process sequences of text rather than structured grids of numbers, the tabular data must first be transformed through a process called serialization. In this pipeline, each sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ is converted into a natural language string. For example, if a dataset has features $F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^{d}$, a single sample is serialized by stating that each feature $f_j$ is equal to a specific value $\mathbf{x}^i_j$.

Once the data is serialized, it is embedded into a prompt alongside a task description (such as a question regarding a medical diagnosis) and a few-shot example demonstrations. These demonstrations consist of previously serialized samples and their corresponding ground-truth labels $\mathbf{y}^i$. The LLM then processes this entire block of text to "attend" to the important features and examples, ultimately generating a predicted class for the target sample. In this black-box approach, the LLM is responsible for both the feature analysis and the final classification in one single pass.

# Computational Cost and Inference Latency

A primary drawback of the end-to-end pipeline is the immense computational overhead. In a traditional machine learning model, such as a linear regression or a decision tree, the inference phase is extremely fast because it involves simple mathematical operations. In contrast, an end-to-end LLM approach requires at least one full LLM inference per sample. 

This requirement leads to substantial inference latency. Because LLMs are massive models with billions of parameters, generating a response for even a single row of data is computationally expensive. When dealing with real-world datasets that may contain thousands or millions of samples, the time and cost associated with sending a query to the LLM for every individual prediction become prohibitive. This makes the end-to-end approach far less scalable than conventional tabular learning methods, where the model is trained once and can then predict on new data almost instantaneously.

# The 'Lost in the Middle' Phenomenon: Prompt Size Limitations

Tabular datasets in industrial and scientific domains often contain a large number of features, sometimes exceeding 100 attributes. When these features are serialized into natural language, the resulting prompt becomes exceptionally long. This introduces a critical failure point known as the "lost in the middle" phenomenon. 

As the serialized text length grows, LLMs often experience a decline in performance. The model may struggle to maintain focus on all the provided information, often failing to effectively "attend" to critical features that are buried in the middle of a lengthy prompt. When the number of features increases to a certain point, the approach can become entirely infeasible, as the prompt size may exceed the LLM's maximum context window. Consequently, the very strength of the LLM—its ability to process a wide array of information—is undermined by the linear nature of text prompts when applied to wide tabular data.

# Dependency on Fine-Tuning vs. API-level Access

To achieve high accuracy in tabular tasks, many existing LLM-based methods rely on parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques, such as LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) or IA3. Fine-tuning allows the model to adapt its internal weights to the specific nuances of a tabular dataset. However, this creates a significant accessibility gap. 

Many of the highest-performing LLMs are closed-source and are only accessible via inference APIs. These APIs provide the ability to send a prompt and receive a response, but they do not provide access to the model's training codebases or tuning APIs. For a practitioner using these top-tier models, fine-tuning is not an option. This forces a reliance on in-context learning, which, while flexible, may not always reach the accuracy of a tuned model and remains susceptible to the prompt size and latency issues mentioned previously.

# The Need for a Shift from Predictor to Feature Engineer

Given these constraints, there is a clear need to move away from treating the LLM as a black-box predictor. The fundamental problem is that the LLM is being asked to perform two different roles simultaneously: it must analyze the data to find the relevant "criteria" for a class and then apply those criteria to every single sample. 

The solution lies in shifting the LLM's role from a predictor to a feature engineer. Instead of asking the LLM to predict the label for every sample, the LLM can be used to infer the underlying rules or conditions that determine a class. For example, instead of predicting whether a specific patient has heart failure, the LLM can be asked to define the general rules that indicate heart failure based on the available features and prior knowledge. 

Once these rules are extracted, they can be converted into binary features (indicating whether a sample satisfies a rule or not). These features can then be fed into a simple, low-complexity downstream machine learning model. This shift eliminates the need to query the LLM at inference time, drastically reduces latency, overcomes prompt size limitations through techniques like bagging, and removes the dependency on expensive fine-tuning. By using the LLM to discover the "criteria" rather than to provide the final answer, we leverage the LLM's reasoning power while maintaining the efficiency of traditional machine learning.

The FeatLLM Framework: Conceptual Overview

# The Core Idea: LLM as an Expert Feature Engineer

In traditional tabular learning, machine learning models are typically tasked with mapping input features directly to a target label. When Large Language Models (LLMs) are applied to this task, a common approach is to treat the LLM as a black-box predictor. In such a setup, the model receives a serialized version of a data sample and is asked to predict the class directly. While this leverages the vast prior knowledge embedded in the LLM, it creates significant bottlenecks: every single sample must be processed through the LLM at inference time, which is computationally expensive, slow, and often restricted by the limited context window (prompt size) of the model.

The core philosophy of the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework is to shift the role of the LLM from a predictor to an expert feature engineer. Rather than asking the LLM to predict the label for every individual sample, \textsf{FeatLLM} asks the LLM to discover the underlying "criteria" or "rules" that determine a class. For example, instead of asking whether a specific patient has heart failure, the framework asks the LLM to identify the general conditions—such as specific combinations of age or blood pressure—that typically indicate heart failure.

By acting as a feature engineer, the LLM uses its prior knowledge and the provided few-shot examples to propose a set of rules. These rules are then used to transform the original complex tabular data into a set of simple binary features. This approach effectively extracts the "reasoning" of the LLM into a format that can be used by a much simpler, traditional machine learning model. Consequently, the LLM's intelligence is used upfront to design the data representation, while the actual prediction is handled by a lightweight model.

# The FeatLLM Pipeline Architecture

The architecture of \textsf{FeatLLM} is designed to transform a tabular dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, where $\mathbf{x}^i$ is a $d$-dimensional vector of features and $\mathbf{y}^i$ is the class label, into a high-performance predictive system. The pipeline begins with the integration of natural language feature names $F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^{d}$ and their definitions. These descriptions are critical because they allow the LLM to understand the semantic meaning of the data, bridging the gap between raw numbers and real-world concepts.

The pipeline operates in several distinct stages. First, the framework uses a structured prompt to elicit rules from the LLM. Second, these natural language rules are parsed into executable code to generate new binary features for the dataset. Third, these features are fed into a simple linear model that estimates class likelihoods. To ensure the system is robust and can handle datasets with many features, the entire process is wrapped in an ensemble and bagging strategy, where multiple sets of rules are generated and combined to make a final prediction.

This architecture ensures that the heavy lifting—the reasoning and rule extraction—happens only during the initial setup phase. Once the rules are established and the weights of the downstream linear model are learned, the LLM is no longer required for inference. This separates the knowledge acquisition phase (using the LLM) from the prediction phase (using a simple mathematical operation), creating a scalable and efficient system.

# Defining 'Rules' in the Context of Tabular Data

In the context of \textsf{FeatLLM}, a "rule" is a specific condition or set of conditions associated with a particular answer class. These rules serve as the bridge between the LLM's high-level reasoning and the binary nature of machine learning features. A rule is essentially a logical test that a sample either passes (1) or fails (0). For instance, if the task is to predict a disease, a rule might be a condition stating that a certain biomarker must be above a specific threshold and the patient's age must be within a certain range.

The generation of these rules is guided by a two-step reasoning process. First, the LLM is asked to infer the general causal relationship or tendency between features and the target, relying on its general common sense and prior knowledge. Second, the LLM uses a few-shot set of example demonstrations to deduce specific, decisive rules for each class. This structured approach prevents the model from simply latching onto spurious correlations found in a small number of examples and instead forces it to ground its rules in both data and prior knowledge.

Because LLMs output natural language, these rules are not initially in a format that a computer can execute. They might be phrased as "the value of feature X is greater than 10." To make these rules useful, \textsf{FeatLLM} utilizes the LLM again to parse these natural language descriptions into Python program code. This code is then executed using Python's \texttt{exec()} function, converting the original tabular values into binary indicators. These binary indicators become the new features used for the final prediction.

# High-Level Workflow: Rule Extraction to Class Likelihood

The workflow of \textsf{FeatLLM} culminates in the translation of discovered rules into a probability distribution over the possible classes. Once the rules are parsed and the data is transformed, every sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ is represented by a set of binary features $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0, 1\}^R$ for each class $k$, where $R$ is the number of rules extracted per class.

To determine the likelihood of a sample belonging to a class, the framework employs a linear model without bias. The importance of each rule is not assumed to be equal; therefore, a trainable weight vector $\mathbf{w}_k$ is learned for each class. The logit for a class $k$ is computed as the dot product of the positive part of the weights and the binary features:
$$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$$
The use of the $\max(\cdot, 0)$ operation ensures that the weights are projected into a positive subspace, meaning that a rule generated by the LLM can only increase or maintain the likelihood of a class, never decrease it. The final probability for each class $\mathbf{p}^i$ is then calculated using the softmax function:
$$\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}({[\text{logit}_{1}^i, ..., \text{logit}_{c}^i]})$$

These weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ are optimized using a few-shot training set by minimizing the cross-entropy loss. This allow the model to learn which of the LLM-generated rules are actually the most informative for the specific dataset. Finally, to increase robustness, the framework uses bagging to select subsets of features or instances and repeats the process multiple times. The final prediction is the average of the probabilities $\mathbf{p}^i$ across $T$ such trials, leveraging the principle of self-consistency to filter out incorrect or unstable rules.

# Key Advantages: Inference Efficiency and Training-Free Feature Discovery

One of the primary advantages of \textsf{FeatLLM} is its extreme inference efficiency. Conventional LLM-based tabular learners require at least one expensive LLM query for every single sample they wish to predict. In contrast, \textsf{FeatLLM} only uses the LLM during the feature engineering phase. At inference time, the model only needs to perform a simple dot product and a softmax operation. This drastically reduces latency and computational costs, making it practical for industrial applications.

Another significant benefit is that the framework requires only API-level access to the LLM. Many high-performance LLMs are available only through restricted APIs, making parameter-efficient fine-tuning (such as LoRA or IA3) impossible for the user. \textsf{FeatLLM} bypasses this requirement entirely by using in-context learning. It extracts rules through prompting rather than updating the model's weights, meaning it can leverage the most powerful available models without needing access to their training codebases or weights.

Finally, \textsf{FeatLLM} effectively solves the "long prompt" problem. When tabular data contains hundreds of features, serializing them all into a prompt often exceeds the LLM's context window or leads to a decline in performance. By employing feature bagging, \textsf{FeatLLM} can process smaller subsets of features across different trials and combine them via ensembling. This allows the framework to maintain high performance even on very large datasets while avoiding the limitations of the LLM's input size.

Designing Prompts for Rule Extraction

# Components of the Basic Information Description

The effectiveness of the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework relies heavily on the quality of the prompt provided to the Large Language Model (LLM). To guide the model toward acting as an expert feature engineer, the prompt must first establish a comprehensive foundation of basic information. This section of the prompt is designed to provide the essential context the LLM needs to understand the specific tabular learning task at hand, mimicking the way a human expert would be briefed on a problem.

A primary component of this description is the task formulation, which is presented as a natural language question. For instance, if the goal is to predict chronic heart failure based on medical data, the prompt might ask, "Does this patient's myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?" By framing the task as a clear question with explicit label information, the LLM can better align its reasoning with the intended classification goal.

In addition to the task description, the prompt provides detailed information about the features of the dataset. This includes the name of each feature and its value type, as well as an optional definition to clarify the meaning of the attribute. For categorical variables, the prompt may include examples of possible categories. Providing this metadata prevents the LLM from guessing the meaning of obscure column names and ensures that the rules it generates are grounded in the actual nature of the data. Finally, the basic information description incorporates a few training samples, which serve as demonstrations of the relationship between input features and the target labels.

# The Power of Serialization: Converting Tables to Text

Because LLMs are fundamentally designed to process and generate sequences of text rather than structured grids or matrices, tabular data must be transformed into a textual format before it can be analyzed. This process, known as serialization, allows the model to utilize its pre-trained linguistic capabilities to "read" a row of a table as if it were a descriptive sentence.

The serialization process in \textsf{FeatLLM} converts each labeled sample into a structured string. Specifically, for a given sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ with label $\mathbf{y}^i$ and a set of feature names $F$, the serialization is defined as $\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) = ``\ f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ ... \ f_d\  \text{is}\  \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\  \mathbf{y}^i\ ”$. In this format, each feature $f_j$ is explicitly paired with its corresponding value $\mathbf{x}^i_j$.

This transformation is critical because it bridges the gap between the heterogeneous nature of tabular data and the textual reasoning strengths of the LLM. By presenting the data in this manner, the model can attend to the important features and examples provided in the prompt, effectively treating the tabular learning problem as a natural language understanding task. This allows the model to identify patterns across different samples and use those patterns to derive the underlying criteria for classification.

# Structured Reasoning: The Two-Step Process

To ensure that the LLM generates high-quality, decisive rules and avoids the common pitfall of identifying spurious correlations—patterns that appear in a small sample size by chance but do not generalize—\textsf{FeatLLM} employs a structured reasoning approach. Rather than asking the model to provide rules immediately, the framework implements a two-step process inspired by the chain-of-thought reasoning technique.

This structured approach forces the model to slow down and reason through the problem logically. By dividing the inference process into two distinct phases, the framework separates the extraction of general domain knowledge from the analysis of the specific few-shot examples. This prevents the model from overfitting to the limited number of samples provided in the prompt, which is a significant risk in few-shot learning regimes where a single misleading example could otherwise skew the resulting rules.

The first step focuses on the general relationship between features and the target, while the second step synthesizes those generalities with the observed data. This sequence ensures that the final rules are not just descriptions of the provided samples, but are instead informed by a combination of broad prior knowledge and specific evidence.

# Step 1: Extracting Prior Knowledge and Causal Relationships

The first phase of the structured reasoning process is dedicated to extracting the LLM's prior knowledge. In this step, the LLM is instructed to infer the causal relationships or general tendencies between the features and the task description without referring to the provided example demonstrations. 

The intuition behind this requirement is that LLMs are trained on massive corpora of text, which often include domain-specific information. For example, in a healthcare-related task, the LLM may already "know" from its training data that advanced age is generally associated with a higher risk of heart disease. By forcing the model to articulate these general principles first, \textsf{FeatLLM} leverages the LLM as a repository of expert domain knowledge.

By isolating this step from the training samples, the framework ensures that the model's initial hypotheses are based on general truths rather than the specific, potentially noisy, instances in the few-shot set. This allows the model to establish a baseline of "common sense" for the problem, which serves as a filter to help the model focus on significant features and ignore uninformative ones during the subsequent rule-generation phase.

# Step 2: Deriving Rules from Few-Shot Examples

Once the general causal relationships have been established, the LLM moves to the second step: deriving decisive rules for each class. In this phase, the model combines the prior knowledge identified in Step 1 with the specific patterns found in the few-shot example demonstrations to create concrete rules that can distinguish between classes.

To maintain a balance between underfitting and overfitting, \textsf{FeatLLM} sets a fixed number of rules—specifically 10 rules per class—to be extracted. This constraint prevents the model from generating too few rules, which would fail to capture the complexity of the task, or too many rules, which could lead to an excessively large prompt or the inclusion of irrelevant noise. Furthermore, the model is instructed to refer back to the feature types defined in the basic information description. This ensures that the rules are logically sound; for instance, it prevents the model from applying a "greater than" operator to a categorical feature.

These rules act as the "criteria" that the LLM believes are most important for identifying a class. By synthesizing general knowledge and specific data, the LLM generates rules that are more robust and generalizable than those derived from data alone, particularly when the number of labeled samples is very small.

# Response Instructions and Formatting for Parsability

The final challenge in the rule extraction process is the transition from natural language to a format that can be used by a machine learning model. Because LLMs generate text, the rules they produce are inherently linguistic and can be syntactically inconsistent. For example, one rule might use the symbol "$>$" while another might use the phrase "greater than."

To mitigate this, \textsf{FeatLLM} includes specific response instructions in the prompt. These guidelines dictate the required format for the response of each class and the structure each individual rule should follow. This guidance helps the LLM produce output that is more consistent and easier to parse. However, even with strict instructions, LLMs can introduce noisy syntactic changes that make traditional programmatic parsing difficult.

To solve this, the framework leverages the LLM once more. Instead of building a complex, rigid parser, the system provides the LLM with the function name, input/output descriptions, and the generated natural language rules. The LLM is then asked to generate a simple piece of Python code that implements these rules. This generated code is executed using Python's \texttt{exec()} function to transform the original tabular data into binary features. Each binary feature indicates whether a sample satisfies a specific rule (1 for yes, 0 for no), effectively converting the LLM's linguistic reasoning into a numerical format suitable for a downstream linear model.

Translating Natural Language Rules into Features

# The Parsing Challenge: Dealing with Syntactic Noise

The primary hurdle in transitioning from the conceptual reasoning of a Large Language Model (LLM) to a functional machine learning model is the inherent variability of natural language. In the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework, the LLM is tasked with generating rules—specific conditions that indicate whether a sample belongs to a particular class. While these rules are conceptually simple, they are produced as text, which creates a significant gap between a human-readable insight and a machine-executable operation.

The core of this problem lies in "syntactic noise." Even when an LLM is provided with strict instructions on how to format its responses, it may introduce subtle variations in how it expresses a condition. For example, a rule intended to identify a numerical threshold might be written as "$>$ 50" in one instance and as "greater than 50" in another. Such inconsistencies make it nearly impossible to use traditional, rigid parsing techniques—like regular expressions or hard-coded string matching—because the programmer cannot anticipate every possible linguistic variation the LLM might employ. If the parsing mechanism is too rigid, it will fail to recognize valid rules; if it is too loose, it may misinterpret the logic of the rule entirely.

# Leveraging LLMs for Code Generation

To overcome the fragility of manual parsing, \textsf{FeatLLM} adopts an intuitive and innovative strategy: it leverages the LLM to parse itself. Since LLMs are trained on massive corpora of both natural language and programming code, they possess a unique ability to understand the semantic intent behind a piece of text and translate that intent into a precise functional implementation. Instead of attempting to build a complex program to decode the rules, the framework asks the LLM to generate the Python code required to execute those rules.

This process is managed through a specialized prompt. The LLM is provided with the function name, clear descriptions of the expected inputs and outputs, and the specific natural language rules it had previously inferred. By framing the problem as a code-generation task, the framework shifts the burden of interpretation back to the model. The LLM identifies the semantic meaning of phrases like "less than" or "is equal to" and converts them into the appropriate Python operators. This ensures that the original intent of the feature engineering process is preserved, effectively bridging the gap between high-level reasoning and low-level data processing.

# Implementing Rule Execution via Python's exec()

Once the LLM has generated the corresponding Python code for a set of rules, that code must be applied to the actual tabular dataset to transform the raw values into usable features. The framework achieves this by using Python's \texttt{exec()} function. The \texttt{exec()} function allows the system to take a string containing Python code—the output from the LLM—and execute it dynamically within the current environment.

In practical terms, the generated code acts as a transformation function. It takes the original samples, evaluates them against the logic defined in the generated code, and outputs the result. This approach removes the need for the researchers to manually write a parser for every possible rule format. By executing the code directly, \textsf{FeatLLM} can rapidly convert large amounts of tabular data into a new format based on the "criteria" the LLM determined were most relevant to the task, ensuring that the transition from text to data is both automated and accurate.

# Creating Binary Feature Vectors (The Rule Matrix)

The ultimate output of the rule execution process is the creation of a new set of features known as binary feature vectors. In a standard tabular dataset, a sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ is represented by a $d$-dimensional vector of various numerical or categorical values. \textsf{FeatLLM} transforms this representation. For each class $k$ in the classification task, the framework applies a set of $R$ rules (where $R$ is typically set to 10). 

For every sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ and every rule associated with class $k$, the system determines whether the sample satisfies the rule. This results in a binary value: '1' if the rule is satisfied and '0' if it is not. These values are aggregated into a binary feature vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0, 1\}^R$. Consequently, the original complex data is mapped into a "rule matrix" where each column represents the adherence to a specific LLM-generated criterion. This transformation simplifies the downstream learning task, as the model no longer needs to navigate the original raw feature space but instead works with a refined set of indicators that the LLM has already flagged as potentially decisive.

# Mapping Natural Language Conditions to Logical Operators

The mapping of natural language to logical operators is the critical link that allows \textsf{FeatLLM} to utilize an LLM's prior knowledge. When the LLM generates rules, it is guided by the feature descriptions provided in the prompt, such as the value type of each attribute. By referring to whether a feature is categorical or numerical, the LLM can select the appropriate logical operator—such as equality for categories or inequality for numbers—to avoid type mismatches in the resulting code.

These logical mappings are then used by a downstream linear model to estimate class likelihood. The binary vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ is combined with a set of trainable weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ through a dot product. To ensure that the rules generated by the LLM only contribute positively to the likelihood of a class, the framework projects these weights into a positive subspace using the operation $\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$. The final probability for each class $\mathbf{p}^i$ is then computed using a softmax function:

$$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$$
$$\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}({[\text{logit}_{1}^i, ..., \text{logit}_{c}^i]})$$

Through this process, the framework transforms a subjective natural language "rule" into a rigorous mathematical contribution to a class probability, allowing the system to learn the importance of each LLM-generated feature based on the provided few-shot training samples.

The Downstream Predictive Model

# Linear Models for Binary Feature Sets

Once the $\textsf{FeatLLM}$ framework has successfully utilized a Large Language Model (LLM) to engineer a set of rules, the data is transformed from its original tabular format into a new, simplified representation. In this stage, the complex original features are replaced by binary features. For any given sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ and a target class $k$, the framework generates a binary feature vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0, 1\}^R$, where $R$ represents the number of rules extracted for that specific class. Each element in this vector is a binary indicator: it is assigned a value of $1$ if the sample adheres to the corresponding rule and $0$ otherwise.

This transformation effectively shifts the predictive task from the original feature space to a binary rule-based space. To estimate the likelihood that a sample belongs to a particular class, the framework employs a simple downstream machine learning model. Specifically, it uses a linear model without a bias term. By operating on binary sets, the model avoids the need for a complex, end-to-end LLM inference for every single sample at test time, significantly reducing latency and computational overhead while still leveraging the prior knowledge encapsulated in the LLM-generated rules.

# Learning Feature Importance via Trainable Weights

While a simple summation of satisfied rules could provide a baseline for class likelihood, this approach assumes that every rule carries equal weight. In reality, some rules are more decisive or informative than others. To account for this, $\textsf{FeatLLM}$ introduces trainable weights, denoted as $\mathbf{w}_k$, for each class. These weights signify the relative importance of each binary feature generated by the LLM.

The model computes a raw score, known as a logit, by calculating the dot product between the trainable weights and the binary feature vector. This process allows the model to learn from the few-shot training samples which rules are most indicative of the target class and which are less relevant. By adjusting these weights during the training process, the model can prioritize high-quality rules and diminish the influence of rules that do not correlate well with the ground-truth labels.

# The Necessity of Non-Negative Weights

A critical design choice in the $\textsf{FeatLLM}$ predictive model is the enforcement of non-negative weights. In the computation of the logit for class $k$, the weight vector $\mathbf{w}_k$ is projected into a positive subspace using the operation $\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$. The resulting logit for a sample $i$ is calculated as $\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$.

The intuition behind this constraint is rooted in the nature of the feature engineering process. Because the LLM is specifically prompted to generate rules that identify whether a sample belongs to a certain class, these rules are intended to be positive indicators. If the weights were allowed to be negative, the model might learn that the satisfaction of a rule actually reduces the likelihood of the class, which would contradict the semantic purpose of the engineered features. By ensuring that weights are non-negative, the framework guarantees that any rule satisfied by a sample can only maintain or increase the likelihood of that sample belonging to the associated class.

# Computing Class Probabilities with the Softmax Function

After computing the logits for all possible classes, the model must convert these raw scores into a usable probability distribution. For a classification task with $c$ number of classes, the model generates a logit vector consisting of $[\text{logit}_{1}^i, ..., \text{logit}_{c}^i]$. To transform these logits into class probabilities $\mathbf{p}^i$, the framework applies the Softmax function.

The Softmax function normalizes the logits such that each resulting value is between $0$ and $1$, and the sum of all class probabilities for a single sample equals $1$. This allows the model to provide a probabilistic estimate of class membership, which is essential for evaluating performance using metrics such as the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC). The final prediction is based on the probability distribution derived from the weighted sum of the binary rules.

# Optimizing the Model using Cross-Entropy Loss in Few-Shot Regimes

To determine the optimal values for the weights $\mathbf{w}_k$, the model is trained using a small number of labeled samples, characteristic of a few-shot learning regime. The optimization process aims to minimize the cross-entropy loss, which measures the divergence between the predicted class probabilities $\mathbf{p}^i$ and the actual ground-truth labels.

The training is implemented using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of $0.01$, running for $200$ epochs. To ensure the model generalizes well and does not overfit the limited training data, $k$-fold cross-validation is employed to select the optimal epoch. This rigorous optimization allows the model to effectively filter out uninformative rules—those whose learned weights fall below a certain threshold—while retaining the most predictive features engineered by the LLM.

Scaling and Robustness: Bagging and Ensembling

# The Role of Randomness: Temperature and Demonstration Ordering

To achieve a robust system for feature engineering, the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework intentionally introduces randomness into the process of rule generation. In a deterministic setting, a Large Language Model (LLM) might consistently produce the same set of rules for a given prompt, which limits the system's ability to explore different potential interpretations of the data. To counter this, \textsf{FeatLLM} employs a sufficiently high temperature during LLM inference. Temperature is a hyperparameter that controls the stochasticity of the model's output; by increasing it, the model is encouraged to generate a more diverse set of responses across different trials.

Beyond adjusting the temperature, the framework also randomizes the order of the few-shot demonstrations provided in the prompt. This is a critical design choice because the sequence in which examples are presented can significantly influence the final query outcome of an LLM. By shuffling the order of these demonstrations, the framework ensures that the model does not become biased toward a specific pattern of examples. Together, the use of temperature and demonstration randomization creates the necessary diversity in the generated rules, which is the foundation for the subsequent ensemble process.

# Feature Bagging: Overcoming Prompt Size Constraints

A significant challenge in applying LLMs to tabular learning is the limitation of the input prompt size, often referred to as the context window. When working with tabular datasets that contain a large number of features—such as the 'Communities' or 'Myocardial' datasets, which include over 100 attributes—serializing all features into text can lead to prompts that are excessively long. Such lengthy prompts can make the approach infeasible or lead to a decline in the model's performance.

To resolve this, \textsf{FeatLLM} adopts a technique known as bagging. In this context, bagging is used to select a subset of features or instances for each individual trial. By limiting the amount of data passed to the LLM in a single query, the framework ensures that the prompt remains within a manageable size while still capturing a portion of the available information. While a single rule set generated from a subset of features might not capture every relationship within the data, the use of bagging allows the system to explore different combinations of features across multiple trials, thereby mitigating the constraints of the LLM's prompt size.

# The Ensemble Process: Averaging Probabilities across Multiple Trials

The core of the \textsf{FeatLLM} inference strategy is an ensemble approach that combines the results of multiple independent trials. The process begins by repeating the entire rule extraction and weight tuning cycle $T$ times. For each trial $t$, the framework generates a set of rules, converts them into binary features, and trains a simple linear model to determine the importance of those features. This results in a collection of trained weights $\{\mathbf{w}[t]\}_{t=1}^T$.

To make a final prediction for a sample $\mathbf{x}^i$, the framework computes the class probability $\mathbf{p}^i$ for each trial using the learned weights. Specifically, for a class $k$, the logit $\text{logit}_k^i$ is calculated as the dot product of the non-negative weights and the binary feature vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ associated with that class: $\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$. The projection into a positive subspace via the $\max$ operation ensures that the generated features do not reduce the likelihood of a class. These logits are then converted into probabilities using the softmax function: $\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}({[\text{logit}_{1}^i, ..., \text{logit}_{c}^i]})$. The final prediction is achieved by averaging the probability $\mathbf{p}^i$ across all $T$ trials.

# Self-Consistency: Why Repeated Inferences Lead to Better Rules

The use of an ensemble of various "weak learners" (individual rule sets) enhances the overall robustness of the framework through a concept known as self-consistency. Because LLMs are prone to occasional errors or the generation of irrelevant rules, relying on a single set of features is risky. However, by executing the process multiple times, the framework can compensate for inaccuracies. If the LLM generates an incorrect or noisy rule in one trial, that error is dampened by the correct rules generated in other trials.

Rules that are consistently inferred across multiple trials are more likely to be accurate and based on genuine causal relationships rather than spurious correlations. This "wisdom of the crowd" effect allows the ensemble to nullify the impact of incomplete feature or instance coverage that might occur in any single trial, particularly when bagging is used. By aggregating these diverse perspectives, \textsf{FeatLLM} transforms a potentially noisy rule-generation process into a stable and high-performing predictive model.

# Trade-offs Between the Number of Ensembles and Performance

The performance of \textsf{FeatLLM} is influenced by two primary hyperparameters: the number of ensembles $T$ and the number of rules extracted per class. Analysis shows that as the number of ensembles increases, the predictive performance generally improves. However, there is a point of diminishing returns; the performance gains begin to converge around $T=20$, suggesting that beyond this point, additional trials provide marginal benefit.

Similarly, there is a trade-off involved in deciding how many rules to extract per class. While it might seem that more rules would provide more information, this increases the input dimensionality for the downstream linear model. In low-shot regimes, where only a few labeled samples are available, having too many rules can lead to overfitting. The framework finds that extracting 10 rules per class provides an optimal balance, keeping the prompt length manageable while providing enough information to maintain high performance without succumbing to the risks of overfitting.

Evaluating FeatLLM: Experimental Results

# Dataset Selection: From Real-World to Synthetic Data

To rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework, the researchers employed a diverse set of 13 tabular datasets. These datasets were selected to cover a wide range of binary and multi-class classification tasks across various domains, ensuring that the framework's performance was not limited to a specific type of data. The real-world datasets include common benchmarks such as Adult (predicting annual earnings over \$50,000), Bank (predicting term deposit subscriptions), Blood (predicting donor return), and Car (predicting car quality). Other health-related datasets include Diabetes, Heart (predicting coronary artery disease), and Myocardial (predicting chronic heart failure). Additionally, the set includes Communities for crime rate prediction and Credit-g for assessing credit risk.

A critical aspect of the dataset selection process was the inclusion of data that the Large Language Model (LLM) could not have "memorized" during its pre-training phase. To ensure the results reflected the model's ability to reason and engineer features rather than simply recall training data, the researchers included datasets donated to the UCI Machine Learning Repository after September 2023, such as Cultivars (predicting soybean grain yield) and NHANES (predicting age groups). Furthermore, two entirely synthetic datasets were created: Sequence-type (classifying arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, or Collatz sequences) and Solution-mix (predicting whether a mixture concentration exceeds 0.5).

The chosen datasets also varied significantly in complexity and dimensionality. While some were relatively simple, others, such as the Communities and Myocardial datasets, contained over 100 attributes. This high dimensionality is particularly important for testing the limitations of LLMs, as the large number of features can lead to excessively long prompts that may exceed the context window limits of the model or cause a decline in the quality of the generated output.

# Performance Metrics: Understanding AUC

The primary metric used to evaluate the performance of \textsf{FeatLLM} and its competitors is the Area Under the ROC Curve, commonly referred to as AUC. In the context of this research, AUC serves as a measure of the model's ability to distinguish between different classes. For each dataset, the experiments were repeated three times with different random selections of training and test sets to ensure statistical reliability, and the results are presented as the average AUC and its standard deviation.

The utility of AUC extends beyond the final model prediction. The researchers also used AUROC to analyze the quality of the individual rules generated by the LLM before they were integrated into the final predictive model. By calculating the AUROC between a newly generated binary feature (based on a rule) and the target label, the researchers could determine how informative a specific rule was. A rule is considered "useful" if it achieves an AUROC higher than 0.5, indicating that the rule provides a signal that is better than random guessing in predicting the target class.

# Comparative Analysis: FeatLLM vs. TabLLM, TabPFN, and XGBoost

The performance of \textsf{FeatLLM} was compared against nine distinct baselines, which can be categorized into three groups: traditional machine learning, specialized few-shot learners, and other LLM-based approaches. Traditional baselines included Logistic Regression (LogReg), XGBoost, and RandomForest. Specialized learners included SCARF and STUNT, which leverage unlabeled data, and TabPFN, which uses a pre-trained Bayesian neural network trained on synthetic data. Finally, LLM-based baselines included standard In-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM, the latter of which utilizes parameter-efficient tuning via IA3.

The results demonstrate that \textsf{FeatLLM} consistently ranks as the top or second-best performer across the majority of the 13 datasets. A particularly striking finding is the framework's efficiency in low-shot regimes. For instance, \textsf{FeatLLM} utilizing only 4 labeled samples (4-shot) achieved performance comparable to traditional machine learning baselines that required 32 samples. This highlights the framework's ability to substitute a large volume of labeled data with the rich prior knowledge encapsulated within the LLM.

Furthermore, \textsf{FeatLLM} showed significant robustness where other LLM-based methods failed. While models like TabLLM, TABLET, and basic In-context learning struggled with datasets containing a large number of features, \textsf{FeatLLM} maintained satisfactory performance. This is attributed to the use of bagging and ensemble techniques, which mitigate the prompt size limitations and the risk of the model focusing on irrelevant features. When faced with "spurious correlations"—irrelevant columns added to the dataset—\textsf{FeatLLM} experienced the least performance degradation compared to other baselines, as its reasoning process forces a connection between the task description and the features based on prior knowledge.

# Analysis of Training and Inference Time/Cost

One of the primary motivations for \textsf{FeatLLM} was to reduce the computational burden associated with using LLMs for tabular learning. Traditional LLM-based approaches are often "black-box" systems that require at least one LLM inference per sample at the time of prediction. This makes them computationally expensive and slow for real-time applications.

In a comparison using the Adult dataset with 16 training shots, \textsf{FeatLLM} demonstrated an inference time comparable to traditional machine learning methods. This is because the LLM is only used during the feature engineering phase to generate rules; once those rules are converted into binary features, the actual prediction is performed by a simple linear model. In contrast, In-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM exhibited significantly higher inference times because they require the LLM to process every single test sample.

Regarding training costs, \textsf{FeatLLM} is comparable to other LLM-based methods. It requires a small number of API queries (approximately 30) to generate the necessary rules, which is a relatively low budget burden and can be easily parallelized. Notably, while TabLLM requires significant hardware resources (such as four A100 GPUs) for model parallelism, \textsf{FeatLLM} operates efficiently on a single A100 GPU.

# Evaluating Feature Quality: The AUROC of Generated Rules

To understand how the framework actually improves prediction, the researchers investigated the informativeness of the rules generated by the LLM. By measuring the average AUROC between the generated binary features and the target labels, they analyzed the "quality" of these features both before and after the weight tuning process.

The initial analysis, labeled "Before tuning," revealed that a majority of the rules created by the LLM were indeed relevant to the target attribute. This suggests that the two-step reasoning process—first inferring causal relationships using prior knowledge and then deducing rules from examples—successfully filters out uninformative features and extracts high-quality rules.

The effectiveness of the framework is further enhanced by the weight tuning process of the downstream linear model. The model calculates the probability of a class using the formula:
$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$
where $\mathbf{w}_k$ represents the trainable weight (importance) of each rule and $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ is the binary feature vector. By analyzing these weights, the researchers found that rules with little relevance to the data were automatically filtered out; specifically, rules with learned weights below a threshold of 0.1 were discarded. This "After tuning" analysis confirmed that the linear model effectively prunes the LLM's output, keeping only the most predictive rules and further improving the overall robustness of the classification.

Advanced Analysis and Critical Discussion

# Ablation Studies: Evaluating the Impact of Reasoning and Tuning

To understand the specific contributions of the various components within the $\textsf{FeatLLM}$ framework, it is essential to perform ablation studies. An ablation study involves systematically removing or altering a specific part of the model to observe how the overall performance changes. In the case of $\textsf{FeatLLM}$, four primary components were analyzed: the weight tuning process, the ensemble mechanism, the inclusion of feature descriptions, and the structured reasoning instructions. The results of these studies indicate that the removal of any of these components leads to a degradation in the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), confirming that each part plays a vital role in the framework's success.

The tuning process involves using a linear model to learn the importance of the binary features generated by the LLM. When this process is omitted—a condition referred to as $\textsf{-Tuning}$—the model simply sums the binary features to compute the logit. The analysis reveals that the benefit of weight tuning becomes more pronounced as the number of few-shot samples increases. Intuitively, with more data, the model can more accurately estimate the specific importance of each rule, allowing the linear model to distinguish between highly informative rules and less relevant ones. When data is extremely scarce, the relative importance of each rule is harder to determine, but as the sample size grows, the ability to tune weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ becomes a significant performance driver.

Conversely, the impact of feature descriptions ($\textsf{-Description}$) and the first step of the reasoning process ($\textsf{-Reasoning}$) is most significant in very low-shot regimes. The reasoning instruction specifically guides the LLM to first infer the causal relationship between features and the target task based on general knowledge before looking at the provided examples. When this step is removed, the model's performance drops, especially when few examples are available. This suggests that the prior knowledge encapsulated within the LLM acts as a crucial supplement when the available data is insufficient to establish clear patterns. By utilizing a structured reasoning path, the LLM can leverage its internal "common sense" to focus on significant features, effectively filling the gap left by a lack of training samples.

Finally, the ensemble approach, which utilizes bagging and averaging across multiple trials, consistently improves performance across all settings. Because LLM outputs can be stochastic, relying on a single set of rules can be risky. The ensemble process allows the framework to compensate for incorrect rules generated in some trials with correct rules from others. This creates a system of self-consistency where rules that are repeatedly inferred across different trials are more likely to be accurate and robust.

# Handling Spurious Correlations: Prior Knowledge as a Filter

A common challenge in few-shot tabular learning is the risk of the model learning spurious correlations—patterns that appear to be predictive in a small sample but do not hold true in the broader population. To test the robustness of $\textsf{FeatLLM}$ against such noise, researchers conducted an experiment using the Heart dataset. In this setup, irrelevant columns from the Adult dataset were randomly attached to the Heart dataset, effectively introducing "noise" that had no actual relationship with the target labels of the heart disease task.

The results demonstrated that $\textsf{FeatLLM}$ experienced the least performance degradation compared to other baseline models as the number of irrelevant columns increased. The intuition behind this robustness lies in the framework's rule-extraction process. Unlike standard models that might find a random mathematical correlation between a noisy column and a label in a small dataset, $\textsf{FeatLLM}$ requires the LLM to first consider the relationship between the task and the features based on its prior knowledge. 

Because the LLM is asked to reason about the "criteria" of the problem (e.g., why certain medical indicators lead to heart failure) before extracting rules from the data, it is less likely to be fooled by irrelevant columns. The LLM recognizes that a column from the Adult dataset (such as annual income) has no logical or causal connection to a medical diagnosis of heart failure. Consequently, the framework generates very few rules based on these noisy columns—accounting for only 1\% to 2\% of the total rules. This demonstrates that prior knowledge acts as a semantic filter, preventing the model from overfitting to irrelevant patterns that often plague small-scale tabular datasets. However, it is noted that if the attached noisy columns are from a closely related domain, the LLM may still be susceptible to learning spurious correlations.

# Sensitivity to Hyper-parameters (Number of Rules and Ensembles)

The performance of $\textsf{FeatLLM}$ is influenced by two primary hyper-parameters: the number of ensembles and the number of rules extracted per class during each LLM query. Understanding the relationship between these parameters and the resulting accuracy is key to optimizing the framework for different tasks.

The number of ensembles refers to how many times the entire process of rule generation and linear model training is repeated. Experimental evidence shows that as the number of ensembles increases, the prediction performance generally improves. However, this improvement follows a trend of diminishing returns; the performance gains begin to converge around 20 ensembles. At this point, the diversity provided by bagging and the self-consistency of the rules have largely been captured, and adding further ensembles does not yield significant additional accuracy.

The number of rules per class presents a different trade-off. While increasing the number of rules potentially provides more information and a higher-dimensional feature space for the linear model to utilize, it also increases the length of the input prompt. If a prompt becomes too long, the LLM may experience a decline in performance or hit context window limits. Furthermore, in a low-shot regime, having too many rules can lead to overfitting, where the model creates overly specific rules that do not generalize to the test set. After analyzing the trade-off between prompt length and performance, a fixed number of 10 rules per class was determined to be the most appropriate. This balance ensures the model has enough information to capture the underlying patterns of the class without introducing excessive noise or exceeding the LLM's processing capabilities.

# Broader Impacts: Ethical Considerations and Societal Biases in Prior Knowledge

The $\textsf{FeatLLM}$ framework has significant implications for real-world applications, particularly in fields where data labeling is expensive or difficult, such as healthcare and finance. In these domains, the cost of human expertise required to label thousands of samples is prohibitive. By leveraging the vast prior knowledge embedded in LLMs—which have been pretrained on massive corpora of medical and financial texts—$\textsf{FeatLLM}$ can achieve high accuracy with only a handful of labeled examples.

However, the reliance on an LLM's prior knowledge introduces critical ethical considerations. Because the LLM's internal knowledge is derived from a diverse and potentially biased set of internet text, the "rules" it generates may reflect societal biases or misinformation present in its training data. Since the framework allows the LLM's prior knowledge to guide the feature engineering process, these biases can directly influence, or even dominate, the final predictions.

For example, if an LLM has internalized a bias regarding a specific demographic's likelihood of having a certain disease or credit risk, it may generate rules that reinforce this bias regardless of the actual evidence provided in the few-shot samples. This makes the analysis of societal biases and misinformation essential for the widespread adoption of the framework. Ensuring that the prior knowledge used to augment data-efficient learning does not perpetuate harmful stereotypes is a necessary step for the responsible deployment of LLM-based tabular learning.

# Future Directions: Interpretability and Larger-Scale Tabular Learning

While $\textsf{FeatLLM}$ demonstrates strong performance in low-shot regimes, the researchers have identified several avenues for future development to expand its utility. Currently, the framework is specifically designed for situations where labeled data is scarce. A primary goal for future work is to adapt and expand these capabilities to datasets with a much larger number of samples, determining how the interplay between LLM-generated rules and large-scale empirical data can be optimized.

Beyond the current focus on binary rules (where a sample either satisfies a rule or it does not), there is an opportunity to explore more complex feature types. Moving beyond simple binary indicators could allow the model to capture more nuanced relationships within the data, potentially increasing the precision of the downstream predictive model.

Furthermore, increasing the interpretability of the system is a key objective. Because $\textsf{FeatLLM}$ transforms the "black-box" nature of LLM predictions into a set of explicit, executable rules and a linear model with trainable weights $\mathbf{w}_k$, it is already more transparent than end-to-end LLM inference. By further refining how these rules are presented and how the weights are analyzed, the framework could provide practitioners with clear, human-readable insights into why a specific prediction was made. This level of interpretability is particularly crucial in high-stakes environments like clinical diagnostics or financial auditing, where understanding the "why" is as important as the prediction itself.
