\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### In the progressive extension strategy, why is a second search used instead of further fine-tuning?
Question:
I'm confused about the transition from 256k to 2048k. The paper says they fine-tune the model to 256k, but then they use a 'secondary search' to reach 2048k without any further fine-tuning. If fine-tuning was necessary to move from 4k to 256k, why is it not necessary to move from 256k to 2048k? Is the non-uniform interpolation search powerful enough to maintain model coherence at 2 million tokens without updating the actual weights of the LLM?
Answer:
The reason a second search is used instead of further fine-tuning to reach $2048\text{k}$ tokens is primarily due to the prohibitive computational costs and data scarcity associated with extremely long sequences. As the paper explains, fine-tuning a model on sequences of $2048\text{k}$ tokens would require immense GPU resources and an abundance of texts of that length, which are rarely available. Furthermore, the authors note that fine-tuning becomes increasingly unstable and difficult to converge when the extension ratio is too large (in this case, a $512\times$ extension from the original $4\text{k}$ window).

The transition from $256\text{k}$ to $2048\text{k}$ is possible without further fine-tuning because of a specific empirical finding: the non-uniform positional interpolation search is capable of handling up to an $8\times$ extension in non-fine-tuning scenarios. Because the ratio between the fine-tuned length ($256\text{k}$) and the target length ($2048\text{k}$) is exactly $8$, the "secondary search" can identify a set of optimal rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and token position thresholds $\hat{n}$ that maintain model coherence without needing to update the actual weights of the LLM.

Intuitively, the first stage of fine-tuning (up to $256\text{k}$) serves to "stretch" the model's capability and adapt its weights to handle longer-range dependencies. Once the model is robust at $256\text{k}$, the non-uniform search acts as a sophisticated "calibration" of the positional embeddings. By exploiting non-uniformities across RoPE dimensions and token positions—rather than using a simple linear scale like Position Interpolation (PI)—the search minimizes information loss and prevents the "crowding" of positional embeddings. This allows the model to retain its ability to distinguish between tokens even at $2\text{M}$ tokens, provided the extension ratio remains within that $8\times$ "safe" zone discovered by the authors.
