\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 4: # The Theory of Non-Uniformity in Interpolation

To understand how large language models (LLMs) can be extended to handle millions of tokens, one must first understand the mechanism of positional embeddings. Transformer models are inherently permutation-invariant, meaning they do not naturally understand the order of tokens in a sequence. To resolve this, models like LLaMA2 and Mistral utilize Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE). In a simplified form, for a token at position index $n$, the RoPE encoding consists of a series of cosine and sine pairs: $\left[ \cos(n\theta_0), \sin(n\theta_0), \cos(n\theta_1), \dots, \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}), \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1}) \right]$, where $d$ is the embedding dimension and $\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$ represents the rotation frequencies.

When the goal is to extend the context window from an original length $L$ to a larger length $L'$, we define the extension ratio as $s = \frac{L'}{L}$. The most straightforward approach to this extension is Position Interpolation (PI), which linearly reduces the rotation angles across all dimensions by a factor of $\lambda = s$. However, this linear approach creates a "crowding" effect where position information becomes too dense, hindering the model's ability to distinguish between tokens that are close to one another. This suggests that the most effective way to extend a context window is not through uniform scaling, but by exploiting the non-uniformities inherent in how the model processes positional information.

# Non-uniformity across RoPE dimensions: High vs. Low frequency

The first major insight into non-uniformity concerns the different dimensions of the RoPE embedding. RoPE dimensions are not created equal; they operate at different frequencies. Lower dimensions (where $i$ is small) correspond to higher frequencies, while higher dimensions (where $i$ is large) correspond to lower frequencies. Previous attempts to handle this, such as NTK-based interpolation, suggested that interpolation pressure should be distributed unevenly across these dimensions. Specifically, high-frequency dimensions should be scaled less, and low-frequency dimensions should be scaled more.

Linear Position Interpolation (PI) fails because it applies the same rescale factor $\lambda = s$ to every dimension, regardless of its frequency. More advanced methods like YaRN attempted to address this by grouping dimensions into three categories—extrapolation, NTK-based interpolation, and linear interpolation—based on their frequency. However, these groupings are often based on human-led empirical experiments and may be sub-optimal. The theory of non-uniformity posits that there is a more precise, non-linear relationship between the dimension index $i$ and the optimal rescale factor $\lambda_i$. By allowing each dimension to have its own specific rescale factor, the model can better preserve the original structural information of the RoPE embedding, which is critical for maintaining performance during context extension.

# Non-uniformity across token positions: The importance of initial tokens

Beyond the variance across dimensions, there is a significant non-uniformity across the positions of the tokens within a sequence. It is hypothesized that the initial tokens in an input sequence are more critical to the attention mechanism than tokens appearing later in the sequence. This is consistent with observations in other architectural studies, such as Streaming LLM, which suggest that the "attention sinks" at the beginning of a sequence receive disproportionately large attention scores.

Because these initial tokens are so vital, applying positional interpolation to them can be detrimental. If the first few tokens are interpolated (downscaled), the model may lose the precise positional grounding it relies on to stabilize the attention layers. To mitigate this, LongRoPE introduces a token position threshold, denoted as $\hat{n}$. For tokens at positions $n < \hat{n}$, the original RoPE rotary angles are used without any interpolation. For tokens at positions $n \ge \hat{n}$, the searched rescale factors are applied. This ensures that the most important part of the sequence—the beginning—remains untouched by the interpolation process, thereby reducing the loss of information and improving the model's stability.

# Empirical evidence for non-uniformity in LLaMA2

The effectiveness of non-uniform interpolation is not merely theoretical; it has been demonstrated through empirical analysis using LLaMA2-7B. By using an evolutionary search algorithm to find the optimal rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and the optimal threshold $\hat{n}$, researchers compared the resulting perplexity on datasets like PG19 and Proof-pile against standard methods like PI and YaRN.

The evidence shows that when non-uniform factors are used, the model's perplexity is significantly lower, meaning the model is much better at predicting the next token in a long sequence. For instance, without any fine-tuning, the non-uniform approach allowed LLaMA2 to extend its context window from 4k to 32k while maintaining stability. In contrast, traditional methods like PI and YaRN saw perplexity spike rapidly after only a $2\times$ extension. This empirical gap proves that the "human-designed rules" used in previous interpolation methods are sub-optimal and that a data-driven, non-uniform search can uncover a far more effective configuration for the positional embeddings.

# Information entropy in positional embeddings

The core reason why non-uniformity works lies in the complex non-uniform information entropy of the Transformer architecture. Positional embeddings are not just markers of order; they are high-dimensional vectors that the model uses to determine the relationship between tokens. When we use uniform linear interpolation, we essentially "compress" the positional space. This compression leads to a loss of information entropy, as the distinct "signatures" of different positions start to overlap or become too similar.

By utilizing non-uniform positional interpolation, the model can preserve "key dimensions" and "key positions." By scaling different dimensions differently, the model retains a higher degree of entropy in the dimensions that are most critical for distinguishing token positions. This minimizes the information loss caused by the extension process. This preservation of information is particularly crucial for the initialization of fine-tuning; because the non-uniform initialization is closer to the original pre-trained distribution than a linear interpolation, the model converges more easily and avoids the "catastrophic values" that typically occur when a model encounters previously unseen position indices.

# Hypothesizing the optimal rescale factors

To find the optimal non-uniform configuration, the problem is framed as a search for the best set of rescale factors $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i}, \hat{n})$. The rescale factor for the $i^{th}$ dimension is defined as $1$ if $n < \hat{n}$, and $1/\lambda_i$ if $n \ge \hat{n}$. The search space for $\lambda_i$ ranges from $1.0$ (which represents direct extrapolation) to $s \times 1.25$ (which is a slightly more aggressive interpolation than PI). The threshold $\hat{n}$ is searched across a discrete set of values, such as $\{0, 1, 2, \dots, 256\}$.

A key hypothesis guiding this search is the monotonically non-decreasing constraint: $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$. This constraint is grounded in NTK theory, which suggests that lower dimensions (high frequency) require less interpolation (smaller $\lambda_i$) and higher dimensions (low frequency) can tolerate more interpolation (larger $\lambda_i$). By imposing this constraint, the search algorithm can avoid poor solutions and efficiently narrow down the parameters that minimize next-token prediction loss. The resulting set of factors allows the model to stretch its context window to an extreme degree—up to 2048k tokens—without collapsing the positional information that the model learned during its initial training.
