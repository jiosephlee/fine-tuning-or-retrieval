\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 3: Existing Positional Interpolation Techniques

To understand the mechanisms used to extend the context window of Large Language Models (LLMs), it is first necessary to understand the baseline technology: Rotary Positional Embedding, or RoPE. In a Transformer architecture, models require explicit positional information to understand the order of input tokens. RoPE achieves this by representing a token at position index $n$ as a series of rotary angles. Specifically, the encoding is a vector consisting of $\cos(n\theta_0), \sin(n\theta_0), \dots, \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}), \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})$, where $d$ is the embedding dimension and $\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$ represents the rotation frequencies. In most standard implementations, the default base value of $\theta$ is $10000$. When we wish to extend the context window from an original length $L$ to a target length $L'$, we define the context window extension ratio as $s = \frac{L'}{L}$.

# Linear Position Interpolation (PI)

Linear Position Interpolation, commonly referred to as PI, is one of the foundational methods for extending context windows. The core intuition behind PI is to downscale the position indices of new tokens so that they fall within the range that the model was originally trained on. To achieve this for a target extension ratio $s$, PI linearly reduces the rotation angles of all positions by a rescale factor $\lambda = s$ across every single RoPE dimension.

While this approach allows the model to process sequences longer than its original training limit, it introduces a significant drawback. By applying the same reduction factor to every dimension, the positional information becomes overly "crowded." This means that the model's ability to distinguish between tokens that are located close to one another is diminished, as their rotary embeddings become too similar. Consequently, PI tends to underperform when the extension ratio $s$ is high, and it generally necessitates further fine-tuning to remain effective.

# NTK-aware Interpolation and Extrapolation

To address the limitations of linear interpolation, researchers developed NTK-aware methods based on Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory. The fundamental intuition of NTK-aware interpolation is that not all RoPE dimensions should be treated equally. Instead of applying a uniform scale across the entire embedding, NTK-aware methods distribute the "interpolation pressure" across different dimensions based on their frequency.

Specifically, these methods scale the lower dimensions—which correspond to high frequencies—less, while scaling the higher dimensions—which correspond to low frequencies—more. This results in a hybrid approach that combines both positional interpolation and extrapolation, where the rescale factor is defined as $\lambda = s^i$. A further refinement, known as dynamic NTK, allows the model to adjust the extension ratio at each position based on the current length of the sequence. One of the primary advantages of NTK-aware methods is that they can extend the context window in non-fine-tuning scenarios, although they are typically limited to a maximum extension ratio of approximately $4\times$.

# YaRN (Yet Another RoPE extensioN) and frequency-based grouping

YaRN further evolves the concept of non-linear interpolation by introducing frequency-based grouping. Rather than using a single formula for all dimensions, YaRN categorizes RoPE dimensions into three distinct groups based on their frequency, applying a different strategy to each. High-frequency dimensions undergo extrapolation, meaning $\lambda = 1$ and the original frequencies are preserved. Low-frequency dimensions use linear interpolation, as seen in PI. The dimensions that fall between these two extremes employ the NTK-aware approach.

By partitioning the dimensions this way, YaRN attempts to balance the need for precise local positioning (high frequency) with the need to accommodate a much larger global context (low frequency). However, a significant limitation of YaRN is that the specific grouping of these dimensions is based on human-led empirical experiments. This dependency on manual design means the strategy may be sub-optimal when applied to different or newer LLM architectures, as the optimal grouping may vary.

# Comparative analysis: Linear vs. Non-linear interpolation

When comparing linear and non-linear interpolation, the primary difference lies in how they handle the relationship between frequency and position. Linear interpolation (PI) is uniform; it treats every dimension of the rotary embedding with the same rescale factor. This uniformity is simple to implement but ignores the fact that different dimensions of the RoPE embedding carry different types of information and operate at different frequencies.

Non-linear methods, such as NTK and YaRN, recognize that lower dimensions with higher frequencies are more critical for distinguishing nearby tokens and should therefore be interpolated less. Higher dimensions with lower frequencies are more suited for broad positional tracking and can handle more interpolation. Despite these improvements, the current non-linear methods still rely on human-designed rules. As discovered in the analysis of LongRoPE, positional embeddings actually exhibit complex non-uniform information entropy. This means that the optimal rescale factors are not just based on simple groups or power laws, but may vary in more subtle, non-uniform ways across both RoPE dimensions and token positions.

# The 'crowded positions' problem in high-ratio interpolation

The "crowded positions" problem is a critical failure mode that occurs when the extension ratio $s$ becomes very large. In a linear interpolation setting, if we extend a model by a factor of $512\times$, the position embeddings for the original 4k context window are forced into a much narrower region of the embedding space. Because the rotation angles are compressed so aggressively, the difference between the embedding of position $n$ and position $n+1$ becomes nearly negligible.

This crowding leads to a severe loss of information, as the model can no longer effectively resolve the relative distance between tokens. This manifests as a performance drop within the original short context window; a model extended to 2 million tokens might actually perform worse on a 4k sequence than it did before the extension. To mitigate this, it is necessary to reduce the amount of interpolation applied to the initial tokens of a sequence and to specifically readjust the rescale factors for shorter context lengths to ensure that the positions do not become too crowded.
