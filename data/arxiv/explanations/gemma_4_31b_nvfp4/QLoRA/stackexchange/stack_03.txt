\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### How does the memory saving calculation for Double Quantization work?
Question:
In the Double Quantization section, the authors mention that for a blocksize of 64, the memory footprint of quantization constants is reduced from $32/64 = 0.5$ bits per parameter to $8/64 + 32/(64 \cdot 256) = 0.127$ bits. I'm struggling to follow this math. Why is the second term divided by $64 \cdot 256$, and what exactly does the $8/64$ represent in the context of the second quantization step?
Answer:
To understand the memory saving calculation for Double Quantization (DQ), it is helpful to view the process as a hierarchy of constants where each level reduces the precision of the level above it.

In standard block-wise quantization, the weight tensor $\mathbf{W}$ is divided into blocks of size $B=64$. Each block requires one 32-bit floating-point quantization constant ($c_2^{\text{FP32}}$) to scale the values. As you noted, this creates an overhead of $32/64 = 0.5$ bits per parameter. Double Quantization treats these $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ constants as a new tensor and quantizes them a second time.

The first term in the equation, $8/64$, represents the new cost of the first-level constants. Instead of storing $c_2$ as 32-bit floats, the authors quantize them into 8-bit floats ($\text{FP8}$). Since there is still one constant for every 64 parameters, the memory footprint for these constants drops from 32 bits to 8 bits per block, resulting in $8/64$ bits per parameter.

The second term, $32/(64 \cdot 256)$, represents the overhead of the second-level quantization constants ($c_1^{\text{FP32}}$). To quantize the $c_2$ constants into 8-bit, the authors again use block-wise quantization. They group the $c_2$ constants into blocks of 256 and assign one 32-bit constant ($c_1$) to each block. Because one $c_1$ constant serves 256 different $c_2$ constants, and each $c_2$ constant in turn serves 64 weights, a single $c_1$ constant effectively covers $256 \times 64$ original weight parameters. Therefore, the 32 bits of the $c_1$ constant are amortized over $64 \cdot 256$ parameters.

When you sum these two components, $8/64 + 32/(64 \cdot 256)$, you get approximately $0.125 + 0.002 = 0.127$ bits per parameter. This demonstrates that by quantizing the constants themselves, the authors reduce the overhead of the quantization scales by nearly $75\%$, saving roughly $0.373$ bits per parameter. This is a crucial optimization for massive models, as saving a fraction of a bit per parameter translates to several gigabytes of saved GPU memory when scaled to 65 billion parameters.
