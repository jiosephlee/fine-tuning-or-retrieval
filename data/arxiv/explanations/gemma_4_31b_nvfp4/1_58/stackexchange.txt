\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why is the model called "1.58-bit" instead of 2-bit?
Question:
I am reading the paper "The Era of 1-bit LLMs" and I'm confused about the naming. The authors state that the weights are ternary, taking values from $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. In standard computing, representing three distinct values would require 2 bits. Why do they specifically call it "1.58-bit"?
Answer:
The naming of "1.58-bit" instead of "2-bit" comes from a distinction between the physical storage requirement in a standard computer and the theoretical information capacity of the values being stored.

In standard digital computing, we are limited to integer bit-widths. As you noted, to represent three distinct states $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ using standard binary encoding, you would indeed need 2 bits per weight, as 1 bit can only represent $2^1 = 2$ states and 2 bits can represent up to $2^2 = 4$ states. However, the authors are not referring to the hardware allocation of a 2-bit register, but rather to the information-theoretic entropy of the weight distribution.

According to information theory, the number of bits required to represent $N$ equally likely possibilities is given by $\log_2(N)$. Since the weights in BitNet b1.58 are ternary (taking one of 3 possible values), the theoretical number of bits required to represent each weight is $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58496$. By calling the model "1.58-bit," the authors are highlighting that the model is more efficient than a traditional 2-bit quantized model, which would typically support 4 distinct values.

This distinction is important because it emphasizes the "ternary" nature of the architecture. While the weights might be packed into 2-bit containers in current software implementations (as mentioned in the paper's reference to using a "2-bit kernel" for measurement), the mathematical property of the model is that it only utilizes three states. This allows the model to achieve the benefits of "feature filtering" through the 0 value, which is not present in the original 1-bit (binary) BitNet, while remaining significantly closer to 1-bit in terms of complexity than a full 2-bit system.

### How does the "absmean" quantization function prevent weight collapse?
Question:
I'm looking at the quantization formulas where $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1)$ and $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$. I understand that this scales the weights by their average absolute value before rounding. However, I'm struggling to see how this mechanism ensures a diverse distribution of $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ values across the weight matrix. If the weights are updated during training, doesn't this scaling factor $\gamma$ create a dependency between all weights in the matrix?
Answer:
The "absmean" quantization function prevents weight collapse by transforming the quantization process from an absolute thresholding operation into a relative one. In a standard rounding operation without scaling, if the weights $W$ were to grow very large during training, the $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ function would saturate, mapping almost every weight to either $1$ or $-1$. Conversely, if the weights became very small, they would all collapse to $0$. By dividing $W$ by $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$, the model effectively normalizes the weights by their own average magnitude. This ensures that the scaling factor $\gamma$ adapts dynamically to the current distribution of the weights, effectively forcing the "average" weight to be scaled to approximately $1$ before rounding.

This mechanism ensures a diverse distribution of $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ because it creates a floating threshold based on the relative importance of each weight. Since the weights are scaled by their mean absolute value, a weight $W_{ij}$ will be quantized to $1$ if it is significantly larger than the average weight, to $0$ if it is small relative to the average, and to $-1$ if it is significantly negative relative to the average. This prevents the entire matrix from shifting into a single quantized value, regardless of whether the absolute values of the weights are increasing or decreasing during gradient descent.

Regarding your concern about the dependency created by $\gamma$, this is actually a beneficial property similar to the logic used in Layer Normalization or Batch Normalization. While it is true that $\gamma$ couples the weights of the matrix, this coupling serves as a stabilizer. It ensures that the quantization remains meaningful across different layers and training stages by maintaining a consistent dynamic range. Instead of the weights fighting against a fixed grid, they are competing against each other's relative magnitudes, which preserves the expressive power of the $1.58$-bit representation.

### Does BitNet b1.58 use quantization during training or only for inference?
Question:
The paper mentions that BitNet b1.58 is "trained from scratch, with 1.58-bit weights." I am used to post-training quantization (PTQ) where a full-precision model is quantized after training. I am confused about how training works here. Are the weights strictly $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ during the backward pass, or are there "latent" full-precision weights that are quantized only during the forward pass to allow for gradient updates?
Answer:
BitNet b1.58 uses quantization during both training and inference, but it does so in a way that differs fundamentally from post-training quantization (PTQ). In PTQ, you train a model in full precision and then "compress" it. In BitNet b1.58, the model is trained using a process similar to Quantization-Aware Training (QAT).

Your intuition about "latent" weights is correct. In the provided text, the paper defines a quantization function where the weight matrix $W$ is transformed into a ternary matrix $\widetilde{W}$ using $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1)$. During the forward pass, the model uses these quantized weights $\widetilde{W} \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ to perform matrix multiplications. This allows the model to realize the efficiency gains mentioned in the paper, such as replacing floating-point multiplications with integer additions.

However, because the $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ function is non-differentiable (its derivative is zero almost everywhere), it is impossible to perform gradient descent directly on the ternary values. To solve this, BitNet maintains a set of full-precision "latent" weights $W$ that are updated during the backward pass. When the optimizer calculates the gradients, it applies them to these full-precision weights. The quantized weights $\widetilde{W}$ are essentially a "projection" of these latent weights used only for the computation of the forward pass.

This mechanism is crucial because it allows the model to accumulate small gradient updates over time. If the weights were strictly limited to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ without latent precision, a single gradient update would rarely be large enough to "flip" a weight from $0$ to $1$, making the model nearly impossible to train. By maintaining full-precision weights, the model can gradually shift a value until it crosses the rounding threshold, at which point the quantized weight $\widetilde{W}$ switches its value.

In summary, BitNet b1.58 is trained "from scratch" in the sense that it does not start from a pre-trained FP16 model, but it maintains high-precision weights in the background to enable the learning process, while only using ternary weights for the actual data processing.

### How is matrix multiplication performed using only integer addition?
Question:
The authors claim that the computation paradigm of BitNet b1.58 requires "almost no multiplication operations" and only involves integer addition. I understand that if a weight is $1$ or $-1$, multiplication is just addition or subtraction of the activation. But since the activations are 8-bit, how is this actually implemented in a way that provides a speedup over highly optimized FP16 GEMM kernels on a GPU?
Answer:
The claim that $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ requires "almost no multiplication" stems from the fact that its weights are constrained to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. In a standard matrix multiplication, each element of the output is the result of a dot product $\sum_{i} w_i a_i$. When the weights $w_i$ are restricted to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the multiplication operation $w_i \times a_i$ simplifies to a conditional operation: if $w_i = 1$, you add $a_i$ to the accumulator; if $w_i = -1$, you subtract $a_i$; and if $w_i = 0$, you do nothing. Because the activations $a_i$ are 8-bit integers, these operations are simple integer additions and subtractions, which are computationally cheaper and more energy-efficient than floating-point multiplications.

The speedup over highly optimized FP16 GEMM (General Matrix Multiply) kernels on a GPU comes from two primary bottlenecks: compute and memory bandwidth. From a compute perspective, replacing floating-point multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations with integer additions significantly reduces the energy cost—the paper estimates a $71.4\times$ reduction in arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips. While GPUs are designed for massive parallelism in FP16, the sheer reduction in operational complexity and the shift to INT8 additions allow for higher throughput.

Furthermore, the memory bottleneck is drastically reduced. In traditional LLMs, moving massive FP16 weight matrices from DRAM to the on-chip SRAM is a major latency driver. Since $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ weights only require 1.58 bits, the memory footprint is significantly smaller, meaning more weights can be stored on-chip and transferred faster. To realize these gains on current GPU hardware, the authors utilize specialized kernels, such as the 2-bit kernel from Ladder, which are designed to handle these low-precision formats more efficiently than generic FP16 kernels. Ultimately, this paradigm shifts the computational burden from power-hungry floating-point units to simple integer logic, opening the door for specialized hardware (like LPUs) that can execute these additions even faster.

### Clarification on the scaling law equivalence between 1.58-bit and FP16 models
Question:
In the results section, the authors provide equivalences such as "13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient... than 3B FP16 LLM." I am trying to understand the trade-off here. Even if the compute (FLOPs) is replaced by cheaper additions, a 13B model still has significantly more parameters to move from DRAM to SRAM than a 3B model. Does the reduced bit-width of the weights actually offset the increase in the total number of parameters when comparing latency and memory bandwidth?
Answer:
The perceived trade-off between model size and memory bandwidth is resolved by looking at the **total volume of data** transferred, rather than the raw number of parameters. In LLM inference, the primary bottleneck is often the memory bandwidth required to move weights from DRAM to the on-chip SRAM (the memory-bound regime).

To understand why a 13B BitNet b1.58 model can be more efficient than a 3B FP16 model, we can compare the total amount of data that must be moved for a single forward pass. In a standard FP16 model, each parameter requires 16 bits of memory. For a 3B model, the total weight data is:
$$3 \times 10^9 \text{ parameters} \times 16 \text{ bits} = 48 \times 10^9 \text{ bits}$$

In contrast, BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. While these are theoretically 1.58 bits, the paper notes that they utilize a 2-bit kernel for implementation. For a 13B BitNet b1.58 model, the total weight data is:
$$13 \times 10^9 \text{ parameters} \times 2 \text{ bits} = 26 \times 10^9 \text{ bits}$$

Comparing the two, the 13B BitNet model actually requires moving significantly less data from DRAM to SRAM than the 3B FP16 model ($\approx 26\text{G bits}$ vs $\approx 48\text{G bits}$), despite having over four times as many parameters. This is why the authors state that 1-bit LLMs have a much lower memory footprint from both a "capacity and bandwidth standpoint."

Furthermore, the reduction in latency is not just due to less data movement, but also a fundamental shift in the computation paradigm. By reducing weights to ternary values, the expensive floating-point multiplications are replaced by simple integer additions. Since the paper notes that the time cost of these linear layers grows with model size, the massive reduction in the cost per operation (integer addition vs. FP16 multiplication) allows the model to scale in parameter count without the typical linear increase in latency. 

Essentially, BitNet b1.58 decouples "model capacity" (the number of parameters) from "resource cost" (memory bandwidth and compute energy). This allows you to increase the model size to improve performance (perplexity and zero-shot accuracy) while actually reducing the hardware overhead compared to a smaller, full-precision counterpart.

### What is "zero-point quantization" in the context of activations?
Question:
In the Quantization Function section, the authors mention that they scale activations to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token to "get rid of the zero-point quantization." I am not familiar with zero-point quantization in this context. What exactly is a zero-point in activation quantization, and why does removing it make the implementation "more convenient and simple for system-level optimization"?
Answer:
In quantization, a **zero-point** is an integer value used in asymmetric quantization to map the real-value $0$ of a floating-point tensor to a specific integer in the quantized space. When quantizing activations to an unsigned range, such as $[0, Q_b]$, the real value $0$ does not necessarily map to the integer $0$; instead, it maps to a value $z$ (the zero-point). The general formula for such quantization is $q = \text{round}(\frac{r}{s}) + z$, where $s$ is the scale and $z$ is the zero-point.

The authors of the paper mention that instead of scaling activations to the range $[0, Q_b]$, they scale them to a symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$. This effectively implements **symmetric quantization**, where the real-value $0$ is exactly mapped to the integer $0$, meaning $z = 0$.

Removing the zero-point makes the implementation "more convenient and simple for system-level optimization" because of how matrix multiplication is handled in hardware. When you perform a matrix multiplication $Y = WX$ with quantized values, if both the weights $W$ and activations $X$ have non-zero zero-points ($z_w$ and $z_x$), the operation becomes mathematically complex. The product of two quantized values $(q_w - z_w)(q_x - z_x)$ expands into four separate terms: $q_w q_x - q_w z_x - q_x z_w + z_w z_x$. 

To compute this accurately, the system must calculate "correction terms" (the sums of $q_w$ and $q_x$) to account for the offsets created by the zero-points. These extra additions and subtractions introduce significant computational overhead and complicate the design of the CUDA kernels or hardware logic. By scaling activations to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, the zero-point is eliminated ($z=0$), and the operation simplifies back to a standard integer dot product. This allows the model to leverage high-performance integer arithmetic without the need for complex correction logic, which is critical for achieving the latency and energy efficiency gains highlighted in the paper.
