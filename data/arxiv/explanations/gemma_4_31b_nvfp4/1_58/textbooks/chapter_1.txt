Chapter 1: Introduction to Low-Precision Large Language Models

# The growth of LLMs and the 'Memory Wall'

In recent years, the development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has been characterized by a rapid increase in both their size and their capabilities. While this growth has enabled remarkable performance across a vast array of natural language processing tasks, it has simultaneously created significant challenges regarding the deployment of these models. As models grow larger, the economic and environmental costs associated with their operation increase, primarily due to the massive amount of energy required to power the hardware necessary for their execution.

One of the most critical bottlenecks in the deployment of these massive models is the movement of data, often referred to in the context of the "memory wall." During the inference process, model parameters must be transferred from the Dynamic Random-Access Memory (DRAM) to the memory of an on-chip accelerator, such as Static Random-Access Memory (SRAM). This transfer process is computationally expensive and time-consuming. While some attempts have been made to increase the size of SRAM to improve throughput, such hardware modifications are significantly more expensive than utilizing DRAM.

The memory footprint of a model affects both its capacity—how much space the model takes up—and its bandwidth—how quickly the weights can be moved from memory to the processor. Full-precision models have a high memory footprint, which exacerbates the cost and time associated with loading weights from DRAM. By shifting toward 1-bit LLMs, the memory footprint is drastically reduced, which significantly lowers the bandwidth requirements and accelerates the inference process by making the transfer of weights more efficient.

# Energy consumption and environmental impact of floating-point operations

The primary computational burden of any standard LLM is matrix multiplication. In vanilla LLM architectures, these operations are performed using 16-bit floating-point values, specifically in formats such as FP16 or BF16. The majority of the energy cost in these models stems from the necessity of performing floating-point addition and multiplication operations. Because these operations are complex, they require a significant amount of power, which contributes to the high environmental and economic impact of running large-scale AI.

There is a direct relationship between energy consumption and computational speed. In many modern chips, the fundamental limit to compute performance is power; as chips hit their power limits, they cannot process information any faster. Therefore, any reduction in the energy required for a specific operation can be translated directly into faster computation.

By moving away from floating-point operations, the computational paradigm can be shifted. For example, in architectures like BitNet, matrix multiplication is transformed so that it only involves integer addition. This change saves orders of magnitude of energy compared to the floating-point multiplications used in traditional models. By reducing the arithmetic energy consumption, these models not only become more sustainable but also allow for much faster inference speeds.

# Introduction to precision: FP16, BF16, and the move toward low-bit representations

In the context of deep learning, precision refers to the number of bits used to represent each parameter (weight) and activation within a model. Traditional LLMs are built using 16-bit floating-point precision, denoted as FP16 or BF16. This high level of precision allows for very granular adjustments to weights during training and inference, but it comes at the cost of high memory and energy usage.

The trend in the field has been to move toward low-bit representations to mitigate these costs. The goal is to represent the same information using fewer bits without sacrificing the model's performance. The most recent innovation in this direction is the development of 1.58-bit LLMs, such as BitNet b1.58. In these models, every single parameter is ternary, meaning it can only take on one of three values: $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

The designation "1.58-bit" comes from the binary system, as $\log_2(3)$ is approximately $1.58$. By allowing the weights to be $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ rather than just $\{-1, 1\}$, the model gains a critical capability: explicit support for feature filtering. The inclusion of $0$ allows the model to effectively "filter out" certain features, which significantly improves the modeling capability and overall performance of 1-bit LLMs compared to strictly binary versions.

# Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) vs. Training from Scratch

Quantization is the process of reducing the precision of a model's weights and activations to reduce memory and computational requirements. One common method is Post-Training Quantization (PTQ). In PTQ, a model is first trained in full precision (such as FP16) and is subsequently converted to a lower-bit format (such as 4-bit) for the purpose of inference. This approach is widely utilized in industrial LLMs because it allows developers to compress a pre-existing model without needing to re-run the expensive training process.

Despite its popularity, PTQ is considered sub-optimal. Because the model was originally designed to operate in a high-precision space, forcing its parameters into a low-bit representation after the fact can lead to a loss of information and a decrease in performance.

In contrast, models like BitNet b1.58 are trained from scratch using low-precision weights. Instead of compressing a high-precision model, the model is designed to learn and optimize its parameters within the constraints of the 1.58-bit system from the very beginning. This approach ensures that the model is natively optimized for the low-bit representation, allowing it to match the perplexity and end-task performance of full-precision Transformer LLMs of the same size and training token count.

# Limitations of current 4-bit and 8-bit quantization methods

As the industry has sought to reduce the cost of LLMs, there has been a steady progression from 16-bit models to 8-bit and 4-bit variants. Techniques such as GPTQ and AWQ have been developed to facilitate the creation of 4-bit models. While these methods significantly reduce the memory and computational requirements compared to full-precision models, they still operate within a framework that is largely based on the original high-precision training paradigm.

The primary limitation of these existing low-bit methods is that they do not fully redefine the computation paradigm. Even at 4 bits, these models often still rely on operations that are more expensive than simple integer addition. Furthermore, as previously noted, post-training methods often fail to reach the optimal performance ceiling that can be achieved when a model is trained natively in low precision.

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that it is possible to move beyond 4-bit and 8-bit quantization to reach a near-1-bit state without losing the performance of a full-precision baseline. Starting at a model size of 3B parameters, BitNet b1.58 can match the performance of FP16 baselines while being significantly more cost-effective in terms of latency, memory, throughput, and energy consumption, effectively overcoming the limitations inherent in standard quantization methods.
