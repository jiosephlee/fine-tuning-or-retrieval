\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Clarification on the scaling law equivalence between 1.58-bit and FP16 models
Question:
In the results section, the authors provide equivalences such as "13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient... than 3B FP16 LLM." I am trying to understand the trade-off here. Even if the compute (FLOPs) is replaced by cheaper additions, a 13B model still has significantly more parameters to move from DRAM to SRAM than a 3B model. Does the reduced bit-width of the weights actually offset the increase in the total number of parameters when comparing latency and memory bandwidth?
Answer:
The perceived trade-off between model size and memory bandwidth is resolved by looking at the **total volume of data** transferred, rather than the raw number of parameters. In LLM inference, the primary bottleneck is often the memory bandwidth required to move weights from DRAM to the on-chip SRAM (the memory-bound regime).

To understand why a 13B BitNet b1.58 model can be more efficient than a 3B FP16 model, we can compare the total amount of data that must be moved for a single forward pass. In a standard FP16 model, each parameter requires 16 bits of memory. For a 3B model, the total weight data is:
$$3 \times 10^9 \text{ parameters} \times 16 \text{ bits} = 48 \times 10^9 \text{ bits}$$

In contrast, BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. While these are theoretically 1.58 bits, the paper notes that they utilize a 2-bit kernel for implementation. For a 13B BitNet b1.58 model, the total weight data is:
$$13 \times 10^9 \text{ parameters} \times 2 \text{ bits} = 26 \times 10^9 \text{ bits}$$

Comparing the two, the 13B BitNet model actually requires moving significantly less data from DRAM to SRAM than the 3B FP16 model ($\approx 26\text{G bits}$ vs $\approx 48\text{G bits}$), despite having over four times as many parameters. This is why the authors state that 1-bit LLMs have a much lower memory footprint from both a "capacity and bandwidth standpoint."

Furthermore, the reduction in latency is not just due to less data movement, but also a fundamental shift in the computation paradigm. By reducing weights to ternary values, the expensive floating-point multiplications are replaced by simple integer additions. Since the paper notes that the time cost of these linear layers grows with model size, the massive reduction in the cost per operation (integer addition vs. FP16 multiplication) allows the model to scale in parameter count without the typical linear increase in latency. 

Essentially, BitNet b1.58 decouples "model capacity" (the number of parameters) from "resource cost" (memory bandwidth and compute energy). This allows you to increase the model size to improve performance (perplexity and zero-shot accuracy) while actually reducing the hardware overhead compared to a smaller, full-precision counterpart.
