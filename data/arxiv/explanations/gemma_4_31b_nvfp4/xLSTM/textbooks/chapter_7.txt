\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 7: xLSTM as a Large Language Model: Scaling and Performance

# Large-scale training on the SlimPajama dataset

To evaluate the potential of the Extended Long Short-Term Memory (xLSTM) architecture as a viable alternative to current Large Language Models (LLMs), the researchers conducted large-scale training experiments. The primary goal was to see if the modifications introduced in xLSTM—specifically exponential gating and matrix memory—could sustain performance when scaled to billions of parameters. For this purpose, the models were trained on a massive corpus consisting of 300 billion tokens from the SlimPajama dataset. This dataset choice ensures that the models are exposed to a diverse and extensive range of natural language, mirroring the training regimes of state-of-the-art models like Mamba and Griffin.

The experiments focused on a variety of model scales to observe how performance evolves with size. Specifically, four different model sizes were implemented: 125 million, 350 million, 760 million, and 1.3 billion parameters. To ensure a fair comparison, xLSTM was pitted against representatives from three distinct architectural families: the Transformer (represented by Llama), State Space Models (represented by Mamba), and Recurrent Neural Networks (represented by RWKV-4). The evaluation of xLSTM involved different block configurations, denoted as xLSTM[$a$:$b$], where the ratio $a/b$ represents the proportion of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. For instance, xLSTM[1:0] denotes a model consisting entirely of mLSTM blocks, while xLSTM[7:1] incorporates a small number of sLSTM blocks to provide memory mixing capabilities.

# Perplexity and Validation: Comparing xLSTM, Llama, Mamba, and RWKV

One of the primary metrics used to evaluate the quality of a language model is validation perplexity, which measures how well the model predicts the next token in a sequence. In the comparative analysis on the SlimPajama validation set, xLSTM demonstrated superior performance across all tested model sizes. Both the purely mLSTM-based version (xLSTM[1:0]) and the hybrid version (xLSTM[7:1]) emerged as the top-performing models, consistently achieving lower perplexity than Llama, Mamba, and RWKV-4.

This result is particularly significant because it demonstrates that xLSTM's architectural enhancements allow it to capture the underlying patterns of language more effectively than previous iterations of RNNs or even the highly optimized Transformer and SSM architectures. While Llama and Mamba are the current industry standards for scaling, xLSTM's ability to outperform them in next-token prediction suggests that the combination of matrix memory and exponential gating provides a more efficient mechanism for storing and retrieving linguistic information at scale.

# Sequence Length Extrapolation: Maintaining performance across longer contexts

A critical challenge for any language model is sequence length extrapolation, which is the ability of a model to maintain its performance on sequences that are significantly longer than those it encountered during training. In the experiments, models were trained with a context length of $2048$ tokens and then tested on contexts extending up to $16384$ tokens. This test reveals whether the model's memory mechanism degrades as the sequence grows.

The results indicated a stark difference between xLSTM and its competitors. While Llama, Mamba, and RWKV-4 saw their perplexities increase as the context length extended beyond the training limit, the xLSTM models managed to maintain low perplexities for much longer contexts. This suggests that xLSTM is more robust to the "out-of-distribution" nature of longer sequences. This stability is likely a result of the extended memory structures and normalization techniques that prevent the model's internal states from becoming unstable or saturated when processing long-range dependencies.

# Downstream Task Performance: Common sense reasoning and PALOMA benchmarks

Beyond simple token prediction, the researchers evaluated xLSTM's ability to generalize to complex downstream tasks, such as common sense reasoning. Across the vast majority of these tasks and across all model sizes, xLSTM outperformed the other models. The only exception was the ARC task, where Mamba occasionally performed better in some instances. This indicates that xLSTM possesses a strong capacity for semantic understanding and logical reasoning, moving beyond mere statistical pattern matching.

To further refine this analysis, the models were tested on the PALOMA language benchmark, which consists of $571$ different text domains ranging from news sites like nytimes.com to specific community forums like r/depression on Reddit. The results were overwhelmingly in favor of xLSTM, particularly the xLSTM[1:0] variant. In $570$ out of $571$ domains ($99.8\%$), xLSTM[1:0] achieved lower perplexity than RWKV-4. It also beat Mamba in $568$ out of $571$ domains ($99.5\%$) and Llama in $486$ out of $571$ domains ($85.1\%$). This level of dominance across diverse domains suggests that xLSTM's matrix memory is exceptionally capable of adapting to the varying linguistic styles and vocabularies of different real-world contexts.

# Scaling Laws: Predicting the performance of larger xLSTM models

To understand the future potential of xLSTM, the researchers analyzed its scaling laws. Scaling laws describe the power-law relationship between a model's performance (such as loss or perplexity) and its size (number of parameters). By plotting the performance of different models as they scale from 125 million to 1.3 billion parameters, the researchers could observe the "offset" of each architecture.

The analysis showed that while xLSTM, Llama, Mamba, and RWKV-4 all exhibit similar power-law scaling behavior, xLSTM is shifted to a significantly more favorable position. Specifically, xLSTM outperformed Mamba by a margin similar to the one by which Mamba outperformed Llama. This implies that as the model size increases further into the range of tens or hundreds of billions of parameters, xLSTM is predicted to maintain its lead over Transformers and SSMs. This suggests that xLSTM is not just a competitive alternative but potentially a more scalable architecture for the next generation of ultra-large language models.

# Computational Efficiency: Inference throughput, memory complexity, and generation time

A major advantage of xLSTM over the Transformer architecture is its computational efficiency during inference. Transformers suffer from quadratic complexity relative to the sequence length, meaning that as the context grows, the computational cost increases exponentially. In contrast, xLSTM features linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to the sequence length. This makes xLSTM highly suitable for edge computing and industrial applications where memory and processing power are limited.

In terms of generation time, xLSTM exhibits linear scaling, whereas Llama exhibits quadratic scaling. When measuring decoding throughput, xLSTM was able to utilize much higher batch sizes than Llama. This is because Llama requires a key-value cache that grows with the sequence length, consuming significant memory, while xLSTM's compressive memory remains constant. Consequently, xLSTM achieves the highest throughput among the compared models. Although the matrix memory of mLSTM involves $d \times d$ updates and retrievals—which are computationally more expensive than scalar updates—these operations can be fully parallelized on GPUs, resulting in only a minor impact on actual wall-clock time. While sLSTM is not parallelizable due to its memory mixing, optimized CUDA kernels keep it within a reasonable speed range, typically less than two times slower than mLSTM.
