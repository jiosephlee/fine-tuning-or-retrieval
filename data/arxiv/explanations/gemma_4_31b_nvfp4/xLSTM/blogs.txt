# The Return of the LSTM: Introducing xLSTM

For decades, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network was the gold standard for sequence modeling. From early text generation to complex reinforcement learning agents like AlphaStar, LSTMs proved that the "constant error carousel" and gating mechanisms could effectively solve the vanishing gradient problem. However, the arrival of the Transformer in 2017 shifted the paradigm. With parallelizable self-attention at its core, Transformers scaled far more efficiently, eventually leading to the current era of Large Language Models (LLMs).

Despite the dominance of Transformers, LSTMs possess an inherent advantage: linear computation and constant memory complexity relative to sequence length. The research paper "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory" asks a pivotal question: can we scale LSTMs to billions of parameters by mitigating their known limitations and leveraging modern LLM techniques? The answer is xLSTM.

## Addressing the Limitations of Classical LSTMs

To evolve the LSTM into the xLSTM, the authors identified three primary bottlenecks in the original architecture. First, LSTMs struggle to revise storage decisions; once information is stored, it is difficult to update if a more relevant piece of information appears later in the sequence. Second, LSTMs have limited storage capacity because information must be compressed into scalar cell states. Finally, the "memory mixing" (hidden-to-hidden connections) in LSTMs forces sequential processing, preventing the massive parallelization that makes Transformers so fast to train.

xLSTM addresses these issues through two main innovations: exponential gating and modified memory structures.

## Exponential Gating and Stabilization

The most fundamental change in xLSTM is the introduction of exponential gating. By replacing traditional sigmoid gates with exponential activation functions, the model gains a much more flexible ability to revise and update stored information. 

Because exponential functions can quickly lead to numerical overflows, xLSTM introduces a stabilizer state $m_t$ to keep the values manageable. Additionally, a normalizer state $n_t$ is implemented to track the cumulative strength of the input and forget gates. This ensures that the hidden state remains stable and normalized, allowing the model to scale effectively without diverging during training.

## Two New Variants: sLSTM and mLSTM

xLSTM isn't a single architecture but a family that includes two distinct memory types: sLSTM and mLSTM.

The sLSTM (scalar LSTM) maintains the traditional scalar memory but incorporates the new exponential gating and a revised memory mixing approach. By using multiple heads and memory mixing within each head, sLSTM is particularly adept at "state tracking" problems—tasks that require the model to keep track of a specific state over a long narrative or evaluate complex code.

The mLSTM (matrix LSTM) replaces the scalar cell with a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. Instead of a simple scalar update, it uses a covariance update rule:
$$\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$
In this setup, the model stores pairs of key ($\mathbf{k}_t$) and value ($\mathbf{v}_t$) vectors. Retrieval is performed via a matrix multiplication with a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$, similar to the mechanism in Transformers. Crucially, because mLSTM abandons hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections, it is fully parallelizable, allowing it to be trained with efficiency similar to modern linear attention models.

## The xLSTM Architecture and Performance

These variants are integrated into xLSTM blocks and residually stacked into a deep architecture. Depending on the memory type, the blocks use different residual structures: sLSTM utilizes post up-projection, while mLSTM utilizes pre up-projection to maximize its memory capacity in high-dimensional space.

The empirical results are compelling. When trained on 15 billion and 300 billion tokens from the SlimPajama dataset, xLSTM consistently outperformed Transformers (like Llama) and State Space Models (like Mamba) in validation perplexity across various model sizes. One of the most striking findings is xLSTM's ability to extrapolate to longer contexts. While other models saw perplexity spike as sequence lengths increased, xLSTM maintained low perplexity even when tested on contexts up to $16384$ tokens, despite being trained on only $2048$.

From an efficiency standpoint, xLSTM offers the best of both worlds. It maintains the linear scaling of recurrent models, avoiding the quadratic memory explosion of Transformers. While sLSTM remains inherently sequential, the authors developed a fast CUDA implementation that minimizes the overhead. Meanwhile, mLSTM provides a high-capacity memory that can be processed in parallel on GPUs.

By updating the LSTM with exponential gating and matrix memories, xLSTM demonstrates that recurrent architectures are not just viable alternatives to Transformers, but are potentially superior competitors for the next generation of Large Language Models.



# Scalar vs. Matrix Memory: How xLSTM Remembers Better

For decades, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture was the gold standard for sequence modeling. By introducing the "constant error carousel" and gating mechanisms, LSTMs solved the vanishing gradient problem that plagued early recurrent neural networks. However, the industry eventually pivoted toward Transformers, which offered massive parallelizability and superior scaling for Large Language Models (LLMs).

The xLSTM (Extended LSTM) research asks a compelling question: can we bring LSTMs back into the LLM era by fixing their fundamental limitations? The answer lies in two major innovations: exponential gating and a dual-memory structure consisting of scalar (sLSTM) and matrix (mLSTM) components.

## The LSTM Legacy and Its Limits

Traditional LSTMs struggle with three primary issues:
1. **Inability to revise storage:** Once a value is stored in a cell, LSTMs find it difficult to "overwrite" or revise that decision when more relevant information appears later in a sequence.
2. **Limited storage capacity:** Information must be compressed into scalar cell states, which leads to poor performance on "rare token prediction" where the model needs to remember specific, infrequent details.
3. **Sequential bottlenecks:** Memory mixing—the hidden-to-hidden connections between time steps—prevents the model from being fully parallelized, making training much slower than Transformers.

xLSTM addresses these issues by reimagining how memory is gated and stored.

## Exponential Gating: Learning to Revise

The most critical update in xLSTM is the shift from sigmoid gates to exponential gating. In a standard LSTM, gates are squashed between 0 and 1. xLSTM introduces exponential activation functions for the input and forget gates, allowing the model to be much more decisive about what to keep and what to discard.

Because exponential functions can lead to numerical instability and overflows, the researchers introduced two safeguards:
* **Normalization:** A normalizer state $n_t$ tracks the sum of the input gate multiplied by all future forget gates, ensuring the hidden state remains scaled.
* **Stabilization:** A stabilizer state $m_t$ is used to track the maximum log-values of the gates, preventing the exponential functions from exploding.

This combination allows xLSTM to "revise" its storage decisions, making it far more effective at tasks like Nearest Neighbor Search, where the model must update its stored value as it finds more similar vectors.

## sLSTM: The Power of State Tracking

The sLSTM (scalar LSTM) retains the scalar memory of the original LSTM but incorporates the new exponential gating and a new approach to memory mixing. 

sLSTMs are particularly adept at "state tracking"—the ability to keep track of entities or logic over a long narrative. This is achieved through recurrent connections between hidden states. While this memory mixing prevents full parallelization, the researchers developed a fast CUDA implementation that minimizes the performance hit. sLSTMs are typically integrated into residual blocks with post up-projection to project the summary of the past into a higher-dimensional space for better linear separability.

## mLSTM: Expanding Storage with Matrix Memory

To solve the capacity problem, the mLSTM (matrix LSTM) replaces the scalar cell $c$ with a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This transforms the memory into an associative memory, where the model stores key-value pairs.

The mLSTM uses a covariance update rule to store information:
$$\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

In this setup, $\mathbf{v}_t$ is the value and $\mathbf{k}_t$ is the key. Retrieval is performed via matrix multiplication with a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$. By using a matrix memory, mLSTM can store significantly more information than sLSTM, which directly improves its ability to predict rare tokens. 

Crucially, because mLSTM abandons hidden-to-hidden memory mixing, it is fully parallelizable. This allows it to scale similarly to Transformers or State Space Models (SSMs) during training.

## Architecture and Performance

An xLSTM architecture is built by residually stacking xLSTM blocks. Depending on the goal, these blocks can be a mix of sLSTM and mLSTM. For example, a configuration denoted as xLSTM[7:1] would feature seven mLSTM blocks for every one sLSTM block.

The results of this architectural shift are significant:
* **Linear Complexity:** Unlike Transformers, which have quadratic complexity relative to sequence length, xLSTM maintains linear computation and constant memory complexity.
* **Superior Scaling:** In large-scale experiments on the SlimPajama dataset, xLSTM outperformed Transformers (Llama, GPT-3) and SSMs (Mamba) in validation perplexity across various model sizes.
* **Context Extrapolation:** xLSTM demonstrates a strong ability to maintain low perplexity even when extrapolating to context lengths far beyond those seen during training.

By combining the state-tracking capabilities of sLSTM with the massive storage capacity of mLSTM, xLSTM proves that the recurrent philosophy is not just viable, but potentially superior, for the next generation of Large Language Models.



# Breaking the Quadratic Barrier: xLSTM and the Future of Long Contexts

For years, the Transformer architecture has dominated the world of Large Language Models (LLMs). Its core mechanism, self-attention, allows models to capture complex dependencies across a sequence. However, this power comes with a significant cost: quadratic complexity. As the input sequence length $L$ grows, the computational and memory requirements increase by $L^2$. This "quadratic barrier" makes processing extremely long documents or maintaining long-term memory computationally expensive and memory-intensive.

The research paper "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory" proposes a way forward by revisiting and evolving the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture. By addressing the fundamental limitations of traditional LSTMs, xLSTM achieves the performance of modern Transformers while maintaining linear computation and constant memory complexity relative to sequence length.

## Overcoming the Limitations of Classic LSTMs

While LSTMs were the predecessors to today's LLMs, they fell out of favor because they struggled with three main issues: an inability to revise storage decisions, limited storage capacity (compressing information into scalar cell states), and a lack of parallelization during training.

xLSTM solves these problems through two new architectural variants: sLSTM and mLSTM. 

The **sLSTM (scalar LSTM)** introduces exponential gating. By using exponential activation functions for input and forget gates, the model can more effectively revise what it stores in memory. To prevent the numerical instability (overflows) that exponential functions can cause, the authors introduce a stabilizer state $m_t$ and a normalizer state $n_t$. The sLSTM also features "memory mixing," allowing it to excel at state tracking—a task where Transformers and State Space Models often struggle.

The **mLSTM (matrix LSTM)** addresses the storage capacity problem. Instead of a scalar cell, it uses a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This allows the model to store key-value pairs using a covariance update rule: $\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. Because it abandons the hidden-hidden recurrent connections found in sLSTM, the mLSTM is fully parallelizable, allowing it to be trained efficiently on GPUs.

## Linear Efficiency and Higher Throughput

The shift from quadratic to linear complexity has profound implications for how we deploy AI. In a Transformer, the memory required for the KV cache grows linearly with the sequence length, which eventually exhausts GPU memory. In contrast, xLSTM uses compressive memory, meaning its memory footprint remains constant regardless of how long the sequence becomes.

This efficiency translates directly into hardware advantages:
* **Faster Text Generation:** xLSTM demonstrates linear scaling in generation time, whereas Transformers scale quadratically.
* **Higher Throughput:** Because xLSTM requires significantly less memory per sequence, it can handle much larger batch sizes than Transformer-based models like Llama, leading to higher overall decoding throughput.
* **Edge Deployment:** The constant memory complexity makes xLSTM an ideal candidate for industrial applications and deployment on edge devices where RAM is strictly limited.

## Performance at Scale

The researchers didn't just optimize for speed; they scaled xLSTM to billions of parameters to see if it could compete with state-of-the-art LLMs. Training on the SlimPajama dataset, xLSTM consistently outperformed Transformers, State Space Models (SSMs), and other RNNs in validation perplexity.

One of the most impressive findings is xLSTM's capability for sequence length extrapolation. While models trained on a context length of 2048 often struggle when faced with longer sequences, xLSTM models maintained low perplexity even when extrapolated to contexts of up to 16,384 tokens. This suggests that xLSTM's memory structures are significantly more robust at handling long-range dependencies than current Transformer-based architectures.

By combining the parallelizability of matrix updates with the expressive power of exponential gating, xLSTM proves that we do not have to accept the quadratic trade-off to achieve world-class language modeling. It opens a promising path toward AI that is not only more powerful but also significantly more efficient.



# Beyond Transformers: Can Recurrent Models Win the Scaling War?

For years, the narrative in Large Language Model (LLM) development has been dominated by the Transformer. Its parallelizable self-attention mechanism allowed models to scale to billions of parameters, effectively pushing the original Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks into the background. However, the "scaling war" is not over. The introduction of xLSTM (Extended Long Short-Term Memory) suggests that recurrent architectures can not only compete with but potentially outperform Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs) at scale.

## The Evolution of the LSTM

The original LSTM was designed to solve the vanishing gradient problem using a "constant error carousel" and gating mechanisms. Despite its success, it faced three critical bottlenecks: an inability to revise storage decisions, limited memory capacity (restricted to scalar states), and a lack of parallelizability.

xLSTM addresses these by introducing two new variants: sLSTM and mLSTM. The sLSTM uses scalar memory and introduces exponential gating, which allows the model to effectively "revise" what it stores. The mLSTM replaces the scalar memory with a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and uses a covariance update rule. This shift from scalars to matrices significantly increases storage capacity and, crucially, allows the mLSTM to be fully parallelized, removing the sequential processing bottleneck that plagued previous recurrent models.

## Performance: Perplexity and Downstream Tasks

To test the efficacy of xLSTM, researchers trained it alongside Llama (Transformer), Mamba (SSM), and RWKV (RNN) on 15 billion and 300 billion tokens from the SlimPajama dataset. The results were telling. Across multiple model sizes—ranging from 125M to 1.3B parameters—xLSTM consistently achieved the lowest validation set perplexity.

This performance advantage extends beyond simple token prediction. In evaluations on the PALOMA language benchmark, which covers 571 diverse text domains, xLSTM[1:0] (a variant using only mLSTM blocks) outperformed Mamba in 99.5% of the domains and Llama in 85.1% of the domains. Whether it is common sense reasoning or domain-specific text generation, xLSTM demonstrates a superior ability to model language compared to its contemporaries.

## The Scaling Law Advantage

The most compelling argument for xLSTM as a future LLM backbone lies in its scaling laws. When analyzing how performance improves as model size increases, xLSTM exhibits a power-law scaling behavior similar to Transformers and SSMs, but with a significant offset. 

The data indicates a clear hierarchy: xLSTM performs better than Mamba, and Mamba performs better than Llama. This suggests that as we move toward even larger models—tens or hundreds of billions of parameters—xLSTM will likely maintain or widen its lead over Transformer-based architectures.

## Efficiency and Long-Context Handling

One of the primary drawbacks of the Transformer is its quadratic complexity relative to sequence length. xLSTM breaks this trend, offering linear computation and constant memory complexity. This makes it an ideal candidate for edge deployments and industrial applications where memory is a constraint.

Furthermore, xLSTM shows a remarkable ability to extrapolate to longer contexts. While most models see a spike in perplexity when processing sequences longer than those seen during training, xLSTM maintains stability. When trained on a context length of 2048 and tested up to 16,384, xLSTM kept perplexities low, outperforming Llama, Mamba, and RWKV-4.

## Conclusion

xLSTM proves that the core ideas of recurrent networks—specifically gating and memory cells—are still incredibly powerful when combined with modern scaling techniques. By solving the storage and parallelization issues of the original LSTM, xLSTM has emerged as a serious competitor to the Transformer. With its linear scaling, superior memory capacity, and promising scaling laws, xLSTM is well-positioned to be a cornerstone of the next generation of billion-parameter models.
