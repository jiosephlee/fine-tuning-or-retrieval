\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Smarter, Faster, Leaner: Understanding Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

Mathematical reasoning is one of the steepest challenges for large language models (LLMs). While general-purpose models can be impressive, the precision required for competition-level math often requires specialized training. The researchers behind DeepSeekMath have introduced a new approach to this problem, culminating in a model that approaches the performance of GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra. While their data selection pipeline is impressive, one of the most significant technical contributions is a new reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

## The Problem with Traditional RL (PPO)

To understand GRPO, we first need to look at the industry standard: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). PPO is an actor-critic algorithm. In this setup, the "actor" is the policy model we are training, and the "critic" is a separate value function model. The critic's job is to estimate the expected reward of a state, which serves as a baseline to calculate the "advantage" of a specific action.

The problem is that the critic model is typically the same size as the policy model. If you are training a 7B parameter model, you effectively need the memory and computational resources for another 7B parameter model just to act as the critic. This creates a massive memory bottleneck and increases the complexity of training, especially when trying to train an accurate value function at every single token.

## Introducing GRPO: Removing the Critic

GRPO simplifies this process by completely removing the critic model. Instead of relying on a separate neural network to estimate a baseline, GRPO uses a group-based relative approach. 

For every question $q$, the model samples a group of $G$ different outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$ from the current policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. Instead of a critic model providing a baseline, the algorithm calculates the average reward of these $G$ outputs. The "advantage" $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for a specific output is then determined by how its reward compares to the group's mean and standard deviation.

By using the group's own performance as the baseline, GRPO achieves several goals:
* **Memory Efficiency:** Because there is no critic model to maintain, the memory overhead is significantly reduced.
* **Alignment with Reward Models:** Most reward models are trained on pairwise comparisons (which answer is better than the other). GRPO's group-relative mechanism mirrors this comparative nature.
* **Stability:** GRPO regularizes the training by adding the KL divergence between the trained policy and a reference policy directly into the loss function, rather than just adding a penalty to the reward.

## Refining the Signal: Outcome vs. Process Supervision

The researchers also explored how to provide rewards to the model using GRPO. They looked at two primary methods:

* **Outcome Supervision:** The model receives a single reward at the end of its entire response. The advantage for every token in that response is set to the normalized reward of the final answer.
* **Process Supervision:** This is a more granular approach where a process reward model scores each individual reasoning step. The advantage for a token is the sum of the normalized rewards from that point forward.

They found that process supervision generally provides superior performance because it gives the model a more precise "roadmap" of where its reasoning went right or wrong, rather than just judging the final answer.

## Does it Actually Work?

The results indicate that GRPO is highly effective. Starting from the instruction-tuned DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, the RL phase using GRPO boosted the accuracy on the GSM8K benchmark from $82.9\%$ to $88.2\%$ and the competition-level MATH benchmark from $46.8\%$ to $51.7\%$. 

Interestingly, the researchers found that RL didn't necessarily increase the model's fundamental ability to find a correct solution (Pass@K accuracy remained relatively stable). Instead, it improved the "majority vote" accuracy (Maj@K). This suggests that GRPO works by making the model's output distribution more robust—essentially training the model to consistently pick the correct reasoning path from the options it already knows how to generate.

By removing the bulky critic model and leveraging group dynamics, GRPO proves that we can achieve state-of-the-art mathematical reasoning without the unsustainable memory costs of traditional RL.
