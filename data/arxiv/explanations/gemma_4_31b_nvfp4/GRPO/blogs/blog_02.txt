\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Mining the Web for Math: The Secret Behind the DeepSeekMath Corpus

Mathematical reasoning is one of the steepest challenges for large language models (LLMs). While proprietary models like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra have set a high bar, open-source models have historically struggled to keep pace. The team behind DeepSeekMath has bridged this gap with DeepSeekMath 7B, a model that achieves a 51.7% score on the competition-level MATH benchmark without external tools.

The secret to this performance isn't just in the architecture or the reinforcement learning—it is in the "data alchemy" used to create the DeepSeekMath Corpus.

## The Challenge of Sourcing Math Data

High-quality mathematical data is scarce. While many researchers rely on arXiv papers or small, curated datasets, these often lack the scale or diversity needed to push a model to a professional level. DeepSeekMath takes a different approach by mining the Common Crawl (CC), a massive repository of web pages, to extract 120 billion math-related tokens. 

The challenge with the Common Crawl is the "noise." Most web pages are not mathematical, and simply keyword-searching for "math" would yield too many irrelevant results. To solve this, the team developed a meticulously engineered iterative data selection pipeline.

## The Iterative Filtering Pipeline

The process begins with a "seed corpus"—a small but high-quality set of mathematical texts, in this case, OpenWebMath. This seed is used to train a fastText classifier to distinguish between math-related and non-math-related web pages.

The pipeline follows an iterative loop to constantly improve the quality and diversity of the data:

1.  **Initial Recall**: The fastText classifier is trained using 500,000 positive examples from the seed corpus and 500,000 negative examples from the Common Crawl. It then scans the deduplicated Common Crawl to recall high-scoring mathematical pages.
2.  **Domain Identification**: To avoid missing diverse math content, the team identifies "math-related domains." If more than 10% of the pages from a specific base URL (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`) were collected in the first iteration, that domain is flagged.
3.  **Human Refinement**: Researchers manually annotate URLs within these identified domains to find additional mathematical content.
4.  **Seed Enrichment**: These newly discovered high-quality pages are added back into the seed corpus, and the fastText classifier is retrained.

After four iterations of this cycle, the team successfully collected 35.5 million mathematical web pages, totaling 120 billion tokens. To ensure the results were legitimate, they implemented a strict decontamination process, removing any text that matched 10-gram strings from common benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH.

## The ArXiv Surprise

One of the most intriguing findings in the DeepSeekMath paper is the role of arXiv papers. It is common practice in the AI community to include arXiv LaTeX files in math pre-training. However, the DeepSeek team's experiments suggested that arXiv papers may actually be ineffective for improving performance on standard mathematical benchmarks.

By training models on various arXiv-only corpora (such as MathPile and RedPajama), they observed no notable improvements—and in some cases, even deterioration—across benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH, and MMLU-STEM. While this doesn't mean arXiv data is useless—it might help with theorem informalization or prove beneficial at much larger model scales—it suggests that for general mathematical reasoning, high-quality web data is a far more powerful driver.

## The Synergy of Code and Math

The data strategy didn't stop at web scraping. The researchers found that starting from a code-pretrained model (DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B) provided a significant advantage. 

Their experiments revealed that code training improves a model's ability to perform mathematical reasoning both with and without the use of tools. This provides a partial answer to a long-standing question in the field: *Does code training improve general reasoning?* In the context of mathematics, the answer appears to be a resounding yes.

By combining a code-strong foundation with a massive, iteratively-mined web corpus, DeepSeekMath demonstrates that the quality and scale of data are just as critical as the training algorithms themselves.
