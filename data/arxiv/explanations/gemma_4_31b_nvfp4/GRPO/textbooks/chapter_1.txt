\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 1: # Introduction to Mathematical Reasoning in Large Language Models

Mathematical reasoning represents one of the most formidable challenges in the development of large language models (LLMs). Unlike general language tasks, which often rely on pattern recognition and linguistic fluency, mathematical reasoning requires the model to navigate complex and structured environments. The difficulty arises from the fact that math is not merely about predicting the next word in a sequence, but about adhering to strict logical rules where a single error in a multi-step derivation can lead to an entirely incorrect conclusion. This structural complexity necessitates a different approach to training and evaluation than that used for standard natural language understanding.

# The complexity of mathematical reasoning vs. general language tasks

The primary distinction between general language tasks and mathematical reasoning lies in the nature of the output and the process required to reach it. General language tasks often involve summarizing text, sentiment analysis, or creative writing, where the model can rely on the statistical likelihood of word associations. In contrast, mathematical reasoning is inherently structured and requires the model to maintain logical consistency across a series of operations. Because mathematical problems are often multi-step, the model must possess the ability to perform "reasoning," which involves breaking down a complex problem into smaller, manageable components and solving them sequentially.

To address this complexity, researchers utilize specific reasoning frameworks. One such approach is Chain-of-Thought (CoT), where the model generates a step-by-step textual explanation to arrive at an answer. Another is Program-of-Thought (PoT), where the model writes a program—typically in Python—to handle the computations, effectively delegating the "arithmetic" to a tool and focusing its internal capabilities on the "logic" of the problem. There is also tool-integrated reasoning, which combines natural language reasoning with external tool use. These methods highlight that mathematical reasoning is not a monolithic task but a composite of logical planning and precise execution, making it significantly more demanding than standard linguistic prediction.

# Evaluation benchmarks: GSM8K, MATH, and competition-level reasoning

To quantify the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, the field relies on a variety of benchmarks that span different levels of difficulty and domains. At the foundational level is GSM8K, which focuses on grade-school mathematics. These problems typically require basic arithmetic and simple multi-step logic, serving as a baseline for whether a model can handle elementary quantitative reasoning. While GSM8K is essential, it does not capture the depth of reasoning required for advanced mathematics.

For more rigorous testing, the MATH benchmark is employed. This is a competition-level dataset that requires much more sophisticated reasoning and covers a wide array of mathematical fields. Achieving high accuracy on the MATH benchmark indicates that a model can handle problems similar to those found in high school mathematics competitions, necessitating a higher degree of logical precision and a deeper understanding of mathematical concepts. Beyond these, researchers use benchmarks like SAT, OCW Courses, and MMLU-STEM to test general collegiate-level knowledge.

Reasoning is also evaluated across different languages and formats. For instance, benchmarks such as CMATH and Gaokao-MathQA assess a model's ability to solve problems in Chinese, ensuring that the model's reasoning is not limited to English-centric data. Furthermore, the field distinguishes between informal mathematics—where proofs are written in natural language—and formal mathematics. Formal mathematics involves using proof assistants like Isabelle to verify the correctness of a proof. Benchmarks like miniF2F are used to evaluate a model's ability to perform autoformalization, which is the process of converting an informal mathematical statement and its proof into a formal, machine-verifiable language.

# The gap between open-source models and proprietary models (GPT-4, Gemini-Ultra)

In the current AI landscape, there is a significant performance disparity between proprietary, closed-source models and those available to the open-source community. Cutting-edge proprietary models, such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra, have demonstrated a high level of proficiency in mathematical reasoning, often approaching human-like capabilities in their ability to solve complex problems. However, the weights and specific training methodologies of these models are not publicly available, creating a "black box" that hinders academic research and open-source development.

Open-source models have historically trailed behind these proprietary giants, particularly in competition-level reasoning. While many open-source models can perform well on basic tasks like GSM8K, they often struggle with the rigorous requirements of the MATH benchmark. This gap is often attributed to differences in the quality and scale of the pre-training data, as well as the sophistication of the alignment and reinforcement learning processes used by proprietary labs. The goal of projects like DeepSeekMath is to bridge this gap, demonstrating that a smaller, open-source model can approach the performance of GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra through meticulous data selection and innovative training algorithms.

# Overview of the DeepSeekMath architecture and training stages

DeepSeekMath is designed as a domain-specific language model tailored for mathematical reasoning. The development of the model follows a structured, multi-stage pipeline: initialization, pre-training, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and reinforcement learning (RL).

The process begins with initialization. Instead of starting from a general-purpose language model, DeepSeekMath is initialized with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B. This choice is based on the observation that training on code before training on mathematics significantly improves a model's reasoning abilities, both when the model is solving problems through text and when it is using programming tools. Following initialization, the model undergoes a massive pre-training phase using the DeepSeekMath Corpus, which consists of 120B high-quality math tokens sourced from Common Crawl. This corpus is augmented with code and natural language data to ensure the model maintains general reasoning and linguistic capabilities.

After the base model is established, it undergoes Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). During this stage, the model is trained on a curated dataset of 776K examples that include solutions in CoT, PoT, and tool-integrated formats. This teaches the model how to structure its reasoning and how to interface with external tools. The final stage is Reinforcement Learning, specifically utilizing a new algorithm called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO is a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) that improves the model's accuracy by optimizing the policy based on group relative rewards, notably without the need for a computationally expensive critic model. This rigorous pipeline allows DeepSeekMath to achieve a level of mathematical proficiency that rivals much larger models.
