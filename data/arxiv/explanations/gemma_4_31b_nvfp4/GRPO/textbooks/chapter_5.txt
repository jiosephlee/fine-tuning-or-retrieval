Chapter 5: Advanced RL Strategies for Reasoning

# Outcome Supervision: Rewards based on final answer correctness

In the context of enhancing mathematical reasoning in large language models, outcome supervision serves as a fundamental reinforcement learning (RL) strategy where the model is rewarded based solely on the correctness of the final answer. Within the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) framework, this approach avoids the need for a complex critic model by instead utilizing a group-based baseline to determine the advantage of a specific response. The intuition is that by comparing multiple sampled outputs for the same question, the model can discern which reasoning paths are more likely to lead to the correct conclusion relative to others.

Formally, for any given question $q$, the model samples a group of $G$ outputs, denoted as $\{o_1, o_2, \dots, o_G\}$, from the current policy model $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. A reward model is then employed to score each of these outputs, producing a set of rewards $\mathbf{r} = \{r_1, r_2, \dots, r_G\}$. To stabilize training and provide a relative signal, these rewards are normalized. This normalization is achieved by subtracting the group average and dividing by the group standard deviation, resulting in a normalized reward $\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$.

Under outcome supervision, this normalized reward is applied as the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for every single token $t$ within the output $o_i$. This means the entire sequence of tokens is reinforced or penalized uniformly based on whether the final result was correct. While this method is effective for simpler tasks, it provides a coarse signal, as it does not distinguish between a reasoning chain that was mostly correct but failed at the final step and one that was fundamentally flawed from the beginning.

# Process Supervision: Step-by-step reward modeling for complex reasoning

Process supervision is designed to overcome the limitations of outcome supervision by providing more granular feedback throughout the reasoning process. In complex mathematical tasks, a single final reward is often insufficient to guide the model toward the correct logic. Instead of rewarding only the end result, process supervision assigns rewards at the end of each individual reasoning step. This ensures that the model is reinforced for every correct logical transition it makes, rather than just for arriving at the right answer by chance or through a flawed process.

To implement this, a process reward model scores each step $j$ within each sampled output $o_i$. This results in a set of rewards for the group, where $\mathbf{R} = \{ \{r_1^{index(1)}, \dots, r_1^{index(K_1)}\}, \dots, \{r_G^{index(1)}, \dots, r_G^{index(K_G)}\} \}$, and $index(j)$ represents the token index where the $j$-th step ends. Similar to outcome supervision, these step-level rewards are normalized using the mean and standard deviation of all rewards across the group: $\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)} = \frac{r_i^{index(j)} - \text{mean}(\mathbf{R})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{R})}$.

The advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for a specific token $t$ is then calculated as the sum of the normalized rewards from all subsequent steps. Specifically, $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$. This summation allows the model to understand the "future value" of its current token choices. By providing these fine-grained, step-aware gradient coefficients, process supervision can more efficiently guide the policy model through intricate multi-step problems, which is essential for achieving high performance on competition-level mathematics.

# Iterative RL: Updating the reward model and policy in a feedback loop

A significant challenge in reinforcement learning is the distribution shift that occurs as the policy model improves. As the policy model $\pi_{\theta}$ evolves, the samples it generates change, and the original reward model $r_{\varphi}$—which was trained on a different distribution of data—may no longer provide accurate or sufficient supervision. To address this, iterative RL creates a continuous feedback loop between the policy model and the reward model, ensuring that the reward signal remains aligned with the current capabilities of the actor.

In the iterative GRPO process, the system does not simply train the policy once. Instead, it proceeds through multiple iterations. In each iteration, the model generates new training sets for the reward model by sampling outputs from the current policy. The reward model is then updated using these new samples, which effectively "teaches" the reward model how to evaluate the current, more advanced outputs of the policy. Once the reward model is updated, it is used to reinforce the policy model again.

During this cycle, the reference model $\pi_{ref}$, used to calculate the KL divergence to prevent the policy from collapsing or deviating too far from the initial SFT model, is updated to the current policy. This iterative refinement allows the model to continually push the boundaries of its reasoning capabilities. Experimental evidence suggests that this process is particularly impactful during the first iteration, leading to substantial gains in mathematical accuracy.

# Replay mechanisms for stable reward model training

When updating the reward model during iterative RL, there is a risk that the model may overfit to the most recent samples or suffer from catastrophic forgetting of previously learned reward signals. To maintain stability and ensure the reward model remains robust across a diverse range of reasoning paths, a replay mechanism is incorporated into the training process.

The replay mechanism works by blending new data with historical data. When the reward model is continuously trained using the samples generated by the current policy model, a portion of historical training data—specifically 10%—is re-introduced into the training set. By mixing a small amount of old data with the new exploration results, the reward model is forced to remain consistent with its previous evaluations while simultaneously adapting to the new distribution of the policy's outputs.

This stability is crucial because the reward model serves as the ground truth for the gradient coefficients in the GRPO objective. If the reward model becomes unstable or inaccurate due to the lack of historical context, the policy model may be reinforced in the wrong direction. The replay mechanism ensures that the reward signal remains reliable throughout the iterative process, facilitating a steady improvement in the model's mathematical reasoning.

# Comparing outcome-based vs. process-based RL performance

Comparing outcome-based RL (GRPO+OS) and process-based RL (GRPO+PS) reveals a clear advantage in the latter. Because process supervision provides fine-grained, step-aware feedback, it allows for more precise reinforcement of correct reasoning steps and more accurate penalization of logical errors. This is reflected in the performance metrics, where GRPO+PS consistently outperforms GRPO+OS, highlighting that the ability to adjust gradient coefficients based on the specific step of a derivation is more efficient than applying a single reward to the entire output.

Furthermore, it is important to understand how RL generally affects model performance. Analysis of Pass@K and Maj@K accuracy suggests that RL does not necessarily enhance the fundamental capabilities of the model—meaning it does not necessarily increase the maximum potential accuracy (Pass@K). Instead, RL enhances the robustness of the output distribution. It improves the Maj@K performance by boosting the probability that the most frequent response among multiple samples is the correct one. 

In essence, RL corrects a "misalignment" present in the initial Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) model. While the SFT model might be capable of finding the correct answer in some of its sampled paths, RL trains the model to make those correct paths the most probable ones. This shift in the distribution, driven by the relative rewards of GRPO and the precision of process supervision, allows a 7B parameter model to approach the performance levels of much larger closed-source models on challenging benchmarks like MATH.
