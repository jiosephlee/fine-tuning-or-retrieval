\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 6: A Unified Paradigm for Alignment and RL

# The Unified Gradient Equation: Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm

To understand the various methods used to align large language models and enhance their reasoning capabilities, it is helpful to view them through a single, unified mathematical lens. The researchers propose a generalized gradient equation that describes how the parameters $\theta$ of a model are updated across different training regimes. The objective function's gradient can be expressed as $\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}] \left( \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q, o, t, \pi_{rf}) \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})\right)$. In this equation, the update to the model is determined by three fundamental components: the data source, the reward function, and the algorithm.

The first component, the data source $\mathcal{D}$, defines the pool of training data, consisting of questions $q$ and corresponding outputs $o$. The second component, the reward function $\pi_{rf}$, serves as the source of the training signal, providing the "feedback" that tells the model whether a particular output is desirable or not. Finally, the algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ is the mechanism that processes the data and the reward signal to produce a gradient coefficient $GC$. This coefficient is crucial because it determines the magnitude and direction—either penalty or reinforcement—of the update for each token in the output sequence. By decomposing alignment methods into these three parts, we can see that every technique is essentially a different way of choosing where the data comes from, how success is measured, and how that measurement is translated into a parameter update.

# Comparing SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO within the paradigm

Within this unified paradigm, we can categorize several popular training methods by examining their specific choices of data sources and algorithms. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) is the most straightforward approach, where the model is fine-tuned on a static dataset of human-selected, high-quality examples. In this case, the data source is fixed and provided by humans, and the goal is simply to maximize the likelihood of these gold-standard responses.

Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) represent a shift toward using the model's own generated outputs. In RFT, the SFT model samples multiple outputs for a given question, and only those that are correct (filtered based on the answer's correctness) are used for further fine-tuning. DPO takes a similar approach by using augmented outputs sampled from the SFT model, but it employs a pair-wise loss to refine the model's preferences. Both RFT and DPO are considered offline methods because they rely on data sampled from the initial SFT model rather than the model as it is currently being updated.

Online Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (Online RFT), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) move the process into the online domain. Unlike their offline counterparts, these methods use a real-time policy model to sample outputs during training. While Online RFT simply fine-tunes the model on correct responses sampled from the current policy, PPO and GRPO use more complex reinforcement learning algorithms to optimize the policy based on a reward signal, allowing for a more nuanced update than simple correctness filtering.

# Online vs. Offline Sampling: The superiority of real-time exploration

A critical distinction in the unified paradigm is whether the data source $\mathcal{D}$ is derived from offline sampling or online sampling. Offline sampling involves using a snapshot of the model—usually the initial SFT model—to generate the training data. Online sampling, however, means that the training data is generated by the real-time training policy model. The research demonstrates that online training is significantly more effective than offline training, particularly as the training process progresses.

In the early stages of training, the real-time actor and the initial SFT model are very similar, meaning the data they sample is nearly identical. However, as the model improves, the outputs produced by the current policy begin to differ significantly from those of the original SFT model. By sampling from the current model, the training process can explore the "frontier" of the model's evolving capabilities. Real-time exploration allows the algorithm to identify and reinforce the specific types of reasoning paths the current model is attempting, rather than being limited by the distribution of the static SFT model. Consequently, Online RFT outperforms standard RFT, especially in the later stages of training, highlighting the necessity of real-time exploration for maximum performance.

# Gradient Coefficients: How GRPO handles positive and negative reinforcement differently than RFT

The gradient coefficient $GC$ is the mechanism that determines how the reward signal actually changes the model's weights. The research distinguishes between using a "Rule" (e.g., checking if the final answer is correct) and a "Model" (a neural reward model trained to score responses) to generate this signal. A key difference emerges when comparing GRPO to Online RFT.

Online RFT treats the reward signal in a relatively binary fashion. It identifies responses with correct answers and reinforces them uniformly, while it does not explicitly penalize incorrect responses. In other words, Online RFT focuses only on positive reinforcement of correct outcomes without utilizing a nuanced scale of "how correct" or "how incorrect" a response is.

In contrast, GRPO utilizes the reward model to adjust its gradient coefficient based on the actual value of the reward. This allows GRPO to perform differential reinforcement and penalization. If a response is significantly better than the average of a sampled group, it receives strong positive reinforcement. If it is worse, it receives a penalty. By adjusting the magnitude of the update according to the reward value, GRPO can more efficiently guide the model toward optimal reasoning paths. This capability to both reward and penalize based on relative performance is a major reason why GRPO surpasses Online RFT in efficiency and final performance.

# The 'Misalignment' problem: Understanding Maj@K vs. Pass@K performance

To understand why reinforcement learning (RL) is so effective, the researchers analyzed the model's performance using two different metrics: Pass@K and Maj@K. Pass@K measures the probability that at least one of the $K$ sampled outputs is correct, essentially testing if the model is *capable* of finding the right answer. Maj@K measures the accuracy of the majority vote among $K$ sampled outputs, testing how *consistently* the model chooses the correct answer.

The findings reveal a fascinating phenomenon: RL significantly enhances Maj@K performance but does not notably improve Pass@K. This suggests that RL does not necessarily increase the model's fundamental capability to solve a problem that it previously could not solve. Instead, RL addresses a "misalignment" problem. The SFT model often has the correct reasoning capability (as evidenced by Pass@K), but its output distribution is not robust; it might produce the correct answer only occasionally among many incorrect ones. 

RL works by reshaping the output distribution, boosting the probability of the correct responses that were already present in the Top-K samples. By making the model more consistent in its choice of the correct path, RL improves the model's reliability and overall accuracy without needing to "teach" it entirely new mathematical concepts. The improvement is therefore attributed to rendering the output distribution more robust rather than expanding the base capabilities of the model.
