\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: Scaling Mathematical Pre-training

# The DeepSeekMath Corpus: Sourcing 120B tokens from Common Crawl

The foundation of a language model's mathematical reasoning capability lies heavily in the quality and quantity of the data it encounters during pre-training. In the development of DeepSeekMath, the researchers recognized that while curated mathematical datasets exist, there is a vast amount of untapped mathematical knowledge residing in the general web. To harness this, they constructed the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a massive collection of 120B mathematical tokens extracted from Common Crawl. This corpus represents a significant scaling effort, as it is approximately seven times the size of the mathematical web pages utilized by Minerva and nine times the size of the OpenWebMath dataset.

The scale of this corpus is critical because mathematical reasoning is a complex, structured task that requires the model to see a wide variety of problem-solving patterns and formal notations. By extracting 120B tokens, the DeepSeekMath Corpus provides the model with a much denser and more diverse set of examples than previously available in open-source mathematical pre-training. The researchers found that a smaller model trained on such a high-quality, large-scale corpus could achieve performance comparable to much larger models, suggesting that data quality and volume can be as influential as parameter count in developing reasoning abilities.

# Iterative Data Selection Pipeline: Using fastText classifiers and human annotation

To transform the raw, noisy data of Common Crawl into a high-quality mathematical corpus, the researchers implemented a meticulously engineered iterative data selection pipeline. The process began with a seed corpus, specifically OpenWebMath, which served as the initial set of high-quality mathematical web texts. Using this seed, they trained a fastText classifier to distinguish between mathematical and non-mathematical web pages. This classifier was trained using 500,000 data points from the seed corpus as positive examples and 500,000 random web pages from Common Crawl as negative examples. The classifier used a vector dimension of 256, a learning rate of 0.1, and a maximum word n-gram length of 3.

Once the classifier was trained, it was used to recall mathematical web pages from a deduplicated version of Common Crawl consisting of 40B HTML pages. To ensure high quality, only the top-ranking pages—those with the highest predicted scores from the fastText model—were preserved. However, the researchers noted that a single iteration often lacked diversity because the initial seed corpus was limited. To solve this, they introduced a domain-based discovery mechanism. They organized Common Crawl into disjoint domains based on base URLs. Any domain where more than 10\% of the pages were collected in the first iteration was flagged as math-related, such as $\text{mathoverflow.net}$.

Following the identification of these domains, human annotators manually identified specific URLs within those domains that contained mathematical content. These newly discovered positive instances were added back into the seed corpus to retrain and improve the fastText classifier. This cycle of recalling, human refining, and retraining was performed over four iterations. By the fourth iteration, the collection process had reached a point of diminishing returns, with 98\% of the data already captured in the third iteration. Ultimately, this pipeline yielded 35.5M mathematical web pages totaling 120B tokens.

# Data Decontamination: Preventing benchmark leakage

A critical challenge in training large-scale models is benchmark contamination, where the model inadvertently sees the questions or answers of the evaluation sets during its pre-training phase. This leads to inflated performance scores that reflect memorization rather than true reasoning capability. To prevent this, the DeepSeekMath team implemented a rigorous decontamination process for the English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks, including GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, and AGIEval.

The decontamination strategy relied on exact string matching using n-grams. For any text segment in the training corpus, the researchers searched for 10-gram strings that matched exactly with any substring from the evaluation benchmarks. If a match was found, the corresponding web page was removed. For shorter benchmark texts—those with fewer than 10 grams but at least 3 grams—the team used exact matching to ensure that even brief but unique problem statements were filtered out. This ensured that the model's performance on academic benchmarks was a genuine reflection of its ability to generalize and reason.

# The role of code training: Why initializing with DeepSeek-Coder helps mathematical reasoning

The researchers explored the hypothesis that training on computer code enhances a model's general reasoning abilities, specifically in the mathematical domain. To test this, DeepSeekMath-Base was initialized using DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B rather than a general-purpose language model. This decision was based on the observation that the structured nature of code—which requires strict logic, syntax, and sequential execution—mirrors the structured requirements of mathematical proof and problem solving.

Through a series of experiments, the team compared two-stage training (where the model is trained on code first, then on math) against one-stage training (where code and math are mixed). They found that code training significantly benefits program-aided mathematical reasoning. In the two-stage setting, the initial phase of code training alone provided moderate enhancements to the model's ability to solve GSM8K and MATH problems using Python. Subsequent math training further amplified these gains, leading to the best overall performance. Interestingly, code training also improved mathematical reasoning even when the model was not using external tools, suggesting that the logical patterns learned from code translate to a more efficient learning process for mathematics.

However, the researchers noted a trade-off in one-stage training. While mixing code and math tokens helped prevent the "catastrophic forgetting" of coding skills that occurs in two-stage training, it actually compromised mathematical reasoning when tool use was disallowed. They conjecture that for a model of 7B parameters, there may be a limited capacity to simultaneously assimilate the distinct patterns of both code and mathematics without sacrificing the purity of the reasoning process.

# Analysis of data sources: The surprising ineffectiveness of arXiv papers

In many mathematical LLM research papers, training on arXiv scientific papers is considered a standard practice. However, the DeepSeekMath researchers conducted a detailed analysis to determine if this data actually contributes to reasoning capabilities. They tested models of different sizes, including a 1.3B and a 7B parameter model, using two different arXiv-heavy corpora: MathPile, which consists largely of scientific arXiv papers, and ArXiv-RedPajama, which contains arXiv LaTeX files with macros and bibliographies removed.

Contrary to common expectations, the results indicated that training on arXiv papers was largely ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning. When trained on arXiv-only corpora, the models showed no notable improvements and, in some cases, even experienced a deterioration in performance across various benchmarks. This lack of improvement was observed across quantitative reasoning tasks like GSM8K and MATH, multiple-choice challenges like MMLU-STEM, and even formal mathematics tasks on the miniF2F benchmark. While they acknowledged that arXiv data might still be useful for specific tasks not tested—such as converting formal statements to informal versions—their findings suggest that for core mathematical reasoning, web-mined data is far more impactful than scientific paper repositories.

# Multilingual mathematical data and its impact on cross-lingual reasoning

Most existing mathematical corpora, such as Proof-Pile-2, are heavily English-centric. The researchers of DeepSeekMath intentionally diverged from this approach by ensuring that their pre-training corpus was multilingual, with English and Chinese being the most represented languages. This was achieved by mining mathematical content from the global nature of Common Crawl and specifically incorporating high-quality non-English data.

The impact of this multilingual approach was evident in the model's evaluation. Training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus led to significant performance gains in both English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks. In contrast, models trained on English-only corpora often showed limited improvement or even a decrease in performance when tasked with Chinese mathematical reasoning. This demonstrates that mathematical reasoning is not limited to a single language and that providing a model with diverse, multilingual examples of mathematical logic allows it to develop a more robust and flexible reasoning capability that transcends linguistic boundaries.
