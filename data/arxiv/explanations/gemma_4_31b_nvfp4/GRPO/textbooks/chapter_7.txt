\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 7: Analysis, Limitations, and Future Directions

# Does RL improve fundamental capability or output distribution?

A central question in the development of mathematical language models is whether reinforcement learning (RL) actually teaches the model new mathematical concepts or if it simply refines how the model presents information it already possesses. To understand this, we must look at the distinction between Pass@$K$ and Maj@$K$ accuracies. Pass@$K$ measures the probability that at least one correct answer is found within $K$ generated samples, while Maj@$K$ measures the accuracy of the majority vote across those $K$ samples.

In the analysis of DeepSeekMath, it was observed that reinforcement learning significantly enhances Maj@$K$ performance but does not notably improve Pass@$K$. This distinction provides a critical intuition: RL does not necessarily expand the model's fundamental capabilities—meaning it doesn't suddenly enable the model to solve problems it was previously incapable of solving in any of its attempts. Instead, RL modifies the output distribution. It makes the model's reasoning more robust by increasing the probability that the correct response is the most frequent one among the top samples. Essentially, the model may have already had the "knowledge" to solve the problem (as indicated by a stable Pass@$K$), but RL aligns the model's preferences so that it consistently chooses the correct path.

This phenomenon suggests a misalignment problem inherent in models that have only undergone supervised fine-tuning (SFT). In SFT, the model learns to mimic specific gold-standard trajectories. However, this does not guarantee that the model will consistently prefer the correct reasoning path during independent generation. RL acts as a correction mechanism, boosting the correct responses from the top candidates. By rendering the output distribution more robust, RL ensures that the model's internal capabilities are more reliably surfaced during inference.

# Current limitations: Geometry, theorem proving, and few-shot capabilities

Despite the impressive benchmarks achieved by DeepSeekMath, the model exhibits specific weaknesses when compared to top-tier proprietary models. One of the most prominent limitations is in the domains of geometry and formal theorem proving. During experimental "dry runs," the model struggled significantly with problems involving specific geometric shapes, such as triangles and ellipses. This indicates that while the model is highly capable of quantitative reasoning, it lacks the specialized spatial or structural intuition required for complex geometry. These failures suggest a potential data selection bias in both the pre-training and fine-tuning stages, where the corpus may not have contained sufficient high-quality examples of geometric reasoning.

Furthermore, DeepSeekMath shows a notable gap in few-shot capabilities when compared to models like GPT-4. In a few-shot setting, a model is provided with a few examples of a problem and its solution before being asked to solve a new problem. Proprietary models often show significant performance gains as the number of few-shot examples increases. In contrast, DeepSeekMath demonstrates similar performance in both zero-shot and few-shot evaluations. This suggests that the model is less capable of "in-context learning," meaning it cannot as effectively leverage the patterns or logic provided in a prompt to improve its immediate performance.

These limitations highlight the difference between a model that is highly optimized for a specific benchmark and one that possesses a generalized, flexible mathematical intelligence. While the base and RL-tuned models are powerful, the lack of progress in few-shot learning and the struggle with geometry point to a need for more diverse and specialized training data to achieve true parity with the most advanced closed-source systems.

# Future Directions: Advanced decoding and Weak-to-Strong alignment

To move beyond the current plateau, future research must address the "Data Source" and "Algorithm" components of the learning paradigm. Currently, the RL process relies on naive nucleus sampling to generate outputs for training. To truly enhance fundamental capabilities, researchers are looking toward advanced sampling and decoding strategies. One such approach is the use of tree-search methods, which allow the model to explore multiple reasoning paths more systematically rather than relying on a single linear sequence of tokens. By combining this with efficient inference techniques to increase exploration speed, the model could discover a wider variety of correct reasoning paths, potentially improving its Pass@$K$ and fundamental problem-solving ability.

Another critical area of growth is the development of algorithms that are robust against noisy reward signals. In current RL setups, the model "trusts" the reward function entirely. However, reward functions are often imperfect; for example, the PRM800K dataset—despite being carefully annotated—contains approximately $20\%$ incorrect annotations. If a model is trained on a noisy reward signal, it may learn to optimize for "reward hacking" rather than actual correctness.

To combat this, the concept of "weak-to-strong" alignment is being explored. This approach seeks to develop learning algorithms that can derive strong, accurate reasoning capabilities even when the supervising signal (the reward model) is relatively weak or imperfect. By creating a system that does not blindly follow the reward signal but instead treats it as a noisy guide, models can achieve a more stable and truthful form of alignment.

# Improving Reward Model generalization and uncertainty estimation

The effectiveness of reinforcement learning is fundamentally capped by the quality of the reward function. To advance the state of the art, reward models must evolve in three specific directions: generalization, uncertainty estimation, and granularity. First, reward models must generalize better to handle out-of-distribution (OOD) questions and complex outputs generated by advanced decoding strategies. If a reward model cannot accurately score a novel approach to a problem, the RL process will merely stabilize the existing distribution of the model rather than pushing it to discover new, more efficient reasoning methods.

Second, there is a need for reward models to reflect their own uncertainty. A reward model that provides a score without indicating its confidence level can mislead the policy model. If the reward model can communicate that it is "unsure" about a particular output, this uncertainty can serve as a bridge to the aforementioned weak-to-strong learning algorithms, allowing the policy model to weigh the reward signal more cautiously.

Finally, the transition from outcome supervision to process supervision is vital. Outcome supervision only provides a reward at the end of a solution, which is often too coarse for complex, multi-step math problems. Process reward models (PRMs) provide fine-grained, step-aware signals, rewarding the model for each correct logical leap. Building high-quality, large-scale process reward models is a primary goal for future iterations of mathematical LLMs, as it provides the precise feedback necessary to correct specific logical fallacies in a model's chain-of-thought.

# Conclusion: The path toward open-source mathematical parity

The journey of DeepSeekMath demonstrates that the gap between open-source and proprietary mathematical models can be narrowed through a combination of high-quality data engineering and algorithmic efficiency. By utilizing a meticulously filtered corpus of $120\text{B}$ tokens from Common Crawl and introducing Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the project proved that a $7\text{B}$ parameter model could approach the performance of much larger, closed-source models on benchmarks like MATH.

The path forward involves moving beyond the mere stabilization of output distributions. By implementing advanced tree-search decoding, addressing the noise in reward signals through weak-to-strong alignment, and developing process-based reward models that generalize to out-of-distribution tasks, the research community can move toward models that do not just mimic correctness but possess fundamental mathematical reasoning. The success of DeepSeekMath suggests that the key to parity lies not necessarily in increasing model size, but in the relentless pursuit of data quality and the refinement of the alignment process.
