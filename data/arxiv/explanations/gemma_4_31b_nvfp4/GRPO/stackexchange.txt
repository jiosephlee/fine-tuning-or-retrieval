\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Difference between Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Program-of-Thought (PoT) in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
I am reading the Supervised Fine-Tuning section of the DeepSeekMath paper. The authors mention that they used a mixture of Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Program-of-Thought (PoT), and tool-integrated reasoning formats for their 776K training examples. I understand that CoT involves step-by-step natural language reasoning, but I'm not entirely clear on what constitutes a 'Program-of-Thought' approach and how it differs from 'tool-integrated reasoning'. Could someone clarify these three distinctions?
Answer:
In the context of the DeepSeekMath paper, these three formats represent a spectrum of how a model handles the transition from a mathematical problem to a final answer, moving from purely linguistic reasoning to purely algorithmic execution, and finally to a hybrid of both.

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) is the most straightforward approach, where the model generates a sequence of natural language steps to arrive at a solution. Intuitively, this is like a student "thinking out loud" or writing out their work on a chalkboard. The reasoning is entirely contained within the language model's own weights; the model predicts the next token based on the linguistic logic of the previous steps. While powerful, CoT is prone to "calculation errors" because the model is essentially simulating arithmetic through token prediction rather than performing actual math.

Program-of-Thought (PoT) shifts the burden of computation from the model's linguistic capabilities to an external execution environment. Instead of writing out the steps in English, the model generates a program—specifically Python code in the case of DeepSeekMath—to solve the problem. As noted in the paper, the model leverages libraries such as $\textit{math}$ and $\textit{sympy}$ for intricate computations. The intuitive difference here is that the model acts as a programmer rather than a mathematician; it defines the logic in code, and the final answer is the output of the program's execution. This eliminates the common arithmetic hallucinations found in CoT, as the precision is guaranteed by the Python interpreter.

Tool-integrated reasoning is a hybrid paradigm that combines the strengths of both CoT and PoT. Rather than choosing between a text-only solution or a code-only solution, the model interleaves natural language reasoning with tool calls. For example, it might use natural language to strategize the approach or interpret a geometric property and then switch to a Python snippet to solve a complex equation. This mirrors how a human mathematician actually works: using a mix of conceptual intuition (natural language) and a calculator or computer algebra system (tools) to ensure accuracy. In the paper, this is used to further enhance the model's performance on benchmarks like MATH, allowing it to approach a higher accuracy by integrating the flexibility of language with the rigor of code.

### Why did training on arXiv papers fail to improve mathematical reasoning in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
In section 5.1.2, the authors state that arXiv papers seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning, noting that training on MathPile and ArXiv-RedPajama showed no notable improvements or even deterioration. This is surprising to me because arXiv is the primary source of high-level mathematical discourse. Is there a theoretical reason why formal scientific papers would be less useful for LLM reasoning compared to the web-scraped data in the DeepSeekMath Corpus?
Answer:
The finding that arXiv papers were ineffective for improving mathematical reasoning in DeepSeekMath is indeed counter-intuitive given that arXiv is the gold standard for professional mathematical research. However, the authors' results suggest a distinction between "high-level mathematical knowledge" and "mathematical reasoning capability."

In their experiments, the authors trained both a $1.3\text{B}$ and a $7\text{B}$ parameter model on arXiv-heavy corpora, specifically MathPile (which is over $85\%$ arXiv papers) and ArXiv-RedPajama (the entirety of arXiv LaTeX files). They observed that training on these arXiv-only datasets led to no notable improvements, and in some cases, even a deterioration in performance across benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH, and MMLU-STEM.

The theoretical reason for this discrepancy lies in the nature of the data. Academic papers on arXiv are written for experts; they are designed to present novel results and proofs efficiently. In this professional discourse, authors frequently omit intermediate algebraic steps or use phrases like "it is trivial to see that," which creates a "gap" in the reasoning chain. For a language model to learn *how* to reason (specifically the step-by-step Chain-of-Thought process), it needs dense, pedagogical examples where every logical leap is explicitly documented.

In contrast, the DeepSeekMath Corpus is sourced from the web (Common Crawl). Web-based mathematical content—such as forum discussions on MathOverflow or educational tutorials—tends to be much more discursive. These sources often break down complex problems into smaller, digestible steps to help other learners, providing the exact kind of "reasoning traces" that enable a model to solve competition-level problems.

It is important to note that the authors provide a few caveats. They suggest that arXiv data might still be useful for tasks not covered in their benchmarks, such as the "informalization of theorems" (converting formal proofs back into natural language). They also acknowledge that the benefits of arXiv papers might only manifest at a larger model scale or when mixed with other data types, rather than being used as a standalone training set.

### How does Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) estimate the baseline without a critic model?
Question:
I'm struggling with the transition from PPO to GRPO. In standard PPO, the advantage $A_t$ is computed using a learned value function $V_{\psi}$ (the critic). The paper says GRPO "foregoes the critic model" and instead uses the average reward of multiple sampled outputs for the same question as the baseline. Looking at the objective function in Equation 3, I don't see the explicit subtraction of a baseline. How is the "relative" advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ actually calculated from the group scores to achieve variance reduction without a separate network?
Answer:
In standard PPO, the critic model $V_{\psi}$ serves as a baseline by estimating the expected return from a given state. By subtracting this baseline from the actual observed return, PPO computes the advantage $A_t$, which tells the model whether a specific action was better or worse than the "average" expected behavior. This process is crucial for variance reduction; without it, the gradient updates would be subject to the high variance of raw reward signals.

GRPO replaces this learned value function with a group-based statistical baseline. Instead of training a separate network to guess the average reward for a prompt, GRPO samples a group of $G$ outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \dots, o_G\}$ from the current policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$ for the same question $q$. The "relative" advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is then computed by comparing the reward of a specific output $r_i$ against the empirical mean and standard deviation of the rewards within that specific group. 

In the case of outcome supervision, the advantage for all tokens in an output is simply the normalized reward:
$$\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$$
where $\mathbf{r} = \{r_1, r_2, \dots, r_G\}$ is the set of rewards for the group. This normalization is the "explicit subtraction of the baseline" you were looking for; the $\text{mean}(\mathbf{r})$ acts as the baseline, and the division by $\text{std}(\mathbf{r})$ scales the advantage. When this $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is plugged into the objective function in Equation 3, it provides the necessary signal to reinforce outputs that performed better than the group average and penalize those that performed worse.

For more complex tasks, the paper also mentions process supervision, where rewards are given at each reasoning step. In this setting, the rewards for each step across the group are normalized similarly, and the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for a token is calculated as the sum of the normalized rewards from that token's position until the end of the sequence: $\hat{A}_{i, t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$.

Intuitively, this approach is highly effective because reward models are typically trained on pairwise comparisons (preference data). By using a group relative baseline, GRPO mimics this comparative nature, effectively asking "which of these $G$ attempts is the best?" rather than "how good is this attempt in an absolute sense?". This not only reduces the memory overhead by eliminating the critic model but also provides a more stable and grounded baseline based on the actual current distribution of the policy's outputs.

### Why does RL improve $\text{Maj@K}$ but not $\text{Pass@K}$ in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
In the 'Why RL Works?' section (5.2.2), the authors provide a graph showing that reinforcement learning improves $\text{Maj@K}$ accuracy but does not significantly improve $\text{Pass@K}$. They conclude that RL makes the output distribution more robust by boosting the correct response from the TopK, rather than enhancing fundamental capabilities. I don't quite understand this. If the model is now more likely to sample the correct answer (increasing $\text{Maj@K}$), hasn't its fundamental ability to solve the problem actually improved?
Answer:
To understand why RL improves $\text{Maj@K}$ but not $\text{Pass@K}$, it is first necessary to distinguish what these two metrics actually measure regarding a model's probability distribution over possible answers.

$\text{Pass@K}$ measures the probability that at least one of the $K$ sampled outputs is correct. This acts as a proxy for the model's "fundamental capability": if a model can solve a problem in $K$ attempts, the knowledge required to reach the correct answer exists somewhere within its distribution. If $\text{Pass@K}$ remains stagnant after RL, it implies that the RL process is not teaching the model how to solve new, harder problems that it previously could not solve at all.

$\text{Maj@K}$ (often associated with self-consistency) measures the accuracy of the majority vote among $K$ samples. This is a measure of "robustness" or "calibration." For $\text{Maj@K}$ to improve while $\text{Pass@K}$ stays the same, the model must already have been capable of producing the correct answer occasionally (keeping $\text{Pass@K}$ constant), but it was doing so infrequently or inconsistently. RL then "boosts" the probability mass of those existing correct paths, making the correct answer the most frequent one in the sample group.

The authors describe this as addressing a "misalignment problem" inherent in Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). In an SFT model, the distribution may be "spread out," where the correct reasoning path is present but not dominant. RL acts as a filter that concentrates the probability distribution around the correct responses that were already present in the $\text{Top-}K$.

Intuitively, you can think of it this way: the model already had the "idea" of the correct solution (fundamental capability), but it was "unsure" and frequently sampled incorrect paths. RL didn't give the model a new "idea" (which would have increased $\text{Pass@K}$), but it taught the model to be more confident in the correct "idea" it already had, thereby increasing the consistency of its output (increasing $\text{Maj@K}$).

### Understanding the 'Unified Paradigm': How does the Gradient Coefficient $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ relate to different RL algorithms?
Question:
The paper introduces a unified paradigm in Equation 8 to compare SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO, where the gradient is expressed as: $\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}] \left( \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q, o, t, \pi_{rf}) \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t}) \right)$. I'm confused about how to map specific algorithms into this general formula. Specifically, what is the $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ for Online RFT versus GRPO, and how does this coefficient explain why GRPO is more efficient at penalizing incorrect responses?
Answer:
The unified paradigm presented in Equation 8 simplifies various fine-tuning and reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms by expressing their gradients in a common form. In this framework, the gradient is essentially the log-probability of the output tokens $\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})$, scaled by a weight called the Gradient Coefficient, $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$. Intuitively, $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ acts as a signal that tells the model whether to increase the probability of a specific response (if $GC_{\mathcal{A}} > 0$), decrease it (if $GC_{\mathcal{A}} < 0$), or ignore it (if $GC_{\mathcal{A}} = 0$).

To understand the difference between Online RFT and GRPO, we look at how they determine this coefficient:

In **Online Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (Online RFT)**, the $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ behaves like a binary filter. The model samples outputs, and if an output is correct (based on a rule or reward model), it is treated as a positive example for supervised fine-tuning. In this case, $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ is essentially a constant positive value for correct responses and zero for incorrect ones. Consequently, Online RFT only performs "positive reinforcement"—it encourages the model to produce correct answers but does not actively penalize the production of incorrect ones.

In **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, the $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ is derived from the group relative advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$. For a group of $G$ sampled outputs, GRPO computes the rewards $\{r_1, \dots, r_G\}$ and normalizes them by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation:
$$\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$$
In the case of outcome supervision, the gradient coefficient is this normalized reward: $GC_{\mathcal{A}} \approx \hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i$. 

This relative approach is why GRPO is more efficient at penalizing incorrect responses. Because of the normalization, any response that performs worse than the group average will have a negative $\widetilde{r}_i$, leading to a negative $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$. A negative coefficient flips the sign of the gradient, effectively instructing the model to reduce the probability of those tokens. While Online RFT simply ignores wrong answers, GRPO actively pushes the model away from them.

Broadly, this illustrates the transition from "filtering" (RFT) to "relative optimization" (GRPO). By using the group mean as a baseline, GRPO eliminates the need for a separate, memory-intensive critic model (as required in PPO) while still providing the nuanced positive and negative signals necessary to refine complex mathematical reasoning.
