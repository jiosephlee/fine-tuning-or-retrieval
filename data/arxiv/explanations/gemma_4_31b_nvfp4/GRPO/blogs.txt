\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Can a 7B Model Really Beat the Giants? Meet DeepSeekMath

For a long time, there has been a noticeable gap in mathematical reasoning between closed-source industry giants like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra and the available open-source community. While larger models often dominate benchmarks through sheer scale, the team behind DeepSeekMath has demonstrated that strategic data selection and innovative training can allow a much smaller model to punch way above its weight class. DeepSeekMath 7B achieves a score of $51.7\%$ on the competition-level MATH benchmark, bringing it remarkably close to the performance of the world's most powerful proprietary models.

## Scaling Math Pre-training

The foundation of DeepSeekMath is the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a massive dataset of 120 billion math-related tokens sourced from Common Crawl. Instead of relying on a static dataset, the researchers developed an iterative data selection pipeline. They started with a seed corpus (OpenWebMath) and trained a fastText-based classifier to identify similar mathematical content across the web. By repeatedly refining this classifier through human annotation and domain-based filtering, they were able to extract high-quality data at a scale nearly seven times larger than that used by Minerva.

Interestingly, the researchers discovered that while arXiv papers are a staple in many math-centric models, they provided no notable improvements across the benchmarks used in this study. Instead, the real breakthrough came from the model's initialization. DeepSeekMath-Base was initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B. The team found that starting with a model already trained on code significantly improves mathematical reasoning capabilities, both when the model is solving problems through natural language and when it is using tools like Python.

## From Instruction Tuning to RL

After pre-training, the model underwent Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) using a variety of formats, including chain-of-thought (CoT), program-of-thought (PoT), and tool-integrated reasoning. This produced DeepSeekMath-Instruct, which already outperformed most 7B counterparts and rivaled some 70B models. However, the most significant gains came from the reinforcement learning (RL) phase.

The researchers introduced Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a streamlined variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). In standard PPO, a "critic" model is required to estimate a baseline for the advantage function, which consumes a massive amount of memory and computational power because the critic is typically as large as the policy model itself. GRPO eliminates the critic model entirely. Instead, it samples a group of outputs for each question and uses the average reward of that group as the baseline.

To keep the model stable, GRPO regularizes the training by adding the KL divergence between the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$ and a reference policy $\pi_{ref}$ directly to the loss function. This efficiency allows the model to be reinforced using only a subset of instruction-tuning data while still achieving substantial gains in both in-domain and out-of-domain mathematical tasks.

## Why It Works and What It Means

The results are striking: DeepSeekMath-RL 7B outperforms Minerva 540B—a model 77 times its size—on several benchmarks. The team analyzed why RL provides such a boost and found that it doesn't necessarily increase the model's fundamental ability to find a correct answer (the Pass@K metric). Instead, RL makes the model's output distribution more robust, meaning the model is more likely to generate the correct answer as its primary response (improving the Maj@K metric).

DeepSeekMath serves as a powerful reminder that model size isn't the only path to intelligence. By prioritizing high-quality, domain-specific data and optimizing the RL process to be more computationally efficient, the gap between open-source and proprietary models continues to shrink.



# Mining the Web for Math: The Secret Behind the DeepSeekMath Corpus

Mathematical reasoning is one of the steepest challenges for large language models (LLMs). While proprietary models like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra have set a high bar, open-source models have historically struggled to keep pace. The team behind DeepSeekMath has bridged this gap with DeepSeekMath 7B, a model that achieves a 51.7% score on the competition-level MATH benchmark without external tools.

The secret to this performance isn't just in the architecture or the reinforcement learning—it is in the "data alchemy" used to create the DeepSeekMath Corpus.

## The Challenge of Sourcing Math Data

High-quality mathematical data is scarce. While many researchers rely on arXiv papers or small, curated datasets, these often lack the scale or diversity needed to push a model to a professional level. DeepSeekMath takes a different approach by mining the Common Crawl (CC), a massive repository of web pages, to extract 120 billion math-related tokens. 

The challenge with the Common Crawl is the "noise." Most web pages are not mathematical, and simply keyword-searching for "math" would yield too many irrelevant results. To solve this, the team developed a meticulously engineered iterative data selection pipeline.

## The Iterative Filtering Pipeline

The process begins with a "seed corpus"—a small but high-quality set of mathematical texts, in this case, OpenWebMath. This seed is used to train a fastText classifier to distinguish between math-related and non-math-related web pages.

The pipeline follows an iterative loop to constantly improve the quality and diversity of the data:

1.  **Initial Recall**: The fastText classifier is trained using 500,000 positive examples from the seed corpus and 500,000 negative examples from the Common Crawl. It then scans the deduplicated Common Crawl to recall high-scoring mathematical pages.
2.  **Domain Identification**: To avoid missing diverse math content, the team identifies "math-related domains." If more than 10% of the pages from a specific base URL (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`) were collected in the first iteration, that domain is flagged.
3.  **Human Refinement**: Researchers manually annotate URLs within these identified domains to find additional mathematical content.
4.  **Seed Enrichment**: These newly discovered high-quality pages are added back into the seed corpus, and the fastText classifier is retrained.

After four iterations of this cycle, the team successfully collected 35.5 million mathematical web pages, totaling 120 billion tokens. To ensure the results were legitimate, they implemented a strict decontamination process, removing any text that matched 10-gram strings from common benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH.

## The ArXiv Surprise

One of the most intriguing findings in the DeepSeekMath paper is the role of arXiv papers. It is common practice in the AI community to include arXiv LaTeX files in math pre-training. However, the DeepSeek team's experiments suggested that arXiv papers may actually be ineffective for improving performance on standard mathematical benchmarks.

By training models on various arXiv-only corpora (such as MathPile and RedPajama), they observed no notable improvements—and in some cases, even deterioration—across benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH, and MMLU-STEM. While this doesn't mean arXiv data is useless—it might help with theorem informalization or prove beneficial at much larger model scales—it suggests that for general mathematical reasoning, high-quality web data is a far more powerful driver.

## The Synergy of Code and Math

The data strategy didn't stop at web scraping. The researchers found that starting from a code-pretrained model (DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B) provided a significant advantage. 

Their experiments revealed that code training improves a model's ability to perform mathematical reasoning both with and without the use of tools. This provides a partial answer to a long-standing question in the field: *Does code training improve general reasoning?* In the context of mathematics, the answer appears to be a resounding yes.

By combining a code-strong foundation with a massive, iteratively-mined web corpus, DeepSeekMath demonstrates that the quality and scale of data are just as critical as the training algorithms themselves.



# Smarter, Faster, Leaner: Understanding Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

Mathematical reasoning is one of the steepest challenges for large language models (LLMs). While general-purpose models can be impressive, the precision required for competition-level math often requires specialized training. The researchers behind DeepSeekMath have introduced a new approach to this problem, culminating in a model that approaches the performance of GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra. While their data selection pipeline is impressive, one of the most significant technical contributions is a new reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

## The Problem with Traditional RL (PPO)

To understand GRPO, we first need to look at the industry standard: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). PPO is an actor-critic algorithm. In this setup, the "actor" is the policy model we are training, and the "critic" is a separate value function model. The critic's job is to estimate the expected reward of a state, which serves as a baseline to calculate the "advantage" of a specific action.

The problem is that the critic model is typically the same size as the policy model. If you are training a 7B parameter model, you effectively need the memory and computational resources for another 7B parameter model just to act as the critic. This creates a massive memory bottleneck and increases the complexity of training, especially when trying to train an accurate value function at every single token.

## Introducing GRPO: Removing the Critic

GRPO simplifies this process by completely removing the critic model. Instead of relying on a separate neural network to estimate a baseline, GRPO uses a group-based relative approach. 

For every question $q$, the model samples a group of $G$ different outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$ from the current policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. Instead of a critic model providing a baseline, the algorithm calculates the average reward of these $G$ outputs. The "advantage" $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for a specific output is then determined by how its reward compares to the group's mean and standard deviation.

By using the group's own performance as the baseline, GRPO achieves several goals:
* **Memory Efficiency:** Because there is no critic model to maintain, the memory overhead is significantly reduced.
* **Alignment with Reward Models:** Most reward models are trained on pairwise comparisons (which answer is better than the other). GRPO's group-relative mechanism mirrors this comparative nature.
* **Stability:** GRPO regularizes the training by adding the KL divergence between the trained policy and a reference policy directly into the loss function, rather than just adding a penalty to the reward.

## Refining the Signal: Outcome vs. Process Supervision

The researchers also explored how to provide rewards to the model using GRPO. They looked at two primary methods:

* **Outcome Supervision:** The model receives a single reward at the end of its entire response. The advantage for every token in that response is set to the normalized reward of the final answer.
* **Process Supervision:** This is a more granular approach where a process reward model scores each individual reasoning step. The advantage for a token is the sum of the normalized rewards from that point forward.

They found that process supervision generally provides superior performance because it gives the model a more precise "roadmap" of where its reasoning went right or wrong, rather than just judging the final answer.

## Does it Actually Work?

The results indicate that GRPO is highly effective. Starting from the instruction-tuned DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, the RL phase using GRPO boosted the accuracy on the GSM8K benchmark from $82.9\%$ to $88.2\%$ and the competition-level MATH benchmark from $46.8\%$ to $51.7\%$. 

Interestingly, the researchers found that RL didn't necessarily increase the model's fundamental ability to find a correct solution (Pass@K accuracy remained relatively stable). Instead, it improved the "majority vote" accuracy (Maj@K). This suggests that GRPO works by making the model's output distribution more robust—essentially training the model to consistently pick the correct reasoning path from the options it already knows how to generate.

By removing the bulky critic model and leveraging group dynamics, GRPO proves that we can achieve state-of-the-art mathematical reasoning without the unsustainable memory costs of traditional RL.



# The Coding Connection: Why AI Needs to Learn Python to Master Calculus

A long-standing question in the development of Large Language Models (LLMs) is whether training a model on computer code improves its general reasoning abilities. While it might seem that Python and Calculus belong to different worlds, the researchers behind DeepSeekMath have provided compelling evidence that coding is, in fact, a powerful catalyst for mathematical reasoning.

By introducing DeepSeekMath 7B, the team explored how different training trajectories—specifically the inclusion of code—affect a model's ability to solve complex math problems. Their findings suggest that the structured logic of programming is deeply synergistic with the structured logic of mathematics.

## The "Code-First" Strategy

The architecture of DeepSeekMath 7B was not built from a general-purpose LLM. Instead, it was initialized with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B. This choice was intentional: the researchers hypothesized that starting from a model already proficient in code would provide a stronger foundation for mathematical reasoning than starting from a general language model.

To test this, the team compared different training regimes using a 1.3B parameter model, comparing two-stage training (sequential) against one-stage training (mixed).

## Boosting Program-Aided Reasoning

The most intuitive benefit of code training is the improvement in program-aided mathematical reasoning, often referred to as Program-of-Thought (PoT). In this setting, the model solves a problem by writing and executing a Python program using libraries like *math* and *sympy*.

The results were clear: code training significantly enhances the model's ability to solve benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH through Python. Whether the code training happened as a separate first stage or was mixed with math data, the model's ability to leverage external tools improved. Interestingly, mixing code and math tokens in a single stage helped mitigate "catastrophic forgetting," allowing the model to maintain its general coding skills while simultaneously improving its program-aided math capabilities.

## The Surprise: Reasoning Without Tools

Perhaps the most significant finding is that code training improves mathematical reasoning even when the model is prohibited from using any external tools. In these "natural language" or Chain-of-Thought (CoT) tasks, the model must solve the problem purely through text.

The researchers found that a two-stage training process—training on 400B code tokens followed by 150B math tokens—yielded the best performance for tool-free reasoning. The initial stage of code training provided a moderate boost in reasoning capabilities and, more importantly, increased the efficiency of the subsequent math training.

This suggests that the process of learning to code teaches a model a form of logical rigor and structural thinking that is directly transferable to solving mathematical proofs and equations in natural language.

## The Trade-off: Mixing vs. Sequencing

While code training is beneficial, the method of delivery matters. For smaller models (such as the 1.3B version tested), the researchers observed a trade-off:

*   **Two-Stage Training (Code $\rightarrow$ Math):** Maximized the model's "mental" math reasoning (without tools), but risked forgetting some coding proficiency.
*   **One-Stage Training (Mixed Code + Math):** Protected coding skills and aided program-aided reasoning, but slightly compromised the model's tool-free mathematical reasoning.

The researchers conjecture that smaller models may lack the capacity to fully assimilate both code and mathematical data simultaneously, making the sequential approach more effective for pure reasoning.

## Conclusion

The development of DeepSeekMath demonstrates that code is more than just a utility for automation; it is a training ground for logic. By initializing with coding data and strategically layering mathematical pre-training, the researchers were able to push a 7B model to approach the performance of much larger proprietary models. For those building the next generation of reasoning AI, the lesson is clear: if you want your model to master the complexities of mathematics, start by teaching it how to code.
