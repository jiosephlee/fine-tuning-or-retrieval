\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why did training on arXiv papers fail to improve mathematical reasoning in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
In section 5.1.2, the authors state that arXiv papers seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning, noting that training on MathPile and ArXiv-RedPajama showed no notable improvements or even deterioration. This is surprising to me because arXiv is the primary source of high-level mathematical discourse. Is there a theoretical reason why formal scientific papers would be less useful for LLM reasoning compared to the web-scraped data in the DeepSeekMath Corpus?
Answer:
The finding that arXiv papers were ineffective for improving mathematical reasoning in DeepSeekMath is indeed counter-intuitive given that arXiv is the gold standard for professional mathematical research. However, the authors' results suggest a distinction between "high-level mathematical knowledge" and "mathematical reasoning capability."

In their experiments, the authors trained both a $1.3\text{B}$ and a $7\text{B}$ parameter model on arXiv-heavy corpora, specifically MathPile (which is over $85\%$ arXiv papers) and ArXiv-RedPajama (the entirety of arXiv LaTeX files). They observed that training on these arXiv-only datasets led to no notable improvements, and in some cases, even a deterioration in performance across benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH, and MMLU-STEM.

The theoretical reason for this discrepancy lies in the nature of the data. Academic papers on arXiv are written for experts; they are designed to present novel results and proofs efficiently. In this professional discourse, authors frequently omit intermediate algebraic steps or use phrases like "it is trivial to see that," which creates a "gap" in the reasoning chain. For a language model to learn *how* to reason (specifically the step-by-step Chain-of-Thought process), it needs dense, pedagogical examples where every logical leap is explicitly documented.

In contrast, the DeepSeekMath Corpus is sourced from the web (Common Crawl). Web-based mathematical content—such as forum discussions on MathOverflow or educational tutorials—tends to be much more discursive. These sources often break down complex problems into smaller, digestible steps to help other learners, providing the exact kind of "reasoning traces" that enable a model to solve competition-level problems.

It is important to note that the authors provide a few caveats. They suggest that arXiv data might still be useful for tasks not covered in their benchmarks, such as the "informalization of theorems" (converting formal proofs back into natural language). They also acknowledge that the benefits of arXiv papers might only manifest at a larger model scale or when mixed with other data types, rather than being used as a standalone training set.
