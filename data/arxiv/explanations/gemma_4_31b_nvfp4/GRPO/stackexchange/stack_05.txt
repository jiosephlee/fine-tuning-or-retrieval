\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Understanding the 'Unified Paradigm': How does the Gradient Coefficient $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ relate to different RL algorithms?
Question:
The paper introduces a unified paradigm in Equation 8 to compare SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO, where the gradient is expressed as: $\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}] \left( \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q, o, t, \pi_{rf}) \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t}) \right)$. I'm confused about how to map specific algorithms into this general formula. Specifically, what is the $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ for Online RFT versus GRPO, and how does this coefficient explain why GRPO is more efficient at penalizing incorrect responses?
Answer:
The unified paradigm presented in Equation 8 simplifies various fine-tuning and reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms by expressing their gradients in a common form. In this framework, the gradient is essentially the log-probability of the output tokens $\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})$, scaled by a weight called the Gradient Coefficient, $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$. Intuitively, $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ acts as a signal that tells the model whether to increase the probability of a specific response (if $GC_{\mathcal{A}} > 0$), decrease it (if $GC_{\mathcal{A}} < 0$), or ignore it (if $GC_{\mathcal{A}} = 0$).

To understand the difference between Online RFT and GRPO, we look at how they determine this coefficient:

In **Online Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (Online RFT)**, the $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ behaves like a binary filter. The model samples outputs, and if an output is correct (based on a rule or reward model), it is treated as a positive example for supervised fine-tuning. In this case, $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ is essentially a constant positive value for correct responses and zero for incorrect ones. Consequently, Online RFT only performs "positive reinforcement"—it encourages the model to produce correct answers but does not actively penalize the production of incorrect ones.

In **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, the $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ is derived from the group relative advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$. For a group of $G$ sampled outputs, GRPO computes the rewards $\{r_1, \dots, r_G\}$ and normalizes them by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation:
$$\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$$
In the case of outcome supervision, the gradient coefficient is this normalized reward: $GC_{\mathcal{A}} \approx \hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i$. 

This relative approach is why GRPO is more efficient at penalizing incorrect responses. Because of the normalization, any response that performs worse than the group average will have a negative $\widetilde{r}_i$, leading to a negative $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$. A negative coefficient flips the sign of the gradient, effectively instructing the model to reduce the probability of those tokens. While Online RFT simply ignores wrong answers, GRPO actively pushes the model away from them.

Broadly, this illustrates the transition from "filtering" (RFT) to "relative optimization" (GRPO). By using the group mean as a baseline, GRPO eliminates the need for a separate, memory-intensive critic model (as required in PPO) while still providing the nuanced positive and negative signals necessary to refine complex mathematical reasoning.
