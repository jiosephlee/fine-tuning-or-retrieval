\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does RL improve $\text{Maj@K}$ but not $\text{Pass@K}$ in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
In the 'Why RL Works?' section (5.2.2), the authors provide a graph showing that reinforcement learning improves $\text{Maj@K}$ accuracy but does not significantly improve $\text{Pass@K}$. They conclude that RL makes the output distribution more robust by boosting the correct response from the TopK, rather than enhancing fundamental capabilities. I don't quite understand this. If the model is now more likely to sample the correct answer (increasing $\text{Maj@K}$), hasn't its fundamental ability to solve the problem actually improved?
Answer:
To understand why RL improves $\text{Maj@K}$ but not $\text{Pass@K}$, it is first necessary to distinguish what these two metrics actually measure regarding a model's probability distribution over possible answers.

$\text{Pass@K}$ measures the probability that at least one of the $K$ sampled outputs is correct. This acts as a proxy for the model's "fundamental capability": if a model can solve a problem in $K$ attempts, the knowledge required to reach the correct answer exists somewhere within its distribution. If $\text{Pass@K}$ remains stagnant after RL, it implies that the RL process is not teaching the model how to solve new, harder problems that it previously could not solve at all.

$\text{Maj@K}$ (often associated with self-consistency) measures the accuracy of the majority vote among $K$ samples. This is a measure of "robustness" or "calibration." For $\text{Maj@K}$ to improve while $\text{Pass@K}$ stays the same, the model must already have been capable of producing the correct answer occasionally (keeping $\text{Pass@K}$ constant), but it was doing so infrequently or inconsistently. RL then "boosts" the probability mass of those existing correct paths, making the correct answer the most frequent one in the sample group.

The authors describe this as addressing a "misalignment problem" inherent in Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). In an SFT model, the distribution may be "spread out," where the correct reasoning path is present but not dominant. RL acts as a filter that concentrates the probability distribution around the correct responses that were already present in the $\text{Top-}K$.

Intuitively, you can think of it this way: the model already had the "idea" of the correct solution (fundamental capability), but it was "unsure" and frequently sampled incorrect paths. RL didn't give the model a new "idea" (which would have increased $\text{Pass@K}$), but it taught the model to be more confident in the correct "idea" it already had, thereby increasing the consistency of its output (increasing $\text{Maj@K}$).
