\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) estimate the baseline without a critic model?
Question:
I'm struggling with the transition from PPO to GRPO. In standard PPO, the advantage $A_t$ is computed using a learned value function $V_{\psi}$ (the critic). The paper says GRPO "foregoes the critic model" and instead uses the average reward of multiple sampled outputs for the same question as the baseline. Looking at the objective function in Equation 3, I don't see the explicit subtraction of a baseline. How is the "relative" advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ actually calculated from the group scores to achieve variance reduction without a separate network?
Answer:
In standard PPO, the critic model $V_{\psi}$ serves as a baseline by estimating the expected return from a given state. By subtracting this baseline from the actual observed return, PPO computes the advantage $A_t$, which tells the model whether a specific action was better or worse than the "average" expected behavior. This process is crucial for variance reduction; without it, the gradient updates would be subject to the high variance of raw reward signals.

GRPO replaces this learned value function with a group-based statistical baseline. Instead of training a separate network to guess the average reward for a prompt, GRPO samples a group of $G$ outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \dots, o_G\}$ from the current policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$ for the same question $q$. The "relative" advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is then computed by comparing the reward of a specific output $r_i$ against the empirical mean and standard deviation of the rewards within that specific group. 

In the case of outcome supervision, the advantage for all tokens in an output is simply the normalized reward:
$$\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$$
where $\mathbf{r} = \{r_1, r_2, \dots, r_G\}$ is the set of rewards for the group. This normalization is the "explicit subtraction of the baseline" you were looking for; the $\text{mean}(\mathbf{r})$ acts as the baseline, and the division by $\text{std}(\mathbf{r})$ scales the advantage. When this $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is plugged into the objective function in Equation 3, it provides the necessary signal to reinforce outputs that performed better than the group average and penalize those that performed worse.

For more complex tasks, the paper also mentions process supervision, where rewards are given at each reasoning step. In this setting, the rewards for each step across the group are normalized similarly, and the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for a token is calculated as the sum of the normalized rewards from that token's position until the end of the sequence: $\hat{A}_{i, t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$.

Intuitively, this approach is highly effective because reward models are typically trained on pairwise comparisons (preference data). By using a group relative baseline, GRPO mimics this comparative nature, effectively asking "which of these $G$ attempts is the best?" rather than "how good is this attempt in an absolute sense?". This not only reduces the memory overhead by eliminating the critic model but also provides a more stable and grounded baseline based on the actual current distribution of the policy's outputs.
