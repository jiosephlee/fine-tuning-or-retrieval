# Why Your LLM Might Be Collapsing: Moving from Token to Sequence Optimization

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the primary engine for scaling the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). By encouraging models to engage in deeper, longer reasoning processes, RL has enabled breakthroughs in complex mathematics and programming. However, as models grow in size and responses become longer, researchers have encountered a recurring nightmare: catastrophic and irreversible model collapse.

In this post, we explore why popular RL algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) can be unstable and how a new approach called Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) provides the stability needed to scale the latest generation of models, including Qwen3.

## The Fragility of Token-Level RL

To improve sample efficiency, RL training typically partitions rollout data into mini-batches for gradient updates. This creates an "off-policy" setting where the responses being optimized were generated by an old version of the policy ($\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$) rather than the current one ($\pi_{\theta}$). To prevent the model from drifting too far and becoming unstable, algorithms like PPO and GRPO use a clipping mechanism based on "importance ratios."

In GRPO, this importance ratio is calculated at the token level. For a token $y_{i,t}$, the ratio is defined as:

$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$

While this seems intuitive, it introduces a fundamental flaw: the unit of optimization (the token) does not match the unit of reward (the entire sequence). Because the reward is granted to the whole response, applying off-policy correction at every single token introduces high-variance noise. This noise accumulates as the response length increases, and when combined with the clipping mechanism, it can precipitate a total model collapse. Once this happens, the damage is often irreversible—reverting to checkpoints or tuning hyperparameters rarely fixes the issue.

## Introducing GSPO: Sequence-Level Stability

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) addresses this instability by shifting the importance ratio from the token level to the sequence level. The core philosophy is simple: if the reward is sequence-level, the optimization and clipping should be sequence-level too.

GSPO defines the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ based on the likelihood of the entire sequence:

$s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right)$

By using length normalization (the exponent $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$), GSPO ensures that the importance ratio remains within a unified numerical range regardless of how long the response is. This prevents a few highly volatile tokens from causing dramatic fluctuations in the ratio.

The resulting objective function $\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)$ applies clipping to the entire response. This eliminates the erratic token-level weighting seen in GRPO, where different tokens in the same response can have wildly different weights. In GSPO, all tokens in a response are weighted equally, creating a much more reliable and stable learning signal.

## Solving the MoE Stability Problem

The stability benefits of GSPO are particularly evident when training Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. MoE models suffer from "expert-activation volatility," where a single gradient update can significantly change which experts are activated for the same input. 

In token-level algorithms like GRPO, this volatility causes the importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to fluctuate drastically, hindering convergence. To fix this, developers previously relied on "Routing Replay"—a complex strategy that caches and forces the use of old routing modes. 

GSPO fundamentally resolves this issue without needing Routing Replay. Because GSPO focuses on the overall sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)$ rather than individual token likelihoods, it is not sensitive to which specific expert was triggered for a specific token. This simplifies the training pipeline and allows MoE models to utilize their full capacity without artificial constraints.

## Simplifying the RL Infrastructure

Beyond algorithmic stability, GSPO offers practical advantages for RL infrastructure. There is often a precision discrepancy between the inference engines (like vLLM or SGLang) used for rollouts and the training engines (like Megatron) used for updates. 

Traditionally, the training engine must recompute token-level likelihoods to ensure precision. However, because sequence-level likelihoods are much more tolerant of these discrepancies, GSPO allows the model to use likelihoods returned directly from the inference engine. This reduces computational overhead and simplifies the design of disaggregated training-inference frameworks.

## Conclusion

By aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward, GSPO transforms RL training from a fragile process prone to collapse into a robust, scalable foundation. The results are clear: superior training efficiency, stable MoE convergence, and significant performance gains in the Qwen3 models across benchmarks like AIME'24 and CodeForces. For anyone scaling LLMs for long-form reasoning, moving from token-level to sequence-level optimization may be the key to avoiding the collapse.



# Taming the MoE Beast: How GSPO Stabilizes Mixture-of-Experts Training

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have become a cornerstone for scaling large language models, offering a way to increase parameter count without a linear increase in computational cost. However, bringing these models through the rigors of reinforcement learning (RL) is notoriously difficult. Many state-of-the-art RL algorithms, including Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), suffer from severe stability issues when training gigantic MoE models, often leading to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse.

The team behind the latest Qwen3 models identified a fundamental culprit for this instability: **expert-activation volatility**. In this post, we'll explore what this phenomenon is and how a new algorithm called Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) eliminates the need for complex workarounds, allowing MoE models to reach their full potential.

## The Problem: Expert-Activation Volatility

To understand why MoE models struggle with RL, we have to look at how algorithms like GRPO handle "off-policy" data. During training, a large batch of responses is generated by an old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and then used to update the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$. To correct for the fact that the data was sampled from a different version of the model, GRPO uses a token-level importance ratio:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$$

In a dense model, this ratio is relatively stable. But in an MoE model, the "experts" activated for a specific token can change significantly after even a single gradient update. For instance, in the Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, researchers found that roughly 10% of the activated experts change for the same sample after one update.

This "volatility" causes the token-level importance ratios to fluctuate drastically. Because these ratios are multiplied by the advantage and accumulated over long sequences, they introduce high-variance noise into the gradients. This noise progressively builds up, eventually triggering a model collapse that cannot be fixed by simply reverting to a checkpoint or tuning hyperparameters.

## The "Band-Aid": Routing Replay

Before GSPO, the primary way to handle this was a strategy called **Routing Replay**. The idea was to cache the experts activated by the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and force the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$ to use those same routing modes when computing the importance ratios. 

While Routing Replay allows MoE models to converge, it comes with a price. It introduces additional memory and communication overhead and, more importantly, it places an artificial constraint on the model by limiting its ability to explore new, potentially better routing paths.

## The Solution: Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

GSPO solves this problem by shifting the unit of optimization. The researchers observed a core principle: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of the reward. Since rewards are typically granted to an entire sequence, it makes sense to perform off-policy correction at the sequence level rather than the token level.

GSPO replaces the token-level weight with a length-normalized sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta} (y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right)$$

By using the sequence likelihood, GSPO becomes indifferent to which specific expert fired for which token. As long as the model maintains its overall language modeling capability, the sequence likelihood remains stable, even if the internal routing changes.

## Why GSPO is a Game Changer for MoE

The transition to sequence-level optimization provides several critical advantages:

*   **Inherent Stability:** Because it is not sensitive to individual token likelihoods, GSPO fundamentally resolves expert-activation volatility. This means it converges stably without needing the Routing Replay workaround.
*   **Full Capacity Utilization:** Without the constraints of Routing Replay, the MoE model can leverage its full capacity and explore routing modes more freely.
*   **Infrastructure Simplification:** GSPO is more tolerant of precision discrepancies between inference engines (like vLLM) and training engines (like Megatron). This allows the system to use likelihoods directly from the inference engine, avoiding the need for costly recomputations in the training engine.

By stabilizing the training dynamics, GSPO has enabled the continued scaling of RL for the Qwen3 models, delivering superior training efficiency and performance over GRPO across complex reasoning benchmarks. For those training large-scale sparse models, moving the importance ratio from the token to the sequence level may be the key to avoiding the dreaded model collapse.



# The Secret Sauce of Qwen3: Scaling RL for Complex Reasoning

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a critical driver for scaling the reasoning capabilities of large language models. By utilizing large-scale RL, models can develop the ability to tackle competition-level mathematics and complex programming tasks through longer, deeper reasoning processes. However, scaling RL is not without its hurdles. As models grow in size and response lengths increase, maintaining stable training dynamics becomes a significant challenge.

In the development of the latest Qwen3 models, a key breakthrough came from the introduction of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). This algorithm addresses the stability issues found in previous methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), allowing for more robust scaling and superior performance on benchmarks such as AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces.

## The Problem with Token-Level Optimization

To understand GSPO, we first need to look at the limitations of GRPO. In an off-policy RL setting, models often use a clipping mechanism to prevent the current policy $\pi_\theta$ from drifting too far from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. GRPO attempts to manage this by applying importance weights at the token level.

The researchers found that GRPO’s objective is fundamentally "ill-posed." While rewards are granted to an entire sequence, GRPO applies importance sampling weights to individual tokens. Because these weights are based on single samples from a next-token distribution, they introduce high-variance noise into the training gradients. This noise accumulates over long sequences and, when combined with the clipping mechanism, can lead to catastrophic and often irreversible model collapse.

## Introducing GSPO: Shifting to the Sequence Level

The core insight behind GSPO is that the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of the reward. Since rewards are sequence-level, the importance ratio should be as well. 

GSPO defines the importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ based on the likelihood of the entire sequence:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$$

By using length normalization (the exponent $1/|y_i|$), GSPO keeps the importance ratio within a unified numerical range regardless of sequence length. This prevents the likelihood changes of a few tokens from causing dramatic fluctuations. Instead of clipping individual tokens, GSPO clips entire responses, which effectively excludes overly off-policy samples from the gradient estimation.

The result is a much more stable learning signal. Empirically, GSPO allows for continuous performance improvements as training compute increases and generation lengths are extended, without the risk of the sudden collapse seen in GRPO.

## Stabilizing Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Training

One of the most significant advantages of GSPO is its impact on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. MoE models suffer from "expert-activation volatility," where a single gradient update can significantly change which experts are activated for a given response. In GRPO, this volatility causes token-level importance ratios to fluctuate wildly, hindering convergence.

Previously, this was managed using a strategy called "Routing Replay," which cached activated experts from the old policy to ensure consistency. However, Routing Replay introduces memory and communication overhead and can limit the model's capacity. 

GSPO eliminates the need for Routing Replay entirely. Because it focuses on sequence likelihood rather than individual token likelihood, it is not sensitive to the specific experts activated for each token. This allows MoE models to converge normally and leverage their full capacity without artificial constraints.

## Efficiency and Infrastructure Gains

Beyond stability, GSPO offers practical advantages for RL infrastructure:

*   **Sample Efficiency:** Interestingly, GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens than GRPO. Despite using fewer tokens for gradient estimation, it achieves higher training efficiency and better benchmark accuracy, proving that sequence-level signals are far more effective than noisy token-level ones.
*   **Precision Tolerance:** There are often precision discrepancies between inference engines (like vLLM) and training engines (like Megatron). Because sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of these discrepancies, GSPO allows the use of likelihoods directly from the inference engine, removing the need for costly recomputations in the training engine.

For cases requiring finer-grained control, such as multi-turn RL, the researchers also introduced **GSPO-token**. This variant maintains the numerical stability of the sequence-level approach while allowing for token-wise advantage customization.

By moving from token-level to sequence-level optimization, GSPO provides the scalable and robust foundation necessary to push the boundaries of complex reasoning in the Qwen3 family of models.



# Bridging the Gap: How GSPO Simplifies AI Training Infrastructure

Scaling reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models (LLMs) is as much a challenge of infrastructure as it is of algorithmic design. As models grow in size—particularly with the shift toward Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures—and response lengths increase, the stability of the training pipeline becomes precarious.

The latest Qwen3 models achieved significant performance leaps by moving away from token-level optimization. The secret lies in **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**. While GSPO offers clear gains in stability and performance, it also provides a major practical advantage for ML engineers: it significantly simplifies the RL training infrastructure.

## The Problem with Token-Level Importance Sampling

To understand the infrastructure benefits of GSPO, we first need to look at the limitations of its predecessor, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO utilizes a token-level importance ratio to correct for the distributional mismatch between the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ (which generated the samples) and the current policy $\pi_\theta$ being optimized.

The token-level importance ratio is defined as:
$$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$$

The authors of the GSPO paper identify this as an "ill-posed" objective. Since rewards are granted to the entire sequence, applying off-policy correction at the individual token level introduces high-variance noise. This noise accumulates over long sequences and is amplified by clipping mechanisms, often leading to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse.

## GSPO: Shifting to the Sequence Level

GSPO resolves this by aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. Instead of per-token ratios, GSPO defines a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ based on the total sequence likelihood:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$$

By utilizing length normalization, GSPO keeps the importance ratios within a unified numerical range, reducing variance and ensuring that the clipping mechanism acts on entire responses rather than individual tokens.

## Infrastructure Gains: Solving the Precision Gap

In a production RL pipeline, there is often a divide between the **inference engine** (e.g., vLLM or SGLang), which handles the rollout of responses, and the **training engine** (e.g., Megatron), which computes gradients. 

Because these engines are implemented differently, they often exhibit precision discrepancies. For token-level algorithms like GRPO, these small differences in calculated likelihoods can be problematic. To ensure accuracy, engineers typically have to use the training engine to recompute the likelihoods of sampled responses under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ before performing the update. This recomputation adds significant overhead to the training loop.

GSPO fundamentally changes this dynamic. Because it relies on sequence-level likelihoods, it is far more tolerant of precision discrepancies between the inference and training engines. This allows for:

*   **Direct Likelihood Usage:** The ability to use likelihoods returned directly by the inference engine for optimization, bypassing the need for expensive recomputation in the training engine.
*   **Disaggregated Frameworks:** Paving the way for truly disaggregated RL frameworks where rollout and optimization are decoupled more efficiently.
*   **Simplified Multi-turn RL:** Reducing the complexity of managing state and likelihoods across multiple turns of dialogue.

## Stability in MoE Training

Beyond general infrastructure, GSPO solves a specific pain point for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models: **expert-activation volatility**. 

In GRPO, a single gradient update can change which experts are activated for a specific token. This causes the token-level importance ratios to fluctuate drastically, hindering convergence. Previously, this required a complex "Routing Replay" strategy to cache and force the use of old routing modes. 

GSPO eliminates the need for Routing Replay entirely. Because it focuses on the overall sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y_i | x)$ rather than individual token likelihoods, it is not sensitive to the specific expert activated for any single token. This allows MoE models to converge stably while utilizing their full capacity without artificial routing constraints.

## Conclusion

By moving the optimization objective from the token level to the sequence level, GSPO does more than just stabilize training—it strips away layers of infrastructure complexity. By reducing the dependency on precision alignment between inference and training engines and removing the need for MoE-specific workarounds, GSPO provides a more robust and scalable foundation for the next generation of large-scale RL training.
