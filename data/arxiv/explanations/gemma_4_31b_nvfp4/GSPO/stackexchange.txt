\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why is the sequence importance ratio in GSPO length-normalized?
Question:
In the GSPO paper, the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is defined as the sequence likelihood ratio raised to the power of $1/|y_i|$, specifically: $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. I'm confused about why this length normalization is necessary. In standard importance sampling, the weight is usually just the ratio of the two distributions. Wouldn't using the raw sequence likelihood ratio be more theoretically sound, and why does the authors' claim that this controls the numerical range matter for the clipping mechanism?
Answer:
The length normalization in GSPO is primarily a numerical stabilization technique designed to make the sequence-level importance ratio compatible with a fixed clipping mechanism across responses of varying lengths.

In standard importance sampling, the weight for a sequence $y_i$ would indeed be the raw ratio of the likelihoods: $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta\text{old}}(y_i|x)}$. However, because the likelihood of a sequence is the product of the likelihoods of its individual tokens, the raw ratio is also a product: $\prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$. Since these ratios are multiplied over the length of the sequence $|y_i|$, the resulting value grows or shrinks exponentially with the sequence length. Even a very small average change in token probability can lead to a sequence-level ratio that is either astronomically large or nearly zero for long responses.

This creates a significant problem for the clipping mechanism. The clipping function $\text{clip}(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)$ uses a fixed hyperparameter $\varepsilon$ to prevent the policy from moving too far from the old policy. If GSPO used the raw sequence ratio, a response with 10 tokens and a response with 1,000 tokens would require vastly different clipping ranges to achieve the same effect; the 1,000-token response would almost always be clipped because its raw ratio is far more volatile.

By defining $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta\text{old}}(y_i|x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$, the authors are essentially calculating the geometric mean of the token-level importance ratios. This transformation maps the sequence-level ratio back into a numerical range similar to that of a single token's ratio. As the paper notes, this controls $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range and reduces variance, ensuring that the clipping range $\varepsilon$ remains meaningful and consistent regardless of whether a response is short or long. This allows GSPO to perform sequence-level optimization while maintaining the stability benefits of the proximal clipping used in algorithms like PPO and GRPO.

### Why is the GRPO objective considered 'ill-posed' for long sequences?
Question:
The authors argue that GRPO is unstable because it applies importance weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ at each token position. They claim this introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences, leading to 'catastrophic model collapse'. I understand that importance sampling is meant to correct distributional mismatch, but I don't understand why applying the weight at the token level specifically fails whereas applying it at the sequence level succeeds. What exactly is the 'misapplication' of importance sampling happening in GRPO?
Answer:
The "ill-posed" nature of the GRPO objective stems from a fundamental mismatch between the mathematical purpose of importance sampling and how it is applied at the token level. 

In standard importance sampling, the goal is to estimate the expectation of a function $f(z)$ under a target distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ using samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$. This is achieved by weighting the samples by the ratio $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$. For this to actually "correct" the distributional mismatch and provide a reliable estimate, the expectation must be averaged over many samples ($N \gg 1$). 

The misapplication in GRPO occurs because it applies the importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ at each individual token position $t$. At any given token, the model is not averaging over multiple possible tokens that could have appeared at that position; it is dealing with a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from the behavior policy. Because it is a single-sample estimate, the importance weight fails to perform a statistical distribution correction and instead acts as a source of high-variance noise.

This problem is severely amplified in long sequences because this variance accumulates over the entire response length $|y_i|$. Since the reward $r(x, y)$ is granted to the entire sequence, the "unit of reward" is the sequence, but the "unit of optimization" in GRPO is the token. This mismatch means that a single "noisy" token weight—potentially exacerbated by the clipping mechanism—can disproportionately swing the gradient for the entire sequence. In large models or Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, where small parameter updates can lead to significant changes in token-level probabilities (or expert routing), this accumulated noise can lead to "catastrophic model collapse," where the training dynamics become irreversibly unstable.

GSPO resolves this by shifting the importance ratio to the sequence level: $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. By treating the entire sequence as the unit of sampling, the importance weight now reflects how far the overall response $y$ deviates from the old policy, which naturally aligns with the sequence-level reward. In the resulting gradient, all tokens in a response are weighted equally by $s_i(\theta)$, eliminating the token-level variance and providing a much more stable learning signal.

### Understanding the stop-gradient ($\mathrm{sg}$) operation in GSPO-token
Question:
In the GSPO-token variant, the ratio $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is defined as $\mathrm{sg} [ s_{i}(\theta) ] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} [ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) ] }$. The paper states that this is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$. If the numerical value is the same, why use this complex formulation involving $\mathrm{sg}$ (stop-gradient/detach) operations? Does this change how the gradients are backpropagated compared to simply using $s_i(\theta)$ in the objective function?
Answer:
The use of the stop-gradient ($\mathrm{sg}$) operation in the GSPO-token variant is a technique used to decouple the **numerical value** of a term from its **gradient**. While the authors note that $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$, the way the gradient is computed is fundamentally different.

To understand why this is necessary, we must look at what happens if we simply used $s_i(\theta)$ in a token-level objective. The sequence-level importance ratio is defined as $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta} (y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. Because $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)$ is the product of all token probabilities in the sequence, $s_i(\theta)$ is a global property of the entire response. If you were to take the gradient $\nabla_{\theta} s_i(\theta)$, the result would involve a summation of gradients across every single token in the sequence. If this were used as a weight for a specific token's advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$, the gradient for token $t$ would be "contaminated" by the gradients of all other tokens in that sequence, making it impossible to perform precise, token-wise advantage customization.

The $\mathrm{sg}$ formulation solves this by using two specific tricks. First, $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ treats the sequence-level importance ratio as a constant coefficient. This ensures that the sequence-level weight scales the gradient without contributing its own complex gradient flow. Second, the term $\frac{\pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg} [\pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})]}$ is a common identity in deep learning: numerically, any value divided by itself is $1$, but the gradient of this expression is $\frac{\nabla_{\theta} \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | \dots)}{\pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | \dots)}$, which is exactly $\nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | \dots)$.

By combining these, the gradient of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ becomes:
$$\nabla_{\theta} s_{i,t}(\theta) = s_i(\theta) \cdot \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$$
This allows GSPO-token to achieve a specific goal: it weights the update for token $t$ using the global sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ (maintaining the stability of GSPO), but the actual direction of the update is driven only by the log-likelihood of the specific token $t$ and its associated advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$.

In summary, this "complex" formulation is a way of performing "gradient surgery." It allows the model to use the stable, sequence-level importance weight for numerical scaling while maintaining the ability to optimize tokens individually based on fine-grained rewards. Without the $\mathrm{sg}$ operations, the optimization would lose its token-level granularity and revert to a sequence-wide update.

### How does GSPO's gradient weighting differ from GRPO's in practice?
Question:
Looking at the gradient analysis in Section 4.2, it seems that in GRPO, each token's log-likelihood gradient is weighted by its specific token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. In GSPO, every token in the sequence is weighted by the same sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$. If we are trying to optimize the overall sequence, why does weighting all tokens equally based on the sequence average lead to more stability than weighting them individually based on their specific contribution to the shift?
Answer:
The fundamental difference between GRPO and GSPO lies in whether the off-policy correction (the importance ratio) is applied to individual tokens or to the sequence as a whole. In GRPO, each token $y_{i,t}$ in a response is weighted by its own importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$. This means that during the gradient update, different tokens in the same sequence can be pushed with wildly different magnitudes based on how much their individual probabilities shifted between the old and current policies.

The instability in GRPO arises because this token-level importance sampling is mathematically ill-posed. According to the principle of importance sampling, the ratio $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ is intended to correct for distributional mismatch when averaging over many samples. However, in GRPO, $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is calculated using only a single sample $y_{i,t}$ for each token position $t$. This introduces high-variance noise into the gradients. Because this noise is additive across the sequence, it accumulates as the response length increases, which can lead to catastrophic model collapse—a phenomenon the authors note is often irreversible.

GSPO addresses this by aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. Since the reward $r(x, y)$ is granted to the entire sequence, GSPO uses a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, which is defined as the length-normalized geometric mean of the token-level ratios: $s_i(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right)$. In the resulting gradient $\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}$, every token in the sequence is weighted by this same value $s_i(\theta)$.

Intuitively, this acts as a smoothing mechanism. Instead of allowing a single "erratic" token to drastically pivot the gradient for that specific position, GSPO looks at the overall shift in the likelihood of the entire response. This is particularly critical for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. In MoE training, "expert-activation volatility" occurs when the model chooses different experts for the same token between $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$. This causes the token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to fluctuate violently. By using the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$, GSPO ignores these volatile token-level shifts and focuses on the stable overall sequence likelihood, eliminating the need for complex stabilization strategies like Routing Replay.

### How does GSPO resolve 'expert-activation volatility' in MoE models without Routing Replay?
Question:
The paper explains that in MoE models, GRPO fails because different experts might be activated for the same token after a policy update, making the token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ fluctuate wildly. The authors claim GSPO resolves this because it is 'not sensitive to the individual token likelihood'. However, the sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y|x)$ is just the product of individual token likelihoods $\prod \pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})$. If the individual token likelihoods are fluctuating due to different expert activations, wouldn't the product (the sequence likelihood) also fluctuate? I don't see how GSPO is fundamentally 'insensitive' to this volatility if the underlying components are still the same token-level probabilities.
Answer:
The key to understanding why GSPO is "insensitive" to expert-activation volatility lies not in the value of the sequence likelihood itself, but in how that likelihood is used to **weight the gradients** during optimization.

In GRPO, the importance ratio is applied at the token level: $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$. In a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model, a small update to $\theta$ can cause the router to switch which expert is activated for a specific token. Because $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ might now be computed by entirely different neural networks (different experts), the ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can fluctuate drastically. Since GRPO multiplies each token's gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | \dots)$ by its own specific $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, a single "volatile" token can introduce a massive, noisy spike into the total gradient, leading to the instability and model collapse described in the paper.

GSPO resolves this by shifting the importance ratio from the token level to the sequence level. The sequence-level ratio is defined as $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. While you are correct that the sequence likelihood is the product of token likelihoods, the length normalization (the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ exponent) effectively converts this product into a geometric mean of the token ratios. Mathematically, this is expressed as:
$s_{i}(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$.

By averaging the log-ratios across the entire sequence, the volatility of a few individual tokens switching experts is smoothed out. A jump in a single token's likelihood is dampened by the other tokens in the sequence, preventing the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ from fluctuating as wildly as the individual $w_{i,t}(\theta)$.

More importantly, look at the resulting gradient. In GRPO, each token has a different weight, meaning the model can be pushed aggressively in directions dictated by the most volatile tokens. In GSPO, as shown in Equation $\ref{equ:gspo_grad}$, all tokens in a response are weighted **equally** by the same shared $s_i(\theta)$. This eliminates the "unequal weights" problem; the optimizer no longer sees high-variance noise from individual token-level switches, but rather a stable, unified signal based on the overall sequence performance. Consequently, GSPO maintains training stability without needing "Routing Replay" to force the experts to remain consistent between policy updates.
