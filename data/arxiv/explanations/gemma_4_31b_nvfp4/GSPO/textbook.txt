\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Introduction to RL for Large Language Models

# The role of RL in LLM scaling

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become a fundamental paradigm for scaling the capabilities of large language models. While initial training stages typically focus on predicting the next token from a dataset, RL allows models to optimize for higher-level goals, enabling them to handle significantly more sophisticated problems. This is particularly evident in domains that require rigorous logical precision, such as competition-level mathematics and computer programming.

The primary benefit of scaling RL is that it encourages the model to develop deeper and longer reasoning processes. Rather than providing a direct answer, RL can train a model to "think" through a problem, exploring multiple steps of logic to arrive at a correct solution. By rewarding correct final outcomes, RL guides the policy to prefer trajectories of reasoning that lead to success, thereby expanding the model's cognitive boundaries beyond what is possible with standard supervised fine-tuning.

# Review of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

One of the most influential algorithms for this purpose is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). In the context of language modeling, we define an autoregressive policy $\pi_\theta$, parameterized by $\theta$. When training, PPO utilizes samples generated from an "old" version of the policy, denoted as $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The goal of PPO is to update the policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize rewards while ensuring that the update does not move the policy too far away from the old one, which could lead to training instability.

To achieve this, PPO introduces a clipping mechanism that constrains the policy update within a proximal region. The objective function $\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta)$ is defined as:

$\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \min \left( w_{t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{t},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( w_{t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon}\right) \widehat{A}_{t} \right) \right]$

In this expression, $x$ is a query from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and $y$ is a response generated by the old policy. The term $|y|$ represents the number of tokens in the response. The importance ratio $w_{t}(\theta)$ represents the relative probability of generating the token $y_t$ under the current policy versus the old policy:

$w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t}) }$

The term $\widehat{A}_{t}$ is the advantage of the token $y_t$, and $\varepsilon$ is the clipping range. By clipping the importance ratio, PPO prevents the gradient updates from being overly influenced by samples that have become too "off-policy," meaning they are too different from the distribution that originally generated them.

# The computational burden of Value Models in PPO

A significant practical challenge with PPO is its heavy reliance on a value model. To estimate the advantage $\widehat{A}_{t}$ for each token, PPO requires a separate model (the value model) to predict the expected return from a given state. This architecture introduces a considerable computational and memory burden because the value model typically needs to be similar in size to the policy model itself. For gigantic language models, this effectively doubles the memory requirements during training.

Beyond the hardware costs, the effectiveness of PPO is strictly tied to the reliability of the value model's estimates. If the value model provides inaccurate advantage estimates, the policy updates will be flawed. Ensuring that a value model remains reliable and scalable—especially as response lengths increase and tasks become more complex—is a daunting challenge in the scale-up of RL for language models.

# Introduction to Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

To resolve the inefficiencies associated with value models, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was introduced. GRPO completely bypasses the need for a separate value model by instead leveraging the relative performance of multiple responses to the same query. Instead of estimating an absolute value for a state, GRPO looks at a group of responses and determines how much better or worse a specific response is compared to the others in that group.

The objective function for GRPO is formulated as:

$\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \min \left( w_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( w_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon}\right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) \right]$

Here, $G$ represents the group size—the number of different responses generated for a single query $x$. Much like PPO, GRPO still employs a token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ and a clipping mechanism to maintain stability during off-policy learning. However, by replacing the value model with a group-based approach, it significantly reduces the memory and computational overhead.

# The core mechanism of group-based advantage estimation

The defining characteristic of GRPO is how it calculates the advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ for each token. In GRPO, the advantage is not estimated by a neural network but is instead derived from the rewards of the responses within the generated group. Every token in a specific response $y_i$ shares the same advantage $\widehat{A}_i$.

The advantage for the $i$-th response is computed by normalizing the reward $r(x, y_i)$ against the mean and standard deviation of the rewards for all $G$ responses in the group:

$\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) }{ \mathrm{std} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) }$

Intuitively, this means that if a response receives a reward higher than the average of the group, it is assigned a positive advantage, signaling the policy to increase the probability of that sequence. Conversely, responses that perform worse than the group average receive a negative advantage. This relative scoring mechanism provides a stable baseline for the policy to learn from, effectively treating the group as a self-contained reference for what constitutes a "good" or "bad" response to a specific query.

# The Theoretical Flaws of Token-Level Importance Sampling

# Foundations of Importance Sampling: Target vs. Behavior distributions

To understand the instability present in many reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms used for large language models, one must first understand the fundamental principle of importance sampling. In the context of policy optimization, we often want to estimate the expectation of a function $f$ under a target distribution, denoted as $\pi_\text{tar}$. However, it is often computationally impractical or impossible to sample directly from this target distribution. Instead, we draw samples from a different, known distribution called the behavior distribution, $\pi_\text{beh}$.

The core mechanism of importance sampling allows us to estimate the expectation under the target distribution by re-weighting the samples drawn from the behavior distribution. Mathematically, this is expressed as:
$\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} [ f(z) ] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} [ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z) } f(z) ]$.
Here, the ratio $\frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z) }$ serves as the importance weight. This weight corrects for the distributional mismatch between the behavior distribution and the target distribution. For this correction to be effective and to produce a reliable estimate, the principle relies on averaging over a large number of samples ($N \gg 1$) drawn from the behavior distribution.

In the context of RL for language models, this is particularly relevant during off-policy learning. When we partition a large batch of rollout data into mini-batches for gradient updates, the responses $y$ were generated by an "old" policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ (the behavior distribution), while the current policy $\pi_\theta$ being optimized is the target distribution. To ensure the gradients are accurate for the current policy, an importance ratio must be used to account for the fact that the data was not sampled from $\pi_\theta$.

# The 'Ill-Posed' nature of GRPO's objective

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is a popular RL algorithm that attempts to scale training by removing the need for a separate value model. Instead, it computes the relative advantage of a response by comparing it to a group of other responses generated for the same query. While this simplifies the architecture, the objective function of GRPO is fundamentally "ill-posed" because of a mismatch between the unit of rewarding and the unit of optimization.

In GRPO, rewards are granted at the sequence level; a verifier $r$ provides a score $r(x, y)$ for the entire response $y$ to a query $x$. However, GRPO applies the importance sampling weight at the token level. The objective function is defined as the average over a group of $G$ responses, where for each response $y_i$, the algorithm sums the clipped importance ratios for every individual token $t$ in that sequence:
$\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \min \left( w_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( w_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon}\right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) \right]$.

The problem here is that the importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is applied to each token $y_{i,t}$ based on its specific probability under the current policy versus the old policy. This creates a theoretical contradiction: the algorithm is trying to perform off-policy correction at the finest possible grain (the token), while the signal driving that optimization (the reward/advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$) is applied at the coarsest grain (the entire sequence).

# Token-level vs. Sequence-level weights: The variance problem

The primary failure of token-level importance weights lies in how they handle variance. In GRPO, the importance ratio for a token is defined as $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t}) }$. Because this weight is calculated based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution, it does not actually perform the distribution-correction role intended by the theory of importance sampling. Instead of stabilizing the gradient, it introduces high-variance noise into the training process.

In contrast, Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) proposes using a sequence-level importance weight $s_i(\theta)$. This weight reflects how far the entire response $y$ sampled from the old policy deviates from the current policy. To keep the weights numerically stable and reduce variance, GSPO employs length normalization, defining the ratio as:
$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$.

By using the sequence-level ratio, GSPO ensures that the unit of optimization matches the unit of rewarding. While GRPO weights the gradients of the log likelihoods of tokens unequally based on their individual importance weights—which can fluctuate wildly—GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally. This eliminates the instability caused by token-specific fluctuations and provides a more reliable learning signal.

# The impact of response length on gradient noise

The instability introduced by token-level importance sampling is not constant; it scales with the length of the generated responses. In GRPO, the noise generated by the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is present at every single token position $t$. As the length of the response $|y|$ increases, this high-variance noise accumulates.

When we analyze the gradients, the distinction becomes clear. In GRPO, the gradient involves a sum where each token's log likelihood gradient is multiplied by its own importance ratio. Because these ratios can vary significantly—ranging from $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages or $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages—the resulting gradient estimate becomes highly unpredictable.

As responses become longer, the cumulative effect of these unequal weights leads to unpredictable consequences. This is further exacerbated by the clipping mechanism, which is intended to manage off-policy discrepancy but, in the case of GRPO, can actually amplify the noise. This makes the training of models on tasks requiring long-form reasoning particularly precarious, as the increased sequence length directly correlates with increased gradient instability.

# Understanding catastrophic model collapse in large-scale RL

The accumulation of high-variance noise and the misapplication of importance sampling can eventually lead to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. This phenomenon is a primary hurdle in scaling RL for gigantic language models. Once a model collapses, it is often impossible to recover, even if the practitioner reverts to a previous checkpoint, meticulously tunes hyperparameters like clipping ranges, extends the generation length, or changes the query set.

This collapse is particularly aggressive in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models due to a phenomenon called expert-activation volatility. In MoE models, different experts are activated for different tokens. After a gradient update, the experts activated for the same response under the new policy $\pi_\theta$ can differ significantly from those under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. For a 48-layer model, it has been observed that roughly 10% of activated experts can change after a single update. 

In GRPO, this volatility causes the token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to fluctuate drastically, invalidating the importance sampling weights and precipitating collapse. Because GSPO focuses on the overall sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y|x)$ rather than individual token likelihoods, it is not sensitive to which specific experts were activated for which tokens. As long as the model maintains its general language modeling capability, the sequence likelihood remains stable, allowing GSPO to resolve the stability challenges of MoE training and prevent catastrophic collapse.

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

# The GSPO objective function

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is designed to provide a stable and efficient framework for the reinforcement learning (RL) of large language models. At its core, GSPO seeks to optimize a policy $\pi_\theta$ by maximizing the expected reward of generated responses while ensuring that the update does not deviate too far from a previous version of the policy, denoted as $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. This is achieved through an objective function that utilizes a group-based approach to estimate advantages and a clipping mechanism to maintain training stability.

The GSPO objective function is formulated as an expectation over a set of queries $\mathcal{D}$ and groups of responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for each query $x$. The objective is defined as follows:
$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \min \left( s_{i}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i} \right) \right]$$
In this expression, $G$ represents the group size, which is the number of responses generated for a single query. The term $s_i(\theta)$ is the sequence-level importance ratio, and $\widehat{A}_i$ is the group-based advantage. By taking the minimum of the weighted advantage and the clipped weighted advantage, the algorithm prevents the policy from making excessively large updates based on a single batch of samples, which is a critical safeguard against model collapse.

The advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ is computed by comparing the reward of a specific response $r(x, y_i)$ against the average reward of all responses in the group. It is defined as:
$$\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) }{ \mathrm{std} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) }$$
This relative advantage allows the model to identify which responses in a group are better or worse than the average, providing a clear signal for optimization without the need for a separate, computationally expensive value model.

# Defining the sequence-level importance ratio ($s_i$)

To understand the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, one must first understand the principle of importance sampling. In RL, we often sample data from a "behavior" distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ but wish to optimize a "target" distribution $\pi_\theta$. To correct for the mismatch between these two distributions, we use an importance weight. While previous algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) applied this weight at the token level, GSPO defines the importance ratio based on the likelihood of the entire sequence.

The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ reflects how much the likelihood of a complete response $y_i$ under the current policy $\pi_\theta$ deviates from its likelihood under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Mathematically, it is defined as:
$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$
where $|y_i|$ is the number of tokens in the response. This can be rewritten in a more computationally stable form using the sum of log-probabilities of the individual tokens:
$$s_{i}(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$$
By calculating the ratio at the sequence level, GSPO ensures that the importance weight is a meaningful indicator of the total distributional shift for the response. This addresses a fundamental flaw in token-level ratios, which often introduce high-variance noise into the gradients. Because the sequence-level ratio aggregates the information across the entire response, it provides a more stable and theoretically grounded correction for off-policy learning.

# The necessity of length normalization in sequence likelihoods

A critical component of the GSPO importance ratio is the inclusion of length normalization, represented by the exponent $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ in the likelihood ratio or the average within the exponential sum. Without this normalization, the sequence-level importance ratio would be the product of the ratios of every single token in the sequence. In the context of long-form generation, such a product would be extremely volatile.

The primary reason for this normalization is the reduction of variance. If length normalization were omitted, the likelihood changes of just a few tokens could cause the total sequence ratio to fluctuate dramatically. This volatility would introduce significant noise into the gradient estimation, potentially destabilizing the training process. By averaging the log-likelihood ratios across the length of the sequence, GSPO ensures that the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ remains within a controlled and unified numerical range, regardless of whether the response is short or exceptionally long.

Furthermore, length normalization is essential for the consistent application of the clipping mechanism. In PPO-style algorithms, the clipping range $\varepsilon$ is used to bound the importance ratio to prevent overly aggressive updates. If responses of different lengths had different scales of importance ratios, a single clipping range $\varepsilon$ would be ineffective; short sequences might rarely be clipped, while long sequences might be clipped almost constantly. Normalizing by the sequence length allows GSPO to use a consistent clipping range across all samples in a batch, regardless of their token count.

# Sequence-level clipping: Aligning reward units with optimization units

The design of GSPO is motivated by the observation that the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of the reward. In standard LLM RL tasks, a verifier provides a reward $r(x, y)$ for the entire completed response $y$. Despite the reward being sequence-level, algorithms like GRPO perform optimization and off-policy correction (clipping) at the token level. This mismatch is "ill-posed" because it applies a token-level importance weight to a sequence-level reward, which can lead to catastrophic model collapse, especially in large models or tasks requiring long responses.

GSPO resolves this by moving the clipping mechanism to the sequence level. Instead of clipping the ratio of each individual token $y_t$, GSPO clips the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$. This ensures that the algorithm excludes entire samples that are too "off-policy" from the gradient estimation. By aligning the unit of optimization (the sequence) with the unit of rewarding (the sequence), the learning signal becomes significantly more reliable.

Intuition suggests that when a reward is granted to a whole sequence, the most stable way to update the policy is to treat that sequence as the atomic unit of learning. By performing sequence-level clipping, GSPO avoids the accumulation of token-level noise that plagues GRPO. This alignment is particularly beneficial for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, where token-level ratios can fluctuate wildly due to changes in expert activation (expert-activation volatility), whereas the overall sequence likelihood remains far more stable.

# Comparison of clipping ranges between GSPO and GRPO

Because GSPO and GRPO define their importance ratios differently, the numerical values used for their respective clipping ranges $\varepsilon$ differ by orders of magnitude. In GRPO, the importance ratio is calculated per token: $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | \dots)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | \dots)}$. These token-level ratios are highly sensitive and fluctuate rapidly, requiring clipping ranges typically in the range of $0.2$ to $0.27$ to maintain stability.

In contrast, GSPO utilizes the length-normalized sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$. Because this ratio is an average of log-probabilities across the entire sequence, it is naturally much smoother and less volatile than a single token's ratio. Consequently, the clipping ranges used in GSPO are significantly smaller. For example, empirical results show GSPO utilizing left and right clipping ranges as small as $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$.

This disparity in magnitude does not imply that GSPO is "less" constrained; rather, it reflects the different scales of the importance ratios being clipped. Despite the fact that GSPO may clip a much larger fraction of tokens (or sequences) compared to GRPO, it achieves higher training efficiency. This counter-intuitive result suggests that GRPO's token-level gradients are inherently noisier, and that by using a more stable, sequence-level signal—even if it means clipping more frequently—GSPO provides a more effective path for sample exploitation and model improvement.

Gradient Analysis and Algorithm Variants

# Deriving the gradient of the GSPO objective

To understand how Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) influences the training of a language model, it is essential to examine the calculus underlying its objective function. The goal of GSPO is to optimize the policy $\pi_\theta$ by maximizing the expected advantage of generated sequences, while ensuring that the updates do not move the policy too far from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The objective function is defined as the expectation over queries $x$ from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ and groups of responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ sampled from the old policy:

$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \min \left( s_{i}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i} \right) \right]$

In this expression, $s_i(\theta)$ represents the sequence-level importance ratio and $\widehat{A}_i$ is the group-relative advantage. To derive the gradient $\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta)$, we temporarily omit the clipping mechanism for clarity. The gradient of the objective involves taking the derivative of the sequence-level importance ratio. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is defined as the length-normalized ratio of the sequence likelihoods:

$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$

Using the chain rule, the gradient of the objective becomes the expectation of the group sum of the importance ratio multiplied by the advantage, times the gradient of the log of that importance ratio. Specifically:

$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_{i}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \nabla_{\theta} \log s_{i}(\theta) \right]$

By substituting the definition of $\log s_i(\theta)$, which is the average of the log-probability ratios across all tokens in the sequence, we arrive at the final gradient form:

$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$

# Comparative analysis: How GSPO vs. GRPO weight token log-likelihoods

The fundamental difference between GSPO and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) becomes apparent when we compare their respective gradients. In GRPO, the optimization is performed at the token level. The gradient for the GRPO objective is expressed as:

$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$

When we examine these two equations, we see that they both aim to update the model based on the log-likelihoods of the tokens $\nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$. However, they differ radically in how they weight these gradients. In GRPO, each individual token $t$ in a sequence is weighted by its own specific token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$. This means that within a single response, different tokens can have very different weights depending on how much the current policy deviates from the old policy at that specific token position.

In contrast, GSPO weights all tokens within a single response $y_i$ equally. The weighting factor $s_i(\theta)$ is a single value calculated for the entire sequence. As shown in the GSPO gradient derivation, the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ and the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ are factored out of the inner summation over tokens. Consequently, the gradient update for every token in a given response is scaled by the same sequence-level importance ratio, rather than by token-specific ratios.

# The stability benefit of equal token weighting

The decision to move from token-level weighting to equal sequence-level weighting is driven by the need for training stability. In GRPO, the token-level importance weights are not negligible; they can vary significantly, ranging between $0$ and $1+\varepsilon$ when the advantage is positive, or between $1-\varepsilon$ and infinity when the advantage is negative. Because these weights are based on a single sample from the next-token distribution, they fail to properly correct for the distributional mismatch between the old and new policies.

This misalignment introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients. In the context of large language models, where responses can be very long, this noise accumulates over the course of the sequence. The clipping mechanism, intended to stabilize training, can actually exacerbate this issue when combined with token-level ratios. The paper identifies that this cumulative noise can lead to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse, where the model's performance degrades so severely that reverting to previous checkpoints or tuning hyperparameters cannot recover the training process.

By applying a single, length-normalized importance ratio to the entire sequence, GSPO eliminates this source of instability. Weighting all tokens in a response equally removes the unpredictable fluctuations associated with token-level ratios. This approach aligns the unit of optimization (the sequence) with the unit of reward (the sequence-level reward), resulting in a more reliable and effective learning signal that allows for stable scaling, especially when training long-response tasks or using Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models.

# GSPO-token: Enabling token-wise advantage customization

While sequence-level optimization is stable, certain applications—such as multi-turn reinforcement learning—may require a finer level of control. In such cases, it may be desirable to assign different advantages to different tokens within a sequence rather than using a single advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ for the whole response. To accommodate this, the GSPO-token variant was introduced. The objective function for GSPO-token is defined as:

$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \min \left( s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) \right]$

The core innovation in this variant is the definition of the importance ratio $s_{i,t}(\theta)$:

$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg} \left[ s_{i}(\theta) \right] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }$

Here, $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ represents the stop-gradient operator (equivalent to `detach` in PyTorch), which means the operation takes the numerical value of the term but prevents gradients from flowing through it. Numerically, the term $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }$ is always equal to 1. Therefore, $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically identical to the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$. However, the way the gradient is computed changes. The gradient of the GSPO-token objective is:

$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$

# Mathematical equivalence between GSPO and GSPO-token under uniform advantages

GSPO-token is designed as a flexible generalization of the original GSPO algorithm. When we consider the scenario where every token in a response is assigned the same advantage—that is, $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$ for all tokens $t$ in response $y_i$—the two methods become mathematically equivalent.

If we substitute $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$ into the gradient for GSPO-token, the advantage term can be moved outside the inner summation over tokens. The resulting gradient expression becomes identical to the gradient derived for the standard GSPO objective. Furthermore, because $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$, the optimization objective and the clipping conditions are also numerically identical.

This equivalence demonstrates that GSPO is a special case of GSPO-token. While GSPO provides the stability of sequence-level weighting for standard rewards, GSPO-token preserves that same stability—by using the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ for weighting—while adding the flexibility to customize advantages at the token level $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. This allows researchers to choose the appropriate granularity of advantage adjustment without sacrificing the stability benefits provided by the sequence-level importance ratio.

RL for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Models

# Sparse Activation and Expert-Activation Volatility

To understand the challenges of applying reinforcement learning (RL) to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, one must first understand the nature of sparse activation. Unlike dense models where every parameter is utilized for every token, MoE models only activate a subset of their total parameters—the "experts"—for any given input. This architecture allows for massive model capacity while keeping the computational cost of a single forward pass relatively low. However, this sparsity introduces a unique stability challenge during RL training known as expert-activation volatility.

Expert-activation volatility refers to a phenomenon where the specific experts selected by the model's routing mechanism to process a token can change significantly after a gradient update. In a stable training environment, one would expect the model to maintain a consistent routing logic for the same input. However, in the context of RL, it has been observed that after only one or more gradient updates, the experts activated for the same response can shift. For example, in a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, approximately $10\%$ of the experts activated under a new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ are different from those activated under the previous policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ for the same rollout sample. This volatility is particularly prominent in deeper MoE models, creating a moving target for the optimization process.

# Why Token-Level Importance Ratios Fail in MoE Architectures

The problem of expert-activation volatility becomes catastrophic when paired with RL algorithms that rely on token-level importance ratios, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). In these algorithms, the importance ratio for a token $y_{i,t}$ is defined as $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$. The goal of this ratio is to correct for the fact that the data was sampled from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ rather than the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$ being optimized.

In a dense model, the change in the probability of a token usually evolves smoothly. In an MoE model, however, if the routing mechanism decides to switch which expert processes a token $y_{i,t}$, the likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ can fluctuate drastically. Because the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is calculated using these individual token probabilities, the volatility in expert activation leads to extreme fluctuations in the weights. This effectively invalidates the importance sampling mechanism, introducing high-variance noise into the gradients. When this noise accumulates over long sequences, it can lead to unpredictable training dynamics and potentially cause an irreversible model collapse.

# The Routing Replay Strategy: Background and Limitations

To combat expert-activation volatility, a technique called Routing Replay was previously developed. The intuition behind Routing Replay is to force the model to be consistent in its routing during the calculation of importance ratios. Specifically, the system caches the experts that were activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. When computing the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, the training process "replays" these cached routing modes in the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$.

By ensuring that $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ share the same activated network for each token $y_{i,t}$, Routing Replay restores stability to the token-level importance ratios. It ensures that the optimization focuses on the consistent activated network across gradient updates, preventing the drastic jumps in likelihood caused by routing shifts. While this strategy is essential for the normal convergence of algorithms like GRPO in MoE models, it comes with significant drawbacks. Routing Replay introduces additional memory and communication overhead due to the need to cache and retrieve routing decisions. More importantly, by forcing the model to use the old policy's routes, it imposes an artificial constraint on the model, which can limit the actual capacity and flexibility of the MoE architecture.

# How GSPO's Sequence-Level Focus Bypasses Routing Instability

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) provides a fundamental resolution to the MoE stability problem by changing the unit of optimization. Rather than calculating importance ratios at the token level, GSPO defines the importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ based on the sequence likelihood:

$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right)$

The critical insight here is that GSPO is concerned with the likelihood of the entire sequence $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)$ rather than the individual likelihood of each token $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$. While the routing for a specific token might change—causing a spike or drop in that token's individual probability—the overall language modeling capability of the MoE model remains relatively stable. Consequently, the sequence-level likelihood does not fluctuate as drastically as token-level likelihoods.

By shifting the focus to the sequence level, GSPO becomes inherently insensitive to the volatility of individual expert activations. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ provides a reliable and smooth signal for the clipping mechanism and gradient estimation, allowing the model to converge normally without the need for specialized routing interventions.

# Benefits of Removing Artificial Routing Constraints on Model Capacity

The ability of GSPO to handle MoE models without Routing Replay has profound implications for model performance and infrastructure. When a model is subjected to Routing Replay, it is effectively being told that its current routing decisions must adhere to previous ones to maintain mathematical stability. This artificial constraint prevents the model from fully exploring and utilizing its routing capacity during the RL process.

By eliminating the dependency on Routing Replay, GSPO allows the MoE model to leverage its full capacity. The model can evolve its routing logic naturally and dynamically as it learns from the RL rewards, without the risk of triggering a model collapse. This simplifies the training pipeline by removing the need for complex caching and replaying mechanisms, reducing memory and communication overhead. Ultimately, this algorithmic stability and removal of artificial constraints contribute to the superior performance seen in large-scale MoE RL training, such as in the Qwen3 models, where the model can truly unleash the power of its sparse architecture.

Empirical Evaluation and Infrastructure Implications

# Performance benchmarks: AIME, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces

To evaluate the efficacy of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), researchers conducted extensive experiments using a cold-start model fine-tuned from the Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base architecture. The goal was to determine if GSPO could provide the stability and performance gains necessary to scale reinforcement learning (RL) for complex reasoning tasks. The evaluation focused on three rigorous benchmarks: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench (specifically the 202410-202502 period), and CodeForces.

For the AIME'24 and LiveCodeBench benchmarks, the models were evaluated using the average Pass@1 metric, which measures the probability that at least one generated sample is correct. Specifically, AIME'24 was measured over 32 samplings, while LiveCodeBench was measured over 8 samplings. For CodeForces, performance was quantified using the Elo Rating, a standard measure of relative skill levels in competitive programming.

The results demonstrated that training with GSPO remains stable throughout the entire process. Unlike previous methodologies that often suffer from sudden model collapse, GSPO allows the model to maintain steady progress. These empirical results strongly validate that GSPO is a viable algorithmic foundation for the RL training of the latest Qwen3 models, successfully unleashing the potential of RL scaling for large-scale language models.

# Analysis of training efficiency and compute scaling

A critical requirement for any RL algorithm used in large-scale language modeling is the ability to scale effectively with increased computational resources. The researchers analyzed how GSPO behaves as more "compute" is invested into the training process. They found that GSPO facilitates continuous performance improvements through three primary levers: increasing the total training compute, regularly updating the set of queries used for training, and extending the generation length of the responses.

When comparing GSPO to the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) baseline, GSPO demonstrated superior training efficiency. This efficiency is measured by the model's ability to achieve higher training accuracy and better benchmark performance while using the same amount of training compute and the same number of consumed queries. This suggests that GSPO is better at extracting a learning signal from the available data.

The stability of GSPO is further highlighted by its interaction with the training pipeline. In the experimental setup, large batches of rollout data were partitioned into four mini-batches for gradient updates. Despite the off-policy nature of this setup, GSPO maintained robust training dynamics. This scalability ensures that as developers increase the complexity of tasks or the size of the models, the RL process remains reliable and productive.

# The paradox of clipping fractions: Why more clipping can lead to better efficiency

One of the most counter-intuitive findings in the evaluation of GSPO involves the "clipping fraction," or the proportion of data that is excluded from gradient estimation via the clipping mechanism. In RL algorithms like PPO and GRPO, clipping is used to prevent the policy from moving too far away from the old policy, which helps maintain stability. However, GRPO applies this clipping at the token level, whereas GSPO applies it at the sequence level.

Empirical observations revealed a striking disparity: GSPO clips a significantly larger fraction of tokens than GRPO—the difference is roughly two orders of magnitude. Under normal assumptions, one might expect that clipping more data would reduce the efficiency of the learning process because the model has fewer samples to learn from. However, the opposite occurred; GSPO achieved higher training efficiency despite the much higher clipping rate.

This paradox provides deep intuition into the failure of token-level importance weights. The researchers argue that the token-level gradient estimates in GRPO are inherently noisy and inefficient. Because GRPO's weights vary per token, they introduce high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences. By clipping entire responses that are too "off-policy," GSPO removes these noisy signals entirely. The fact that GSPO performs better with less unclipped data indicates that the sequence-level learning signal is far more reliable and effective for sample exploitation than the token-level signal.

# Precision discrepancies between training and inference engines

In practical large-scale RL deployments, there is often a technical divide between the inference engine (such as SGLang or vLLM), which generates the response samples, and the training engine (such as Megatron), which computes the gradients and updates the model. A recurring problem in these systems is the presence of precision discrepancies: the two engines may calculate the likelihood of a sequence slightly differently due to differences in numerical precision or software implementation.

To mitigate this, standard practice requires the training engine to recompute the likelihoods of all sampled responses under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ before performing the update. This recomputation step is computationally expensive and adds significant overhead to the RL infrastructure. This is because token-level importance ratios, like those used in GRPO, are highly sensitive to minute differences in probability values for each individual token.

GSPO fundamentally changes this dynamic by shifting the focus from token-level likelihoods to sequence-level likelihoods. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ is defined as the $1/|y_i|$-th power of the ratio of sequence likelihoods:
$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$
Because the sequence likelihood is an aggregate measure, it is naturally much more tolerant of the precision discrepancies between different engines.

# Simplifying RL infrastructure through sequence-level likelihoods

The increased tolerance of GSPO to precision discrepancies has direct and positive implications for the design of RL infrastructure. Because the sequence-level likelihood is stable across different engines, it becomes possible to use the likelihoods returned directly by the inference engine for optimization, completely bypassing the need for the training engine to recompute them.

This simplification offers several architectural advantages. First, it reduces the computational burden on the training engine, leading to faster iteration cycles. Second, it is particularly beneficial for complex training scenarios, such as partial rollouts and multi-turn RL, where managing likelihoods across multiple steps can become administratively complex.

Finally, this efficiency is highly advantageous for training-inference disaggregated frameworks, where the generation and optimization processes are physically or logically separated. By removing the requirement for precise token-level recomputation, GSPO allows for a more streamlined and flexible infrastructure that can scale more easily to larger models and more sophisticated reasoning tasks.
