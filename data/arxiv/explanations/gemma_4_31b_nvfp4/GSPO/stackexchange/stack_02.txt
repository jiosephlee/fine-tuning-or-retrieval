\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why is the GRPO objective considered 'ill-posed' for long sequences?
Question:
The authors argue that GRPO is unstable because it applies importance weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ at each token position. They claim this introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences, leading to 'catastrophic model collapse'. I understand that importance sampling is meant to correct distributional mismatch, but I don't understand why applying the weight at the token level specifically fails whereas applying it at the sequence level succeeds. What exactly is the 'misapplication' of importance sampling happening in GRPO?
Answer:
The "ill-posed" nature of the GRPO objective stems from a fundamental mismatch between the mathematical purpose of importance sampling and how it is applied at the token level. 

In standard importance sampling, the goal is to estimate the expectation of a function $f(z)$ under a target distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ using samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$. This is achieved by weighting the samples by the ratio $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$. For this to actually "correct" the distributional mismatch and provide a reliable estimate, the expectation must be averaged over many samples ($N \gg 1$). 

The misapplication in GRPO occurs because it applies the importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ at each individual token position $t$. At any given token, the model is not averaging over multiple possible tokens that could have appeared at that position; it is dealing with a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from the behavior policy. Because it is a single-sample estimate, the importance weight fails to perform a statistical distribution correction and instead acts as a source of high-variance noise.

This problem is severely amplified in long sequences because this variance accumulates over the entire response length $|y_i|$. Since the reward $r(x, y)$ is granted to the entire sequence, the "unit of reward" is the sequence, but the "unit of optimization" in GRPO is the token. This mismatch means that a single "noisy" token weight—potentially exacerbated by the clipping mechanism—can disproportionately swing the gradient for the entire sequence. In large models or Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, where small parameter updates can lead to significant changes in token-level probabilities (or expert routing), this accumulated noise can lead to "catastrophic model collapse," where the training dynamics become irreversibly unstable.

GSPO resolves this by shifting the importance ratio to the sequence level: $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. By treating the entire sequence as the unit of sampling, the importance weight now reflects how far the overall response $y$ deviates from the old policy, which naturally aligns with the sequence-level reward. In the resulting gradient, all tokens in a response are weighted equally by $s_i(\theta)$, eliminating the token-level variance and providing a much more stable learning signal.
