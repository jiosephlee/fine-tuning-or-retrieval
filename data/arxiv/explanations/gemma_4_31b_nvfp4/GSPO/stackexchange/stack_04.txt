\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does GSPO's gradient weighting differ from GRPO's in practice?
Question:
Looking at the gradient analysis in Section 4.2, it seems that in GRPO, each token's log-likelihood gradient is weighted by its specific token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. In GSPO, every token in the sequence is weighted by the same sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$. If we are trying to optimize the overall sequence, why does weighting all tokens equally based on the sequence average lead to more stability than weighting them individually based on their specific contribution to the shift?
Answer:
The fundamental difference between GRPO and GSPO lies in whether the off-policy correction (the importance ratio) is applied to individual tokens or to the sequence as a whole. In GRPO, each token $y_{i,t}$ in a response is weighted by its own importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$. This means that during the gradient update, different tokens in the same sequence can be pushed with wildly different magnitudes based on how much their individual probabilities shifted between the old and current policies.

The instability in GRPO arises because this token-level importance sampling is mathematically ill-posed. According to the principle of importance sampling, the ratio $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ is intended to correct for distributional mismatch when averaging over many samples. However, in GRPO, $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is calculated using only a single sample $y_{i,t}$ for each token position $t$. This introduces high-variance noise into the gradients. Because this noise is additive across the sequence, it accumulates as the response length increases, which can lead to catastrophic model collapse—a phenomenon the authors note is often irreversible.

GSPO addresses this by aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. Since the reward $r(x, y)$ is granted to the entire sequence, GSPO uses a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, which is defined as the length-normalized geometric mean of the token-level ratios: $s_i(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right)$. In the resulting gradient $\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}$, every token in the sequence is weighted by this same value $s_i(\theta)$.

Intuitively, this acts as a smoothing mechanism. Instead of allowing a single "erratic" token to drastically pivot the gradient for that specific position, GSPO looks at the overall shift in the likelihood of the entire response. This is particularly critical for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. In MoE training, "expert-activation volatility" occurs when the model chooses different experts for the same token between $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$. This causes the token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to fluctuate violently. By using the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$, GSPO ignores these volatile token-level shifts and focuses on the stable overall sequence likelihood, eliminating the need for complex stabilization strategies like Routing Replay.
