# Why Your LLM Might Be Collapsing: Moving from Token to Sequence Optimization

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the primary engine for scaling the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). By encouraging models to engage in deeper, longer reasoning processes, RL has enabled breakthroughs in complex mathematics and programming. However, as models grow in size and responses become longer, researchers have encountered a recurring nightmare: catastrophic and irreversible model collapse.

In this post, we explore why popular RL algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) can be unstable and how a new approach called Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) provides the stability needed to scale the latest generation of models, including Qwen3.

## The Fragility of Token-Level RL

To improve sample efficiency, RL training typically partitions rollout data into mini-batches for gradient updates. This creates an "off-policy" setting where the responses being optimized were generated by an old version of the policy ($\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$) rather than the current one ($\pi_{\theta}$). To prevent the model from drifting too far and becoming unstable, algorithms like PPO and GRPO use a clipping mechanism based on "importance ratios."

In GRPO, this importance ratio is calculated at the token level. For a token $y_{i,t}$, the ratio is defined as:

$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$

While this seems intuitive, it introduces a fundamental flaw: the unit of optimization (the token) does not match the unit of reward (the entire sequence). Because the reward is granted to the whole response, applying off-policy correction at every single token introduces high-variance noise. This noise accumulates as the response length increases, and when combined with the clipping mechanism, it can precipitate a total model collapse. Once this happens, the damage is often irreversible—reverting to checkpoints or tuning hyperparameters rarely fixes the issue.

## Introducing GSPO: Sequence-Level Stability

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) addresses this instability by shifting the importance ratio from the token level to the sequence level. The core philosophy is simple: if the reward is sequence-level, the optimization and clipping should be sequence-level too.

GSPO defines the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ based on the likelihood of the entire sequence:

$s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right)$

By using length normalization (the exponent $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$), GSPO ensures that the importance ratio remains within a unified numerical range regardless of how long the response is. This prevents a few highly volatile tokens from causing dramatic fluctuations in the ratio.

The resulting objective function $\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)$ applies clipping to the entire response. This eliminates the erratic token-level weighting seen in GRPO, where different tokens in the same response can have wildly different weights. In GSPO, all tokens in a response are weighted equally, creating a much more reliable and stable learning signal.

## Solving the MoE Stability Problem

The stability benefits of GSPO are particularly evident when training Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. MoE models suffer from "expert-activation volatility," where a single gradient update can significantly change which experts are activated for the same input. 

In token-level algorithms like GRPO, this volatility causes the importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to fluctuate drastically, hindering convergence. To fix this, developers previously relied on "Routing Replay"—a complex strategy that caches and forces the use of old routing modes. 

GSPO fundamentally resolves this issue without needing Routing Replay. Because GSPO focuses on the overall sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)$ rather than individual token likelihoods, it is not sensitive to which specific expert was triggered for a specific token. This simplifies the training pipeline and allows MoE models to utilize their full capacity without artificial constraints.

## Simplifying the RL Infrastructure

Beyond algorithmic stability, GSPO offers practical advantages for RL infrastructure. There is often a precision discrepancy between the inference engines (like vLLM or SGLang) used for rollouts and the training engines (like Megatron) used for updates. 

Traditionally, the training engine must recompute token-level likelihoods to ensure precision. However, because sequence-level likelihoods are much more tolerant of these discrepancies, GSPO allows the model to use likelihoods returned directly from the inference engine. This reduces computational overhead and simplifies the design of disaggregated training-inference frameworks.

## Conclusion

By aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward, GSPO transforms RL training from a fragile process prone to collapse into a robust, scalable foundation. The results are clear: superior training efficiency, stable MoE convergence, and significant performance gains in the Qwen3 models across benchmarks like AIME'24 and CodeForces. For anyone scaling LLMs for long-form reasoning, moving from token-level to sequence-level optimization may be the key to avoiding the collapse.
