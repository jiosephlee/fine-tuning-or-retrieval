\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the Cayley transform ensure the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$?
Question:
In the section on Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the authors mention that they employ Cayley parameterization, defined as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix ($\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$). I understand that this is used to ensure $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout the training process, but I am struggling with the mathematical intuition. How does the skew-symmetry of $\bm{Q}$ specifically guarantee that $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$?
Answer:
The Cayley transform provides a way to map any skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ to an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. To understand why this guarantees that $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, we can examine the properties of the transpose of $\bm{R}$ and how the skew-symmetry of $\bm{Q}$ interacts with the identity matrix.

Given the parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, let us first find the transpose $\bm{R}^\top$. Recall that for any matrices $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$, $(\bm{AB})^\top = \bm{B}^\top \bm{A}^\top$ and $(\bm{A}^{-1})^\top = (\bm{A}^\top)^{-1}$. Applying this to $\bm{R}$, we get $\bm{R}^\top = ((\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1})^\top (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^\top$. Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$) and the identity matrix $\bm{I}$ is symmetric, this simplifies to $\bm{R}^\top = (\bm{I} - (-\bm{Q}))^{-1} (\bm{I} - \bm{Q}) = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$.

Now, we compute the product $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}$:
$$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = [(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})] \cdot [(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}]$$
The key to simplifying this expression is noticing that the terms $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ and $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})$ commute. We can verify this by expanding their product: $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})(\bm{I}+\bm{Q}) = \bm{I} + \bm{Q} - \bm{Q} - \bm{Q}^2 = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2$, and similarly $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}) = \bm{I} - \bm{Q} + \bm{Q} - \bm{Q}^2 = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2$. Since they commute, we can rearrange the middle terms of the product $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}$ as follows:
$$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1} [(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})] (\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1} [(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})] (\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$$
The terms $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})$ and $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ both reduce to the identity matrix $\bm{I}$, leaving us with $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$.

Intuitively, the Cayley transform acts as a bridge between the Lie algebra of skew-symmetric matrices and the Lie group of orthogonal matrices. In the context of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), maintaining orthogonality is difficult because the constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$ is non-linear; updating $\bm{R}$ using standard gradient descent would likely break this property. By optimizing $\bm{Q}$ instead, the model only needs to maintain the linear constraint $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$ (which is trivial to implement by setting $\bm{Q}_{ij} = -\bm{Q}_{ji}$), and the Cayley transform automatically "projects" this into a valid rotation matrix $\bm{R}$ at every step of the training process.
