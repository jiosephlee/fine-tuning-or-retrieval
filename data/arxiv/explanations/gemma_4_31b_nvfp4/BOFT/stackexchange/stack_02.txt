\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why is the butterfly structure considered 'dense' while the block-diagonal structure is not?
Question:
I am confused about the terminology used in the paper. Both the block-diagonal structure in original OFT and the butterfly structure in BOFT use sparse matrices to reduce the number of parameters. However, the paper states that the block-diagonal structure cannot construct a dense matrix $\bm{R}$, whereas BOFT is designed to generate a 'dense orthogonal matrix' via factorization. If the individual factors $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}$ are sparse, in what sense is the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ actually dense?
Answer:
The confusion stems from a distinction between the **individual factors** used to build the matrix and the **resulting product matrix** $\bm{R}$. In both block-diagonal OFT and BOFT, the trainable components are indeed sparse to ensure parameter efficiency. However, the "density" the paper refers to describes the connectivity of the final matrix $\bm{R}$ that is actually applied to the weights.

In the block-diagonal structure of original OFT, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is defined as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$. Because this is a single-step structure (or a product of matrices that all share the same block-diagonal sparsity pattern), it can never become a dense matrix. If two indices $i$ and $j$ belong to different blocks, the element $R_{ij}$ will always be zero, regardless of the values learned within those blocks. From the paper's "information transmission" perspective, this means the neurons are divided into isolated groups; information can flow within a block, but it can never jump from one block to another.

BOFT takes a fundamentally different approach by representing $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ sparse matrices: $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. While each individual factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}$ is sparse, the product of sparse matrices is not necessarily sparse. The butterfly structure is specifically designed so that each successive multiplication spreads the "information flow" further across the dimensions. 

Intuitively, you can think of this as a communication network. A block-diagonal matrix is like a set of disconnected local area networks (LANs); no matter how much data you move within a LAN, you cannot reach a computer in a different LAN. The butterfly structure, however, acts like a series of switching stages. Even though each switch (each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}$) only connects a few nodes, the combined effect of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ stages allows every single node in the input to eventually reach every single node in the output. 

Consequently, while the number of trainable parameters remains low—$\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ for BOFT versus $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a full dense matrix—the resulting product $\bm{R}$ is mathematically "dense" because it can potentially have non-zero entries at every position. This allows BOFT to approximate a much wider range of orthogonal transformations and recover classic linear transforms (like the Fourier transform), which is impossible for a block-diagonal matrix.
