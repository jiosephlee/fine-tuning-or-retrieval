\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Introduction to Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT)

# The challenge of training foundation models from scratch

The emergence of large foundation models, such as ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion, has demonstrated a remarkable ability to generalize across a wide array of tasks. These models achieve such high levels of performance due to their immense scale, which is characterized by a dramatic increase in the number of parameters. For instance, GPT-3 contains approximately 175 billion parameters. While this scale allows the models to capture complex patterns and extensive knowledge, it also creates a significant barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners.

The primary challenge lies in the fact that training a model of this magnitude from scratch is prohibitively expensive. The computational resources, time, and financial costs required to initialize and optimize hundreds of billions of parameters are beyond the reach of most organizations. Because of this, it is no longer practical to build a new foundation model for every specific downstream application. Instead, the focus of the field has shifted toward the ability to adapt existing, pretrained foundation models to new tasks without the need for full retraining.

# Overview of PEFT categories: Model Finetuning, Adapter Tuning, and Prompt Tuning

To address the challenges of retraining, researchers have developed various parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) strategies. These methods aim to adapt a model to a specific task while only updating a small fraction of the total parameters. These strategies can be broadly categorized into three main approaches: model finetuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning.

Model finetuning is a straightforward approach where the original architecture of the model remains unchanged, but a specific subset of the existing model parameters is updated during the training process. This method is particularly advantageous because it is simple and powerful, and most importantly, it introduces no additional inference latency since the model structure remains identical to the pretrained version.

Adapter tuning takes a different approach by introducing additional trainable parameters into the original model. Rather than modifying the pretrained weights directly, adapters add new layers or modules that are trained to specialize the model for the downstream task. While effective, this differs from model finetuning as it changes the architecture by adding new components.

Prompt tuning is another strategy that focuses on the input side of the model. In this approach, additional trainable prefix tokens are attached to the input. The model learns to optimize these tokens to guide the frozen foundation model toward the desired output for a specific task, avoiding the need to modify the internal weights of the model itself.

# The trade-off between parameter efficiency and model performance

In the context of adaptation, there is a fundamental tension between parameter efficiency and model performance. Parameter efficiency refers to the goal of reducing the number of trainable parameters to lower the computational cost and storage requirements. However, reducing the number of trainable parameters can potentially limit the model's expressiveness, which may impact its ability to adapt to a complex downstream task.

This trade-off is evident when comparing different structured approaches. For example, some methods use low-rank structures or sparse matrices to reduce the parameter budget. While these constraints make the training process more efficient, they may introduce inductive biases. If these biases are too restrictive, the model may fail to capture the nuances of the new task. Conversely, if the parameter space is too large, the model may overfit the downstream data and lose the generalizability it gained during pretraining. The goal of an ideal PEFT method is to find a hypothesis class that is small enough for efficiency and regularity but large enough to provide the necessary expressivity for the task.

# Introduction to the goal of preserving pretraining knowledge during adaptation

A critical objective during the finetuning process is ensuring that the pretrained model and the finetuned model remain similar. This is based on the principle that the foundation model already possesses vast amounts of semantic knowledge from its initial training; if this knowledge is discarded or corrupted during adaptation, the model may lose its generalizability and stability. This process of preserving pretraining knowledge is often viewed as maintaining a balance between the "foundation" and the "adaptation."

One common, although ad hoc, method to achieve this is by employing a very small learning rate. By limiting the magnitude of the weight updates, researchers encourage a small discrepancy between the pretrained and finetuned weights, thereby preventing the model from deviating too far from its original state. 

A more principled approach focuses on preserving the relational information within the weight matrices. Specifically, this involves preserving the pairwise angles between neurons in the same layer. If these angles are maintained, the relative orientation of the neurons—and thus the internal representations learned during pretraining—is preserved throughout the finetuning process. This insight allows for a structured form of adaptation, such as orthogonal transformations, where the weights are modified in a way that provably protects the pretraining knowledge while still allowing the model to specialize for a downstream task.

Foundations of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

# The Concept of Preserving Pairwise Angles Between Neurons

In the context of adapting large foundation models to downstream tasks, a primary challenge is to update the model's parameters without destroying the vast amount of semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining. Conventional finetuning often relies on an ad hoc approach, such as employing a very small learning rate, to ensure that the finetuned model does not deviate too drastically from the pretrained version. However, a more principled approach involves preserving the relational information embedded within the weight matrices of the model. Specifically, the relational information is characterized by the pairwise angles between neurons.

Neurons in a linear layer can be viewed as vectors. The geometric relationship—the angles—between these vectors represents the structural knowledge the model has learned. If these angles are preserved during the adaptation process, the model is more likely to retain its generalizability and the fundamental knowledge captured during its initial training. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is built upon this insight. By applying an orthogonal transformation to the neurons in a layer, OFT ensures that the pairwise angles between neurons are provably preserved. This prevents the "catastrophic" loss of pretrained knowledge and provides a more stable foundation for adapting the model to a specific new task.

# Mathematical Formulation of OFT: The Multiplicative Orthogonal Update

To understand the mathematical implementation of OFT, we must first consider a standard pretrained linear layer. Let $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ represent the frozen pretrained weight matrix. In a standard forward pass, given an input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, the output vector $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is calculated as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. While other parameter-efficient methods, such as LoRA, update the weights by adding a low-rank matrix to the original weights, OFT employs a multiplicative update strategy.

In OFT, a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is optimized to transform the neurons of the layer. The forward pass is modified to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. In this formulation, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ acts on the weights of the pretrained model before the input $\bm{x}$ is processed. To ensure that $\bm{R}$ remains strictly orthogonal throughout the training process—meaning $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$—OFT utilizes the Cayley parameterization. This method defines the orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix such that $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. By optimizing the entries of the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ is guaranteed to be orthogonal, thereby maintaining the preservation of the pairwise angles between neurons.

# The Role of the Identity Matrix in Zero Initialization

A critical requirement for any finetuning method is that it should start from the exact state of the pretrained model. This ensures that the adaptation process begins with the full knowledge of the foundation model and only deviates as the training data guides the optimization. In additive methods like LoRA, this is achieved through "zero initialization," where the trainable low-rank matrices are initialized to zero, making the initial update term vanish.

In the case of OFT, the equivalent of zero initialization is achieved by initializing the learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as an identity matrix $\bm{I}$. Within the Cayley parameterization framework, this means initializing the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as a zero matrix. When $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$, the formula $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1}$ simplifies to $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$. Because the identity matrix does not alter the pretrained weights ($\bm{I}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0$), the model starts the finetuning process exactly as it was after pretraining. This starting point is essential for maintaining training stability and ensuring that the model does not deviate into an unstable parameter space before it has begun to learn the downstream task.

# Limitations of Block-Diagonal Structures in OFT: Parameter Efficiency vs. Expressivity

While a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ provides maximum expressivity, it comes with a significant computational cost. A full dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters, which can be prohibitively large for the high-dimensional layers found in foundation models. To mitigate this, original OFT implementations introduce a block-diagonal structure. In this setup, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is a small orthogonal matrix and $br = d$.

This block-diagonal approach significantly improves parameter efficiency, as the number of trainable parameters is reduced to a fraction of the original $d^2$. However, this efficiency introduces a restrictive inductive bias. Specifically, it assumes that the dimensions of a neuron—which is a column vector of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$—can be divided into $r$ groups based on their indices, and that dimensions in different groups should be transformed independently. This is an arbitrary division that does not account for the actual semantic relationship between different dimensions of the neuron. 

Consequently, the block-diagonal structure limits the expressivity of the model. It cannot approximate a general dense orthogonal matrix and is unable to recover classic linear transforms. This creates a tension between parameter efficiency and expressivity: smaller block sizes $b$ lead to fewer parameters but further restrict the transformation's ability to adapt to the task, while larger block sizes increase the parameter count. This limitation highlights the need for a method that can generate a dense orthogonal matrix while maintaining a low number of trainable parameters.

# The Information Transmission Framework

To understand how to make orthogonal finetuning more parameter-efficient, it is helpful to move away from thinking of matrix multiplication solely as an algebraic operation and instead view it through the lens of information transmission. In the context of adapting large foundation models, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) utilizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to transform neurons in a layer, which helps preserve the relational information—specifically the pairwise angles between neurons—learned during pretraining. However, a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is often prohibitively expensive. The information transmission framework provides a principled way to decompose this dense matrix into a sequence of sparse matrices, thereby reducing the number of trainable parameters while maintaining the desired properties of the transformation.

# Mapping Matrix Multiplication to Grid-Structured Graphs

The core of this framework is the conceptual mapping of the product of multiple square matrices to a directed graph. Consider the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ sparse square matrices: $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \dots \bm{B}_1$. This mathematical sequence can be visualized as the flow of information across a grid-structured graph containing $d \times (m+1)$ nodes. In this visualization, the nodes are arranged into $m+1$ levels, where each level contains $d$ nodes.

Each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ in the factorization serves as the connectivity matrix that defines how information moves from the $i$-th level of nodes to the $(i+1)$-th level. Specifically, an element at the $(j_1, j_2)$ position of the matrix $\bm{B}_i$ determines whether a directed edge exists from the $j_2$-th node in level $i$ to the $j_1$-th node in level $i+1$. If the element is non-zero, information can be transmitted across that edge; if the element is zero, no path exists between those two specific nodes at those levels. By unrolling the matrix multiplication in this way, the complex operation of multiplying $m$ matrices is transformed into a study of paths through a layered graph.

# Interpreting Dense Matrices as Full Connectivity Between Source and Receiver Nodes

From this graphical perspective, a dense matrix can be interpreted as a state of full connectivity. A $d$-dimensional dense square matrix implies that every single node in the source set is connected to every single node in the receiver set. If the resulting product matrix $\bm{R}$ has a zero at position $R_{ij}$, it indicates a failure in the network: information from the $j$-th node in the first level cannot reach the $i$-th node in the $(m+1)$-th level.

When we decompose $\bm{R}$ into a product of sparse matrices $\bm{B}_m \dots \bm{B}_1$, we are essentially designing a communication network. For the final matrix $\bm{R}$ to be dense, the sparsity patterns of the individual factors $\bm{B}_i$ must be arranged such that every node in the first level has at least one valid path leading to every node in the final level. If the factorization is insufficient—for example, if a product of only a few of these matrices fails to connect node 1 to node 3—then the resulting product matrix will necessarily contain zeros, failing to be a dense matrix. Therefore, the goal of parameter-efficient factorization is to ensure this "global" connectivity using the fewest "local" edges possible.

# Desiderata for Efficient Factorization: Dense Connectivity and Minimum Free Edges

The information transmission framework allows us to define a set of specific desiderata, or design goals, to achieve parameter efficiency without sacrificing the expressiveness of the orthogonal transformation. The first goal is dense connectivity. As established, for a matrix $\bm{R}$ to be dense, the set of directed edges across the $m$ levels must be configured so that every source node in the first level can transmit information to every receiver node in the last level.

The second goal is the minimization of "free edges." The objective is to achieve the aforementioned dense connectivity while keeping the total number of non-zero entries across all $\bm{B}_i$ matrices as small as possible. This is because each non-zero entry potentially represents a trainable parameter. By treating the problem as a search for the most efficient graph topology, researchers can identify structures—such as the butterfly structure used in the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform—that can connect $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges. This is a significant improvement over the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges required by a single dense matrix.

# The Impact of Orthogonality Constraints on Graph Edges

While the information transmission framework is based on graph connectivity, the fact that these matrices must be orthogonal introduces critical constraints on the possible edges. Orthogonality is not a neutral property; it dictates the minimum requirements for the graph's structure and the dependencies between its edges. For any matrix $\bm{B}_i$ to be orthogonal, it must first be full-rank. In graph terms, this means there must be at least $d$ edges between any two adjacent levels to form a bijection between all nodes of level $i$ and level $i+1$. Because these $d$ edges are necessary for the matrix to be orthogonal and are often fixed as $\pm 1$, they are not counted as "trainable" parameters.

Furthermore, the orthogonality constraint introduces algebraic dependencies that mean some edges are implicitly linked. For example, in a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrix, if there is a zero at the $(1,1)$ position, the $(2,2)$ position must also be zero to maintain orthogonality. In the context of the information transmission graph, this implies that the absence of one edge may force the absence of another. Consequently, the orthogonality constraint can both add and remove edges from a feasible set, making the design of the sparsity pattern a delicate balance between the algebraic requirements of the orthogonal group and the connectivity requirements of the information flow.

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) Parameterization

# Inspiration from the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)

To understand the mechanism behind Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), one must first understand the challenge of parameter efficiency in Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). In OFT, a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is updated by multiplying it with a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. While the use of orthogonal matrices preserves the pairwise angles between neurons—thereby protecting the semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining—a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ of dimension $d \times d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. This dimensionality makes full orthogonal finetuning prohibitively expensive for large models.

The researchers propose viewing the construction of this orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem. In this framework, multiplying a series of sparse matrices to create a dense result is analogous to transmitting information across a grid-structured graph. For a final matrix $\bm{R}$ to be dense, every node in the input level must have a path to every node in the output level. A simple block-diagonal structure, as used in previous versions of OFT, fails this requirement because it separates dimensions into independent groups, preventing information from flowing between different blocks.

The inspiration for solving this comes from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm. In the FFT, a large discrete Fourier transform is recursively broken down into smaller transforms. This process induces a "butterfly" structure where a local change in the frequency domain can result in a global change in the spatial domain. This global connectivity is exactly what is needed for parameter-efficient finetuning: a way to achieve dense connectivity (where every input node can influence every output node) using a very small number of edges. By adopting this butterfly topology, BOFT can generate a dense orthogonal matrix through the product of several sparse orthogonal matrices, effectively trading a small amount of computation time during training for a massive reduction in parameter space.

# Defining the Butterfly Factor and the Recursive Butterfly Matrix

The technical construction of BOFT begins with the definition of a butterfly factor, denoted as $\bm{B}^F(k)$. Assuming $k$ is a power of 2, the butterfly factor is a $k \times k$ matrix defined as:
$$\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix} \text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\ \text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4) \end{bmatrix}$$
where each $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ is a vector. This structure ensures that the matrix is sparse, containing only a few non-zero entries, yet it provides the necessary connectivity to shuffle information across the dimensions of the matrix.

To scale this to a $d$-dimensional matrix, the butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ is defined recursively. For a dimension $d = 2^N$, the butterfly matrix is constructed as:
$$\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d) \cdot \begin{bmatrix} \bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2}) & \bm{0} \\ \bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2}) \end{bmatrix}$$
By expanding this recursion, the butterfly matrix can be written as a product of butterfly components: $\bm{B}(d) = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\frac{d}{2})\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$. A butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is defined as a block-diagonal matrix $\text{diag}(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\cdots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k))$, where each block is a butterfly factor of size $k$.

To ensure that the resulting product $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout the finetuning process, each individual butterfly component must be orthogonal. This is achieved using the Cayley transform, which parameterizes an orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix ($\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$). By applying this to the $2 \times 2$ blocks within the butterfly components, the researchers ensure that the product of these sparse matrices remains a valid orthogonal transformation.

# Block Butterfly Components and Their Construction

To further generalize the framework and provide more flexibility in the trade-off between expressivity and parameter count, BOFT introduces block butterfly components. In the basic butterfly factor, the elements of $\bm{d}_i$ are scalars. In a block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$, each entry in $\bm{d}_i$ is replaced by a $b \times b$ matrix. This means that instead of rotating individual dimensions, the model rotates blocks of dimensions.

To maintain orthogonality in this block-based setup, the researchers parameterize each $2b \times 2b$ block matrix within the butterfly component to be orthogonal. Since the non-zero pattern of every other butterfly component is essentially a block-wise permutation of the non-zero pattern of the initial $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$ component, they can all be parameterized as orthogonal matrices using the same principles.

The complete forward pass in BOFT is then expressed as:
$$\bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x}$$
Here, $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is the final orthogonal matrix, formed by the product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components: $\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. This construction allows the model to start from the pretrained weights (by initializing the skew-symmetric matrices in the Cayley transform to zero, resulting in an identity matrix) and gradually learn the necessary orthogonal transformation to adapt to the downstream task.

# Complexity Analysis: $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ vs. $\mathcal{O}(d)$ vs. $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ Parameters

One of the primary motivations for BOFT is the drastic reduction in the number of trainable parameters. To appreciate this, we can compare it to other orthogonal parameterization strategies. A full, dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. For large-scale models where $d$ is high, this is computationally impractical. 

The original version of OFT attempted to solve this using a block-diagonal structure. In a block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, the matrix consists of $r$ independent blocks where $br = d$. This reduces the parameter complexity to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$. If $b$ is small, this is very efficient, but it limits the expressivity because dimensions in different blocks cannot interact. To achieve a dense orthogonal matrix using the block-diagonal approach, one would have to set $b=d$, returning to the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ complexity.

BOFT provides a middle ground by using the butterfly structure. For a configuration where $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$, BOFT can generate a dense orthogonal matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. More generally, BOFT($m, b$) utilizes $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters. This represents a significant improvement: BOFT can achieve the expressivity of a dense matrix (which traditionally requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$) while maintaining a parameter count closer to the efficiency of a block-diagonal matrix. While this requires performing more matrix multiplications during the training phase, it introduces no additional latency during inference, as the learned $\bm{R}$ can be pre-multiplied into the frozen weights $\bm{W}^0$.

# Generalizing BOFT: From Block-Diagonal OFT to Full Orthogonal Matrices

BOFT is designed as a generalized framework that subsumes original Orthogonal Finetuning as a special case. This relationship can be understood by varying the number of butterfly components $m$ and the block size $b$. For instance, when $m=1$, BOFT($1, b$) is mathematically identical to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. If we further set $m=1$ and $b=d$, BOFT reduces to the original OFT using a full, unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix.

Beyond these cases, BOFT can be extended into what is known as a kaleidoscope hierarchy. While a single butterfly matrix is highly efficient, researchers have found that multiplying multiple butterfly matrices—specifically using the form $\bm{R}^G = \bm{R}_{l,1}\bm{R}_{l,2}^\top \dots \bm{R}_{1,1}\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top$—can represent the entire orthogonal group if the number of layers $l$ is sufficiently large (e.g., $l = d-1$). 

This hierarchy illustrates the trade-off between expressivity and regularity. While a full orthogonal matrix has universal expressiveness, it often lacks the regularization needed to prevent overfitting during finetuning. By restricting the hypothesis class to a structured subset of the orthogonal group via butterfly factorization, BOFT introduces a beneficial inductive bias. This bias is rooted in the fact that the butterfly structure is shared by many classic linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier and Hadamard transforms, allowing BOFT to find a more stable and generalizable solution for downstream tasks.

Practical Implementation and Training Stability

# Maintaining Orthogonality via the Cayley Transform

A core requirement of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is that the transformation matrix $\bm{R}$ must remain orthogonal throughout the entire finetuning process. Orthogonality is not merely a mathematical curiosity; it is fundamental to the method's success because it preserves the pairwise angles between neurons and the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. If the matrix $\bm{R}$ were to lose its orthogonality during gradient descent, the model would lose the stability and generalization benefits that define the orthogonal finetuning paradigm. However, ensuring that a matrix remains orthogonal during optimization is challenging because the set of orthogonal matrices forms a manifold rather than a simple linear space.

To solve this, BOFT employs the Cayley Transform, a method that allows the optimization of an orthogonal matrix by instead optimizing a skew-symmetric matrix. A skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ is defined by the property $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$, meaning its transpose is its own negative. The Cayley Transform maps such a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ to an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ using the formula $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. 

The intuition behind this approach is that any matrix $\bm{R}$ generated via this transform is guaranteed to be orthogonal, regardless of the specific values within $\bm{Q}$, provided that the inverse $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ exists. By parameterizing the butterfly components through $\bm{Q}$, the training process can use standard gradient-based optimization on $\bm{Q}$ without ever needing to explicitly constrain $\bm{R}$ to be orthogonal. This transforms a constrained optimization problem into an unconstrained one, ensuring that the structural integrity of the orthogonal transformation is maintained across every training iteration.

# Identity Initialization Strategies for Weight Preservation

In the context of adapting large foundation models to downstream tasks, it is critical that the finetuning process begins from the exact state of the pretrained model. This prevents the model from deviating too sharply from the powerful representations it learned during pretraining, which helps avoid catastrophic forgetting and ensures faster convergence. In many parameter-efficient finetuning methods, such as LoRA, this is achieved through zero initialization of the additive low-rank weights, effectively making the initial update an identity operation.

Because BOFT uses a multiplicative weight update rather than an additive one, zero initialization is not applicable. Multiplying the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ by a zero matrix would destroy all pretrained knowledge. Instead, BOFT employs identity initialization. The goal is for the initial orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to be the identity matrix $\bm{I}$, such that the initial forward pass $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ is identical to the original pretrained forward pass $(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$.

This is implemented through the Cayley Transform. To make $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, the corresponding skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ must be initialized as a zero matrix. When $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$, the formula $(\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1}$ simplifies directly to $\bm{I}$. By initializing all the butterfly components as identity matrices, BOFT ensures that the finetuning process starts exactly at the pretrained model's weights, providing a stable baseline for adaptation.

# Multiplicative Dropout: Adapting Dropout for Multiplicative Weight Updates

Regularization is essential in finetuning to prevent the model from overfitting to the often smaller datasets used in downstream tasks. Traditional dropout techniques, which randomly set individual elements of a weight matrix to zero, are highly effective for additive updates. However, such an approach is incompatible with the multiplicative structure of BOFT. Zeroing out an element in a product of orthogonal matrices would not only break the orthogonality of the resulting transformation but could also completely block the flow of information through the butterfly network.

To address this, BOFT introduces a specialized multiplicative Dropout. Instead of replacing elements with zeros, this method replaces specific structural components with identity matrices. Because the final orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is composed of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, the multiplicative Dropout operates at two levels of granularity. First, it randomly selects a percentage $p_1$ of the entire butterfly components and replaces them with identity matrices. Second, it randomly selects a percentage $p_2$ of the diagonal blocks within each butterfly component and replaces those blocks with identity matrices.

The intuition here is that by replacing a component with an identity matrix, the dropout mechanism effectively "bypasses" that specific transformation. The information flows through the network as if that particular layer or block were not present, forcing the model to distribute its learning across other components. This encourages redundancy and robustness in the learned transformations, providing the benefits of regularization while strictly preserving the orthogonality of the overall matrix $\bm{R}$.

# The Training Pipeline: From Frozen Pretrained Weights to Learned Butterfly Components

The practical implementation of BOFT follows a structured pipeline designed to maximize parameter efficiency while eliminating inference latency. The process begins with a frozen pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ of size $d \times n$. Unlike full finetuning, where all elements of $\bm{W}^0$ are updated, BOFT keeps $\bm{W}^0$ completely static throughout the training process.

During the forward pass, the model learns a transformation matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$, which is parameterized as the product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components: $\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. The input vector $\bm{x}$ is processed by the modified linear layer as $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. The only trainable parameters in this setup are the skew-symmetric matrices $\bm{Q}$ used to construct each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. By using the butterfly structure, the number of parameters is reduced from the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ required for a dense orthogonal matrix to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ when using the standard butterfly matrix configuration ($m=\log d, b=2$).

A significant advantage of this pipeline is the transition from training to inference. Once the finetuning stage is complete and the optimal butterfly components have been learned, the final orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is computed. This matrix can then be pre-multiplied into the frozen weights to create a new weight matrix: $\bm{W}_{final} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because $\bm{W}_{final}$ has the same dimensions as the original $\bm{W}^0$, the final model architecture is identical to the original. Consequently, BOFT introduces zero additional latency during inference, as the complex product of butterfly matrices is collapsed into a single weight matrix before the model is deployed.

# Theoretical Insights: Expressivity and Spectral Properties

The effectiveness of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) as a parameter-efficient finetuning method is not merely empirical; it is grounded in several critical mathematical properties. Understanding these properties—specifically how BOFT approximates orthogonal matrices, how it preserves the spectral integrity of pretrained weights, and the inductive biases it introduces—is essential for understanding why it outperforms traditional block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning (OFT) and other low-rank adaptation methods.

# Expressivity: Comparing BOFT and block-diagonal OFT

In the context of orthogonal finetuning, expressivity refers to the ability of a parameterized orthogonal matrix to approximate any arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix. A dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ provides the maximum possible flexibility for transforming neurons in a layer, but it requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is prohibitively expensive for large models. To combat this, original OFT utilizes a block-diagonal structure, which significantly reduces parameters but limits expressivity because it cannot approximate any matrix that requires connectivity between different blocks.

BOFT addresses this limitation by replacing the block-diagonal structure with a butterfly factorization. Through the use of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices, BOFT can generate a dense orthogonal matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. When comparing the two, BOFT is provably more expressive than OFT for a given block size. For instance, empirical simulations involving the approximation of random dense orthogonal matrices of size $512 \times 512$ reveal that BOFT achieves much lower approximation errors than OFT when the parameter budget is kept constant. A specific example of this efficiency is seen when comparing $\text{BOFT}(9, 2)$ to $\text{BOFT}(1, 16)$; the former exhibits better expressiveness than the latter despite using significantly fewer trainable parameters.

The general rule is that BOFT configurations with smaller block sizes $b$ and a larger number of factors $m$ are generally more parameter-efficient. This indicates that the butterfly structure is a more effective way of navigating the orthogonal group than the block-diagonal structure. While the block-diagonal approach creates a rigid partition of neurons, the butterfly structure allows for a more fluid exchange of information across the entire dimensionality of the neuron, enabling the model to find a better hypothesis class for downstream tasks.

# The Kaleidoscope Hierarchy: Approximating the full orthogonal group

While the standard BOFT configuration provides a highly efficient approximation, it is possible to extend this framework to represent the entire orthogonal group. This is achieved through a generalization known as the kaleidoscope hierarchy. In this setup, the final orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}^G$ is constructed as a sequence of butterfly matrices and their transposes:

$\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)=\bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^T(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^T(m_2,b_2)$

where $\bm{R}_{i,j}^T(m,b)$ represents the orthogonal matrix used in the BOFT parameterization. According to the theory of kaleidoscope hierarchies, if the parameters are set such that $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and $l=d-1$, the resulting matrix $\bm{R}^G$ can represent any matrix within the full orthogonal group.

This theoretical capability demonstrates that BOFT is not just a limited approximation but a building block for universal orthogonal representation. However, the authors note that maximum expressivity is not always the objective in finetuning. Full finetuning often results in unsatisfactory performance due to overfitting or instability. Therefore, the value of BOFT lies in its ability to provide a structured subset of the orthogonal group. This allows for a controlled trade-off between expressivity (the ability to change the model) and regularity (the ability to preserve pretrained knowledge), helping the model generalize better to new tasks.

# Spectral Norm Preservation: Why orthogonal updates improve training stability

One of the most significant advantages of orthogonal finetuning over additive methods like LoRA is the preservation of the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. The spectral norm is essentially the largest singular value of the matrix, and preserving it is known to greatly benefit training stability and the overall generalization of the model.

To understand this intuitively, consider the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$:

$\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top$

In this decomposition, $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal matrices, and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values. When applying BOFT, the finetuned weights are calculated by multiplying the pretrained weights by a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$:

$\text{Finetuned Weights} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0 = \bm{R} \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top$

Because the product of two orthogonal matrices is itself an orthogonal matrix, the term $\bm{R} \bm{U}$ remains orthogonal. Consequently, the singular values—which are stored in $\bm{\Sigma}$—remain completely unchanged. This means that the spectral norm of the weight matrix is perfectly preserved throughout the entire finetuning process. Unlike methods that add low-rank matrices to the weights, which can shift the singular values and potentially lead to instability, BOFT ensures that the "energy" and scale of the pretrained weights are maintained, leading to more stable convergence.

# Inductive Bias: The relationship between butterfly structures and classic linear transforms

The choice of the butterfly structure in BOFT is not arbitrary; it is designed to introduce a beneficial inductive bias. An inductive bias is a set of assumptions a learner uses to predict outputs given inputs it has not encountered. In the case of BOFT, the butterfly structure mirrors the architecture of many classic fast linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier transform, the discrete cosine transform, and the Hadamard transform.

Because the butterfly structure can easily recover these classic transforms, it imposes a mathematical prior on the type of transformations the model can learn. This restricts the hypothesis class to a structured subset of the orthogonal group. This restriction is beneficial because it prevents the model from overfitting to the downstream data, acting as a form of regularization.

Furthermore, the butterfly structure allows BOFT to create a "smoother interpolation" between an identity matrix (which represents maximum regularity and no change to the pretrained model) and a full orthogonal matrix (which represents maximum expressivity). By navigating this structured space, BOFT can find a solution that balances the preservation of pretrained semantic knowledge with the need to adapt to a new task. This is a significant improvement over the block-diagonal structure of original OFT, which cannot recover these classic linear transforms and thus lacks this specific, mathematically grounded inductive bias.

# Empirical Applications and Results

The theoretical framework of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) provides a sophisticated mechanism for adapting large foundation models through the use of sparse matrix factorization. However, the true utility of any finetuning paradigm lies in its empirical performance across diverse tasks. The transition from a pretrained foundation model to a specialized downstream model requires a balance between expressivity—the ability of the model to learn new patterns—and regularity—the ability to preserve the vast amount of knowledge already captured during pretraining. BOFT is designed to navigate this trade-off by utilizing a butterfly structure to construct dense orthogonal matrices with significantly fewer parameters than traditional methods.

# Adapting Large Language Models (LLMs)

Large Language Models, such as GPT-3, possess an enormous number of parameters, often reaching into the hundreds of billions. This scale makes full finetuning computationally prohibitive for most researchers. BOFT addresses this challenge by applying its parameter-efficient orthogonal transformations to the linear layers of these models. In the context of natural language processing, the goal is to adapt the model to specific downstream tasks without erasing the general linguistic capabilities acquired during the pretraining phase.

By employing the butterfly factorization, BOFT allows the model to learn a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ that transforms the neurons of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. Because BOFT can represent a dense matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, it can maintain a high level of expressivity while keeping the number of trainable parameters low. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that BOFT outperforms current state-of-the-art parameter-efficient finetuning methods by a considerable margin in various natural language tasks, validating its effectiveness as a generic tool for LLM adaptation.

# Finetuning Large Vision Transformers (ViTs)

The versatility of BOFT extends beyond the domain of text to the realm of computer vision, specifically when applied to Large Vision Transformers (ViTs). Adaptation in vision tasks often requires the model to refine its understanding of spatial features and object relationships to suit a specific dataset or task. Applying BOFT to these architectures involves the same principle of multiplying the frozen pretrained weights by a learnable orthogonal matrix composed of butterfly components.

The application of BOFT to ViTs showcases its capacity as a general-purpose finetuning method. By preserving the pairwise angles between neurons through orthogonal transformations, BOFT ensures that the semantic knowledge of the pretrained vision model is largely preserved. The results in computer vision applications mirror those found in language processing, where BOFT exhibits superior parameter-efficiency and generalization ability compared to existing methods. This suggests that the inductive bias introduced by the butterfly structure is beneficial across different modalities of data.

# Controllable Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

The roots of orthogonal finetuning were initially established through Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) in the context of controllable text-to-image diffusion models. The primary objective in these models is to adapt the generative process to produce images that adhere to specific constraints or styles. OFT demonstrated that preserving the relational information of weight matrices—specifically the angles between neurons—leads to more stable training and better generalizability than additive methods.

BOFT subsumes OFT as a special case, meaning it encompasses the original orthogonal finetuning framework while providing a more efficient parameterization. In text-to-image diffusion tasks, BOFT replaces the restrictive block-diagonal structure of OFT with a butterfly structure. This allows the model to achieve a dense orthogonal transformation without the parameter explosion associated with full orthogonal matrices. By doing so, BOFT improves the efficiency of adapting these generative models, proving that the butterfly-based approach is not only effective for discriminative tasks in vision and language but also for complex generative tasks.

# Comparative Analysis: BOFT vs. LoRA and Standard OFT

To understand the advantages of BOFT, it is helpful to compare it with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) and the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). LoRA achieves parameter efficiency by adding a product of two low-rank matrices to the pretrained weights. In contrast, BOFT uses a multiplicative orthogonal update. One of the most significant advantages of the orthogonal approach is the preservation of the spectral norm. Because $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix, the product $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ does not change the largest singular value of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. This property is believed to greatly benefit training stability and generalization, a feature that is not inherently guaranteed by the additive nature of LoRA.

When compared to standard OFT, the primary difference lies in the structure of the orthogonal matrix. Standard OFT often relies on a block-diagonal structure to remain parameter-efficient. While this reduces the number of parameters, it limits expressivity because dimensions in different blocks are transformed independently, and it cannot approximate classic linear transforms. A full dense orthogonal matrix in OFT would require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is often too costly. BOFT breaks this dichotomy by using butterfly factorization to produce a dense orthogonal matrix with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. Consequently, BOFT is provably more expressive than block-diagonal OFT with the same block size, allowing it to find a better trade-off between expressivity and regularity.

# Discussion on Inference Latency and Training Overhead

A critical consideration for any finetuning method is the cost associated with its deployment and training. One of the most attractive features of BOFT is that it introduces zero additional inference latency. After the finetuning process is complete, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is simply multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. This results in a new weight matrix that can be used in the standard forward pass of the model, meaning the final architecture is identical to the original pretrained model.

However, there is a trade-off during the training phase. Because the final orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ in BOFT is constructed as the product of $m$ separate orthogonal butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, the training runtime overhead is slightly larger than that of standard OFT. In standard OFT, a single block-diagonal matrix is applied; in BOFT, multiple sparse matrices must be multiplied in each iteration. Despite this slight increase in training time, the significant reduction in the number of trainable parameters and the resulting improvements in generalization and stability make BOFT a compelling choice for adapting large-scale foundation models.
