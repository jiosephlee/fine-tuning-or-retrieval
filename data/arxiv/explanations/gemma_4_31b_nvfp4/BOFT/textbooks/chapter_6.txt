\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 6: # Theoretical Insights: Expressivity and Spectral Properties

The effectiveness of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) as a parameter-efficient finetuning method is not merely empirical; it is grounded in several critical mathematical properties. Understanding these properties—specifically how BOFT approximates orthogonal matrices, how it preserves the spectral integrity of pretrained weights, and the inductive biases it introduces—is essential for understanding why it outperforms traditional block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning (OFT) and other low-rank adaptation methods.

# Expressivity: Comparing BOFT and block-diagonal OFT

In the context of orthogonal finetuning, expressivity refers to the ability of a parameterized orthogonal matrix to approximate any arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix. A dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ provides the maximum possible flexibility for transforming neurons in a layer, but it requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is prohibitively expensive for large models. To combat this, original OFT utilizes a block-diagonal structure, which significantly reduces parameters but limits expressivity because it cannot approximate any matrix that requires connectivity between different blocks.

BOFT addresses this limitation by replacing the block-diagonal structure with a butterfly factorization. Through the use of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices, BOFT can generate a dense orthogonal matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. When comparing the two, BOFT is provably more expressive than OFT for a given block size. For instance, empirical simulations involving the approximation of random dense orthogonal matrices of size $512 \times 512$ reveal that BOFT achieves much lower approximation errors than OFT when the parameter budget is kept constant. A specific example of this efficiency is seen when comparing $\text{BOFT}(9, 2)$ to $\text{BOFT}(1, 16)$; the former exhibits better expressiveness than the latter despite using significantly fewer trainable parameters.

The general rule is that BOFT configurations with smaller block sizes $b$ and a larger number of factors $m$ are generally more parameter-efficient. This indicates that the butterfly structure is a more effective way of navigating the orthogonal group than the block-diagonal structure. While the block-diagonal approach creates a rigid partition of neurons, the butterfly structure allows for a more fluid exchange of information across the entire dimensionality of the neuron, enabling the model to find a better hypothesis class for downstream tasks.

# The Kaleidoscope Hierarchy: Approximating the full orthogonal group

While the standard BOFT configuration provides a highly efficient approximation, it is possible to extend this framework to represent the entire orthogonal group. This is achieved through a generalization known as the kaleidoscope hierarchy. In this setup, the final orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}^G$ is constructed as a sequence of butterfly matrices and their transposes:

$\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)=\bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^T(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^T(m_2,b_2)$

where $\bm{R}_{i,j}^T(m,b)$ represents the orthogonal matrix used in the BOFT parameterization. According to the theory of kaleidoscope hierarchies, if the parameters are set such that $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and $l=d-1$, the resulting matrix $\bm{R}^G$ can represent any matrix within the full orthogonal group.

This theoretical capability demonstrates that BOFT is not just a limited approximation but a building block for universal orthogonal representation. However, the authors note that maximum expressivity is not always the objective in finetuning. Full finetuning often results in unsatisfactory performance due to overfitting or instability. Therefore, the value of BOFT lies in its ability to provide a structured subset of the orthogonal group. This allows for a controlled trade-off between expressivity (the ability to change the model) and regularity (the ability to preserve pretrained knowledge), helping the model generalize better to new tasks.

# Spectral Norm Preservation: Why orthogonal updates improve training stability

One of the most significant advantages of orthogonal finetuning over additive methods like LoRA is the preservation of the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. The spectral norm is essentially the largest singular value of the matrix, and preserving it is known to greatly benefit training stability and the overall generalization of the model.

To understand this intuitively, consider the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$:

$\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top$

In this decomposition, $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal matrices, and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values. When applying BOFT, the finetuned weights are calculated by multiplying the pretrained weights by a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$:

$\text{Finetuned Weights} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0 = \bm{R} \bm{U} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{V}^\top$

Because the product of two orthogonal matrices is itself an orthogonal matrix, the term $\bm{R} \bm{U}$ remains orthogonal. Consequently, the singular values—which are stored in $\bm{\Sigma}$—remain completely unchanged. This means that the spectral norm of the weight matrix is perfectly preserved throughout the entire finetuning process. Unlike methods that add low-rank matrices to the weights, which can shift the singular values and potentially lead to instability, BOFT ensures that the "energy" and scale of the pretrained weights are maintained, leading to more stable convergence.

# Inductive Bias: The relationship between butterfly structures and classic linear transforms

The choice of the butterfly structure in BOFT is not arbitrary; it is designed to introduce a beneficial inductive bias. An inductive bias is a set of assumptions a learner uses to predict outputs given inputs it has not encountered. In the case of BOFT, the butterfly structure mirrors the architecture of many classic fast linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier transform, the discrete cosine transform, and the Hadamard transform.

Because the butterfly structure can easily recover these classic transforms, it imposes a mathematical prior on the type of transformations the model can learn. This restricts the hypothesis class to a structured subset of the orthogonal group. This restriction is beneficial because it prevents the model from overfitting to the downstream data, acting as a form of regularization.

Furthermore, the butterfly structure allows BOFT to create a "smoother interpolation" between an identity matrix (which represents maximum regularity and no change to the pretrained model) and a full orthogonal matrix (which represents maximum expressivity). By navigating this structured space, BOFT can find a solution that balances the preservation of pretrained semantic knowledge with the need to adapt to a new task. This is a significant improvement over the block-diagonal structure of original OFT, which cannot recover these classic linear transforms and thus lacks this specific, mathematically grounded inductive bias.
