Chapter 1: Introduction to Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT)

# The challenge of training foundation models from scratch

The emergence of large foundation models, such as ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion, has demonstrated a remarkable ability to generalize across a wide array of tasks. These models achieve such high levels of performance due to their immense scale, which is characterized by a dramatic increase in the number of parameters. For instance, GPT-3 contains approximately 175 billion parameters. While this scale allows the models to capture complex patterns and extensive knowledge, it also creates a significant barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners.

The primary challenge lies in the fact that training a model of this magnitude from scratch is prohibitively expensive. The computational resources, time, and financial costs required to initialize and optimize hundreds of billions of parameters are beyond the reach of most organizations. Because of this, it is no longer practical to build a new foundation model for every specific downstream application. Instead, the focus of the field has shifted toward the ability to adapt existing, pretrained foundation models to new tasks without the need for full retraining.

# Overview of PEFT categories: Model Finetuning, Adapter Tuning, and Prompt Tuning

To address the challenges of retraining, researchers have developed various parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) strategies. These methods aim to adapt a model to a specific task while only updating a small fraction of the total parameters. These strategies can be broadly categorized into three main approaches: model finetuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning.

Model finetuning is a straightforward approach where the original architecture of the model remains unchanged, but a specific subset of the existing model parameters is updated during the training process. This method is particularly advantageous because it is simple and powerful, and most importantly, it introduces no additional inference latency since the model structure remains identical to the pretrained version.

Adapter tuning takes a different approach by introducing additional trainable parameters into the original model. Rather than modifying the pretrained weights directly, adapters add new layers or modules that are trained to specialize the model for the downstream task. While effective, this differs from model finetuning as it changes the architecture by adding new components.

Prompt tuning is another strategy that focuses on the input side of the model. In this approach, additional trainable prefix tokens are attached to the input. The model learns to optimize these tokens to guide the frozen foundation model toward the desired output for a specific task, avoiding the need to modify the internal weights of the model itself.

# The trade-off between parameter efficiency and model performance

In the context of adaptation, there is a fundamental tension between parameter efficiency and model performance. Parameter efficiency refers to the goal of reducing the number of trainable parameters to lower the computational cost and storage requirements. However, reducing the number of trainable parameters can potentially limit the model's expressiveness, which may impact its ability to adapt to a complex downstream task.

This trade-off is evident when comparing different structured approaches. For example, some methods use low-rank structures or sparse matrices to reduce the parameter budget. While these constraints make the training process more efficient, they may introduce inductive biases. If these biases are too restrictive, the model may fail to capture the nuances of the new task. Conversely, if the parameter space is too large, the model may overfit the downstream data and lose the generalizability it gained during pretraining. The goal of an ideal PEFT method is to find a hypothesis class that is small enough for efficiency and regularity but large enough to provide the necessary expressivity for the task.

# Introduction to the goal of preserving pretraining knowledge during adaptation

A critical objective during the finetuning process is ensuring that the pretrained model and the finetuned model remain similar. This is based on the principle that the foundation model already possesses vast amounts of semantic knowledge from its initial training; if this knowledge is discarded or corrupted during adaptation, the model may lose its generalizability and stability. This process of preserving pretraining knowledge is often viewed as maintaining a balance between the "foundation" and the "adaptation."

One common, although ad hoc, method to achieve this is by employing a very small learning rate. By limiting the magnitude of the weight updates, researchers encourage a small discrepancy between the pretrained and finetuned weights, thereby preventing the model from deviating too far from its original state. 

A more principled approach focuses on preserving the relational information within the weight matrices. Specifically, this involves preserving the pairwise angles between neurons in the same layer. If these angles are maintained, the relative orientation of the neurons—and thus the internal representations learned during pretraining—is preserved throughout the finetuning process. This insight allows for a structured form of adaptation, such as orthogonal transformations, where the weights are modified in a way that provably protects the pretraining knowledge while still allowing the model to specialize for a downstream task.
