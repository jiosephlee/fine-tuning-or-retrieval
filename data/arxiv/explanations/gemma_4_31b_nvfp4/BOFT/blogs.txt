# Beyond LoRA: Introducing a Smarter Way to Fine-Tune Giant AI Models

As foundation models like GPT-3 and Stable Diffusion grow to hundreds of billions of parameters, training them from scratch has become prohibitively expensive. To make these models useful for specific downstream tasks, researchers rely on Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT). The goal of PEFT is to adapt a model to new data without retraining the entire network, which would be computationally impossible for most.

While many of us are familiar with LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), which adds small, trainable low-rank matrices to the model, there is a more principled approach to preserving a model's original knowledge: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). This post explores the evolution of these techniques and introduces Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), a new method that balances extreme parameter efficiency with high expressive power.

## The Problem with Standard Adaptation

Most fine-tuning methods attempt to keep the tuned model similar to the pretrained model to avoid "catastrophic forgetting." LoRA achieves this by adding an update matrix, but this is an additive process. A more structured approach is to use a multiplicative orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to transform the neurons in a layer. 

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) uses this method to preserve the pairwise angles between neurons, ensuring that the semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining remains intact. However, OFT faces a significant scaling problem: a dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. To reduce this, original OFT used a block-diagonal structure, but this limited the model's expressivity—it essentially forced the model to treat groups of neurons independently, which isn't always ideal.

## BOFT: The Butterfly Approach

The researchers behind Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) asked a critical question: *Can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix without losing parameter efficiency?*

To solve this, they framed matrix multiplication as an information transmission problem. Imagine the matrix as a graph where information flows from input nodes to output nodes. For a matrix to be "dense," every input node must have a path to every output node. Instead of one giant, heavy matrix, BOFT uses a sequence of sparse matrices to achieve this connectivity.

Taking inspiration from the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm, BOFT employs a "butterfly structure." By multiplying $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse orthogonal matrices—each with only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries—BOFT can simulate a dense orthogonal matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. 

In practical terms, if you are fine-tuning a linear layer $\bm{W}^0$, BOFT modifies the forward pass to:
$$\bm{z}=(\bm{R}(m,b)\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$$
Where $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is the product of multiple orthogonal butterfly components. This allows BOFT to maintain the benefits of a dense transformation while keeping the parameter count incredibly low.

## Why BOFT is a Game Changer

BOFT offers several theoretical and practical advantages over previous PEFT methods:

*   **Better Expressivity:** Unlike the block-diagonal structure of OFT, the butterfly structure can recover classic linear transforms (like the Fourier or Hadamard transforms). This provides a better trade-off between the regularity needed to prevent overfitting and the expressivity needed to learn new tasks.
*   **Training Stability:** Because BOFT uses orthogonal matrices, it perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$. This prevents the weights from exploding or vanishing, leading to more stable training and better generalization.
*   **Zero Inference Latency:** Because $\bm{R}$ is simply multiplied by the weights, the learned orthogonal matrix can be merged directly into the pretrained model after training. This means there is no additional computational overhead during inference.
*   **Smarter Regularization:** To prevent overfitting, BOFT introduces "Multiplicative Dropout," which randomly replaces selected butterfly components or blocks with identity matrices during training.

## Summary

BOFT represents a shift from simply reducing the rank of updates (like LoRA) to intelligently structuring the transformation of existing knowledge. By leveraging the efficiency of butterfly factorization, BOFT allows us to adapt massive vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion models with a fraction of the parameters, all while preserving the stability and integrity of the original foundation model.



# The Butterfly Effect: How FFT Logic is Revolutionizing AI Training

Training foundation models from scratch is prohibitively expensive, leading the AI community to focus on Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT). While methods like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) are popular, a different approach called Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) has shown great promise by preserving the relational information—specifically the pairwise angles between neurons—of the pretrained model.

However, OFT faces a dilemma: a dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is too many, while the block-diagonal matrices used to reduce parameters limit the model's expressivity. Enter **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**, a method that uses the logic of the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to achieve the best of both worlds: dense expressivity with sparse parameter counts.

## The Information Transmission Perspective

To understand how BOFT works, the researchers propose viewing matrix multiplication as an information transmission problem. Imagine a grid-structured graph where nodes represent dimensions. Multiplying a vector by a matrix is equivalent to moving information from the nodes of one level to the nodes of the next.

For a final weight matrix $\bm{R}$ to be "dense," every node in the first level must have a path to every node in the final level. In a standard dense matrix, this is achieved by having an edge between every single single pair of nodes, which leads to the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameter explosion. The goal of BOFT is to achieve this "dense connectivity" using the minimum number of "free edges" (trainable parameters).

## Borrowing from the Cooley-Tukey FFT

The inspiration for BOFT comes from the Cooley-Tukey algorithm used in Fast Fourier Transforms. The FFT is famous for reducing the complexity of discrete Fourier transforms by using a specific "butterfly" graph topology. This structure allows information from different nodes to be exchanged efficiently.

In BOFT, a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is decomposed into a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal matrices:
$$\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$$

Each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a "butterfly component." By using this recursive butterfly structure, BOFT can generate a dense matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. For example, if $d=512$, a dense matrix would require over 260,000 parameters, whereas a butterfly structure could achieve similar connectivity with a fraction of that cost.

## Why BOFT is a Step Forward

BOFT isn't just about saving space; it introduces several theoretical and practical advantages over previous finetuning methods:

*   **Superior Expressivity:** Unlike block-diagonal OFT, which cannot approximate general linear transforms, BOFT can recover many classic linear transforms (like the Hadamard or Fourier transforms). This provides a better trade-off between the regularity of the pretrained model and the expressivity needed for a new task.
*   **Spectral Stability:** Because BOFT uses orthogonal matrices, it perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. This stability often leads to better generalization and more consistent training compared to additive methods like LoRA.
*   **Zero Inference Latency:** While the product of sparse matrices is used during training, the resulting $\bm{R}$ can be pre-computed and merged into the pretrained weights after finetuning. This means there is no additional computational overhead during model deployment.

## Broad Applicability

The researchers demonstrated that BOFT is a generic and powerful framework. It was applied to a diverse range of foundation models, including:
*   **Large Language Models (LLMs)** for natural language processing.
*   **Large Vision Transformers** for computer vision tasks.
*   **Text-to-Image Diffusion Models** for controllable image generation.

In these applications, BOFT outperformed state-of-the-art PEFT methods, proving that the inductive bias provided by the butterfly structure helps prevent overfitting and enhances the model's ability to adapt to downstream tasks.



# One Framework, Many Worlds: BOFT's Versatility Across LLMs, Vision, and Diffusion

Adapting foundation models to specific downstream tasks is a central challenge in modern AI. Whether it is a Large Language Model (LLM) like GPT-3 or a text-to-image model like Stable Diffusion, the sheer size of these models makes training from scratch prohibitively expensive. While Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA have become the industry standard, there is always room for more principled approaches that preserve the rich knowledge embedded in pretrained weights.

Enter **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**, a novel finetuning framework that combines the stability of orthogonal transformations with the extreme parameter efficiency of butterfly structures.

## From Orthogonal Finetuning to the Butterfly Structure

To understand BOFT, we first have to look at Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). The core idea of OFT is to update a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by multiplying it by a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. Because orthogonal matrices preserve the pairwise angles between neurons, OFT is designed to keep the semantic knowledge of the pretrained model intact. However, a dense orthogonal matrix for a layer of size $d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is often too many. To solve this, OFT previously used block-diagonal structures, but this limited the model's expressiveness and introduced rigid inductive biases.

BOFT solves this trade-off by treating the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem. Drawing inspiration from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm, BOFT uses a **butterfly factorization**. Instead of one massive dense matrix, BOFT represents the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal matrices:

$$\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$$

By using this structure, BOFT can generate a dense orthogonal matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. This allows the model to maintain the expressiveness of a dense transformation while remaining computationally lean.

## Versatility Across Modalities

The true strength of BOFT lies in its versatility. While previous orthogonal methods were primarily tested on specific tasks like controllable text-to-image generation, the authors of the BOFT paper demonstrate that this framework is a generic tool for adaptation across entirely different domains:

*   **Large Language Models (LLMs):** BOFT can efficiently adapt the linguistic capabilities of LLMs to specific downstream natural language processing tasks.
*   **Vision Transformers (ViTs):** The framework proves effective in adapting large-scale vision models to various computer vision tasks.
*   **Diffusion Models:** In text-to-image models like Stable Diffusion, BOFT enables precise control and adaptation without the instability often seen in additive methods like LoRA.

Across these diverse "worlds," BOFT consistently outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods, validating its superior generalization ability and parameter efficiency.

## Why BOFT Works: Stability and Bias

Beyond the numbers, BOFT provides several theoretical advantages that explain its success:

**Spectral Stability.** Unlike additive updates, BOFT preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. Because the product of an orthogonal matrix and the original weights does not change the largest singular value, the training process remains significantly more stable, which is critical when finetuning massive models.

**Beneficial Inductive Bias.** The butterfly structure is not arbitrary; it is the foundation of many classic linear transforms, such as the Discrete Fourier Transform and the Hadamard Transform. By constraining the finetuning process to this structured subset of the orthogonal group, BOFT introduces an implicit inductive bias that helps prevent overfitting and improves generalizability.

**Zero Inference Latency.** One of the biggest practical advantages of BOFT is that it introduces no overhead during inference. Once the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is learned, it can be multiplied directly into the pretrained weights. The resulting model has the exact same architecture as the original, meaning there is no need for additional "adapter" layers during deployment.

## Conclusion

BOFT demonstrates that we don't have to choose between the expressiveness of dense matrices and the efficiency of sparse ones. By leveraging butterfly factorization, BOFT provides a unified framework capable of adapting LLMs, vision models, and diffusion models with minimal parameters and maximum stability. As foundation models continue to grow, structured approaches like BOFT will be essential for making these giants flexible and accessible.



# The Geometry of Knowledge: Why Orthogonality Matters in AI

When adapting massive foundation models to downstream tasks, the central challenge is balancing new learning with the preservation of pre-existing knowledge. Most parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods, such as LoRA, focus on adding low-rank updates to weights. However, a different, more geometric approach exists: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). By treating the model's weights as a geometric structure, we can adapt models while keeping their internal "knowledge geometry" intact. 

The latest evolution of this idea is Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which combines the stability of orthogonal transformations with the efficiency of sparse matrix factorization.

## Preserving the Angles Between Neurons

At its core, OFT is based on the insight that the semantic knowledge of a pretrained model is encoded in the relational information between its neurons—specifically, the pairwise angles between them. If we represent a pretrained linear layer as $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, traditional finetuning often shifts these weights in ways that distort these angles, potentially erasing the "wisdom" gained during pretraining.

OFT addresses this by reparameterizing the weights as the product of a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and the frozen pretrained weights: $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. Because $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix, it performs a rotation in the feature space. This rotation preserves the inner products and angles between neurons, ensuring that the fundamental relational structure of the pretrained model remains unchanged even as the model adapts to a new task.

## The Efficiency Dilemma: Dense vs. Sparse

While orthogonal transformations are powerful, they are computationally expensive to parameterize. A dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ of dimension $d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is prohibitively large for modern LLMs or Vision Transformers. To solve this, early versions of OFT used block-diagonal structures to reduce parameters. However, this creates a trade-off: the smaller the blocks, the more parameter-efficient the model becomes, but the less expressive it is, as it can only transform neurons within fixed, isolated groups.

BOFT breaks this dilemma by using a "butterfly factorization." Drawing inspiration from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm, BOFT represents a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices. 

From an information transmission perspective, this can be viewed as a grid-structured graph where information flows from source nodes to receiver nodes. By utilizing the butterfly structure, BOFT can generate a dense orthogonal matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. This allows the model to maintain the expressivity of a dense matrix—meaning every neuron can potentially interact with every other neuron—while remaining as lean as sparse methods.

## Stability through Spectral Norms

Beyond parameter efficiency, the geometry of orthogonality provides a significant advantage in training stability. In deep learning, the spectral norm (the largest singular value) of a weight matrix often dictates the stability of the gradient flow.

We can see this through Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). If we decompose the pretrained weights as $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, where $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix of singular values, the BOFT update results in weights $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Because the product of two orthogonal matrices ($\bm{R}\bm{U}$) is itself orthogonal, the singular values $\bm{\Sigma}$ remain completely unchanged. 

By perfectly preserving the spectral norm of the pretrained weights, BOFT avoids the instabilities that often plague additive updates. This geometric constraint acts as a powerful regularizer, preventing the model from overfitting and allowing for more stable convergence during finetuning.

## Inductive Bias and Generalization

The use of butterfly structures does more than just save memory; it introduces a beneficial inductive bias. Many classic linear transforms, such as the Discrete Fourier Transform and the Hadamard Transform, share this butterfly pattern. By constraining the finetuning process to this structured subset of the orthogonal group, BOFT finds a smoother interpolation between the identity matrix (preserving all pretraining) and a full orthogonal transformation (maximum expressivity).

Ultimately, BOFT demonstrates that by respecting the internal geometry of a model—preserving angles and spectral norms—we can achieve adaptation that is not only more efficient but also more robust and generalizable across vision, language, and generative tasks.
