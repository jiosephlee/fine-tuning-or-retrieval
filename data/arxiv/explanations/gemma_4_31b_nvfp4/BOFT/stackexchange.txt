\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the Cayley transform ensure the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$?
Question:
In the section on Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the authors mention that they employ Cayley parameterization, defined as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix ($\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$). I understand that this is used to ensure $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout the training process, but I am struggling with the mathematical intuition. How does the skew-symmetry of $\bm{Q}$ specifically guarantee that $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$?
Answer:
The Cayley transform provides a way to map any skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ to an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. To understand why this guarantees that $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, we can examine the properties of the transpose of $\bm{R}$ and how the skew-symmetry of $\bm{Q}$ interacts with the identity matrix.

Given the parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, let us first find the transpose $\bm{R}^\top$. Recall that for any matrices $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$, $(\bm{AB})^\top = \bm{B}^\top \bm{A}^\top$ and $(\bm{A}^{-1})^\top = (\bm{A}^\top)^{-1}$. Applying this to $\bm{R}$, we get $\bm{R}^\top = ((\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1})^\top (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^\top$. Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$) and the identity matrix $\bm{I}$ is symmetric, this simplifies to $\bm{R}^\top = (\bm{I} - (-\bm{Q}))^{-1} (\bm{I} - \bm{Q}) = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$.

Now, we compute the product $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}$:
$$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = [(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})] \cdot [(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}]$$
The key to simplifying this expression is noticing that the terms $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ and $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})$ commute. We can verify this by expanding their product: $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})(\bm{I}+\bm{Q}) = \bm{I} + \bm{Q} - \bm{Q} - \bm{Q}^2 = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2$, and similarly $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}) = \bm{I} - \bm{Q} + \bm{Q} - \bm{Q}^2 = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2$. Since they commute, we can rearrange the middle terms of the product $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}$ as follows:
$$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1} [(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})] (\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1} [(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})] (\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$$
The terms $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})$ and $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ both reduce to the identity matrix $\bm{I}$, leaving us with $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$.

Intuitively, the Cayley transform acts as a bridge between the Lie algebra of skew-symmetric matrices and the Lie group of orthogonal matrices. In the context of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), maintaining orthogonality is difficult because the constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$ is non-linear; updating $\bm{R}$ using standard gradient descent would likely break this property. By optimizing $\bm{Q}$ instead, the model only needs to maintain the linear constraint $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$ (which is trivial to implement by setting $\bm{Q}_{ij} = -\bm{Q}_{ji}$), and the Cayley transform automatically "projects" this into a valid rotation matrix $\bm{R}$ at every step of the training process.

### Why is the butterfly structure considered 'dense' while the block-diagonal structure is not?
Question:
I am confused about the terminology used in the paper. Both the block-diagonal structure in original OFT and the butterfly structure in BOFT use sparse matrices to reduce the number of parameters. However, the paper states that the block-diagonal structure cannot construct a dense matrix $\bm{R}$, whereas BOFT is designed to generate a 'dense orthogonal matrix' via factorization. If the individual factors $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}$ are sparse, in what sense is the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ actually dense?
Answer:
The confusion stems from a distinction between the **individual factors** used to build the matrix and the **resulting product matrix** $\bm{R}$. In both block-diagonal OFT and BOFT, the trainable components are indeed sparse to ensure parameter efficiency. However, the "density" the paper refers to describes the connectivity of the final matrix $\bm{R}$ that is actually applied to the weights.

In the block-diagonal structure of original OFT, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is defined as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$. Because this is a single-step structure (or a product of matrices that all share the same block-diagonal sparsity pattern), it can never become a dense matrix. If two indices $i$ and $j$ belong to different blocks, the element $R_{ij}$ will always be zero, regardless of the values learned within those blocks. From the paper's "information transmission" perspective, this means the neurons are divided into isolated groups; information can flow within a block, but it can never jump from one block to another.

BOFT takes a fundamentally different approach by representing $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ sparse matrices: $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. While each individual factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}$ is sparse, the product of sparse matrices is not necessarily sparse. The butterfly structure is specifically designed so that each successive multiplication spreads the "information flow" further across the dimensions. 

Intuitively, you can think of this as a communication network. A block-diagonal matrix is like a set of disconnected local area networks (LANs); no matter how much data you move within a LAN, you cannot reach a computer in a different LAN. The butterfly structure, however, acts like a series of switching stages. Even though each switch (each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}$) only connects a few nodes, the combined effect of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ stages allows every single node in the input to eventually reach every single node in the output. 

Consequently, while the number of trainable parameters remains low—$\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ for BOFT versus $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a full dense matrix—the resulting product $\bm{R}$ is mathematically "dense" because it can potentially have non-zero entries at every position. This allows BOFT to approximate a much wider range of orthogonal transformations and recover classic linear transforms (like the Fourier transform), which is impossible for a block-diagonal matrix.

### How does the 'information transmission' view map to matrix multiplication?
Question:
The authors introduce a grid-structured graph where $\bm{B}_i$ is viewed as the connectivity matrix from level $i$ to $i+1$. They state that for $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \dots \bm{B}_1$ to be dense, every node in the first level must have a path to every node in the $(m+1)$-th level. I'm trying to reconcile this with linear algebra: does this imply that the product of these sparse matrices results in a matrix where almost all entries $R_{ij}$ are non-zero, and how does the butterfly topology specifically satisfy this 'dense connectivity' requirement more efficiently than other sparse patterns?
Answer:
To understand how the "information transmission" view maps to matrix multiplication, it is helpful to think of matrix multiplication not as a series of dot products, but as a process of summing paths across a graph.

In the paper, the authors represent the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ sparse matrices: $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \dots \bm{B}_1$. If we view each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ as an adjacency matrix for a bipartite graph connecting $d$ nodes at level $i$ to $d$ nodes at level $i+1$, then an entry $(\bm{B}_i)_{jk} \neq 0$ represents a directed edge from node $k$ in level $i$ to node $j$ in level $i+1$. When we multiply these matrices, the entry $R_{ij}$ of the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ is the sum over all possible paths starting at node $j$ in the first level and ending at node $i$ in the $(m+1)$-th level. Specifically, $R_{ij} = \sum_{k_1, \dots, k_{m-1}} (\bm{B}_m)_{i, k_{m-1}} (\bm{B}_{m-1})_{k_{m-1}, k_{m-2}} \dots (\bm{B}_1)_{k_1, j}$. If there is no path between node $j$ and node $i$ through the sequence of sparse matrices, the sum is zero, and $R_{ij}$ will be $0$. Therefore, for $\bm{R}$ to be a "dense" matrix (meaning $R_{ij} \neq 0$ for all $i, j$), every node in the first level must have at least one path to every node in the final level.

The butterfly topology satisfies this requirement efficiently by utilizing a recursive structure similar to the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). In a standard block-diagonal structure, information is trapped within independent blocks; if a node starts in block $A$, it can never reach a node in block $B$, resulting in a sparse product $\bm{R}$. In contrast, the butterfly structure uses a series of "butterfly factors" $\bm{B}^F(k)$ that mix information across different groups of nodes. By layering $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ such sparse matrices, the butterfly structure ensures that information from any single input node is distributed across the entire width of the graph by the final level.

The efficiency gain is significant: a fully dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. However, the butterfly factorization achieves the same "dense connectivity" (global information flow) using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. This allows the model to maintain the expressivity of a dense transformation—enabling any neuron to potentially influence any other—while drastically reducing the number of trainable parameters required during finetuning.

### Why does preserving the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ benefit training stability?
Question:
The paper mentions that because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, the finetuned weights $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ preserve the largest singular value (spectral norm) of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. The authors claim this 'greatly benefit[s] training stability and generalization.' I don't quite understand the mechanism here. Why does keeping the spectral norm constant prevent the training from becoming unstable compared to a method like LoRA, which uses additive updates?
Answer:
The benefit to training stability stems from the fact that the spectral norm of a weight matrix—which is its largest singular value $\sigma_{max}$—effectively controls the maximum "gain" or amplification a layer can apply to its input. In deep networks, if the spectral norm of the weights grows too large, the network becomes susceptible to exploding gradients and extreme sensitivity to small perturbations in the input, which manifests as training instability.

To understand the mechanism in the context of the paper, consider the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of the pretrained weights: $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Here, $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values. The spectral norm is simply the largest element in $\bm{\Sigma}$. When using Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) or its butterfly variant (BOFT), the updated weight matrix is $\bm{W}_{ft} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Because $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix, it represents a rotation or reflection in the feature space; it does not change the lengths of vectors or the singular values of the matrix it multiplies. Since the product of two orthogonal matrices $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is itself orthogonal, the singular values of $\bm{W}_{ft}$ remain identical to those of $\bm{W}^0$. Consequently, the spectral norm is perfectly preserved throughout the entire finetuning process.

This is fundamentally different from additive update methods like LoRA, where the weights are modified as $\bm{W}_{ft} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$. Even if $\bm{A}\bm{B}$ is low-rank, the addition can shift the singular values of the original matrix $\bm{W}^0$ in unpredictable ways. If the additive update increases the spectral norm, it can amplify noise and lead to divergent training. By contrast, the multiplicative orthogonal update used in BOFT ensures that the "energy" or scale of the signal passing through the layer remains consistent with the stable state achieved during the massive-scale pretraining of the foundation model. This constraint acts as a powerful regularizer, preventing the weights from drifting into unstable regimes while still allowing the model to rotate its feature representations to adapt to a new downstream task.

### What is the intuition behind the 'multiplicative Dropout' in BOFT?
Question:
The paper proposes a multiplicative Dropout that replaces randomly selected butterfly components or diagonal blocks with identity matrices, rather than using standard dropout which sets elements to zero. Is this necessary because setting elements of an orthogonal matrix to zero would destroy the orthogonality property? If we used standard dropout on the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ instead, would that achieve a similar effect without breaking the orthogonal constraint?
Answer:
The intuition behind multiplicative Dropout in BOFT stems from the fact that BOFT uses a multiplicative weight update, whereas standard Dropout is designed for additive updates. In a standard additive update, such as in LoRA, the model learns a weight $\Delta \bm{W}$ and adds it to the original weights. Setting an element of $\Delta \bm{W}$ to zero via standard Dropout effectively "turns off" that specific parameter's contribution to the sum, as $0$ is the identity element for addition.

In BOFT, however, the pretrained weights are transformed by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ that is composed of a product of sparse orthogonal matrices: $\bm{R} = \prod \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. To "turn off" a component in this multiplicative chain, one must replace it with the identity matrix $\bm{I}$, because $\bm{I}$ is the identity element for matrix multiplication. Replacing a butterfly component or a diagonal block with $\bm{I}$ ensures that the information simply passes through that stage of the transformation unchanged, which is the multiplicative equivalent of setting an additive weight to zero.

You are correct that applying standard Dropout by setting elements of an orthogonal matrix to zero would destroy the orthogonality property. If $\bm{R}$ is no longer orthogonal, the fundamental goal of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)—preserving the pairwise angles between neurons and the spectral norm of the pretrained weights—would be lost. This would undermine the training stability and generalization benefits that BOFT is designed to provide.

Regarding your question about the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, while applying dropout to $\bm{Q}$ (provided that skew-symmetry $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$ is maintained) would technically yield an orthogonal matrix via the Cayley transform, it would not achieve the same regularization effect as the proposed multiplicative Dropout. Setting elements of $\bm{Q}$ to zero modifies the values within a rotation block, but it does not "bypass" the block. Replacing a block in the butterfly structure with $\bm{I}$ (which corresponds to setting the corresponding block in $\bm{Q}$ to $\bm{0}$) effectively removes an entire structured component of the information transmission path. This allows the model to regularize by randomly omitting specific stages of the butterfly factorization, rather than just perturbing the values of the rotations.

### Regarding the trade-off between expressivity and regularity in BOFT
Question:
Theorem 1 and the associated simulations suggest that BOFT is significantly more expressive than block-diagonal OFT, meaning it can better approximate a general orthogonal matrix. However, the authors later state that 'better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance' and that the key is the trade-off between expressivity and regularity. If BOFT allows us to access a larger subset of the orthogonal group, why would that not always be an advantage? In what way could too much expressivity lead to worse generalization in the context of finetuning?
Answer:
In the context of BOFT, **expressivity** refers to the size and flexibility of the hypothesis class—specifically, how closely the parameterization can approximate any arbitrary orthogonal matrix in the orthogonal group $\mathcal{O}(d)$. **Regularity**, on the other hand, refers to the constraints placed on that hypothesis class to prevent the model from drifting too far from the pretrained knowledge.

The reason why maximum expressivity is not always an advantage in finetuning is fundamentally tied to the risk of **overfitting** and the loss of **pretraining knowledge**. In full finetuning, where the model has maximum expressivity, every parameter can be changed arbitrarily. Despite this universal capability, full finetuning often performs poorly on downstream tasks with limited data because the model can easily overfit the training set or "forget" the general features learned during pretraining.

BOFT addresses this by creating a "smoother interpolation" between the identity matrix $\bm{I}$ (maximum regularity, where the pretrained model remains unchanged) and a general dense orthogonal matrix (maximum expressivity). If a model is *too* expressive, the optimization process can find a solution that minimizes the training loss by drastically altering the weight relationships, potentially destroying the semantic structure (the pairwise angles between neurons) that the original foundation model relied upon. By constraining the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to a butterfly structure, BOFT limits the search space to a structured subset of the orthogonal group.

This constraint acts as a beneficial **inductive bias**. The paper notes that butterfly structures are shared by classic linear transforms like the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) and Hadamard transform. By restricting the model to this specific mathematical structure, BOFT ensures that the transformation applied to the weights is "regular" enough to preserve generalizability but "expressive" enough to adapt to the specific needs of the downstream task. 

In short, while Theorem 1 proves BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT, the "trade-off" means that the goal is not to reach *infinite* expressivity (like full orthogonal matrices or full finetuning), but to find the optimal level of constraint that prevents overfitting while still allowing for sufficient adaptation.
