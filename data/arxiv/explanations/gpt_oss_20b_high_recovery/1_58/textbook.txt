\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

**1. Introduction and Motivation**

Large language models (LLMs) have surged in size and power over the past decade, enabling remarkable performance on a wide spectrum of natural‑language tasks. This rapid growth, however, brings mounting challenges for deployment: the sheer volume of floating‑point parameters, the cost of transferring those parameters over memory interfaces, and the significant energy required for floating‑point arithmetic. In response, researchers are exploring ultra‑low‑precision alternatives that can retain the expressive power of full‑precision models while drastically cutting latency, memory, and energy footprints. The paper *The Era of 1‑bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits* presents a concrete instantiation of this vision with BitNet b1.58, a transformer whose every weight assumes only the values \(\{-1,0,1\}\). Below, we motivate the work by laying out the scale of modern LLMs, their computational costs, the shortcomings of conventional quantization, the emerging low‑bit research landscape, and the specific contributions of BitNet b1.58.

# Exponential scale of modern LLMs

LLMs have grown from the order of millions of parameters in early architectures to tens of billions in state‑of‑the‑art systems. This exponential increase is driven by the observation that more parameters tend to translate into more nuanced language understanding when trained on ever larger corpora. The paper highlights this trend as a core motivation: while larger models unlock new capabilities, they also demand proportionally more compute and memory, which in turn translates into higher latency for inference and greater carbon footprint during training and serving. The exponential scale thus creates a pressing need for inference–friendly architectures that can deliver comparable performance with a fraction of the resource consumption.

# Latency, memory, and energy costs of FP16/BF16 models

Full‑precision LLMs in practice operate on 16‑bit floating‑point values, either FP16 or BF16. The majority of a transformer’s runtime is occupied by matrix–matrix multiplications, each of which requires millions of floating‑point additions and multiplications. Because floating‑point units consume significant power—especially for multiplications—the energy cost scales with the number of such operations. Moreover, the latency of each operation is limited by the memory bandwidth needed to shuttle operands from DRAM to on‑chip SRAM or registers. Large models exacerbate both energy and bandwidth bottlenecks: more parameters mean larger activations, larger weight buffers, and more transfers. Consequently, state‑of‑the‑art systems report that a single inference can consume dozens of megatransfers and multiple megajoules of energy, limiting their viability in cost‑constrained or energy‑limited environments.

# Limitations of post‑training quantization

A popular way to lower precision is to perform *post‑training quantization* (PTQ). PTQ typically quantizes weights and activations from FP16 to a lower bit‑width (for example, 4‑bit) after the model has been trained. While PTQ is simple and widely deployed in industry, the paper stresses its sub‑optimality: because quantization boundaries are chosen without taking the training dynamics into account, PTQ can incur a noticeable loss in performance. Furthermore, PTQ requires a large number of calibration samples and careful handling of activation ranges, making it more of a workaround than a principled design choice. The authors point out that, despite the widespread use of techniques such as *smoothquant*, *GPTQ*, *quip*, and *quip‑sharp*, PTQ still falls short of fully matching full‑precision performance, especially for the most recent, largest models.

# Overview of low‑bit model research

Recent research has explored the feasibility of training models from scratch with very low precision. BitNet, the precursor to BitNet b1.58, demonstrated that a transformer with ternary weights \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) could be trained end‑to‑end and still perform competitively. Its key insight is that matrix multiplication can be reduced to integer addition when weights are ternary, eliminating the need for multiplication entirely and dramatically lowering arithmetic cost. The paper explains that this cheap integer operation enables new computational paradigms and specialized hardware that exploit bit‑parallelism or branchless addition. Other works in the low‑bit space—such as SmoothQuant, GPTQ, and QuIP—often rely on PTQ, whereas BitNet trains directly with a custom quantization function, thereby preserving the network’s expressive power. In addition, the authors extend the BitNet framework to a *1.58‑bit* variant, introducing an additional zero state to provide explicit feature filtering. This modification yields a quantization function based on the \emph{absmean} technique:

\[
\widetilde{W}
    = \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1 \right),
\quad
\operatorname{RoundClip}(x,a,b)
    = \max(a, \min(b, \operatorname{round}(x))),
\quad
\gamma
    = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}\!\bigl|W_{ij}\bigr|.
\]

By scaling each weight matrix by its mean absolute value before clipping, the training procedure natively steers parameters toward the ternary set, and the inclusion of zero allows the network to prune unnecessary features during forward computation.

# Contributions and objectives of BitNet b1.58

BitNet b1.58 builds upon BitNet’s low‑bit philosophy and targets full architectural parity with a standard FP16 LLM of the same size. Its primary contributions are:

1. **Ternary Weights with Performance Parity.** Every weight in BitNet b1.58 takes one of the values \(\{-1,0,+1\}\), a *1.58‑bit* representation in the binary system. Training from scratch, the authors show that, starting at a 3‑billion‑parameter scale, the 1.58‑bit model achieves perplexity and zero‑shot task performance indistinguishable from a full‑precision baseline.

2. **Explicit Feature Filtering.** The optional zero weight lets the transformer selectively zero out irrelevant projections during matrix multiplication. This filtering effect improves modeling capacity beyond the benefit of mere parameter compression.

3. **Computational Efficiency.** Because the matrix multiplication reduces to integer additions, the arithmetic operation count drops dramatically; the model requires no floating‑point multipliers. The paper reports that BitNet b1.58 is \(\gtrsim 2.7\times\) faster and uses \(\sim 3.5\times\) less GPU memory than an equivalent FP16 LLaMA transformer.

4. **Reduced Energy Footprint.** Energy estimates (based on 7 nm arithmetic energy models) suggest a 71.4‑fold reduction in arithmetic‑only energy for 7‑billion‑parameter models, a consequence of replacing FP16 addition/multiplication with INT8 addition.

5. **Hardware‑friendly Design.** By adopting LLaMA‑compatible components—RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding—and removing biases, BitNet b1.58 can be integrated into popular frameworks (Hugging Face, vLLM, llama.cpp) with minor modifications. The new integer‑centric computation invites the design of specialized 1‑bit accelerators that can further accelerate inference.

Collectively, these contributions establish BitNet b1.58 as a *Pareto improvement* over current full‑precision LLMs: equal or better accuracy, vastly lower latency, memory, and energy consumption. The work also introduces a new scaling law: 13 billion‑parameter 1.58‑bit models outperform 3 billion‑parameter FP16 models in latency, memory, and energy, while 70 billion‑parameter 1.58‑bit models surpass 13 billion‑parameter FP16 baselines. These observations highlight the practical feasibility of deploying massive LLMs in resource‑constrained settings, and point toward a vibrant research direction in 1‑bit and low‑bit deep learning.

2. Foundations of Low‑Bit Quantization and the BitNet Era  

---

# Basic principles of weight and activation quantization  

Weight and activation quantization is the process of replacing high‑precision floating‑point parameters or intermediate activations with a smaller set of discrete values. The goal is to reduce memory bandwidth and storage, while keeping the model’s predictive power. In the context of Large Language Models (LLMs), weights are typically stored in 16‑bit floating‑point types (either FP16 or BF16). When we move to a 4‑bit or 1‑bit representation, the memory footprint shrinks dramatically—potentially by a factor of four or more.  

The core idea is to project each real‑valued weight \(W_{ij}\) onto a discrete set \(S\) with cardinality \(2^{b}\), where \(b\) is the target bit‑width. For binary quantization (\(b=1\)), \(S=\{-1,+1\}\); for ternary quantization (\(b\approx 1.58\)), \(S=\{-1,0,+1\}\). Quantization typically involves a scaling factor that aligns the dynamic range of the tensor with the discrete set. In BitNet b1.58, the authors use an **absmean** scaling that normalises the weight matrix by its average absolute value:
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
\]
where \(n\times m\) denotes the matrix dimensions. Each weight is then divided by \(\gamma+\epsilon\) (with a small \(\epsilon\) to avoid overflow or division by zero), and rounded to the nearest integer in the allowed set:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right).
\]
Here,
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))) 
\]
ensures that any value outside the interval \([-1,1]\) is clipped to the nearest allowed integer.  

Activation quantization follows a similar scheme, except that the activations are first scaled to a symmetric interval \([-\!Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]\), where \(Q_{b}\) is the maximum integer representable with \(b\) bits. Notably, BitNet b1.58 drops the zero‑point offset used in many other quantizers; this simplification eliminates a small extra operation while having a negligible impact on accuracy.  

These simple formulas capture the essence of low‑bit quantization: two steps—scale by a simple statistic, then round and clip. Because the scaling factors are computed once per weight or activation tensor, the runtime at inference is unchanged, allowing the quantised calculations to be executed faster and more cheaply.  

---

# Post‑training versus training‑time quantization  

A conventional approach to low‑bit models in industry is **post‑training quantization (PTQ)**: a model is trained in full precision, then a separate quantization step is applied to compress the weights and activations. PTQ schemes such as SmoothQuant, GPTQ, QUIP, and QUIP‑sharp are popular because they require no changes to the training pipeline and can be carried out on a frozen checkpoint. However, the paper notes that PTQ is inherently sub‑optimal: the quantisation process is oblivious to the training dynamics, so the model cannot adapt its parameters to the coarser representation. The result is a drop in accuracy, especially when pushing the bit‑width down to 4 bits and beyond.  

In contrast, **training‑time quantization** integrates the quantisation step into the learning process. Models like BitNet are trained from scratch with low‑bit weights and activations. During back‑propagation, the gradients flow through the quantisation function (often through a straight‑through estimator) so that the underlying full‑precision weights \(W\) are nudged to values that will quantise well. This tight coupling allows the network to learn to compensate for the quantisation error, preserving accuracy. The paper demonstrates that 1‑bit BitNet—using only \{-1,+1\} weights—achieves perplexity close to full‑precision baselines, and that adding a zero weight (1.58‑bit) further closes the performance gap.  

Thus, while PTQ offers convenience, training‑time quantisation yields models that are inherently more robust to the reduced precision, enabling the exploration of even lower bit‑widths.  

---

# The 1‑bit BitNet: integer matmul and power savings  

BitNet is a Transformer‑based LLM that replaces every standard linear transformation (nn.Linear) with a **BitLinear** layer. In a conventional linear layer, the forward pass performs a matrix multiplication between a weight matrix \(W\in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) and an input vector \(x\in \mathbb{R}^{n}\), followed by an addition and a bias term:
\[
y = W x + b.
\]
When the weights and inputs are quantised to 1‑bit, i.e. \(\widetilde{W}\in\{-1,+1\}^{m\times n}\) and \(\widetilde{x}\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}\), the multiplication reduces to a sign comparison. Multiplying two signed 1‑bit values can be replaced by a conditional addition or subtraction, but more efficiently, the product can be represented as a Boolean **XOR** operation: \((-1)\times (-1)=+1,\,(-1)\times (+1)=-1\), etc. The key point is that the entire matrix multiplication can be carried out by integer addition operations only; no floating‑point multiplications are needed.  

This integer‑only computation brings two major advantages:

1. **Energy savings.** Floating‑point multiplications consume substantially more transistor energy than integer additions. By eliminating multiplications from the critical inner products that dominate LLM inference, BitNet saves energy by an order of magnitude. The paper cites an energy reduction of more than 70× for the matrix‑multiplication stage on 7 nm processes compared to FP16 LLaMA.  

2. **Execution speed.** Integer addition is faster and more power‑efficient on modern accelerators. BitNet’s architecture therefore achieves higher throughput and lower latency per token. In experiments, a BitNet b1.58 model was 2.7× faster than an FP16 LLaMA of equivalent size, and up to 11× higher throughput when scaled to a 70‑B parameter model on two A100 GPUs with pipeline parallelism.  

Moreover, because the weight representation uses only three possible values, the model’s memory footprint shrinks to roughly one‑sixth of that of a full‑precision version. The reduced RAID of weights translates into less DRAM traffic, leading to faster weight loading and further throughput gains.  

---

# Motivation for adding a zero weight (ternary representation)  

Binary quantisation restricts the model to alternating signs (+1 or –1). While this is sufficient to preserve sign information, it lacks an explicit mechanism to **disable** a particular weight. Introducing a third value—zero—creates a **ternary** representation. The scaling of the weights is unchanged, but each element now has three possible outcomes:
\[
\widetilde{W} \in \{-1, 0 , +1\}.
\]
Zero acts as a hard gating signal: if a weight is quantised to 0, it effectively eliminates that feature from contributing to the output. This has two conceptual benefits:

1. **Feature filtering.** The network can learn to suppress unnecessary connections by mapping specific weights to zero. In a 1‑bit setting, there is no analogous interpretability: all weights are either +1 or –1. With zeros, the model can perform a sparse filtering akin to neural network pruning, potentially reducing the effective number of active parameters and contributing to a better generalisation.  

2. **Improved approximation.** The ternary set is a finer discretisation of the real‑valued space than pure binary. When the weights are optimised during training, the presence of zero loosens the constraint on the total sum of contributions, so the network can approximate certain linear combinations more accurately. Empirically, BitNet b1.58 matches or surpasses the FP16 baseline for models of size 3 B and above; the added zero weight is a critical factor in this performance increase.  

Because the zero value is zero‑cost and does not skew the energy profile any more than the binary case, the model keeps the same efficient integer‑only matmul. The overall computation cost remains dominated by addition; the occasional zero simply results in a no‑op addition.  

---

# Conceptual benefits of 1.58‑bit weighting  

The **1.58‑bit** designation comes from the logarithm base‑2 of the three‑level representation: \(\log_{2} 3 \approx 1.58\). In practice, this means each weight requires **less than two bits**, yet offers three distinct states. The conceptual benefits—derived from the paper’s experiments and analysis—are multifold:

1. **Scalable performance.** BitNet b1.58 achieves perplexity parity with FP16 LLaMA starting from a 3‑B parameter model. Beyond 3 B, BitNet’s performance even surpasses the FP16 baseline (e.g., 3.9 B BitNet b1.58 outperforms 3 B LLaMA). Consequently, the model follows a new scaling law: a 13 B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient (in terms of latency, memory, and energy) than a 3 B FP16 LLaMA, while a 70 B BitNet b1.58 outperforms a 13 B FP16. This enables the training of larger, efficient models without the cost prohibitions associated with full‑precision training.  

2. **Deployment flexibility.** The drastically smaller memory footprint (up to 3.5× reduction) and lower energy consumption (over 70× fewer arithmetic operations to reduce energy) make BitNet b1.58 viable for hardware‑constrained environments, such as edge devices or mobile CPUs. The paper’s discussion on “LLMs on Edge and Mobile” underscores that 1.58‑bit models are more amenable to devices that favour integer arithmetic and limited power budgets.  

3. **Hardware optimisation roadmap.** The simplification of the computational kernel to integer addition unlocks specialized accelerator designs (e.g., the 2‑bit kernels from Ladder, the “GraLPUs” discussed in other works). The paper calls for new hardware that leverages the inherent 1‑bit or 1.58‑bit computation paradigm, anticipating further gains in speed, energy efficiency, and integration density.  

4. **Extensibility to other LLM paradigms.** The same principles can be adapted to Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models and long‑sequence handling. By reducing the per‑parameter cost, MoE’s inter‑chip communication overhead diminishes, and the increased activation bandwidth facilitates double‑the‑context‑length for the same hardware budget.  

In sum, 1.58‑bit weighting marries the extreme efficiency of 1‑bit models with the expressive power of ternary representations, thereby redefining the cost‑effectiveness frontier in LLM research.

3. BitNet b1.58 Architecture and Quantization Techniques  

The BitNet b1.58 model marks a decisive step toward practical, memory‑efficient large language models.  Its design philosophy is two‑fold:  (1) replace the floating‑point linear transforms that dominate Transformer workload with a lightweight bit‑wise analogue, and (2) weave together LLaMA‑style architectural choices that have demonstrated strong performance in open‑source models.  In this chapter we dissect each of these ingredients in depth, explaining how the ternary weight representation, the 8‑bit activations, and the accompanying training‑time machinery cooperate to produce a 1‑bit LLM that matches full‑precision baselines while delivering orders‑of‑magnitude gains in latency, memory and energy.  

# Transformer Backbone with BitLinear Modules  

Standard Transformers rely heavily on dense matrix–matrix multiplications performed in the `nn.Linear` layer.  The BitNet b1.58 backbone replaces these floating‑point layers with the custom `BitLinear` module.  In a `BitLinear` transform, every weight entry \(W_{ij}\) is restricted to the ternary set \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\).  During inference, dot product operations become computations over signed integers: a \(-1\) contribution multiplies the input by \(-1\) and a \(+1\) contribution adds it; zeros simply drop out.  Consequently, the GEMM kernel consists almost exclusively of integer additions (and bare integer sign inversions) and no floating‑point multiplications, enabling a 2‑bit (sign + 0) arithmetic stream that can be accelerated aggressively on GPU or ASIC fabrics.

The bitwise granularity of `BitLinear` introduces a layer‑wise sparseness that operates as a learned feature filter: the presence of 0 in a weight entry selectively silences a channel, reducing computational payload during both forward and backward passes.  The network learns not only the sign but also the sparsity pattern needed to preserve representational capacity, a key advantage over earlier 1‑bit schemes that lacked the zero‑weight value.

# LLaMA‑like Components: RMSNorm, SwiGLU, Rotary Embeddings  

To anchor BitNet b1.58 to a proven, high‑performance backbone, the authors adopt all major building blocks of the LLaMA family:

* **RMSNorm** replaces the conventional LayerNorm.  It normalises activations by the root‑mean‑square of the vector, avoiding the bias and scaling parameters of LayerNorm, thereby reducing memory traffic and simplifying the quantisation schedule.
* **SwiGLU** is a gated feed‑forward expansion that uses a SiLU gate followed by a gated linear unit.  It expands hidden dimensionality to \(\frac{4}{3}\) of the query dimension and allows efficient implementation with bit‑wise operations because the gate values can be quantised without excessive precision loss.
* **Rotary positional embeddings** provide a parameter‑free, non‑learned encoding that augments each token with an absolute positional context while remaining fully compatible with a low‑bit linear backbone.
* **Bias elimination**: all layers are instantiated without bias terms.  The bias removal reduces model size, eliminates a source of floating‑point addition during inference, and streamlines the quantisation process because no extra per‑channel bias values need to be stored.

Together, these components preserve the competitive performance profile of LLaMA while simplifying the arithmetic to an extent that allows a 1‑bit weight scheme to maintain accuracy.

# Bias Removal Strategy  

Bias terms in neural networks help the optimiser capture affine shifts, but they add a scalar value per output dimension that must be stored, multiplied with the input, and summed at every inference step.  Eliminating these scalar offsets removes a row of FP16 values from the model, thereby shrinking the weight matrix and freeing cycles that would otherwise be spent on a final bias addition.  Since `BitLinear` already implements a per‑token sign decision, an entire bias vector would corrupt the simplicity of the 1‑bit transform.  The authors therefore adopt a bias‑free design across all layers.  

Although this might appear to limit representational flexibility, the combined effect of the sparse ternary weights, RMSNorm, and SwiGLU gating distributes the expressive capacity across the architecture, obviating the need for learned bias offsets.  Empirical tests show that a bias‑free architecture does not deteriorate zero‑shot perplexity or downstream task metrics.

# AbsMean Quantization of Weights: Scaling and RoundClip  

To enforce the ternary constraint during training, weights undergo the AbsMean quantisation function:
\[
\widetilde{W}= \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},-1,1\right),
\qquad
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max\!\big(a,\min\!\big(b,\mathrm{round}(x)\big)\big),
\quad
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}\!\lvert W_{ij}\rvert .
\]
Here, \(W\) is the full‑precision master weight matrix, \(n\) and \(m\) are its dimensions, and \(\gamma\) is the global per‑matrix average absolute value of the weights.  By normalising against \(\gamma\), the algorithm preserves the relative magnitudes of entries while mapping them to the nearest integer in \(\{-1,0,1\}\).  

The addition of \(\epsilon\) safeguards against division by zero in degenerate cases where \(\gamma\) approaches zero.  Importantly, this quantisation is applied *after* every gradient update to the floating‑point master weights.  Consequently, the gradient signal remains fully in floating‑point precision, allowing standard optimisers (AdamW, etc.) to converge.  Only the quantised snapshot \(\widetilde{W}\) is used for forward and backward propagation, thereby marrying the stability of full‑precision gradients with the efficiency of ternary weight execution.

# 8‑Bit Activation Quantization to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) Range  

While weights are ternary, activation tensors are quantised to 8‑bit integers to keep them compact yet expressive.  The activation quantiser scales each token independently to the symmetric range \([-Q_b,Q_b]\), where \(Q_b=127\) for unsigned 8‑bit representation centered at zero.  Unlike some earlier works that quantise activations to a positive range and apply a zero‑point offset, BitNet b1.58 directly maps the distributed activations into a signed interval, avoiding a separate zero‑point lookup and simplifying the hardware path.  

Because the network discards the bias signal, the activation distribution is naturally centred around zero, further justifying a symmetric quantisation.  The per‑token scaling procedure guarantees that each token's dynamic range is fully exploited, mitigating quantisation noise even in deeper layers where activations may otherwise saturate.

# Training‑time Gradient Updates with Float Master Weights  

To correct for the coarse quantisation induced by the ternary weight mapping, BitNet b1.58 retains a full‑precision (FP16) copy of every weight matrix.  During back‑propagation, gradients are accumulated on the quantised weight \(\widetilde{W}\) and then applied to the master weight \(W\).  Mathematically, for each element:
\[
W \leftarrow W - \eta \cdot \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \widetilde{W}},
\]
where \(\eta\) is the learning rate and \(\mathcal{L}\) is the loss function.  After the optimiser step, the updated master weight undergoes a fresh quantisation cycle to produce a new \(\widetilde{W}\) that will be used in the next forward pass.  This “straight‑through estimator” approach circumvents the non‑differentiability of the hard quantisation while preserving the efficacy of stochastic gradient descent.  Empirically, the authors found that the inclusion of a float master weight was essential for achieving perplexity comparable to an FP16 baseline.

# Integration with FasterTransformer and the 2‑bit Ladder Kernel  

BitNet b1.58 was deployed on the FasterTransformer runtime, a highly optimised inference codebase for Transformer models.  The authors leveraged its modular GPU kernel library to implement two critical components:

1. **BitLinear kernel**: a custom integer‑only GEMM routine that accepts ternary weight tensors and 8‑bit activations, making use of CUDA tensorcores for acceleration.  
2. **Ladder 2‑bit kernel**: an alternative micro‑kernel that processes the 2‑bit representation of the ternary weights (sign bit + presence bit).  The 2‑bit kernel exhibits even lower arithmetic intensity, so it becomes a preferred choice for larger models (up to 70 B parameters), where memory bandwidth dominates.

By swapping the standard `nn.Linear` implementation for the `BitLinear` module in the FasterTransformer codebase, the authors could evaluate latency, memory footprints, energy characteristics, and throughput on commodity GPUs (such as A100).  The experimental results are captured in the paper via tables and figures, showing that for a 70 B BitNet b1.58 the 2‑bit kernel yields 4.1× speed‑up, 3.3× memory reduction, and a near‑order‑of‑magnitude decrease in arithmetic energy.  

In practice, the integration requires only minimal code changes: the `BitLinear` layer is inserted in the transformer architecture, the quantisation routines are applied during training, and the inference path cascades through the `Int8` or `Skeletony` kernels supplied by FasterTransformer and Ladder.  Given the open‑source nature of these libraries, developers can readily experiment with alternative micro‑kernels or hardware backends to tune the trade‑off between throughput and latency.  

---  

In summary, BitNet b1.58 showcases how a doctrinal shift toward bit‑wise linear arithmetic, combined with thoughtful architectural choices (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, no biases) and a disciplined quantisation pipeline (AbsMean, 8‑bit symmetric activations, float master weights), can deliver a Large Language Model with full‑precision performance while dramatically compressing memory, energy, and latency budgets.  The design is modular, hardware‑friendly, and ready for integration into mainstream inference engines, heralding a new era of practical, 1‑bit LLM deployment.

4. Training from Scratch with a 1.58‑Bit Representation  

Large Language Models (LLMs) in modern practice are almost universally trained with full‑precision floating weights and activations. The seminal BitNet paper shows that a purely ternary weight representation—\{-1, 0, +1\}—can be used from the moment a model is initialized, and that the resulting 1.58‑bit LLMs (hence the name *BitNet b1.58*) can match or even surpass their FP16 counterparts in perplexity and downstream task accuracy. This chapter dissects the training pipeline that makes this possible, providing a step‑by‑step tour of the dataset, tokenisation, optimisation strategy, and, most importantly, how the ternary weights are maintained throughout back‑propagation.

---

# RedPajama dataset and 100B token training configuration  

The authors chose the RedPajama corpus—an open‑source collection designed to replicate the data foundation of commercial LLMs—for pre‑training. This dataset contains an estimated 100 billion tokens after cleaning, filtering, and deduplication, matching the scale of many commercially available models. By training on such a vast amount of data, the authors ensure that the 1.58‑bit model learns linguistic and factual knowledge comparable to that captured by full‑precision baselines.

In practice, the training configuration consists of the following blocks:

1. **Data selection**: RedPajama is partitioned into training, validation, and evaluation splits, ensuring that the 100 B token corpus is strictly used for learning; validation data are held out to monitor overfitting.
2. **Training length**: The training run continues until the entire 100 B tokens have been seen once (i.e., one *epoch* of the dataset). Because the stochastic gradient descent operates on mini‑batches, the effective number of iterations depends on the chosen batch size and sequence length, but every token contributes once to the gradient update schedule.
3. **Per‑token normalisation**: As described in the BitNet architecture, activations are quantised to 8 bits. The input tokens and their contextual embeddings are normalised by RMSNorm (replacing the standard LayerNorm used in LLaMA) before passing through SwiGLU, Rotary, and positional embeddings.

The authors emphasise that the model size, token count, and training hyper‑parameters are deliberately mirrored to those of the FP16 LLaMA baseline, guaranteeing a fair comparison of performance versus cost.

---

# Tokenization and vocabulary considerations  

A crucial early step is mapping natural language into indices that the Transformer can process. BitNet does not alter the tokeniser compared with modern open‑source LLMs; instead it relies on a byte‑pair‑encoding (BPE) vocabulary, similar to the one used by LLaMA. The key points are:

* **Vocabulary size**: The tokenizer includes roughly 32K unique symbols, a standard size that balances coverage and sparsity. Each token is encoded as a 16‑bit integer, but during forward passes the embedding matrix is quantised to 1.58‑bit only for the weight coefficients; the indices themselves remain low‑precision integers.
* **Truncation and padding**: Sequences are truncated or padded to a fixed length (typically 2048 or 4096 tokens for pre‑training). In the training loop, each mini‑batch is constructed with equal‑length sequences, simplifying parallel computation across devices.
* **Caching of embeddings**: The embedding layer is treated like any other linear layer but keeps its weights in full precision, because embeddings provide a dense and critical source of positional information. This design choice preserves the representational power of the model while still benefiting from the 1.58‑bit representations elsewhere.

By keeping the tokenisation pipeline identical to state‑of‑the‑art models, BitNet ensures that its downstream benchmarks (ARC‑Easy, Winogrande, etc.) are evaluated on the same input distribution as LLaMA, tightening the fairness of the comparison.

---

# Optimiser, learning‑rate schedule, batch size  

Although the paper does not spell out every training hyper‑parameter, the reported numbers implicitly reflect the following choices adopted from the standard LLaMA training configuration:

* **Optimiser**: AdamW is used, with typical hyper‑values of β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95, and weight‑decay set to 0.1. Because the quantised weights are wrapped around a float master copy, weight‑decay is applied directly to that master copy before quantisation.
* **Learning‑rate schedule**: A linear warm‑up over the first 4000 steps (≈ 80 M tokens, depending on batch size) is followed by cosine‑annealing down to a negligible value. This schedule is robust to the sparsity newly introduced by ternary weights; the early warm‑up stage gradually acclimates the network to the quantisation operator.
* **Batch size**: A per‑GPU batch of 256 tokens (or a few hundred tokens depending on GPU memory) is a typical setup; to reach full‑gradient updates, gradient‑accumulation is performed over several steps. This yields effective batch sizes in the millions of tokens, which counteracts the variance introduced by the non‑linear quantisation.
* **Sequence length**: As mentioned, a fixed length of 2048 or 4096 tokens is used; longer contexts would necessitate more memory, but the 1.58‑bit representation already shrinks the memory footprint, allowing higher lengths if hardware permits.

The authors emphasise that these hyper‑parameters are deliberately mirrored across their FP16 baseline and the 1.58‑bit model. This alignment isolates the effect of weight quantisation on training dynamics and results.

---

# Maintaining float master weights during quantisation  

The cornerstone of BitNet b1.58’s training pipeline is the preservation of a *float master weight* for every parameter. The workflow proceeds as follows:

1. **Float master copy**: The weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) is stored in full precision (FP16/BF16). Its values evolve under the optimiser’s updates.
2. **Quantised forward weight**: Before each forward pass, the master weights are projected to the ternary set via the *absmean* function:
   \[
   \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
   \]
   \[
   \tilde{W} = \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right),
   \]
   where \(\epsilon\) is a small constant preventing division by a vanishing mean and \(\operatorname{RoundClip}\) maps every real to the nearest integer in \{-1,0,1\}.
3. **Straight‑through estimator (STE)**: Because \(\operatorname{RoundClip}\) is non‑differentiable, the training loop treats it as an identity during back‑propagation. In other words, the gradient \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}\) is taken as the gradient of the loss with respect to \(\tilde{W}\). This “stochastic” gradient is then applied to the *float master* weight. The STE enables stable learning despite the coarse quantisation.
4. **Periodic re‑quantisation**: Since the optimiser operates on the master weight, its values can drift and become negative or far from the ternary set. Re‑applying the quantiser every forward pass keeps the active weight matrix strictly ternary.

By decoupling the forward forward quantised representation from the optimiser’s master copy, the model benefits from the expressive power of dense optimization while still reaping the computational advantages of a ternary matrix multiplication during inference. Empirical results in the paper confirm that this strategy maintains perplexity parity with full‑precision baselines.

---

# Efficient handling of the zero weight state  

The addition of a zero value to the ternary alphabet is more than a mathematical curiosity: it unlocks tangible computational savings. In the forward pass, each matrix multiplication between \( \tilde{W}\) and an activation vector \(\mathbf{x}\) can be implemented as:

\[
y_k = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \tilde{w}_{ki} \, x_i,
\]
where \(\tilde{w}_{ki} \in \{-1,0,+1\}\). The term \(\tilde{w}_{ki} = 0\) simply discards the contribution from \(x_i\), so the processor need not perform any addition or multiplication for that pair. In practice:

* **Sparse‑style skipping**: Hardware kernels can detect zeros on the fly and skip the corresponding load–accumulate cycle. On a many‑core or GPU architecture, this translates to fewer memory accesses and lower compute utilisation per weight.
* **Vectorised implementation**: In SIMD or GPU matrix multipliers, zero entries can be masked out. The resulting instruction counts drop linearly with the zero‑density of the weight tensor, yielding a direct speed‑up in latency and throughput.
* **Feature filtering**: Architecturally, having a zero weight allows the network to effectively “turn off” specific neurons for particular inputs. This can act as a learned feature selector, potentially improving representational efficiency. The paper reports that the inclusion of the zero value gives a measurable performance lift over the pure‑1‑bit BitNet variant.

The paper quantifies the benefit of this design: on the 7 B and 13 B models, the memory and latency improvements grow as the proportion of non‑zero weights rises, but even with a modest sparsity it yields a clear advantage over FP16 LLMs.

---

# 2‑trillion‑token experiment following StableLM‑3B recipe  

To probe the scalability of BitNet b1.58 beyond 100 B tokens, the authors replicated a training run with 2 trillion tokens, mimicking the data recipe of the StableLM‑3B benchmark. Key aspects of this experiment include:

1. **Data recipe**: The StableLM recipe combines a diverse set of corpora (e.g., CommonCrawl, Wikipedia, Project Gutenberg) to reach 2 T tokens, ensuring a broad coverage of domains and styles. BitNet follows the same recipe, using the same preprocessing pipeline (tokenisation, cleaning, dedup).
2. **Model size**: A 3 B parameter incarnation matches StableLM‑3B, providing a fair head‑to‑head comparison on downstream zero‑shot accuracy.
3. **Training duration**: The training stops when 2 T tokens have been processed, which, given the larger token volume, translates into a longer wall‑clock time but yields richer linguistic exposure.
4. **Evaluation**: Zero‑shot accuracy is measured on an established benchmark covering reasoning, commonsense, and factual recall (Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, ARC‑Easy). The authors report that BitNet b1.58 not only retains parity with the full‑precision StableLM‑3B but consistently surpasses it across all tasks.
5. **Interpretation**: The superior performance is attributed to the explicit zero‑weight filtering and the large training token budget. Even with only ternary weights, the network benefits from the broader data exposure, underscoring that the key bottleneck in scaling LLMs is data more than precision.

This experiment demonstrates that 1.58‑bit training is not a brittle, small‑scale trick but a robust paradigm amenable to the same token budgets used by the industry's largest models.

## Closing remarks  

Training an LLM with ternary weights from scratch demands a careful choreography between data processing, optimisation, and quantisation. By retaining float master weights, judiciously applying a straight‑through estimator, and exploiting the computational economy of a zero entry, BitNet b1.58 succeeds in matching, and sometimes exceeding, the performance of full‑precision LLMs at a fraction of the memory, latency, and energy cost. The paper’s empirical evidence—both on 100 B and 2 T token regimes—sets a concrete scaling law for 1.58‑bit models, suggesting that industry‑grade LLMs can now be trained in a manner that is both cost‑effective and energy‑efficient without sacrificing accuracy.

5. Benchmarking: Perplexity, Zero‑Shot Tasks, and Experimental Setup  

# Perplexity on WikiText2 and C4 datasets  

Perplexity is a fundamental metric for evaluating language models.  It is defined as the exponential of the average negative log‑likelihood of a test sequence, and it quantifies how well a probabilistic model predicts unseen data.  If a model assigns higher probability mass to the correct tokens, the average cross‑entropy loss decreases, and the perplexity falls.  In formulae, for a sequence of length \(N\) the perplexity is  

\[
\mathrm{PPL} \;=\; \exp\!\left( - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_{\theta}(x_i \mid x_{<i}) \right),
\]

where \(p_{\theta}\) denotes the model’s probability estimate.  Because perplexity is an exponentiation of the cross‑entropy, it can be interpreted as the “average number of choices” the model faces at each prediction step; thus a lower perplexity signals a closer match to the true language distribution.

In the work on BitNet b1.58, two standard language modeling datasets are used to compute this value: WikiText2 and C4.  WikiText2 is a curated collection of approximately 2.8 M labeled tokens pulled from Wikipedia; it contains a relatively balanced vocabulary and serves as a benchmark for long‑context modeling.  C4, on the other hand, is a massive, publicly available corpus derived from Common Crawl, spanning roughly 800 M tokens after filtering and cleaning.  The two datasets differ in size, domain coverage, and noise level, providing a robust gauge of a model’s capacity to generalize.

The BitNet b1.58 authors pre‑train their models on the RedPajama dataset for 100 B tokens.  After training, they evaluate validation perplexity on the last checkpoint of WikiText2 and C4.  To avoid leakage, the two datasets are untouched during training.  Crucially, the perplexity computation is performed with the same tokenization and vocabulary as the model was trained on.  For each dataset, a single pass through the entire validation split is made, the log‑probabilities of successive tokens are accumulated, and the negative average is exponentiated.  This procedure produces a single scalar that can be compared across full‑precision (FP16) baselines and 1.58‑bit bit‑compressed models.

Because the BitNet b1.58 model uses 1.58‑bit ternary weights and 8‑bit activations but matches the FP16 LLaMA baseline in perplexity when the model size reaches 3 B parameters, the perplexity curves in the paper demonstrate a new scaling law.  In particular, the curve for a 3 B BitNet b1.58 lies very close to (or indistinguishable from) the 3 B FP16 curve, while the curves for 7 B, 13 B, and even 70 B show BitNet surpassing their FP16 counterparts in both perplexity and resource usage.  These results underscore that the 1.58‑bit representation is not a mere toy compression; rather, it preserves the statistical fidelity of language modeling across a wide range of scales.

# Zero‑shot evaluation suite: ARC‑Easy, ARC‑Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ  

Beyond perplexity, real‑world language models are judged on how well they reason, answer questions, and exhibit knowledge.  The BitNet b1.58 paper therefore adopts a battery of zero‑shot benchmarks, meaning that the model receives no task‑specific fine‑tuning and is simply evaluated on prompts designed to probe reasoning.

- **ARC‑Easy**: The “AI2 Reasoning Challenge – Easy” comprises multiple‑choice science questions drawn from middle‑school curricula.  The model must select the correct answer among four candidates without any exposure to the human‑generated hints.

- **ARC‑Challenge**: A more difficult subset of the same problem set, featuring higher‑order reasoning and ambiguous data.  Performance on this set indicates the model’s ability to handle complex inference.

- **HellaSwag**: A large collection of everyday, commonsense scenarios.  For each prompt, the model chooses from four plausible continuations.  The score is computed as the proportion of times the model picks the most intelligible choice.  In zero‑shot settings, a model’s performance reflects its grasp of trivial common sense.

- **Winogrande**: A collection built upon Winograd Schema for coreference resolution.  The task requires identifying which of two candidates (often pronouns) the model attributes a given referent to, again in a multiple‑choice format.

- **PIQA**: The “Physical Interaction Question Answering” dataset tests physical commonsense reasoning.  Prompts ask which of two actions will produce a realistic physical outcome.  The model’s predictions are evaluated against ground‑truth answer keys.

- **OpenbookQA**: A dataset that isolates factual knowledge questions that can be answered with a small set of facts (the “open book”).  The question is usually multiple‑choice and the model must demonstrate its ability to retrieve or infer facts.

- **BoolQ**: Binary (true/false) answer questions derived from open‑domain paragraphs.  The model’s predictions are turned into binary labels and compared against human annotations.

Each benchmark is assessed using the standard accuracy metric.  Because all tasks are multiple‑choice, the model’s predicted probability for each candidate is rounded to a hard choice, and the proportion of correct choices yields the accuracy.  The zero‑shot nature of the evaluation highlights that the model’s internal representations of knowledge, as gathered during training, are sufficient for generalization.

# Use of lm‑evaluation‑harness for metric extraction  

The LM‑Evaluation‑Harness is an open‑source framework that orchestrates the execution of a wide array of language‑model benchmarks.  It takes as input a model wrapper, a batch of prompts, and a set of task specifications.  For BitNet b1.58’s evaluation, the harness is configured to:

1. Load the 1.58‑bit weights and 8‑bit activations, shipping the model to the GPU as a sequence‑to‑sequence head with a vanilla decoder.
2. For each benchmark, send the pre‑tokenized prompt to the model and capture the softmax probabilities over the vocabulary.
3. Translate the token probabilities into logits over the candidate answers (e.g., mapping each choice to the corresponding token or string), often via a custom post‑processing step in the harness.
4. Compute the chosen metric (accuracy, pass@k, or a custom statistical function) defined in the harness’s task configuration.

The harness also logs GPU memory usage and inference latency per token, which the BitNet authors inherited to build their runtime profile.  The harness’s architecture ensures reproducible metric extraction across experiments—a vital feature when comparing models that differ only in precision.  Concretely, to obtain zero‑shot accuracies, the harness runs each task over the test split, records the predicted answer for each prompt, and then compares it with the ground‑truth label.  The aggregate accuracy is the ratio of correct predictions to the total number of prompts.

# Runtime measurement methodology: GPU memory, latency per output token  

Efficient deployment of language models requires not just high accuracy but also low latency and memory consumption.  The BitNet b1.58 paper measures these quantities in a systematic manner, focusing on time‑per‑token and peak GPU memory.

**Latency measurement** is performed by generating a single token at a time in autoregressive mode.  The model’s forward pass is timed from the moment the last hidden state is fed into the final linear layer until the logits for the next token are produced.  This measurement excludes any auxiliary steps (such as decoder caching or  token sequencing) that are not part of the core MMMut operation.  Because the kernel used by BitNet is a 2‑bit approximation that saves integer addition for every multiply‑accumulate, the resulting latencies are measured in the context of the same batch size and sequence length used for throughput (often a single token).  Warm‑up runs are conducted to ensure GPU pipelines are fully saturated and to eliminate startup jitter.  The final reported latency is the median across a large number of such generation steps, providing a stable benchmark.

**Memory measurement** captures the peak amount of GPU global memory allocated by the inference engine during a forward pass.  This includes the storage of the model’s weights, activation buffers, and any intermediate tensors created by the transformer blocks.  The FastTransformer profiler (via torch.cuda.memory_allocated or a dedicated query in the library’s profiling suite) records this value.  The memory footprint for BitNet is significantly lower—a factor of 3.55 times less for the 3 B model—because ternary weights require one bit per parameter while full‑precision FP16 weights require 16 bits.  However, the 8‑bit activation buffers are still needed; nevertheless, the overall compression produces a near‑linear reduction in memory.

Latency and memory are measured under identical conditions for both the BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA LLM baseline.  The same hardware (e.g., NVIDIA A100 GPUs), the same batch size, sequence length, and deployment setup are used so that the reported differences arise purely from the precision and architecture.

# FasterTransformer setup and 2‑bit kernel usage  

The evaluation framework is built atop FasterTransformer, a highly optimized inference engine specifically tuned for transformer workloads on NVIDIA GPUs.  FasterTransformer offers a modular architecture where mixins such as custom kernels may be swapped in, and it exposes profiling hooks for latency and memory.

For the 1.58‑bit BitNet, the authors incorporated the 2‑bit kernel from the Ladder library.  This kernel is designed to exploit the ternary weight format: because each weight can be \(-1, 0,\) or \(+1\), the matrix multiplication can be rewritten as a series of integer additions and subtractions, entirely eliminating floating‑point multiply‑accumulate operations.  The kernel works as follows:

1. **Weight packing**: The ternary weights are packed into 32‑bit integers by mapping \(-1\) to \( \{0x01\}\), \(0\) to \( \{0x00\}\), and \(+1\) to \( \{0xFF\}\).  Thus, a single 32‑bit word stores eight ternary symbols.  This compact representation reduces the number of memory fetches during computation.

2. **Matrix–vector multiplication**: The packed weights interface with a custom ATen kernel that performs a stream of add- or subtract- operations based on the packed bits.  Since all multipliers are ±1 or 0, the kernel mini‑ifies the computation into a tight loop of integer arithmetic.

3. **Quantization of activations**: 8‑bit integer activations are passed to the kernel.  The kernel performs a scaling step to map from integer product space back to the appropriate floating‑point domain before the softmax.

4. **Patch to FasterTransformer**: The kernel is integrated as a new `bitint` module within FasterTransformer’s `CublasGemmEx` alias, using a custom CUDA kernel launch configuration tuned for the target GPU architecture.  This patch ensures that both the FP16 baseline and the 1.58‑bit variant run through the same high-level inference pipeline, enabling a fair comparison of performance.

The combination of FasterTransformer and the 2‑bit kernel yields two major gains: a 2.71× speedup in latency per token for 3 B BitNet versus the FP16 baseline, and a 3.55× reduction in memory usage.  When scaled to larger models (7 B, 13 B, 70 B), the speedup climbs to 4.1× relative to FP16 LLaMA, as the linear layers—which now comprise a larger fraction of the compute budget—receive the most benefit from the integer‑only multiplication.

In summary, the benchmarking pipeline employed by BitNet b1.58’s authors is a cohesive blend of standard language‑model metrics (perplexity, accuracy across a rich zero‑shot suite), rigorous runtime profiling (latency and memory, collected with FasterTransformer and its 2‑bit kernel), and a reproducible evaluation harness (lm‑evaluation‑harness).  This methodological framework ensures that comparisons to full‑precision models reflect genuine fidelity in modeling capacity while exposing the substantial efficiency gains realized by the 1.58‑bit representation.

6. Inference Efficiency: Memory, Latency, Energy, and Throughput

# Memory savings from ternary weights and 8‑bit activations

When a language model grows to billions of parameters, the memory required to store its weights begins to dominate both training and inference workloads. In a conventional 16‑bit floating‑point LLM, each parameter is stored in 16 bits, which immediately pins a 3 B model to roughly \(3\times10^9\times16\) bits ≈ 48 GB of uncompressed weight data. In practice, a fraction of this weight space is further padded and duplicated across the GPU memory hierarchy, meaning that a typical inference run of a 3 B LLaMA model can occupy over 6 GB of GPU RAM once activations are added.

BitNet b1.58 changes the fundamental encoding of the weight tensor. By constraining every value to the ternary set \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) and packing the values using a carefully designed binary representation, the average number of bits per weight drops to 1.58. The quantization procedure (Section 3.1 of the paper) first normalizes the weight matrix by its average absolute value \(\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|\) and then applies a bounded rounding operator yielding a discreet set of three symbols. Because the distribution of weights in large transformers is heavily centered around zero, the zero token occurs far more frequently than +1 or –1, allowing a variable‑length or Huffman‑style encoding that saves the extra 0.42 bits compared to a naïve 2‑bit representation.

Consequently, the raw weight memory for a 3 B model is reduced from \(3\times10^9\times16\) bits to only \(3\times10^9\times1.58\) bits, which is roughly \(0.75\) GB. The paper reports that, under typical inference kernels, this translation of ternary weights into 1.58‑bit tensors allows the overall GPU memory footprint to shrink by a factor of more than 3.5 compared to an FP16 baseline. Moreover, the activations themselves are quantized to 8‑bit integers rather than 16‑bit floats, cutting the temporary footprint of forward‑pass tensors by another half. In practice, for a 512‑token batch and a 3 B BitNet b1.58 model, the memory consumption drops from approximately 6 GB in FP16 to about 2 GB, as measured on NVIDIA A100 GPUs using the FasterTransformer code base with the 2‑bit kernel from Ladder.

It is worth noting that while these savings are dramatic, the embedding layer in the LLaMA architecture is intentionally kept in full precision to preserve the model’s ability to capture fine‑grained token representations. As the model size grows, the embedding layer becomes a smaller fraction of the total model, so the proportionate memory savings from ternary weights increase with scale, which is consistent with the observations reported for the 7 B, 13 B, and 70 B models.

# Latency scaling from 3B to 70B parameter models

Latency, measured as the average time to generate a single output token, is dominated in transformer‑based LLMs by the cost of the attention and feed‑forward linear layers. In a full‑precision model each matrix multiplication requires a sequence of FP16 multiply‑add operations. In BitNet b1.58 the same matrix multiplication is carried out purely using integer addition on 1.58‑bit weights and 8‑bit activations; no floating‑point multiplications are needed. Because an integer addition consumes far less silicon area and state‑transition time than a floating‑point multiply, the arithmetic throughput of these layers increases dramatically.

The paper quantifies this improvement over a wide range of model sizes. For a 3 B BitNet b1.58, the average latency per token is reported to be 2.71 times lower than the corresponding FP16 LLaMA counterpart, while for a 3.9 B model the speed‑up reaches 2.4×. When the model grows further to 70 B parameters, the latency improvement scales to more than 4 ×. This trend is attributed to the fact that the percentage of a model’s execution time that is spent in the linear layers rises with overall size: larger models have deeper attention and feed‑forward modules, and each of those linear layers is now executed by a low‑bit integer kernel.

Hardware‑aware profiling using FasterTransformer confirms that the reduction in per‑token latency comes largely from the `nn.Linear` kernel. In 70 B models where almost 80 % of the FLOPs are spent in linear layers, the migration from FP16 to 1.58‑bit representation translates to an overall inference time reduction of about 4.1× compared to the LLaMA baseline. Importantly, the paper shows that these speed‑ups are not limited to a specific GPU architecture; the same 2‑bit kernel from Ladder and the AMP quantized operations deliver consistent improvements on the tested 80 GB A100s.

Because the architecture of BitNet b1.58 preserves the standard transformer shape (tokenization, positional encoding, multi‑head attention, SwiGLU feed‑forward, RMSNorm), the same latency savings can be directly ported to any LLM deployment framework that supports the underlying quantized kernel, such as HuggingFace, vLLM, and llama.cpp. This means that the latency gains are inherent to the quantization strategy rather than an artifact of any particular implementation fit.

# Energy modelling: INT8 addition vs FP16 multiply‑add

Beyond raw memory and latency metrics, the real-world operational cost of running an LLM hinges on the energy required to perform arithmetic operations. In wide‑scale data centers, where heat dissipation and power limits are dominating factors, low‑bit integer arithmetic can deliver substantial savings in energy consumption.

The paper adopts an analytic energy model grounded in the seminal work on arithmetic energy costs (citing references 14 and 15). According to this model, the energy to perform an INT8 addition on a 7 nm process is roughly \(0.2\) pJ, whereas an FP16 multiply‑add can consume up to \(15\) pJ on the same technology node. By substituting a linear layer’s FLOPs into these per‑operation costs, the authors estimate the total hardware energy per inference step.

In the context of BitNet b1.58, the dominant operation in each linear layer is an INT8 addition between a weight and input activation, rather than an FP16 multiply‑add. Since an INT8 addition is roughly 71.4 × cheaper than its FP16 counterpart (as reported in the paper), this translates into a proportional reduction in the arithmetic‑energy budget for any large transformer. The authors confirm this by measuring that a 7 B BitNet b1.58 model consumes 71.4 × less arithmetic energy than an FP16 LLaMA model of the same size on a 7 nm chip. For even larger models, the savings compound because the proportion of compute spent in the linear layers continues to rise.

Energy measurements are presented for a representative workload of 512 tokens per batch. The cumulative energy charge for a 70 B BitNet b1.58 is far below what would be observed on an equivalent FP16 model. When scaled to thousands of inference requests in an online service scenario, this reduction can translate into considerable cost savings and a smaller carbon footprint.

# 71.4× arithmetic‑energy savings on 7 nm chips

This figure is not arbitrary; it emerges from the direct comparison of the two arithmetic primitives. For a single FLOP in the full‑precision Transformer, one multiply‑add operation is required. In the integer‑based BitNet b1.58, the same mathematically equivalent operation reduces to a series of integer additions. Let’s denote the energy per FP16 multiply‑add as \(E_{\text{FP16}}\) and the energy per INT8 addition as \(E_{\text{INT8}}\). The ratio \(E_{\text{FP16}} / E_{\text{INT8}}\) is found experimentally for deep‑chip 7 nm designs:

\[
\frac{E_{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{INT8}}} \approx \frac{15~\text{pJ}}{0.21~\text{pJ}} \approx 71.4 .
\]

In practice, a BitNet b1.58 layer performs as many additions as there are non‑zero entries in its weight matrix. Because these weights are ternary, the sparsity is high and the number of effective additions can be smaller than the nominal number of multiply‑adds that the full‑precision counterpart must execute. The paper’s profiling on 7 nm chips corroborates that, for all but the smallest model sizes, the total arithmetic energy is reduced by a factor of 71.4 when the 1.58‑bit representation is used.

Beyond the raw per-operation savings, there is also a reduction in dynamic power load on the memory bus. Since each weight is stored in 1.58 bits, the data‑movement cost per token is substantially lower, creating a compound energy advantage that is even more pronounced in data‑center settings with high I/O traffic. Thus, the 71.4× figure is representative of the **dominant** component (arithmetic operations) but the holistic energy savings for a complete inference cycle can be slightly larger when the memory traffic is included.

# Throughput gains with pipeline parallelism (up to 8.9× for 70B)

Throughput, often measured as tokens processed per second (token‑rate), is a compound metric that depends on both latency and the size of the batch that can be processed concurrently on a set of accelerators. The paper investigates throughput for 70 B BitNet b1.58 and the equivalent LLaMA baseline using two 80 GB A100 GPUs, fully leveraging pipelined parallelism (GPipe style). In this setting, the 70 B LLaMA model must be partitioned because its weight matrix cannot fit on a single GPU, whereas the 70 B BitNet b1.58 can reside entirely on a single GPU thanks to its reduced memory footprint.

By gradually increasing the batch size until the GPU memory capacity is hit, the authors observe that a 70 B BitNet b1.58 can accommodate a batch 11 × larger than that of the FP16 baseline. With a constant sequence length of 512 tokens per request (the typical inference fuse length when computing token‑generation rates), this translates into an end‑to‑end throughput advantage of about 8.9 ×:

\[
\text{Throughput}_{\text{b1.58}} \approx 8.9 \times \text{Throughput}_{\text{FP16}} .
\]

This improvement stems from the reduced per‑token latency (section “Latency scaling”) and the ability to handle more requests in parallel because each request occupies less GPU memory. The same conclusion holds for smaller models; for instance, a 3 B BitNet b1.58 retains roughly a 3.5× throughput advantage over the full‑precision baseline, as the 3 × memory savings allow for a correspondingly larger batch.

Importantly, the paper’s throughput experiments use a production‑grade inference engine (FasterTransformer) with an optimized 2‑bit kernel from Ladder, meaning that the throughput gains are accessible without adding any custom hardware or extraordinary software development. This suggests that data‑center customers could immediately increase their token‑throughput capacity by adopting BitNet b1.58 without investing in new GPUs or large migrations.

# Comparative analysis with FP16 LLaMA baseline

The same experimental framework is used throughout the paper to contrast BitNet b1.58 with an engineered FP16 LLaMA model of identical parameter count. The comparative analysis encompasses memory, latency, energy, throughput, and, crucially, performance. Two key points emerge:

1. **Paralleling or surpassing full‑precision performance**: Starting from a 3 B weight configuration, BitNet b1.58 matches the perplexity of a full‑precision LLaMA model trained on the RedPajama dataset for 100 B tokens. Beyond this size, BitNet b1.58 not only preserves perplexity but also improves zero‑shot accuracy on a suite of natural‑language tasks (ARC, Hellaswag, Winogrande, etc.). In particular, the 3.9 B BitNet b1.58 outperforms the 3 B FP16 baseline across all evaluated success metrics while consuming less than a third of the GPU memory and half as much energy.

2. **Scaling advantages**: The paper introduces a new scaling law that maps a 1.58‑bit model of a given size to a FP16 model of a smaller size in terms of both inference cost and performance. For instance, a 13 B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 3 B FP16 model; a 30 B BitNet b1.58 surpasses a 7 B FP16 model; and a 70 B BitNet b1.58 outperforms a 13 B FP16 model. These cross‑roll comparisons are derived from the analytic curves in Figure 2 (latency and memory) and Figure 3 (energy). They give practitioners a simple rule of thumb for aligning a target inference cost with an optimum model size when considering 1.58‑bit quantization.

Beyond the raw numbers, the comparison underscores a more general insight: a higher‑granularity quantization that permits feature filtering (via the “0” weight value) can mitigate the loss of precision that typically plagues aggressive quantization. The inclusion of zeroes allows the network to actively suppress irrelevant activations, a functional capability that compensates for the lower weight resolution and explains why BitNet b1.58 does not experience a decline in perplexity or task accuracy relative to FP16.

In sum, the evidence from the paper demonstrates that 1.58‑bit LLMs provide a Pareto‑improvement over conventional FP16 transformers: they are cheaper to serve, faster to reason, and more energy‑efficient, all while achieving comparable if not better inference outcomes. Future deployments in cloud, edge, or mobile contexts can thus directly benefit from the architecture, the quantization approach, and the hardware‑aware optimization that BitNet b1.58 introduces.

7. Scaling Laws and Comparative Analysis  

The experiments carried out on the BitNet b1.58 variant reveal a striking shift in the conventional trade‑off between model performance and inference cost. Starting at a modest number of parameters, the one‑bit network is able to match and eventually surpass the full‑precision (FP16) LLaMA baseline in both perplexity and a broad set of zero‑shot benchmark tasks, while simultaneously cutting latency, memory footprint, and compute energy by several fold. The following sections unpack these findings in detail, expose the underlying scaling relationships, and discuss how this new Pareto frontier reshapes future directions for large‑scale language models.

# Latency–Memory Trade‑offs and Throughput Comparisons  

The most salient advantage of a 1‑bit architecture lies not just in the numbers reported by perplexity or accuracy, but in the way those numbers translate into inference costs. BitNet b1.58 replaces floating‑point matrix multiplications with their integer‑only counterparts. The CUDA kernels used for inference implement a two‑bit multiplication routine from Ladder, adapted for the ternary weight space \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\). Because multiplication is practically eliminated—only addition and bit‑shifts remain—the arithmetic energy and latency drop dramatically.

For a \(3\)-billion‑parameter model, the time per output token is reduced by a factor of \(2.71\) relative to the FP16 baseline. This creates a 2.71× higher per‑token throughput. Simultaneously, the GPU memory footprint is shrunk by a factor of \(3.55\), a consequence of packing each weight into only one bit of storage. At the largest evaluated scale (\(70\) billion parameters), the same computational paradigm yields a speed‑up of \(4.1\)× per token and a 3.5× reduction in memory footprint. Furthermore, because the overall fraction of time spent in \(\texttt{nn.Linear}\) operations grows with model size, the benefit compounds as we scale to dozens of billions of parameters.

Memory bandwidth is also alleviated. The entire weight matrix can be cached in on‑chip SRAM, and the transfer of activations from DRAM to on‑chip memory, which dominates the energy bill for full‑precision LLMs, is effectively eliminated. Inevitably, this accelerates not only inference but also the overall pipeline latency for batch processing.

These observations are concretized in the throughput experiment described below, where two 80‑GB A100 GPUs were used to run the largest models. For a fixed sequence length of 512 tokens, BitNet b1.58 was able to push the batch size almost to the memory limiter at 11× that of the FP16 baseline, achieving an 8.9× higher sustained token throughput.

# Scaling‑Law Equivalences  

The empirical results establish a new inter‑model “scaling‑law equivalence”: a model in the 1.58‑bit regime can deliver the latency, memory, and energy efficiency of a substantially larger FP16 model. The paper summarises three concrete equivalence points:  

1. **13 B BitNet b1.58** outperforms **3 B FP16 LLaMA** across all three key cost dimensions.  
2. **30 B BitNet b1.58** is superior in latency, memory, and energy to **7 B FP16 LLaMA**.  
3. **70 B BitNet b1.58** surpasses a **13 B FP16 LLaMA** model in the same metrics.  

These points are made precise in Figure \(\ref{fig:latency-memory-energy}\) and Figure \(\ref{fig:energy}\) of the main paper, which plot curves of measured latency and energy consumption for each configuration. The linearity of the “speed‑up vs. model size” curve reflects that the majority of inference cost is due to \(\texttt{nn.Linear}\) operations; as the matrix dimensions grow, the savings from integer-only arithmetic become proportionally larger. Importantly, this equivalence implies that an engineer could replace a \(30\)‑billion‑parameter FP16 LLM with a \(13\)‑billion‑parameter 1.58‑bit LLM and still outperform the former in both accuracy and efficiency, a promise that would dramatically reduce the overhead of scaling to the next generation of models.

# Pareto Front Interpretation: Performance vs. Inference Cost  

In multi‑objective optimization, a Pareto front consists of configurations where improving one objective inevitably deteriorates another. In the context of LLMs, we consider two objectives: **performance** (as measured by perplexity or zero‑shot accuracy) and **cost** (measured by latency, memory consumption, or energy). Conventional training pipelines chronically push the performance axis to the right while moving along the cost axis to the left—larger models for better predictions, more hardware for more throughput. The BitNet b1.58 experiments reveal a *Pareto shift*: the 1.58‑bit models occupy a new region of the front where both axes improve simultaneously. They achieve equal or better performance while simultaneously reducing cost. 

This Pareto improvement manifests most visibly at the \(3.9\)‑billion‑parameter point: the perplexity aligns with that of a \(3\)-billion‑parameter FP16 model, but the latency is almost a third of the full‑precision counterpart and the memory usage is a third as well. At \(70\) billion parameters, the cost difference is amplified to a factor of \(4.1\) in latency. Consequently, any engineer foreseeing incremental size increases in upcoming LLMs will now have a more attractive cost–benefit trade‑off when considering the 1.58‑bit option.

# Implications for Future LLM Scaling Strategies  

The discovery that 1.58‑bit models can match full‑precision performance from a modest size upwards reshapes several strategic considerations in the design of future language models.

**Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) Protocols.**  
MoE architectures aim to reduce arithmetic work by routing inputs to a limited subset of expert sub‑models. However, the high inter‑chip communication and memory requirements of MoE have limited its deployment. The drastic reduction in memory footprint for BitNet b1.58 means that more experts can fit on a single accelerator device without incurring the bandwidth penalties that plague traditional MoE systems. This could enable arbitrarily large MoE designs that are free from the communication bottlenecks that would otherwise nullify their computational savings.

**Native Long‑Sequence Support.**  
Handling long context windows incurs a memory penalty via KV cache propagation. In a 1.58‑bit setting, activations are already reduced to 8 bits; yet the module design permits a lossless further compression to 4 bits or lower. Doubling the context length while keeping memory usage constant becomes a concrete possibility, enabling tasks that require truly long‑range dependencies—histories spanning multiple documents, or multi‑turn dialog with detailed back‑court—as part of the inference pipeline.

**Edge and Mobile Deployment.**  
Edge devices rarely possess the bandwidth or power budgets to run 16‑bit LLMs. In contrast, BitNet b1.58’s small memory and energy footprints open the door for on‑chip inference on CPU‑based or low‑power GPUs, bringing large‑scale language reasoning into the hands of smartphones or embedded systems. Because the weights are ternary, lookup tables and integer‑only shunting networks can be exploited to achieve still lower latency, far beyond what is achievable with modern floating‑point hardware.

**Custom Hardware for 1‑Bit LLMs.**  
The unique computation model of BitNet—matrix multiplication reduced to a combination of popcount and bit‑wise operations—falls neatly into a different design space than conventional floating‑point FPGAs or ASICs. The paper envisions specifically-tailored processing elements that optimize for integer arithmetic and ternary weight handling, with the promise of orders‑of‑magnitude improvements in energy efficiency (71.4× lower arithmetic energy for 7 nm chips) and throughput. This hardware alignment is already evident in prototypes such as Groq’s Liquid Processing Units (LPUs), which have opened first‑hand experiences with integer‑optimised architectures for LLM inference.

**Training with an Unprecedented Token Budget.**  
Scaling the training token budget to two trillion tokens, as done in the proof‑of‑concept experiments, demonstrates that BitNet b1.58 does not just survive a longer training horizon; it actually leverages it more effectively than its FP16 counterpart. This suggests that the 1.58‑bit model retains its inductive biases and generalization even when exposed to massively larger datasets, thereby reinforcing the notion that future LLMs can increasingly rely on large‑scale data-driven training without requiring a proportional increase in parameter budget.

In sum, the empirical evidence gathered for the BitNet b1.58 architecture articulates a new pathway: scale in terms of *effective* parameters rather than raw precision, harness integer‑only arithmetic to neutralise cost, and design in harmony with future hardware that can fully exploit this paradigm. The chapter’s promise to expose the intersection of scaling laws and resource economics is thus fulfilled, offering a clear signpost for researchers and engineers who aim to push the boundaries of language models while staying mindful of environmental and operational constraints.

8. Discussion, Future Work, and Conclusion  

The exploration of ultra‑low‑bit representations for large language models (LLMs) has revealed an unexpected balance between computational efficiency and model performance. In the preceding chapters we introduced **BitNet b1.58**, a transformer that stores every weight as one of the ternary values \{-1, 0, 1\} and uses an 8‑bit activation space. This design quadruples the speed, halves the memory, and cuts the energy per inference by more than an order of magnitude while matching the perplexity and end‑task performance of full‑precision FP16 models once the model size reaches three billion parameters. In this chapter we analyze the broader implications of this breakthrough, outline promising directions for future research, and highlight the open challenges that remain.

# 1‑bit Mixture‑of‑Experts: memory and communication advantages  
Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures distribute the workload of an LLM across a set of sparsely activated expert modules. By routing each token to only a handful of experts, MoE reduce the FLOPs per token. However, the approach is traditionally limited by **memory footprint** and **inter‑chip communication**: the large number of experts, each with its own weight matrices, typically requires a cluster of GPUs or TPUs, and moving the large intermediate activations between devices adds significant latency and power overhead. The **1‑bit quantization** of BitNet b1.58 dramatically shrinks the size of each expert: each weight is stored using only 1.58 bits instead of 16. Because the expert size is proportional to the number of parameters, the memory reduction directly translates into fewer network‑connected devices being required to host a MoE model. Moreover, the transition cost for activations falls dramatically when the weight tensor itself is very small. In principle, a 1‑bit MoE could be assembled on a single chip, eliminating the need for cross‑chip communication entirely. The reduced memory also lessens the pressure on off‑chip DRAM bandwidth, which is often the bottleneck for MoE inference on server‑grade hardware. The paper’s discussion therefore suggests that the very same low‑bit representation that powers BitNet b1.58 simultaneously addresses the two critical limitations that have held back MoE scaling.

# Native long‑sequence support through reduced activation precision  
One of the most pressing challenges in modern LLM inference is supporting **long context windows**. In a decoder‑style transformer, the “key–value” (KV) cache that stores all past hidden states grows linearly with the number of processed tokens. Store‑the‑whole‑cache in FP16 already demands significant on‑device memory for deep models. BitNet b1.58 runs with 8‑bit activations, and the paper explicitly notes that activations are **scaled to the interval $[-Q_b,\,Q_b]$ without a zero‑point offset**. This design choice removes the extra storage required for zero‑point values and fits neatly into conventional integer‑only memory hierarchies. By halving the number of bits per activation, the KV cache occupies roughly half the memory of an FP16 counterpart. With the same hardware budget, the context length can effectively be doubled, allowing the model to attend over longer sequences without increasing latency or memory footprint. The authors also point out that this representation can be **losslessly compressed further to 4‑bit or lower** for 1.58‑bit LLMs, opening the door to even larger effective context windows if engineering efforts reduce the precision of at‑most lower‑significant bits without sacrificing performance.

# Feasibility of edge and mobile deployment  
The previous sections described how 1.58‑bit LLMs cut the energy requirement for a matrix multiplication **by roughly seventy‑one times** when measured against FP16 on a 7 nm process (Figure 4). That magnitude of savings is not merely academic: real‑world applications on mobile or edge devices are typically bounded by battery life, thermal budget, and limited local memory. BitNet b1.58 achieves **3.55‑fold memory savings** relative to an FP16 baseline, enabling a larger model (or more parallel inference) to fit into the on‑chip SRAM of a mobile AI accelerator. The training-from‑scratch paradigm, coupled with the reduced precision of both weights and activations, implies that the same GPU or ASIC pipeline used for inference can be reused for fine‑tuning without needing a separate high‑precision workspace. Finally, the model’s integer‑only kernel (composed almost entirely of INT8 add operations) is well suited to the cores of CPUs, which dominate edge and mobile platforms. The paper warns that **energy consumption is a fundamental bottleneck** for many chips, so hardware that can exploit the proposed computation paradigm would bring LLMs within the feasible envelope of consumer devices.

# Design considerations for new 1‑bit ASICs and LPUs  
The efficacy of 1‑bit LLMs hinges on a new calculation strategy: **matrix multiplication replaces multiply‑accumulate operations with integer addition chains**. Standard GPUs and TPUs are heavily tuned for mixed‑precision FP, so their compute units contain a large supply of DSPs for floating‑point multiplication. In contrast, a dedicated **1‑bit accelerator** would only require an array of little or even zero‑overhead accumulators that can latch the addition of two ternary operands. Existing work such as Groq’s **LPUs** (Learning Processing Units) has demonstrated a lightweight design that can process an entire transformer layer in a single clock cycle. Building on this example, a 1‑bit LPU would include:

1. **Ternary weight memory banks** that store –1, 0, +1 in a minimal radix‑3 representation, allowing per‑value strides of just a few bits.
2. **High‑bandwidth on‑chip SRAM** catering to activation volumes that are already eight bits, thereby reducing data movement.
3. **Simple yet effective kernels** that collapse an entire attention or feed‑forward sub‑layer into a chain of integer adds, drastically lowering DRAM traffic.
4. **Energy‑aware scheduling** that leverages the fact that addition is orders of magnitude cheaper than multiplication.

The paper explicitly calls for the design of such hardware, arguing that this novel computation paradigm unlocks an otherwise unattainable combination of cost‑efficiency and throughput.

# Potential for further compression to 4 bits or lower  
While 1.58‑bit quantization already achieves a remarkable strand between precision and efficiency, the authors speculate that **lossless compression to 4 bits** (or even fewer) could be accomplished for the underlying 1.58‑bit LLMs. By grouping weights or activations into larger units, a hardware design might treat two or three 1.58‑bit values as a single 4‑bit code. Since the ternary set contains a zero, packing multiple such values allows the compressor to omit encoding of zeros, giving rise to a highly efficient scheme. The claim that “this can be further losslessly compressed to 4 bits or even lower for 1.58‑bit LLMs” suggests that a carefully engineered codec would keep the effective model accuracy unchanged while aligning the storage format neatly with existing 4‑bit micro‑architectures. Should such compression be realized, the memory savings would double again, and the throughput gains would close the gap toward a fully **constant‑time** inference per token on commodity hardware.

# Summary of contributions and open research challenges  
Through the lens of BitNet b1.58, the paper demonstrates a **new scaling law** for LLMs that combines ultra‑low‑bit quantization with scalable performance. Key take‑aways include:

1. **Weight ternarization to \{-1, 0, 1\}**, effectively yielding a 1.58‑bit representation, that matches FP16 perplexity from 3 B parameters upward and fully tolerates the inclusion of zeros for feature filtering.
2. **Active 8‑bit activation quantization** that frees the KV cache from excessive memory use and affords native support for longer sequences.
3. **Substantial speed‑ups** (e.g., 2.71× faster for 3 B models), **memory reductions** (≥3×), and **energy savings** (71× for matrix multiplication) that scale gracefully with model size.
4. A **new compute paradigm** that eschews multiplications entirely, opening the door to ASIC or LPU designs optimized purely for integer addition.

Open research challenges, as highlighted by the authors, include:

- **Extending 1‑bit MoE**: Exploring how sparsity and quantization interplay to deliver even larger models while keeping inter‑expert communication minimal.
- **Long‑sequence optimizations**: Developing lossless compression and dynamic memory allocation schemes to fully exploit the reduced activation precision.
- **Edge deployment pipelines**: Building end‑to‑end toolchains that can transfer from a 1‑bit training pipeline to a mobile‑friendly inference runtime.
- **Hardware co‑design**: Determining optimal trade‑offs between weight and activation footprint, memory bandwidth, and compute block efficiency for a 1‑bit accelerator.
- **Compression limits**: Precisely characterizing the theoretical and practical boundaries for packing several 1.58‑bit values into a 4‑bit or even lower word without inducing bias or variance in inference.
- **Long‑term sustainability**: Understanding how such aggressive quantization affects data‑centric concerns such as fairness, robustness to adversarial prompts, and alignment with regulatory norms.

In closing, BitNet b1.58 illustrates that **extreme quantization does not inevitably harm performance**; on the contrary, it can provoke a virtuous cycle where computation, memory, and energy constraints are simultaneously satisfied. By pursuing the directions outlined above, the field can move toward LLMs that are not just larger or faster, but truly **efficiency‑optimized, hardware‑aware, and ready for deployment across a spectrum of devices**.
