\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What does 1.58-bit mean for ternary weights in BitNet b1.58?
Question:
In the abstract of the paper, it is mentioned that every weight of the model is ternary \{-1, 0, +1\} and the resulting bit‑width is 1.58 bits. How is this value calculated? Why not simply say 1.58 bits or 2 bits? Moreover, does the presence of the zero value effectively reduce the information content compared to a binary \{-1, +1\} representation? I would appreciate a short mathematical justification for the 1.58‑bit claim and an intuition behind the effect of the zero on the memory footprint.
Answer:
**Why the paper says “1.58‑bit” for a ternary ‑1/0/+1 weight**

In an LLM each scalar weight is replaced by one of the three symbols  
\(\{-1,0,+1\}\).  From an information‑theoretic point of view the
amount of information carried by a random variable that can take on
\(k\) equiprobable values is

\[
H = \log_{2}k\;\text{bits}.
\]

For a ternary alphabet \(k=3\), giving  

\[
H = \log_{2}3 \;\approx\; 1.585 \;\text{bits}.
\]

The paper therefore calls the effective bit‑width of a ternary
weight **1.58 bits**: on average, one weight needs only
\(1.585\) bits to encode it if the three values occur with equal
frequency.  In practice the distribution of weights is not uniform,
so the actual average entropy can be smaller (e.g. if zeros dominate
the weight set the entropy drops below 1.58).  Nevertheless
\( \log_2 3 \) is the natural upper bound for the *information* that a
ternary weight can hold, and it is the figure the authors use to
compare against binary (1 bit) or full‑precision (16 bits) models.

**Why not just say “2 bits” or “1.58 bits”?**

* 2 bits is the **actual storage requirement** on conventional
   hardware.  A two‑bit code (e.g. 00 → −1, 01 → 0, 10 → +1, 11
   unused) allows each weight to be written in SRAM or DRAM using
   exactly two bits.  A purely binary representation (±1 only)
   would use just one bit.

* 1.58 bits is a **theoretical information‑content figure** that
  highlights how much memory can be saved *in principle* if the
  ternary weights are compressed efficiently (e.g. with an optimal
  entropy coder).  It is not the amount of memory a particular
  implementation will actually occupy, but it gives a benchmark for
  comparing the bit‑efficiency of ternary models against binary or
  full‑precision baselines.

  In other words, the paper advertises a *theoretical* bit‑width that
  captures the savings over a binary model, while acknowledging that
  the *practical* implementation will normally use two bits per
  weight.

**Does the zero value reduce the information content relative to a
binary weight?**

Adding the symbol “0” does not *reduce* the theoretical entropy of
the ternary set; it merely makes the alphabet larger.  If the three
symbols are equiprobable, the entropy rises from 1 bit (binary) to
\( \log_2 3 \approx 1.58 \) bits (ternary).  In practice, however,
zero weights often appear more frequently than the non‑zero ones
because training drives many weights toward zero.  If, say,  
\(p(0)=0.6\) and the other two values share the remaining 0.4 mass
equally, the entropy is

\[
H = -0.6\log_2 0.6 - 0.2\log_2 0.2 - 0.2\log_2 0.2
   \; \approx \; 1.29 \;\text{bits},
\]

which is *below* \( \log_2 3 \).  Thus the actual average bits per
weight can be substantially less than 1.58 due to sparsity.

From a **memory‑footprint** perspective, if you store every weight
with exactly two bits, the total memory is \(2N\) bits for \(N\)
weights.  If you compress all weights using optimal entropy coding,
the expected memory becomes \(HN\) bits, where \(H\) is the entropy
computed above.  For \(H=1.58\) you save about \(21\%\) compared to a
two‑bit implementation, and when zeros dominate you can save even
more.  Moreover, zeros enable the hardware to skip the corresponding
additions, further cutting arithmetic energy.

**Summary**

| Representation | Storage per weight | Effective bits (entropy) |
|----------------|--------------------|--------------------------|
| Binary \{-1,+1\} | 1 bit | 1.00 bit |
| Ternary \{-1,0,+1\} (uniform) | 2 bits | 1.58 bits |
| Ternary \{-1,0,+1\} (zeros frequent) | 2 bits | \< 1.58 bits |
| Full‑precision FP16 | 16 bits | – |

The “1.58‑bit” label therefore means: **the information content of a
ternary weight is equivalent to that of a symbol that can take one of
three equiprobable values**.  In practice the model is stored in two
bits per weight, but the effective memory and computational cost are
close to the theoretical 1.58‑bit figure, especially when many
weights are zero.  This explains why the authors choose a wording
between *binary* and *two‑bit* and why adding the zero value both
improves sparsity (lowering the actual average entropy) and
preserves a more compact representation than a naive binary
encoding.
