\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the absmean quantization function preserve gradient flow during training?
Question:
The paper introduces an \texttt{absmean} quantization where the weight matrix $W$ is first divided by its mean absolute value $\gamma$ and then rounded to $\{-1,0,1\}$. I am unclear about how gradients are propagated through this step during back‑propagation. Do the authors employ a straight‑through estimator (STE)? Why is $\gamma$ defined as the mean absolute value rather than, say, the maximum norm? How does this choice influence the stability of training and the learned scaling factor?
Answer:
The absmean quantization used in **BitNet b1.58** is a two‑step process:

1. **Rescaling** \( \widehat{W} = W / (\gamma+\varepsilon) \)  
2. **Clipping and rounding** \( \widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}(\widehat{W},-1,1) \)

where  
\[
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|
\]
is the mean absolute value of all \(nm\) weights in the tensor.

Because the rounding operation is non‑differentiable, the authors use the common **straight‑through estimator (STE)** that replaces the invisible derivative of the quantization by the identity.  In practice the backward pass is implemented as

\[
\frac{\partial\!\mathcal{L}}{\partial W}
   \;\approx\;
   \frac{\partial\!\mathcal{L}}{\partial \widetilde{W}}\;
   \frac{1}{\gamma+\varepsilon},
\]
i.e. the gradient flowing into the quantized weights is propagated unchanged and only divided by the scaling factor that was used in the forward pass.  The clipping bound \([-1,1]\) is *not* taken into account during the backward pass; the STE simply treats the quantization as an identity mapping for the purpose of gradient flow.  The same STE trick is typically used in other low‑bit quantization papers (e.g. DoReFa, LSQ, BinaryConnect, etc.), and it is also the strategy adopted in the original BitNet implementation.

---

### Why use the mean absolute value \(\gamma\) instead of a maximum norm?

1. **Avoiding outliers.**  
   A maximum‑norm scaling would be determined by a single, possibly very large, weight.  This would keep almost all other weights in the \([-1,1]\) range after division, which in turn would make them all quantized to 0 in the early stages of training and reduce the representational capacity of the layer.  The mean absolute value, on the other hand, captures the *typical* magnitude of the weights and scales the whole tensor so that, on average, a significant portion of the entries lands in the three bins \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).

2. **Differentiability.**  
   The mean absolute value is a smooth function of every weight:  
   \[
   \frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial W_{ij}}=\frac{1}{nm}\;{\rm sign}(W_{ij}).
   \]
   Using the maximum would introduce a piecewise‑defined function that is not differentiable wherever the largest weight changes, thereby complicating gradient‑based training.

3. **Statistical stability.**  
   Since \(\gamma\) changes gradually during training, the effective quantization step size
   \(\Delta = 1/(\gamma+\varepsilon)\) adapts smoothly to the evolving weight distribution.  This keeps the quantization noise roughly constant across layers and prevents abrupt jumps that could destabilise training.

---

### Influence on training stability and the learnt scaling

The rescaling by \(\gamma\) essentially normalises each weight tensor to have an *average* absolute value of approximately one after division.  Because the rounding then limits the number of possible values to \(-1,0,+1\), the optimisation process has to shape the distribution of the raw weights so that most of them lie close to the clipping boundaries.  This pushes the weights toward a balanced ternary set which is well suited for the 1‑bit integer arithmetic that the hardware and lower‑cost kernels assume.

From an optimisation perspective, the presence of \(\gamma\) guarantees that the step size used in the round operation is neither vanishingly small (which would freeze the weights to 0) nor excessively large (which would push almost all weights to the boundary ±1 and leave no room for learning).  Empirically, using the mean absolute value leads to a more regular loss curve and faster convergence compared with a maximum‑norm scaling, as seen in the training curves reported for the 1‑bit models in the paper.

---

In summary, **absmean quantization preserves gradient flow via the standard straight‑through estimator, treating the round‑clip operation as the identity in the backward pass and dividing the upstream gradient by the mean‑absolute scaling factor.**  The mean‑absolute choice for \(\gamma\) ensures a stable, differentiable, and representative scaling that keeps the ternary quantization balanced throughout training and allows the network to learn a well‑behaved weight distribution that is amenable to efficient integer‑only inference.
