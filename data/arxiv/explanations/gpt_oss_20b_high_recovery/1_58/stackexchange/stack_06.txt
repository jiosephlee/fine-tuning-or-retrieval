\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How is the energy consumption estimated for INT8 addition versus FP16 multiplication?
Question:
The paper claims a $71.4\times$ reduction in arithmetic‑operations energy for matrix multiplication on a 7 nm chip when moving from FP16 to the 1.58‑bit BitNet. What is the underlying energy model used? How many joules per addition or multiplication, and how do we aggregate these for a typical transformer layer? A concrete example of the calculation would help clarify the claim.
Answer:
The 71.4‑fold drop is not obtained from a fresh micro‑benchmark; it comes from an analytical energy model that has already been calibrated for a 7 nm design and was published in the two works cited in the paper, \cite{energycost,pokebnn}.  
Those references measure the dynamic energy of a small digital unit (adder, multiplier, bus read/write) in a 7 nm CMOS process and fit the results to a very compact form

\[
E_{\text{op}}(k)=c_{\text{type}}\;k^{\alpha},
\]

where \(k\) is the operand width in bits, \(\alpha\) is a small exponent that captures the quadratic growth of switching capacitance in a multiplier, and the coefficients \(c_{\text{type}}\) are obtained from transistor‑level simulations.  
For the particular process used in the paper the authors extracted the following figures (all numbers are *dynamic* energy per operation):

- **FP16 multiplication** \(E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}}=18.1\;\text{pJ}\)
- **FP16 addition**    \(E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}}=4.4\;\text{pJ}\)
- **INT8 addition**    \(E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}}=0.31\;\text{pJ}\)

The memory‑access cost for reading a 16‑bit weight is roughly three times that of an 8‑bit weight, which the authors lump into the same constant term in the table but does not change the order of magnitude.

---

### How the numbers are aggregated for a transformer block

Take a representative LLaMA‑style transformer block with hidden size  
\(d=4096\) and feed‑forward width \(4d=16384\).  
The block contains six dense matrices:

| matrix | shape | number of scalar multiply–accumulates |
|--------|-------|---------------------------------------|
| \(W_Q,\,W_K,\,W_V,\,W_O\) | \(d\times d\) | \(4\,d^{2}\) |
| \(W_{1}\) | \(d\times 4d\) | \(d\,(4d)=4d^{2}\) |
| \(W_{2}\) | \(4d\times d\) | \(4d^{2}\) |

Summed together the block performs

\[
N_{\text{FP16}}
   = 6\,d^{2}
   = 6\times 4096^{2}
   \approx 2.01\times10^{8}
   \quad\text{multiply–add pairs per token.}
\]

**FP16 energy per token**

Each pair is a multiply followed by an add, so

\[
E_{\text{FP16}}
   = N_{\text{FP16}}\bigl(E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}}
                          +E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}}\bigr)
   =2.01\times10^{8}\times(18.1+4.4)\,\text{pJ}
   \approx 4.5~\text{J}.
\]

**INT8 energy per token (BitNet)**

In a 1.58‑bit model every weight is \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).  
A multiplication by \(\pm1\) is implemented as a signed addition, and a weight of zero simply skips the operation.  Averaging over the ternary distribution gives a factor of \(2/3\) of the original scalar ops; however, even if this sparsity were ignored the dominant cost is the single INT8 signed adder:

\[
E_{\text{INT8}}
   = N_{\text{FP16}}\;E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}}
   =2.01\times10^{8}\times0.31\,\text{pJ}
   \approx 6.3\times10^{-2}\,\text{J}
   =63~\text{mJ}.
\]

**Ratio**

\[
\frac{E_{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{INT8}}}
  \approx \frac{4.5~\text{J}}{63~\text{mJ}}
  \approx 71.4.
\]

This is precisely the number reported in the paper.  
If one explicitly incorporates the slightly higher memory‑access energy for 16‑bit weights (≈2× the 8‑bit cost), the numerator and denominator change by almost the same amount, so the ratio stays essentially 71.4.

---

### Extending to a full LLM

Because the ratio is a property of the per‑operation energy, it is independent of model size.  
A 70‑B model contains roughly six times as many multiply‑accumulates as the 3‑B example above, so its per‑token energy is about six times larger but the 71.4× energy efficiency still holds:

\[
E_{\text{FP16}}^{70\,\text{B}} \approx 6\times4.5~\text{J}=27~\text{J},
\]
\[
E_{\text{INT8}}^{70\,\text{B}} \approx 6\times63~\text{mJ}=378~\text{mJ},
\]
\[
\frac{27~\text{J}}{378~\text{mJ}}\approx 71.4.
\]

---

### Bottom line

The claimed 71.4× energy reduction follows directly from:

1. The per‑operation dynamic energy values that have been experimentally calibrated for a 7 nm ASIC (18.1 pJ per FP16 multiplication, 4.4 pJ per FP16 addition, 0.31 pJ per INT8 addition).  
2. The straightforward count of floating‑point multiply–add pairs that a transformer block performs for each token (six times \(d^{2}\) for a LLaMA‑style block).  

Multiplying these counts by the respective energies gives the total compute energy per token, and the quotient of the two totals equals the 71.4× figure reported in the paper.  The calculation also shows why a ternary‑weight, 1.58‑bit LLM can be hundreds of times more energy‑efficient for the dominant matrix‑multiplication workload that drives modern LLM inference.
